## ABSTRACT
A model's failures can reveal gaps in the behavioral criteria guiding its training. We introduce RubricOpt, a framework that lets a policy revise these criteria within preference optimization. The policy diagnoses its own rubric-free outputs, retains requirements already covered by the rubric, and edits clauses that leave recurring failures unaddressed. A shared semantic representation connects task demands, preference-data contrasts, and rubric clauses, while a deterministic gate checks evidence references and fixed semantic anchors. The rubric then guides Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) during training, with preference pairs, labels, and the objective held fixed, and is removed at evaluation. Our analysis relates the local effect on rubric-free success to semantic alignment and a residual beyond the measured factors. Experiments on capability and open-ended generation benchmarks show that rubric conditioning can improve outcomes over ordinary DPO, with gains and regressions varying across models and datasets. A matched continuation isolates the contribution of revision from additional training and finds mixed incremental effects for the tested edit, whose feedback overlaps the evaluation set. RubricOpt thus brings the behavioral specification itself into the policy's feedback loop.

## 1 Introduction

Language models can supply rewards for their own iterative preference training (Yuan et al., 2024).
They can also improve their judgment skills by assessing their own judgments (Wu et al., 2025a).
The corresponding question for semantic training rubrics is how revising their behavioral requirements affects learned behavior, including when a revision yields gains beyond continuing training with the existing rubric.

Written rubrics specify desired response properties and can supply reward signals (Gunjal et al., 2025) or guide the construction of synthetic preference data (Gallego, 2025).
EvoRubrics jointly trains a policy and a rubric generator to adapt rubric-based rewards as learning proceeds (Ding et al., 2026).
SERPO co-evolves response evidence, query-specific rubrics, and policy parameters, using rubric scores as rewards for test-time reinforcement learning (Wang et al., 2026a).
We study rubric revision in Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) (Rafailov et al., 2023), holding the pairs, labels, and objective fixed while changing the rubric context.
A matched continuation with the existing rubric measures the edit’s incremental contribution.

We introduce RubricOpt to study how a model revises its own training rubric across preference-training rounds (Fig. 1).
Fine-grained evaluation describes response quality through explicit skills (Ye et al., 2024).
We use a fixed eight-factor ontology to connect the model’s task failures, the contrasts in its preference data, and the rubric’s Prefer/Avoid clauses.
Diagnosis-guided self-evolution uses failure histories to direct subsequent learning (Wei et al., 2026).
RubricOpt applies this diagnostic idea to rubric revision, with the current policy reading task outcomes, self-judged factor scores, and judge feedback on its own outputs to attribute recurring failures to the eight factors.
It retains explicit requirements for further learning and proposes clause edits where the wording leaves recurring failures uncovered.
A deterministic gate checks evidence references, factor anchors, and edit size before carrying the rubric into the next round.
Training-only Semantic Rubric Steering uses the resulting rubric to condition both responses of a preference pair during DPO (Rafailov et al., 2023), with the pairs, labels, and objective held fixed.
Its exposure decreases during training, and evaluation uses the resulting policy without the rubric.
This use of training-only information has a precedent in privileged-information learning (Vapnik & Vashist, 2009).
Our analysis relates the extra update induced by a rubric to rubric-free success through its alignment with task demand (Sec. 3.3).

The experiments evaluate how changes to the training specification affect comprehensive capability and open-ended generation.
Fixed-state probes and criterion comparisons show that rubric text changes the learning update and can alter rubric-free outcomes (Sec. 4.1).
Across model families and preference corpora, rubric conditioning improves some capability and generation evaluations over ordinary DPO, with gains and regressions varying by setting (Tabs. 1 and 2).
The dataset comparison relates these effect profiles to the behavioral contrasts available in each corpus (Sec. 4.2).
A matched continuation separates revision from further optimization and finds mixed incremental results from the tested edit (Tab. 3).

RubricOpt makes the behavioral specification itself an object of policy-driven revision within preference learning.
Shared semantic coordinates connect failure diagnosis and preference-data contrasts to editable rubric clauses, while training-only steering connects those clauses to subsequent parameter updates.
This closes the loop from learned behavior to the specification of further learning, with the effect of revision evaluated independently of additional training.

## 2 Related Work

## Preference Optimization and Credit Assignment.

The family descends from RLHF, in which a reward model is fit to pairwise human judgements (Stiennon et al., 2020) and a policy is then optimised against it at assistant scale (Ouyang et al., 2022; Bai et al., 2022).
DPO instead fits chosen–rejected pairs with a classification objective (Rafailov et al., 2023), whose implicit reward is the log density ratio between the policy and the reference (Sugiyama et al., 2012; Chen et al., 2025).
Later objectives change one component of that recipe: IPO replaces it with a squared loss on the pairwise margin (Azar et al., 2024), KTO drops the pairing requirement by scoring each response against a reference point (Ethayarajh et al., 2024), and SimPO removes the reference model and normalises the margin by length (Meng et al., 2024).
Two analyses place the family in a wider frame: one connects these objectives to imitation learning (Xiao et al., 2025), and one disentangles how their update suppresses the rejected response and preserves the chosen one (Chen et al., 2026).
A complementary line assigns credit inside a response rather than to the response as a whole: TDPO reweights tokens by a forward-KL penalty (Zeng et al., 2024), TIS-DPO reweights them by importance sampling against an estimated weight (Liu et al., 2025), SEPO selects tokens using a token-level reward estimate (Yang et al., 2025b), T-REG uses a token-level reward as a regulariser (Zhou et al., 2025), and TIDPO guides the update by token importance (Yang et al., 2026).
RubricOpt operates at a different level: a semantic factor names a behavioral distinction that can span several tokens or reasoning steps, while the pairwise loss itself is left unchanged.

## Criteria and Behavioral Diagnosis.

Written criteria already enter learning through several interfaces.
As reward signals, Rubrics as Rewards scores on-policy responses against a rubric (Gunjal et al., 2025).
As data, Configurable Preference Tuning builds rubric-conditioned synthetic preference pairs (Gallego, 2025).
And as weights, CoRT contrasts rubric-conditioned with rubric-free replays to form token-level weights (Zhang et al., 2026).
Criterion-conditioned training also has close relatives outside preference optimisation: learning using privileged information trains on a signal that is absent at test time (Vapnik & Vashist, 2009).
Context distillation trains on a context and then evaluates without it, introduced as an alignment technique (Askell et al., 2021) and developed as a standalone method (Snell et al., 2022).
And classifier-free guidance interpolates between conditioned and unconditioned predictions (Ho & Salimans, 2022).
On the diagnostic side, fine-grained evaluation describes response quality through interpretable skill sets and error taxonomies—FLASK by grading along skill dimensions (Ye et al., 2024), EvalTree by profiling weaknesses as a hierarchical capability tree (Zeng et al., 2025), and ErrorMap and ErrorAtlas by charting the failure landscape into error categories (Ashury-Tahan et al., 2026)—and diagnosis-driven post-training then acts on what those tools find, with DiagEvo feeding diagnosed errors back into self-evolution through a hierarchical error memory (Wei et al., 2026) and APTER turning expert-grounded rubrics into post-training supervision (Wang et al., 2026b).
RubricOpt uses shared semantic coordinates to connect task diagnosis, preference-data contrasts, and edits to the training rubric.

## Self-Improvement and Recursive Feedback.

Self-improvement methods differ in the component they revise and how that revision enters subsequent behavior.
Self-Refine edits outputs from self-feedback (Madaan et al., 2023), and automatic prompt optimisation edits inference-time text from feedback (Pryzant et al., 2023).
In parameter learning, Self-Rewarding uses the model to generate its own reward signals (Yuan et al., 2024), Meta-Rewarding trains judgment skills through a meta-judge (Wu et al., 2025a), and self-play preference optimisation regenerates preferences (Wu et al., 2025b).
Rubric evolution also enters the reward interface through joint policy–generator learning (Ding et al., 2026) and co-evolving query-specific rubrics during test-time reinforcement learning (Wang et al., 2026a).
RubricOpt places self-revision in the semantic rubric that conditions preference training.
The current policy proposes clause edits from feedback about its own behavior, then learns under the resulting rubric in the next round.
The initial clause bank, factor ontology, task outcomes, and fixed scoring prompt provide the reference points for revision.

## 3 RubricOpt: Method and Analysis

RubricOpt alternates preference learning under a written rubric with revision of that rubric by the resulting policy.

## Problem Setup.

We study preference optimization on a fixed dataset $\mathcal{D}=\{({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}}^{+},{\bm{y}}^{-})\}$ of chosen–rejected pairs, with a trainable policy $\pi_{\theta}$ and a frozen reference $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$ trained by DPO (Rafailov et al., 2023),

 $\mathcal{L}=-\mathbb{E}\left[\log\sigma\left(\beta\left[\bar{\ell}_{\theta}({\bm{y}}^{+}\mid{\bm{x}})-\bar{\ell}_{\mathrm{ref}}({\bm{y}}^{+}\mid{\bm{x}})-\bar{\ell}_{\theta}({\bm{y}}^{-}\mid{\bm{x}})+\bar{\ell}_{\mathrm{ref}}({\bm{y}}^{-}\mid{\bm{x}})\right]\right)\right],$ 

(1)

with $\bar{\ell}({\bm{y}}\mid{\bm{x}})=L^{-1}\log\pi({\bm{y}}\mid{\bm{x}})$ averaged over the $L$ response tokens.
The pairs, labels, and objective are held fixed; only the text that conditions a pair may change, which is the degree of freedom RubricOpt introduces.

Behavior is described by a fixed eight-factor ontology $\mathcal{F}=\{f_{1},\ldots,f_{8}\}$ (appendix A.2), on which a blind judge returns one ordinal score per factor, $z_{k}({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})\in\{0,\ldots,4\}$ (appendix A.3).
Every behavioral quantity here is a contrast between two groups of these scores, and only two contrasts are used.
Responses to a benchmark $B$ are grouped by the task outcome $o\in\{0,1\}$ , and semantic demand is the mean score gap between the groups, measured for one model on one task,

 $D_{m,B,k}=\mathbb{E}[z_{k}\mid o=1,m,B]-\mathbb{E}[z_{k}\mid o=0,m,B].$ 

(2)

Inside a preference pair the two responses are contrasted by $\Delta z_{k}=z_{k}({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}}^{+})-z_{k}({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}}^{-})$ , and semantic supply summarizes its distribution,

 $\mu_{k}=\mathbb{E}[\Delta z_{k}],\qquad C_{k}=\Pr(\Delta z_{k}\neq 0),\qquad P_{k}=\Pr(\Delta z_{k}>0),$ 

(3)

as the mean signed contrast, the contrast coverage, and the share of pairs whose contrast agrees with the label.
Demand is a property of the model–task pair and supply a property of the dataset, and both live on the same eight axes.

The artifact RubricOpt edits is a rubric $R_{r}$ , a short natural-language specification whose clauses are keyed to factors, with $r$ indexing rounds and a frozen clause bank supplying the initial $R_{0}$ .
For factor group $\mathcal{G}\subseteq\mathcal{F}$ the training context is

 $c_{r}=\operatorname{render}(R_{r},\mathcal{G}),$ 

(4)

where the renderer concatenates the current Prefer/Avoid clauses in the fixed factor order and $\varnothing$ denotes the absent criterion.
Here $\mathcal{G}=\mathcal{F}$ except in the factor-set ablation of Sec. 4.2.
A conditioning context is therefore $u\in\{c_{r},\varnothing\}$ , $\pi_{\theta}({\bm{y}}\mid{\bm{x}},u)$ is the policy under that context, the main evaluations use $\pi_{\theta}({\bm{y}}\mid{\bm{x}})$ , and Sec. 4.2 tests rubric reinsertion at inference time.
Appendix A.1 collects the symbols used throughout.

## 3.1 Diagnosis and Conditioning

## Shared Behavioral Coordinates.

With the coordinates of Sec. 3 fixed, the diagnosis is a measurement on one model and one dataset: we score responses from Qwen3-4B on each benchmark and each HelpSteer3 pair, then read out demand $D_{m,B,k}$ (Eq. 2) and supply $\mu_{k}$ , $C_{k}$ , $P_{k}$ (Eq. 3).
We mark demand gaps of at least $0.15$ on the $0$ – $4$ scale as meaningful and contrast coverage below $0.25$ as sparse.
Tab. 7 reports the complete Qwen3-4B demand matrix. Judge checks and length controls appear in appendices A.3 and D.1.

The measurement separates the factors into Common ( $f_{2}$ reasoning validity, $f_{5}$ completeness), Specific ( $f_{4}$ factual grounding, $f_{7}$ clarity, $f_{8}$ efficiency), and Low-demand ( $f_{1}$ comprehension, $f_{3}$ verification, $f_{6}$ instruction adherence).
The shared factor ontology links task diagnosis, preference-data contrasts, and clause-level feedback.
All eight factors enter the initial rubric from the frozen clause bank.
The demand and supply measurements characterize the learning setting; recurring failures whose requirements are incompletely expressed determine subsequent wording edits.

## Training-Only Conditioning.

Each round fixes $R_{r}$ and its full-factor context $c_{r}=\operatorname{render}(R_{r},\mathcal{F})$ .

## 3.2 Evidence-Grounded Recursive Refinement

Given rubric $R_{r}$ , the policy trained with its rendered context $c_{r}$ , and item-level benchmark feedback $\mathcal{B}_{r+1}$ , one iteration is

 $R_{r}\longrightarrow c_{r}\longrightarrow\pi_{r+1}\longrightarrow\mathcal{B}_{r+1}\longrightarrow R_{r+1}.$ 

(6)

Five prompt stages extract benchmark failures, synthesize recurring patterns, attribute them to the eight factors, propose conservative clause updates, and audit the candidate rubric.
The policy $\pi_{r+1}$ performs this analysis and proposes the edits itself, using task outcomes, self-judged factor scores, and fixed-judge feedback on its own rubric-free outputs.
Attribution compares each recurring failure with the current clauses. An explicit requirement is retained for further learning, while an incompletely expressed requirement is eligible for refinement.
A separate deterministic gate checks evidence references, factor anchors, and edit size before accepting a change.
A covered requirement or a failed check returns keep.
The accepted rubric is rendered as $c_{r+1}=\operatorname{render}(R_{r+1},\mathcal{G})$ for the next training round; keep retains the corresponding clauses from $R_{r}$ .
Full prompts, stage contracts, and the deterministic acceptance checks are in appendix C.2.

## CONCLUSION
RubricOpt connects policy self-evaluation to revision of the behavioral specification guiding preference learning.
Its semantic rubric translates failure feedback into training-time criteria while preference pairs, labels, and the optimization objective remain fixed.
Experiments across comprehensive capability and open-ended generation show that rubric semantics can shape rubric-free behavior, with benefits depending on the model and preference corpus.
The tested revision yields mixed incremental results over unchanged-rubric continuation.
This makes the behavioral specification itself adaptable through the policy’s feedback loop.

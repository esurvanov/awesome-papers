Authors: Meihong Jia, Yasemin Timar, Jonathan Taws
Comment: 14 pages, 10 images/figures

## ABSTRACT
This study introduces GuideEvolve, a self-evolving agent framework incorporating expert guidance and three autonomous regulatory mechanisms, collectively termed the 3Ts: Think Fast and Slow, Throwback, and Termination by choice. The framework addresses critical limitations in extant self-evolving agent paradigms through dual innovations: (1) a task-specific expert-evaluation-driven evolutionary pipeline, and (2) the integration of 3Ts mechanisms to facilitate cognitive optimization, performance recovery via temporal rollback, and adaptive termination protocols. Empirical validation across the MGSM and MMLU benchmarks demonstrates that GuideEvolve attains performance metrics comparable to current state-of-the-art models (89.8% accuracy versus Gödel Agent's 90.6% on MGSM; 89.1% versus 87.9% on MMLU), while exhibiting notably reduced variance (25-59%) and enhanced robustness. Furthermore, the framework achieves a 100% evolution success rate, superior to the baseline's 82%, diminishes required optimization iterations by approximately 50% (from 30 iterations in baseline to 15.1 and 13.4 across tasks), and provides improved stability for production deployment.

Abstract.

This study introduces GuideEvolve, a self-evolving agent framework incorporating expert guidance and three autonomous regulatory mechanisms—collectively termed the 3Ts: Think Fast and Slow, Throwback, and Termination by choice. The framework addresses critical limitations in extant self-evolving agent paradigms through dual innovations: (1) a task-specific expert-evaluation-driven evolutionary pipeline, and (2) the integration of 3Ts mechanisms to facilitate cognitive optimization, performance recovery via temporal rollback, and adaptive termination protocols.

Empirical validation across the MGSM and MMLU benchmarks demonstrates that GuideEvolve attains performance metrics comparable to current state-of-the-art models (89.8% accuracy versus Gödel Agent’s 90.6% on MGSM; 89.1% versus 87.9% on MMLU), while exhibiting notably reduced variance (25–59%) and enhanced robustness. Furthermore, the framework achieves a 100% evolution success rate—superior to the baseline’s 82%—and diminishes required optimization iterations by approximately 50% (from 30 iterations in baseline to 15.1 and 13.4 across tasks), and provides improved stability for production deployment.

## 1. Introduction

Self-evolving agents represent a significant advancement in autonomous AI systems, offering the potential for continuous self-improvement through iterative learning and adaptation. While the Gödel Agent framework (Yin et al., 2025) demonstrated promising results using OpenAI models, its practical implementation faces substantial challenges. This paper addresses these challenges through the lens of Amazon Bedrock and Claude models integration, proposing crucial innovations that enhance the robustness and reliability.

The original implementation using OpenAI models, while groundbreaking, exhibited significant stability issues with 4% of times experiencing unexpected termination during evolution process and 14% optimization failure, severely limiting its practical utility in production environments. When replicating the Gödel Agent in Bedrock using Claude models, we identified that the original framework’s evaluation feedback mechanism, which passes task questions and solver responses without optimization, frequently leads to immediate token exhaustion during the evolution process. These challenges are further compounded by Bedrock’s response formatting requirements, necessitating fundamental architectural adjustments for reliable operation.

To address these challenges, we introduce GuideEvolve agent, featuring two key innovations. First, we introduce a novel guided self-evolution framework, where a specialized LLM-based task expert provides analytical feedback to facilitate the agent’s autonomous improvement. The expert module generates task-specific evaluations and recommendations, which are then processed by the self-evolving agent to formulate optimization strategies for the solver. This hierarchical architecture maintains distinct contextual boundaries between evaluation logic and evolution control, enabling efficient token utilization while ensuring focused improvement pathways for task solutions. Notably, rather than implementing direct multi-agent interactions, this framework employs a unidirectional guidance mechanism where the expert’s analytical insights are systematically integrated into the agent’s self-evolving process, leading to iterative refinements in the solver’s operational logic. Second, we develop the 3Ts self-governing mechanisms for the self-evolving agent - Think Fast and Slow, Throwback, and Termination by choice - enabling agents to optimize their cognitive processing, retrieving previous best-performed logic for either recovery from deterioration or facilitate the brainstorm for further improvement on the solutions, and intelligently determine evolution endpoints.

Our framework is designed with practical applications in mind, particularly targeting enterprise use cases such as service improvement recommendations in AWS through Service360 product, and retail business strategy recommendations. By addressing the critical issues of convergence and stability, GuideEvolve agent represents a significant step toward making self-evolving agents viable for practical uses. Through comprehensive resource management and robust error handling, our framework transforms the challenges of Bedrock integration into opportunities for enhanced reliability and performance.

## 2. Background

Self-evolving agents represent an emerging paradigm in artificial intelligence that combines recursive self-improvement with large language models (LLMs). These systems aim to autonomously enhance their problem-solving capabilities through iterative refinement of their solution strategies. The theoretical foundations of self-evolving agents draw from several key areas in AI research. Constitutional AI frameworks (Bai et al., 2022) provide principles for maintaining reliable and controlled behavior in autonomous systems. Recent advances in AI planning and reasoning, such as Plan-and-Solve (Wang et al., 2023) and ReAct (Yao et al., 2023), offer insights into structuring decision-making processes. Additionally, developments in memory management and state tracking, exemplified by GraphRAG ((Han et al., 2024)) and IRCoT (Trivedi et al., 2022), provide crucial mechanisms for maintaining coherent evolution paths.

In the realm of self-evolving agent architectures, Gödel Agent (Yin et al., 2025) stands out as a pioneering framework for autonomous self-modification. Its self-referential architecture enables recursive code-level evolution, marking a significant advance in the field of architecturally mutable agent systems. Several related research efforts provide context for Gödel Agent’s approach. The Darwin Gödel Machine (Zhang et al., 2025) offers a similar paradigm for open-ended evolution, using version control to enable non-linear evolutionary paths. AgentSquare (Shang et al., 2025) takes a more constrained approach, optimizing modular components within a predefined design space. AlphaEvolve (Novikov et al., 2025) applies evolutionary principles to specific algorithmic domains, while ADAS (Hu et al., 2024a) and AFlow (Zhang et al., 2024) focus on higher-level agent system design through formal optimization methods.

Among these, Gödel Agent and the Darwin Gödel Machine represent the most comprehensive implementations of recursive self-improvement, which allow for fundamental structural modifications and demonstrate the field’s progress towards increasingly adaptable systems. However, current approaches often lack efficient evaluation mechanisms, leading to potential degradation in solution quality over iterations. The Gödel Agent framework, for example, reported stability issues with 4% of times experiencing unexpected termination and 14% optimization failure, highlighting the need for more reliable evolution control mechanisms. These challenges are particularly acute in enterprise environments, where system stability and consistent performance are paramount.

## 3. GuideEvolve Framework

The GuideEvolve framework introduces a self-evolution paradigm that strategically separates task expertise from evolution control, as shown in Figure 1. At its core, a self-evolving agent orchestrates the evolution process by coordinating between two specialized LLM-based modules: a solver module focused on task execution and an expert module dedicated to performance evaluation. This architectural design offers two significant advantages. First, it enables unbiased evaluation through an independent expert system that focuses solely on task-specific assessment without knowledge of the solver’s implementation details, while allowing the self-evolving agent to leverage its comprehensive visibility of the environment, historical performance, and solver logic to make informed evolution decisions. Second, this separation addresses the critical challenge of context management in evolution control: by delegating task-specific evaluation to a specialized expert module, the self-evolving agent can more efficiently process and integrate guidance while maintaining essential contextual information about the environment, goals, and evolution strategies. This design fundamentally addresses limitations in existing self-evolving frameworks, where the lack of specialized, task-specific guidance and context overflow often constrain evolution effectiveness.

Expert Evaluation Module. The expert module functions as a specialized analytical consultant, maintaining focused task-specific context derived from dataset descriptions and required capabilities. This module’s isolation from the solver’s implementation details and evolution state ensures purely objective assessment based on performance outcomes. The expert system processes strategically sampled success and failure cases, analyzing task questions and LLM responses to generate comprehensive performance insights. This stateless architecture serves dual purposes: it maintains evaluation objectivity through isolation from evolution mechanics while significantly reducing the context burden that would otherwise impact the self-evolving agent’s efficiency. By processing raw task-solver interactions independently, the expert system can generate focused, high-quality feedback without the overhead of evolution-related contextual information.

Guidance Integration and Evolution Mechanism. The framework implements a structured guidance pipeline that optimizes information flow between its core components. The expert evaluation module generates three critical forms of guidance: quantitative performance metrics, detailed strength/weakness analysis, and actionable insights derived from case studies. The self-evolving agent, as the central control unit, processes this concentrated feedback while maintaining its broader contextual awareness of the environment, historical evolution patterns, and solver behavior. This hierarchical architecture enables efficient information processing: while the evaluation module focuses exclusively on task-specific assessment and recommendations, the self-evolving agent synthesizes these insights with its comprehensive contextual knowledge to formulate targeted evolution strategies. The agent then translates this synthesized understanding into concrete modifications of the solver’s operational logic. This modular design not only ensures more focused and effective guidance but also allows the self-evolving agent to manage its context budget more efficiently, dedicating computational resources to critical evolution decisions rather than raw task evaluation processing. The clear separation of evaluation and evolution control enables the agent to maintain a comprehensive view of the evolution trajectory while receiving specialized task-specific guidance.

## 4. Understanding Guided Evolution Through an Optimization Lens

Our intuition for why expert guidance improves both efficiency and robustness is based on an analogy to optimization theory. While the self-evolution process is not
literally gradient descent, viewing it through this lens helps explain our empirical
observations and suggests which architectural choices matter most.

We model the evolution as optimizing solver logic $x$ to maximize expected performance
 $f(x)$ on the task distribution:

(1)

 $x^{*}=\operatorname*{arg\,max}_{x}\mathbb{E}[f(x)]$ 

Key Insight. The baseline Gödel Agent approach (Yin et al., 2025)
resembles zeroth-order optimization, where the agent must infer improvement
directions from noisy function evaluations alone, without explicit gradient information.
In contrast, GuideEvolve resembles first-order optimization, where the expert
module acts as an oracle providing gradient-like directional guidance. This analogy
predicts the observed efficiency gains and enhanced stability of our approach.

## 4.1. Baseline Evolution as Zeroth-Order Search

Following the Gödel Agent framework (Yin et al., 2025), the self-evolving agent must
simultaneously evaluate performance and determine update directions. At each iteration
 $t$ , the agent’s observation of performance is corrupted by context pollution:

(2)

 $\tilde{f}(x_{t})=f(x_{t})+\epsilon_{\text{eval}}+\epsilon_{\text{ctx}}$ 

where $\epsilon_{\text{eval}}$ represents stochastic sampling noise from evaluating
on limited training examples, and $\epsilon_{\text{ctx}}$ represents systematic
observation bias. Concretely, $\epsilon_{\text{ctx}}$ corresponds to the challenge
of extracting actionable insights when the agent must process raw task-solver
interactions (e.g., question-answer pairs with their full context) mixed with
evolution history within a shared context. The agent then computes updates:

(3)

 $x_{t+1}=x_{t}+\alpha\cdot[\text{direction}(\tilde{f}(x_{t}),\text{history}_{t})+\delta_{\text{proc}}^{(\text{high})}]$ 

where $\delta_{\text{proc}}^{(\text{high})}$ represents processing bias from context
overflow when maintaining long evolution histories containing raw evaluation data
alongside decision-making logic. Just as noisy gradient estimates harm optimization
convergence, the compounding effect of polluted observations ( $\epsilon_{\text{ctx}}$ )
and bloated processing context ( $\delta_{\text{proc}}^{(\text{high})}$ ) leads to
inconsistent evolution trajectories across trials and potential convergence to
suboptimal solutions.

## 4.2. Guided Evolution as First-Order Approximation

Our framework decomposes the optimization into a two-level structure. The expert
module acts as an oracle that processes raw performance data in isolation:

(4)

 $\displaystyle\tilde{f}_{\text{clean}}(x_{t})$ 
 $\displaystyle=f(x_{t})+\epsilon_{\text{eval}}$ 

 $\displaystyle h(x_{t})$ 
 $\displaystyle=\text{expert}(\tilde{f}_{\text{clean}}(x_{t}),x_{t})$ 

The expert processes only task-specific information without access to evolution
mechanics, producing concentrated guidance $h(x_{t})$ analogous to curated gradient
information. In practice, $h(x_{t})$ is a structured report containing
strengths/weaknesses analysis and actionable recommendations (see Appendix E
for an example). This resembles replacing noisy finite-difference gradients with analytical
gradients in optimization. The self-evolving agent then performs guided updates:

(5)

 $x_{t+1}=x_{t}+\alpha\cdot[\text{direction}(h(x_{t}),\text{history}_{t})+\delta_{\text{proc}}^{(\text{low})}]$ 

Critically, GuideEvolve eliminates context bias at two levels: (1) observation
level—the agent receives clean guidance $h(x_{t})$ rather than polluted observations
 $\tilde{f}(x_{t})$ (eliminating $\epsilon_{\text{ctx}}$ ), and (2) processing
level—the agent’s history no longer contains raw evaluation data, substantially
reducing context load such that $\delta_{\text{proc}}^{(\text{low})}\ll\delta_{\text{proc}}^{(\text{high})}$ .

## 4.3. Predicted Benefits from the Analogy

This optimization perspective generates three testable predictions:

Improved Accuracy through Cleaner Signals. The expert’s context
isolation eliminates systematic observation bias, providing cleaner directional
signals. For knowledge-intensive tasks (MMLU), this enables discovery of better local
optima through more accurate gradient approximations, resulting in superior convergence:

(6)

 $\mathbb{E}[f(x^{*}_{\text{GuideEvolve}})]\geq\mathbb{E}[f(x^{*}_{\text{baseline}})]$ 

For tasks with fundamental capability constraints (e.g., MGSM requiring symbolic
computation), both approaches converge to similar accuracy plateaus determined by
underlying model capabilities.

Enhanced Stability through Variance Reduction. The dual-level
elimination of context bias should fundamentally improve trajectory consistency across
independent trials. We can conceptualize the variance in final performance as composed of multiple sources:

(7)

 $\displaystyle\text{Var}[f(x^{*})]\approx$ 
 $\displaystyle\underbrace{\sigma^{2}_{\text{eval}}}_{\text{from }\epsilon_{\text{eval}}}+\underbrace{\sigma^{2}_{\text{ctx}}}_{\text{from }\epsilon_{\text{ctx}}}$ 

 $\displaystyle+\underbrace{\sigma^{2}_{\text{proc}}}_{\text{from }\delta_{\text{proc}}}+\underbrace{\sigma^{2}_{\text{path}}}_{\text{trajectory randomness}}$ 

Under this conceptual model, GuideEvolve should reduce three of the four terms: (a) $\sigma^{2}_{\text{ctx}}$ 
eliminated through expert’s clean observation (removing $\epsilon_{\text{ctx}}$ ),
(b) $\sigma^{2}_{\text{proc}}$ substantially reduced via lean evolution history without
raw evaluation data (reducing $\delta_{\text{proc}}^{(\text{high})}$ to
 $\delta_{\text{proc}}^{(\text{low})}$ ), and (c) $\sigma^{2}_{\text{path}}$ reduced
via consistent, unbiased guidance signals. Note that $\sigma^{2}_{\text{eval}}$ 
should remain unchanged as both approaches use identical sampling strategies.

Faster Convergence through Informed Search. The first-order-like
guidance $h(x_{t})$ should enable more efficient exploration compared to zeroth-order
search with polluted observations and bloated context:

(8)

 $\text{Iter.}_{\text{GuideEvolve}}\ll\text{Iter.}_{\text{baseline}}$

## LIMITATIONS SECTION
Model Confound. Our primary comparison evaluates Claude-based GuideEvolve against GPT-based Gödel Agent. While our ablation study (Table 4) isolates the contribution of our architectural innovations by comparing variants using identical Claude models, we acknowledge that full cross-model validation (e.g., GuideEvolve with GPT models or Gödel with Claude models) would strengthen claims of framework-independent improvements. The observed stability gains are consistent across our Claude-based ablation experiments, suggesting architectural rather than model-driven benefits.

Task Scope. Our evaluation focuses on two benchmark tasks (MGSM and MMLU) with clear ground truth and quantitative metrics. Generalization to open-ended business applications with multifaceted success criteria remains future work, as discussed in Section 8.

Computational Cost. GuideEvolve introduces additional LLM calls through the expert module. While this increases per-iteration cost compared to Gödel, the 50% reduction in required iterations (Table 3) partially offsets this overhead. Detailed cost-benefit analysis for production deployment is warranted.

## CONCLUSION
As noted in the experimental setup, the following analyses use the reduced-scale visualization setup (5 training, 100 test samples) to illustrate mechanisms clearly.

Case Study. To illustrate GuideEvolve’s operation, we examine a representative evolution instance on the MGSM task. Visualization of this instance’s evolution path can be found in Figure 3. Starting with an initial solver accuracy of 0.9 on the test set, the agent first perceives its environment to understand its current state. In its first evolution step, the agent modifies the solver to generate more responses and implement a verification mechanism to select the most reliable answer. Despite achieving 100% accuracy on the validation set, the test set accuracy initially drops to 0.91, revealing a challenging overfitting scenario. The agent continues evolution despite reaching validation set perfection, avoiding early termination through our minimum iteration requirement. This constraint proves valuable as subsequent iterations explore different strategies, such as implementing few-shot learning, which improves test accuracy to 0.94. Through iterations 4-8, the agent experiments with various modifications, revealing two distinct scenarios: when validation failures occur, the task expert provides highly effective and targeted guidance; however, in overfitting cases (100% validation accuracy but low test performance), the task expert’s effectiveness is limited due to lack of observable failures, leading to less efficient exploration by the self-evolving agent. A crucial breakthrough occurs at iteration 9 when the agent calls throwback and modifies previous best-performing logic, achieving 0.97 accuracy. After further exploration without additional improvements, the agent autonomously terminates at iteration 18, exemplifying the framework’s ability to balance exploration with efficient termination.

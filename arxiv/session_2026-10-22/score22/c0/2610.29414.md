Authors: Sami Kharma, Florian Schintke
Comment: 15 pages, 7 figures, 12 tables
Categories: cs.AI

## ABSTRACT
Positional superko (PSK) forbids recreating any earlier whole-board position. It is part of the Chinese rules as written and of the rules used in computer Go. Yet almost all solved Go boards were computed under a simpler basic-ko rule set. PSK makes solving hard: legality depends on the whole game history, and this breaks the three tools that make solving tractable, namely transposition tables (the graph-history interaction problem), static life-and-death recognition, and safe move pruning. We present Sanko, a parallel depth-first proof-number solver that restores all three soundly. Its core is a cheap proof-transfer theorem: a stored proof stays valid on a new path unless a board that the proof's winner must create has already occurred. Sanko proves 5x5 Go in 16 minutes with six threads, where the previous state of the art needed 5.25 hours with six processes, and in about a minute and a half on one 192-thread machine. It also establishes the first PSK values of twenty-three further boards, eleven of which were unsolved under any rules. Superko changes game-theoretic values: 4x7, a full-board win under basic ko, is worth only 12 points under PSK, and we pinpoint the move at which the previously published optimal line fails. Unlike earlier solutions of empty Go boards, we release the solver source together with the command line and outcome of every run behind our values and measurements, so that all reported values can be reproduced.

## 1 Introduction

Solving games exactly is a long-standing benchmark for search algorithms.
Checkers has been solved Schaeffer et al. (2007), and so have Hex on small boards Henderson et al. (2009) and many smaller games.
For Go, computer proofs exist for many boards up to 30 intersections van der Werf et al. (2003); van der Werf and Winands (2009).
However, these classic Go results were computed under a special-purpose rule set designed to make a position’s value independent of how it was reached.
Specifically, they use the basic ko rule, let a pass lift a ko ban, end a game at a repeated position and score the cycle by its passes van der Werf (2004); van der Werf and Winands (2009).

Superko, the rule that forbids recreating earlier whole-board positions, is part of everyday play.
The Chinese rules forbid recreating any earlier whole-board position Chinese Weiqi Association (1988), the American and New Zealand rules forbid it only with the same player to move (situational superko) AGA Rules Committee (1991); New Zealand Go Society (2017), and computer Go commonly uses the Chinese form, for example in the Tromp–Taylor rules Tromp (1995).
Solving under superko is hard, however, and it has received far less attention than basic ko.
We approach solving Go under said positional superko (PSK), which forbids recreating any earlier whole-board position.
Legality of a move therefore depends on the entire history of the game.
This rule breaks three pillars of game solving:

• 

Transposition tables.
Reusing the result of a position reached along another path becomes unsound.
This is the graph-history interaction (GHI) problem Campbell (1985); Kishimoto and Müller (2004).

• 

Static knowledge.
Benson-style life and territory recognition Benson (1976) assumes that the owner can always capture intruders.
Under PSK those captures may be illegal.

• 

Pruning.
Moves that never help under basic ko, such as filling one’s own eye, can become decisive tempo moves van der Werf (2004).

The cost of losing these advantages is very large.
Even with situational superko, the MIGOS solver needed 1.1 hours for $4{\times}4$ , against 3.3 seconds with basic ko van der Werf et al. (2003). Now, Sanko solves $4{\times}4$ so fast that it no longer suits for performance comparisons.
The first PSK solution of $5{\times}5$ , by Expected Work Search (EWS), took 5.25 hours with six processes Randall et al. (2024), a board we solve in minutes.
Beyond $5{\times}5$ , no board with at least two rows had been solved under PSK.
It was also open whether the basic-ko values carry over.

This paper shows that PSK solving can be both sound and fast.
Our contributions are:

• 

A proof-transfer theorem for PSK (Section 3).
Using the observation that only boards created by a proof’s winner can invalidate it, five small attributes per stored proof allow for soundness-preserving proof reuse under PSK.
Proof-transfer under this theorem cuts search effort by a factor of about 6,000 compared to reusing results only in the identical superko game.

• 

Static knowledge that is sound under PSK (Section 4).
We introduce pure-eye analysis, a variant of Benson’s algorithm that is sound under PSK play-out, prove the two conditions it needs beyond Benson’s classic analysis, and give acyclic local region games.

• 

Sanko, a parallel solver (Section 5).
It runs df-pn on shared lock-free tables, and checks proof identity on 128 bits. We show that the probability of a hash collision is magnitudes below that of hardware errors, even on our largest runs.
We also verify against an oracle with no static knowledge.

• 

New results (Section 6).
 $5{\times}5$ takes 16 minutes on six threads, 20 $\times$ faster than EWS.
We give the first PSK values of $4{\times}5$ , $3{\times}7$ , $3{\times}8$ , $3{\times}9$ , $4{\times}6$ , $4{\times}7$ , $5{\times}6$ , $3{\times}10$ , $2{\times}10$ – $2{\times}15$ and $1{\times}10$ – $1{\times}18$ .
Of these, $3{\times}10$ , $2{\times}12$ – $2{\times}15$ and $1{\times}13$ – $1{\times}18$ were unsolved under any rules. A quick overview shows Figure 1.

• 

A rule-dependent value (Section 7).
 $4{\times}7$ is a full-board win under basic ko (value 28), but is worth only 12 points under PSK.
We locate the move at which the published optimal line van der Werf and Winands (2009) fails.

• 

An open, reproducible artifact (Section 9).
Earlier solutions of empty Go boards were published without their solvers, so none of them can be reproduced independently.

## 2 Background

We use the Chinese rules as written Chinese Weiqi Association (1988), whose repetition ban is the positional superko of EWS Randall et al. (2024).
Black moves first on an $R{\times}C$ board.
A move is a pass or the placement of a stone.
Opponent chains without liberties are captured, and suicide is illegal.
The ban on repeating a whole-board position is positional superko: a stone move may not recreate any board that occurred earlier in the game.
Passes are always legal.
Two consecutive passes end the game, which is scored by area: each player counts their stones plus the empty regions bordered only by their stones.
Where human players agree on dead stones, we play the game out, so stones that remain on the board count as alive.
Under area scoring, playing in ones own territory costs neither side anything, so this does not change any value.
The komi $k$ (a half-integer) is added to White’s score.
The value $v$ of a board is Black’s margin under optimal play.
We establish it with two proofs: a Black win at komi $v-\frac{1}{2}$ and a White win at $v+\frac{1}{2}$ .
A full-board win ( $v=R\cdot C$ ) needs only the first.

MIGOS van der Werf et al. (2003); van der Werf and Winands (2009); van der Werf (2004) formalizes the Chinese rules, but replaces their ban on repeated positions by a basic ko rule and special conventions for cycles, passes and dead stones.

A node is a position together with the history that leads to it.
Under PSK, two nodes with the same position but different histories may have different values.
Proof-number search Allis et al. (1994) treats the game as an AND/OR graph of nodes.
Each node carries a proof number $\phi$ and a disproof number $\delta$ , which estimate the effort to prove or disprove it.
Depth-first proof-number search (df-pn) Nagai (2002) explores the graph with thresholds, with the $1{+}\varepsilon$ refinement of Pawlewicz and Lew (2007).
A transposition table (TT) stores proven and unproven nodes, and its hit rate is what makes large proofs feasible.
An expansion is one call of df-pn’s recursive procedure on a node.
The call generates the legal moves and classifies each child as terminal, static leaf, stored proof, or unproven with initial proof numbers.
It stops early at a child that already proves the node, and then repeatedly descends into the most promising child until the node’s thresholds are exceeded.
Each call counts once, including later calls on a node that was already expanded.
We measure search effort in expansions.
The revisit factor of a search is the number of expansions divided by the number of distinct nodes expanded.
EWS reports the number of nodes in its best-first search tree, and MIGOS counts every node visit of its iterative-deepening search van der Werf and Winands (2009), so the three counts are related but not identical.

Superko has three effects.

• 

Transpositions are unsound.
Two paths to the same board can have different sets of earlier boards.
A ko recapture, or any capture that recreates an earlier board, is legal on one path and illegal on the other, so the two nodes can have different values.
Storing ‘won’ for one path and reusing it on the other is unsound: this is the GHI problem Campbell (1985); Breuker et al. (2001); Kishimoto and Müller (2004).
The safe alternative, keying the table on the position and the set of earlier boards, almost eliminates reuse (Section 6.3).
This is why superko slowed MIGOS down by more than 1,000 $\times$ on $4{\times}4$ van der Werf et al. (2003); Kishimoto and Müller (2004).

• 

Static knowledge from basic ko is unsound.
Benson’s unconditional life Benson (1976) certifies groups that the opponent can never capture.
Claiming their territory however, means the owner must actually capture intruders.
Section 4 shows that superko can forbid those captures.
In addition, a static verdict taken just after the owner’s own pass is wrong, because the opponent can pass and end the game immediately at the current position.

• 

Pruning becomes incomplete.
Under PSK, any board-changing move can make a later capture legal, even filling one’s own eye (a move that is never useful under basic ko).
Section 4 gives an example where this is the only winning move.

## 3 Sound Proof Transfer under Superko

Kishimoto and Müller Kishimoto and Müller (2004); Kishimoto and Müller (2005) solve GHI in general: they re-check, or simulate Kawano (1996), a stored proof on the new path, at a cost that grows with the proof.
We instead give a sufficient condition for reuse under PSK that can be checked in time linear in the path length, independent of the proof’s size.

A history $H$ is the sequence of positions from the empty board to the current node $N$ .
We also write $H$ for its set of boards.
A proof $T$ that player $w$ wins from $N$ is a finite tree.
At $w$ -nodes it picks one move, and at opponent nodes it contains every legal move.
Its leaves are terminal wins for $w$ or certified static wins (Section 4).
We say line for a sequence of consecutive moves from a given position, so a path in the game tree.

Definition 1.

For a proof $T$ of a win for $w$ :

• 

 $B_{w}(T)$ is the set of boards created by $w$ ’s stone moves in $T$ .

• 

 $X(T)$ is the set of boards $b\in H$ that made some opponent move in $T$ illegal (exclusion targets).

• 

 $\mathrm{terr}(T)$ is the union of the territories certified at $T$ ’s static leaves.

• 

 $\mathrm{cap}_{w}(H)$ is the set of points at which a $w$ stone was captured along $H$ .

Theorem 1 (Transfer).

Let $T$ prove that $w$ wins from $N$ under history $H$ .
Then $T$ also proves that $w$ wins from $N$ under any history $H^{\prime}$ that satisfies

(i) 

 $B_{w}(T)\cap H^{\prime}=\emptyset$ ,

(ii) 

 $X(T)\subseteq H^{\prime}$ ,

(iii) 

 $\mathrm{cap}_{w}(H^{\prime})\cap\mathrm{terr}(T)=\emptyset$ .

Proof sketch.

Induction over $T$ (full proof in Appendix A).
The line inside $T$ is the same under $H$ and $H^{\prime}$ .
Only the prefix differs.

• 

A stone move of $w$ creates a board $b\in B_{w}(T)$ .
That board was not in $H$ or on the line, because the move was legal.
By (i) it is not in $H^{\prime}$ either, so the move stays legal.

• 

An opponent move that is legal under $H^{\prime}$ but was illegal under $H$ must recreate a board of $H\setminus H^{\prime}$ .
Such a board would lie in $X(T)$ , which (ii) rules out.
So the opponent gains no new options.
Opponent moves that become illegal only remove options.

• 

Terminal scores do not depend on the history.
Static leaves need their own history condition (Lemma 1), and (iii) supplies it on the new prefix.

∎

The theorem is asymmetric, in that only the winner’s boards matter.
If the loser would recreate a board of $H^{\prime}$ , that merely removes a losing option, and the proof survives (Figure 2).
Proofs are stored in negamax form, each for its own winner, so the theorem covers wins of both players.

The exact sets are too large to store.
Each proof entry instead carries conservative summaries that compose bottom-up.
A won node takes its winning child’s attributes plus the board its winning move creates, and a lost node takes the join of all children:

• 

a 64-bit Bloom filter Bloom (1970) of the canonical hashes of $B_{w}(T)$ ,

• 

the stones common to all boards of $B_{w}(T)$ , and their minimum stone count,

• 

the territory bitboard,

• 

a flag that marks proofs with an exclusion target.

A strict ancestor board $a\in H^{\prime}$ can lie in $B_{w}(T)$ only if it hits the Bloom filter, contains the common stones, and has at least the minimum number of stones.
If any of these fails, (i) holds for $a$ (Algorithm 1).
Condition (iii) is checked exactly against the capture bitboard of the current path.
Condition (ii) holds because every proof with an exclusion target is stored path-specifically and is never transferred, so a transferred proof has $X(T)=\emptyset$ .
Path-specific proofs live under a twin key, the position key combined with a non-linear hash of the set of earlier boards, and are reused only on the identical board with an identical set, which the solver recognizes by two independent such hashes.
Note that an XOR of Zobrist keys Zobrist (1990) is not a sound set identifier.
Base entries are written once, and a proof without an exclusion target that finds its base slot taken by another path’s proof goes to one of two alternate slots, where any path can reuse it through Algorithm 1.
Canonical hashing makes the test invariant under the board’s symmetries.
Sibling moves are merged only under symmetries that map every board on the path onto itself.

## 4 Static Knowledge under Superko

Leaves that end the search before the game ends are essential: on $5{\times}5$ , kill-all proofs would otherwise run through every mop-up capture.
Under PSK and play-out scoring they need two corrections.

Let $w$ be a player and $o$ the opponent.
A region is a maximal connected set of non- $w$ points.
A region $E$ is a pure eye of a $w$ -chain $K$ if every point of $E$ , empty or occupied by $o$ , is adjacent to $K$ .
Let $X^{*}$ be the largest set of $w$ -chains in which each chain has two pure eyes bordered only by chains of $X^{*}$ .
Let $T^{*}$ be the union of the regions bordered only by $X^{*}$ in which every point is adjacent to $X^{*}$ .
We call $X^{*}$ and $T^{*}$ the pure-eye analysis.
It follows Benson’s fixpoint Benson (1976) but replaces ‘vital’ by ‘pure’, and together with the conditions of Lemma 1 it is sound under PSK play-out.

Lemma 1 (Static leaves under PSK).

Suppose (C1) no $w$ stone was ever captured on a point of $T^{*}$ during the history, and (C2) the last move was not a pass by $w$ with $o$ to move.
Then $w$ can enforce a final area of at least $|X^{*}|+|T^{*}|$ .

Proof sketch.

The winner follows the strategy $\sigma$ : if an $o$ stone lies in $T^{*}$ , play on a liberty of an $o$ -chain there, and otherwise pass.

## CONCLUSION
The factors in Table 3 multiply.
Proof transfer is by far the largest of them. It restores most of the reuse that superko takes away.
The learned prior and the region solver cut the remaining tree by factors of 4.7 and 2.3.
The region solver helps most in kill-all positions, where proofs would otherwise play out every capture.
Shared-table parallelism adds a factor of 42 on 192 threads, because proofs found by one thread are immediately visible to all others, at the cost of 2.4 times as many expansions.

On every board where both rule sets have a value, the values agree except on $2{\times}2$ , whose superko value MIGOS already reported, and on $4{\times}7$ .
The disagreement does not come from an error in either search.
It comes from rules that matter only at the very end of a game, namely what a pass does to a ko ban, and how many passes end the game.
Values computed under conventions that make repetition harmless should therefore not be quoted as values of superko Go without checking.
Superko also produces strategies with no basic-ko counterpart, such as the eye-filling sacrifice on $1{\times}12$ .

A claimed value rests on the following:

• 

the soundness arguments for transfer, static leaves and pruning (Theorems 1, 1 and A),

• 

differential testing against an oracle with no static knowledge, on the claimed boards themselves,

• 

independent re-solves.

Each claim uses only proofs that are trusted for the reported winner.
Because pruning is applied only to the side being proven, a failed proof is reported as unknown, never as a win for the opponent.

Larger boards split naturally: the $5{\times}6$ upper bound decomposes into ten independent subproblems, one per first move of Black, which can run on separate machines.
Extrapolating Figure 6, $6{\times}6$ would need about $10^{14}$ expansions or more, about 50 days on one of our machines, and boards above 30 points need position keys wider than 64 bits to stay exact.
Stronger knowledge would cut this further.
Our learned prior is a first step, and a stronger model, as in learned solvers Wu et al. (2023), may cut much more.

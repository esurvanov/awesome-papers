## BODY (first 18k chars)
Abstract

We determine the minimum number of directional commands needed to solve a
level of Order of the Sinking Star, modeled as a crystal-exchange
puzzle on an $11\times 11$ board. A command swaps
the player with the nearest crystal in the selected row or column, regardless
of distance; only an empty ray permits an ordinary unit walk. Two gates admit
the player to the exit exactly while all sixteen crystals occupy their target
cells. We give an explicit 93-command solution and exclude every shorter
solution by an exact, cost-bounded breadth-first search. The search represents
a position by its seventeen occupied cells and one active cell. Its admissible
heuristic partitions command costs between a compressed core abstraction and
residual motion, strengthened by a necessary activation detour and a corner
visit condition. The completed computation retained 605,349,215 occupied sets
and 1,863,925,136 states. We prove the reductions and pruning conditions,
describe the exact storage scheme, and provide source code, execution logs,
and a reproduction driver. The result is a computer-assisted proof for the
specified instance, with an explicit implementation trust boundary.

## 1 Introduction

We study a crystal-exchange level from the puzzle game
Order of the Sinking Star.
Nonlocal exchanges make familiar geometric intuitions unreliable in
shortest-path puzzles. A single command can move the player across an entire
row, while placing every crystal on a target need not finish the puzzle:
the player must still reach an exit. The position of the player at the moment
the gates open is therefore part of the optimization problem.

The level was supplied as a diagram together with
its movement rules. Section 2 gives a self-contained
mathematical specification, so the result does not depend on access to a
particular game implementation. Our main result is an exact minimum of
93 directional commands. We give a witness and a computational exclusion
of all routes of length at most 92.

The computational method uses established principles of admissible search.
Pattern databases store exact distances in abstractions of a state
space [1]. Additive pattern databases and action-cost
partitioning permit several lower bounds to be summed when their allocated
costs do not exceed the cost of a real action [2, 3].
Here the partition is fixed and binary: a command contributes to exactly
one of two cost components. We specialize these principles to nonlocal
exchange by retaining occupied points, rather than crystal identities,
and deriving a residual-motion bound from the need to activate an
off-target occupied point.

Our contribution is the exact solution of this instance and a documented
search formulation that makes the result reproducible. We do not claim a
new general-purpose search algorithm, a complexity classification of the
puzzle family, or a proof checked by a formal proof assistant.

## 2 The instance and its exact state space

Rows and columns are numbered from 1 to 11, with rows increasing downward.

## Commands.

The four directions are denoted $U,D,L,R$ . The gates are open if and
only if $C=T$ ; their status is not latched. In a selected
direction, scan the ray from $p$ , stopping at the board boundary or a
closed gate. If the ray contains a crystal, let $q$ be the nearest one
and replace the state by

 $\bigl(q,\ (C\setminus\{q\})\cup\{p\}\bigr).$ 

If the ray contains no crystal, move the player one cell in that direction,
provided that cell is in $V$ and is not a closed gate. Each command costs
one, including a long-distance exchange. Invalid commands can be omitted
from a shortest route; if interpreted as idle commands, deleting them
does not change any subsequent state. The objective is the player’s
first entry into $E$ .

Lemma 2.1 (Goal characterization).

On first entry into $E$ , the crystal set is $T$ .

Proof.

Initially $E$ has no crystal. The only operation that could place a
crystal there is an exchange from a player already at $E$ . Thus a first
entry cannot be an exchange; it is a walk from one of the two gates.
Entering that gate requires $C=T$ . From either gate, its row and column
contain no target cells. While the player remains on a gate or walks
between it and $E$ , no exchange can alter $T$ . Hence $C=T$ on entry.
∎

Definition 2.2 (Occupied-set representation).

Write $U=C\cup\{p\}$ and $s=(U,p)$ .
Then $|U|=17$ , $p\in U$ , and $C=U\setminus\{p\}$ .

An exchange changes only the active cell $p$ . A walk from $p$ to $q$ 
replaces $U$ by $(U\setminus\{p\})\cup\{q\}$ . This is an exact
representation, not an abstraction. By Lemma 2.1, the required
terminal state is

 $s_{*}=(T\cup\{E\},E).$ 

(2)

Theorem 2.3 (Main result; computer-assisted).

For the instance and rules specified above, the minimum number of commands
from $(C_{0}\cup\{p_{0}\},p_{0})$ to first entry into $E$ is exactly $93$ .

The upper bound is explicit in Section 7. The lower bound
combines the mathematical reductions below with the exhaustive
computation reported there.

Lemma 3.1 (Disjoint cost partition).

A unit walk changes exactly one coordinate of either $\pi(p)$ or
 $\eta(p)$ , by one. Charge every exchange and every $\pi$ -changing walk
to cost $\alpha$ , and every $\eta$ -changing walk to cost $\beta$ .
For every route of length $L$ , $L=\alpha+\beta$ .

Proof.

In one dimension the edges $3\leftrightarrow 4,\ldots,8\leftrightarrow 9$ 
change only $\pi$ ; the remaining four edges change only $\eta$ .
A grid walk changes only one original coordinate. The asserted partition
therefore assigns each walk to exactly one class, and exchanges are
assigned to $\alpha$ by definition.
∎

## Stationary labels under exchange.

Temporarily label the seventeen occupied points. A label stays at its
cell during an exchange; only which label is active changes. During a walk,
the active label moves with the player. These are bookkeeping labels of
occupied points, not persistent identities of crystals. Consequently,
only walks move labels, and only $\beta$ -walks change their residual
coordinates. At completion, sixteen labels have residual coordinate
 $(0,0)$ , and one has residual coordinate $(2,2)$ . This observation
permits a displacement lower bound on $\beta$ , even though the
player itself can change residual coordinates in a single exchange.

## 4 A compressed-core lower bound

Let $Q^{\circ}=\{1,\ldots,5\}^{2}$ be the interior of $Q$ .
The abstraction of $s=(U,p)$ is

 $A(s)=(M,a,e),\qquad M=\pi(U\cap K),\quad a=\pi(p),\quad e=\pi(E)=(6,6).$ 

The endpoint corner $e$ is stored explicitly because the pattern table
uses all square symmetries. The integer $n_{\mathrm{out}}=17-|M|$ 
counts occupied points outside $K$ , including the player
when appropriate. Their individual positions are forgotten. Valid states
satisfy $|M|\leq 17$ , $a\in M$ when $a\in Q^{\circ}$ , and $n_{\mathrm{out}}\geq 1$ 
when $a\in\partial Q$ .

## 4.1 Abstract transitions

The following directed transitions have unit cost. Gates are ignored.
Rays exclude their starting cell.

1. 

Exchanges from the interior.
If $a\in Q^{\circ}$ , an exchange may reach the first point of $M$ 
on a chosen ray. If there is none, it may reach the boundary cell on
that ray, provided $n_{\mathrm{out}}\geq 1$ .

2. 

Exchanges from the boundary.
If $a\in\partial Q$ , an exchange may reach the first point of $M$ 
on a ray. It may also reach any other boundary cell on that ray before
a point of $M$ , provided $n_{\mathrm{out}}\geq 2$ .
Exchanges preserve $M$ .

3. 

Walks.
The active cell may move to an adjacent $b\in Q$ if the ray from
 $a$ in that direction contains no point of $M$ . Remove $a$ from
 $M$ if $a\in Q^{\circ}$ , and add $b$ if $b\in Q^{\circ}$ .
Only valid resulting states are retained.

These rules permit configurations of exterior points that need not be
simultaneously realizable on the original board. That extra freedom is
intentional. Exchanges whose endpoints have the same projection need no
abstract transition.

Lemma 4.1 (Simulation).

Every real $\alpha$ -command maps to one abstract edge or no change.
Every real $\beta$ -command maps to no change.

Proof.

A real exchange cannot pass a nearer interior crystal. If it reaches an
exterior point, the corresponding boundary destination is allowed by
the occupied-point count: an exterior-to-exterior exchange uses two
distinct exterior points. Projection preserves row or column alignment
and order along the noncollapsed part of a ray. Thus every exchange
with distinct projected endpoints is included.

A real walk is allowed only when its entire unblocked ray has no
crystal. The only cells hidden behind closed gates are gate cells and
exit-side boundary cells; none belongs to $K$ . Hence there is no
interior occupied point ahead of a legal walk. A $\pi$ -changing walk
satisfies the abstract walk rule, with precisely the stated
update of $M$ . An $\eta$ -changing walk stays outside $K$ , does not
change $\pi(p)$ , and leaves $M$ fixed.
∎

Let $T^{\prime}=\pi(T)$ , and define the base pattern distance

 $d(A)=\operatorname{dist}_{\mathcal{A}}\bigl(A,(T^{\prime},e,e)\bigr),$ 

where $\mathcal{A}$ is the directed abstract graph. Reverse
breadth-first search from the abstract goal computes $d$ .
Lemma 4.1 gives $d(A(s))\leq\alpha$ for every
completion of $s$ .

## 4.2 A necessary northwest visit

Let $N=\{1,2,3\}^{2}$ , whose projection is the northwest corner $o=(0,0)$ .
If $U\cap N\neq\varnothing$ , at least one currently occupied point in
 $N$ must eventually move: neither a target nor the exit lies there.
Before its first walk, that point must be active. Thus the projected
player must visit $o$ , with a visit at the current time permitted.

For an abstract state $A=(M,a,e)$ , let $o(e)$ be the corner opposite
 $e$ , and define

 $d_{N}(A)=\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}B=(M^{\prime},o(e),e)\\
B\text{ valid}\end{subarray}}\bigl[\operatorname{dist}_{\mathcal{A}}(A,B)+d(B)\bigr].$ 

(4)

This value is computed by reverse Dijkstra search, seeding each such
 $B$ with its base distance $d(B)$ . Set

 $H_{\alpha}(s)=\begin{cases}d_{N}(A(s)),&U\cap N\neq\varnothing,\\
d(A(s)),&U\cap N=\varnothing.\end{cases}$ 

(5)

Proposition 4.2.

For every completion of $s$ , $H_{\alpha}(s)\leq\alpha$ .

Proof.

Without the northwest condition this is the simulation lemma. With that
condition, split the projected route at an occurrence of $o$ .
The abstract prefix and suffix have total length at most the real
 $\alpha$ -cost and are candidates in (4).
∎

## 4.3 Symmetry and pattern-table representation

The group $D_{4}$ acts on the abstract board. We transform $M,a,e$ 
together; $o(e)$ transforms with $e$ . Canonicalizing the mask alone
without retaining goal orientation would not justify the distance lookup.
After choosing a representative mask, the implementation minimizes the
pair $(a,e)$ over the mask’s stabilizer.

There are 4,211,744 orbits of all $2^{25}$ masks under $D_{4}$ .
Each mask representative has $49\cdot 4=196$ addressable player/corner
slots, so each byte-valued distance array contains 825,501,824 slots.
The arrays include unused and noncanonical addresses. Each generated
array has 584,039,298 entries different from the sentinel 255.
These are storage counts, not counts of all real positions.
For the bound-93 computation, sentinel 255 is sufficient to reject a
state: all abstract paths of cost at most 92 are represented by the
reverse searches. Values at or beyond the sentinel need not be
distinguished. The initial core lower bound used in the search is 65.

## 5 Residual motion and activation detours

Fix labels $1,\ldots,17$ on the occupied points of the current state.
Write $r_{i}=\eta(u_{i})$ , and let $i_{p}$ be the active label.
For each possible exit-assigned label $e$ , put

 $z_{i}^{(e)}=\begin{cases}(2,2),&i=e,\\
(0,0),&i\neq e,\end{cases}\qquad D_{e}=\sum_{i=1}^{17}\left\lVert r_{i}-z_{i}^{(e)}\right\rVert_{1}.$ 

Every completion induces one such assignment. Since each $\beta$ -walk
moves exactly one residual coordinate by one,

 $D_{0}=\min_{e}D_{e}=\sum_{i}\left\lVert r_{i}\right\rVert_{1}+\min_{e}\bigl(\left\lVert r_{e}-(2,2)\right\rVert_{1}-\left\lVert r_{e}\right\rVert_{1}\bigr)$ 

(6)

is a lower bound on its residual-motion cost.

## 5.1 One necessary activation

Consider an inactive label $v$ with $r_{v}\neq(0,0)$ and $u_{v}\neq E$ .
It is currently on neither a target nor the exit and must eventually
move. Before its first walk, some distinct active label $j$ must exchange
with it. Until that first activation, $u_{v}$ remains fixed. Just before
the activating exchange, $u_{j}$ and $u_{v}$ share a row or column.
In particular, one residual coordinate of $j$ equals the corresponding
coordinate of $r_{v}$ .

For a possible terminal residual coordinate $z$ , define the excess
length forced by this necessary alignment:

 $\delta_{j}(z;v)=\min_{\ell\in\{1,2\}}\left(|r_{j,\ell}-r_{v,\ell}|+|r_{v,\ell}-z_{\ell}|-|r_{j,\ell}-z_{\ell}|\right).$ 

(7)

It is nonnegative by the triangle inequality. Alignment in residual
coordinates is only a necessary condition for real alignment; weakening
the condition can only decrease this bound.
Allowing every possible exit assignment and approaching label gives

 $D_{v}=\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}e\in\{1,\ldots,17\}\\
j\neq v\end{subarray}}\left[D_{e}+\delta_{j}\bigl(z_{j}^{(e)};v\bigr)\right].$ 

(8)

In particular, the approaching label is allowed to be the exit-assigned
label. Excluding that possibility would not be justified.
Define

 $\mathcal{V}(s)=\{v:v\neq i_{p},\ r_{v}\neq(0,0),\ u_{v}\neq E\}$ 

and

 $H_{\beta}(s)=\max\bigl(\{D_{0}\}\cup\{D_{v}:v\in\mathcal{V}(s)\}\bigr).$ 

(9)

Proposition 5.1 (Residual lower bound).

For every completion of $s$ , $H_{\beta}(s)\leq\beta$ .

Proof.

Fix the actual exit assignment $e$ . For any $v\in\mathcal{V}(s)$ ,
let $j\neq v$ be the label that first activates it. The residual path
of $j$ , from $r_{j}$ to $z_{j}^{(e)}$ , passes through a point with at
least one coordinate equal to that of $r_{v}$ . Its length is therefore
at least its direct Manhattan distance plus
 $\delta_{j}(z_{j}^{(e)};v)$ . Every other label requires at least its direct
distance. Summing these disjoint label motions gives the expression
inside (8) for this particular $e,j$ , and hence
 $\beta\geq D_{v}$ . The same reasoning without a detour gives
 $\beta\geq D_{0}$ . Taking the maximum proves the claim.
∎

The maximum in (9) is essential: one motion may help
activate several points, so their detour bounds cannot in general be
added. The implementation computes the strongest two obligations and
the cell identifying the strongest one. This permits the active label’s
obligation to be omitted for any choice of $p$ in the same $U$ .

Lemma 5.2 (Safe reuse along walks).

For a legal walk $s\to s^{\prime}$ , the residual bound is unchanged if the
walk has $\alpha$ -cost. If the walk has $\beta$ -cost, then
 $\lvert H_{\beta}(s^{\prime})-H_{\beta}(s)\rvert\leq 1$ .

Proof.

The active label remains the same and all inactive labels remain fixed.
Thus the candidate set $\mathcal{V}$ and the activation coordinates
are unchanged. For fixed $e,j,v$ and choice of alignment coordinate,
the expression $D_{e}+\delta_{j}(z_{j}^{(e)};v)$ is a sum of path lengths.
If the active label is $j$ , its contribution is a path through a
fixed coordinate; otherwise its contribution is a direct distance.
Changing its residual start coordinate by one changes either length
by at most one. If its residual coordinate does not change, neither
does the expression. Minima and maxima preserve these properties.
The same argument applies to $D_{0}$ .
∎

Corollary 5.3 (Admissible combined heuristic).

The function $h(s)=H_{\alpha}(s)+H_{\beta}(s)$ is a lower bound on the
length of every completion of $s$ .

Proof.

Apply Propositions 4.2 and 5.1 to the
same completion and use $L=\alpha+\beta$ .
∎

The search uses an initial value $h(s_{0})=65+10=75$ .
A separate analytic argument proves that every complete route from
the initial state has $\beta\geq 12$ 
(Appendix A); that stronger initial-state statement
was not used as a statewise residual heuristic in the reported search.

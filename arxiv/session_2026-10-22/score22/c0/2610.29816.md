## ABSTRACT
Incorporating AI systems into safety systems requires monitoring to avoid silent faults and ensure safe and reliable operation. Single monitors often do not provide actionable information for human supervisors, who act as the system's adaptive controller. We present COMET, a coordinated multi-layer runtime monitoring framework for human-supervised AI-enabled safety systems in which each monitor is anchored in a different fault mode. Monitor outputs are combined in a coordination layer and mapped by a communication layer to a traffic-light display. In this work, COMET consists of three monitoring layers: signal integrity, physics consistency, and the AI system's competence envelope. The framework is evaluated on an open dataset of compressed-air leaks, where an AI system detects leaks from acoustic emissions, with held-out conditions simulating covariate shift, semantic shift, and sensor faults, events expected during runtime but unknown in their exact appearance. All monitors are frozen after development and tested zero-shot, and the framework is compared against no-monitoring and single-monitor setups. The bare AI system fails silently and confidently under all three shift types, whereas COMET reduces the trusted-wrong-answer rate to zero on covariate-shifted no-leak data and on three of four sensor faults, at a nominal false-alarm rate of $\approx 1\%$. Single monitors come close in detection but cannot attribute the fault mode. COMET converts their anonymous detection flags into diagnoses. Residual silent misses under semantic shift define the boundary of passive monitoring. Runtime monitoring frameworks for AI-enabled safety systems must therefore combine quantitative fault detection and isolation with qualitative, human-oriented feedback.

## 1 Introduction

## 1.1 Motivation

The diffusion of AI systems from research into real-world applications has accelerated in recent years, driven by cheap compute, capable models, large amount of available data, and increasingly complex systems that need to be managed. Whereas for consumer products reliable operation of AI systems is largely a question of practicality, for safety-critical systems safe and reliable operation is a requirement, not a nice-to-have. This requirement is emphasized by policies and standards such as the EU AI Act (EU AI Act, 2024) and the ISO/IEC TR 5469 (ISO/IEC TR 5469:2024, 2024), which addresses the incorporation of AI systems into systems whose failures can cause severe consequences. One means of ensuring reliable and safe operation is monitoring (Rausand et al., 2021; Leveson, 2012). It provides information about system behavior during runtime and thus contribute to maintaining safe and reliable operation. Throughout this work, the term AI system describes a system consisting of an AI model and its necessary pre- and post-processing steps during runtime, whereas AI-enabled system describes the broader engineered system into which one or more AI systems are integrated (Det Norske Veritas, 2023).

In recent years, much research has focused on developing monitors for AI systems, most commonly are out-of-distribution detectors (Yang et al., 2024; Lu et al., 2024). These works typically aim to improve the performance on new or established benchmarks rather than proposing holistic frameworks, where not only the quantitative aspect of detecting samples outside the learned distribution matters, but also the qualitative aspects on how detections are communicated, and how monitors work together with the human supervisors who are supposed to use them. A few works have begun to combine multiple monitor into unifying frameworks (Dario et al., 2027; Torens et al., 2025; Ferreira, 2023), but these still address detection rather than diagnosis and do not consider the human supervisor. This leads to the question:

How can we design a runtime monitoring framework for AI systems that detects potentially unreliable system behavior, isolates faults, and supports the human supervisor in the loop, so as to enhance safe and reliable operation?

The purpose of this work is to propose and test such a framework under realistic conditions. Derived from fault-tolerant control theory, where adapting the control of a system requires fault diagnosis, i.e., detection, isolation and identification, the framework promotes safe and reliable operation by detecting shifts that can degrade the AI system’s performance and by providing qualitatively meaningful information to the human supervisor. To this end, we select a dataset and an AI system that together represent an AI safety system, and build COMET, a coordinated multi-layer runtime monitoring framework whose monitors cover different fault modes, are fused through a coordination layer, and are mapped to a traffic-light-based display through a communication layer. The framework is derived in Section 2. Section 3 describes the experimental setup, which tests the framework under realistic conditions and compares it with alternative monitoring strategies. The results are presented in Section 4 and discussed in Section 5. Section 6 concludes and outlines future work.

## 1.2 Related work

The need for monitoring deployed AI systems is already established in the MLOps literature (Kreuzberger et al., 2023), where monitoring serves continuous performance improvement. However, its scope is operational rather than safety-related, i.e., the monitored systems are typically not part of safety functions, and reliability and safety implications of unreliable outputs are not the focus. On the regulatory side, the EU AI Act mandates post-market monitoring for high-risk AI systems but does not specify how such monitoring should be realized (EU AI Act, 2024). Similarly, ISO/IEC TR 5469 on AI and functional safety names monitoring as a potential risk-reduction measure without detailing its concrete design (ISO/IEC TR 5469:2024, 2024). This work addresses that gap of a concrete runtime monitoring framework for AI-enabled safety systems, where system reliability is a primary concern and a human supervisor remains in the loop as highest control entity.

The most common monitoring technique is out-of-distribution (OOD) detection (Yang et al., 2024; Lu et al., 2024). In simple terms, it discriminates between samples inside the learned distribution, where the AI system should operate as trained and tested, and samples outside it, where outputs are potentially unreliable. These methods perform well on established benchmarks, with effectiveness depending on the method used. They can be applied using in-distribution data alone (Liu et al., 2023; Heng et al., 2024) or a combination of in-distribution and OOD data (Liu et al., 2020), and many are training-agnostic, operation post-hoc on a deployed model (Hendrycks and Gimpel, 2016a; Sun et al., 2022). In concrete applications, OOD detection has been used to increase trustworthiness of deployed AI systems, for instance in prognostics and health management for renewable energy systems (Xie et al., 2023) and in mechanical fault diagnostic (Xiao et al., 2025). What these method have in common is that they are typically proposed and evaluated as single, isolated monitors, not as part of a holistic runtime monitoring framework.

Combining complementary monitors into holistic runtime monitoring frameworks that cover different fault modes and are potentially more robust than single monitors is underrepresented in current research. The few existing examples (Ferreira, 2023; Dario et al., 2027; Torens et al., 2025) demonstrate that combining monitors can improve detection, but they treat monitoring as a purely algorithmic output where the human supervisor who may need to perceive, interpret and decide on the outputs is not explicitly considered. Moreover, existing combinations fuse monitor to improve detection performance, rather than coordinating local diagnoses toward fault isolation in the sense of classical fault diagnosis. This work addresses both gaps. It develops a runtime monitoring framework with multiple monitor derived fault-tolerant system principles, and it designs the framework around the human in the loop.

Considering the human in the loop means going beyond bare in/out verdict of an OOD detector. Such a verdict may not be sufficient when a supervisor remains responsible and needs a clear basis for judgment. To take over from an AI system or override a potentially unreliable output, they must understand what kind of event is occurring. Otherwise, even well-intended monitoring can contribute to situations similar to automation surprise (Sarter and Woods, 1997). Integrating a communication layer and building a more holistic runtime monitoring framework can thus promote more resilient, reliable, and safe system operation. This work address how outputs from multiple monitors can be fused into qualitatively richer feedback for humans in the loop.

## 1.3 Contributions

Based on the related work and the limitations identified above, this works makes the following contributions to the field of runtime monitoring for AI systems:

1. 

We present COMET, a coordinated multi-layer runtime monitoring framework in which multiple monitors derive their authority from different fault modes. In this work, we have three monitors: engineering rules (signal integrity), first-principle physics (spectral leak template), and learned behavioral statistics (feature-space competence envelope). This results in fault modes that are disjoint by construction. The monitor outputs are combined by a coordination layer and fused into a fixed taxonomy with a traffic-light mapping, designed as the communication layer to the human supervisor in the loop.

2. 

We demonstrate the quantitative performance via an empirical evaluation on an open dataset that serves as a proxy for a safety-critical application. Covariate shift, semantic shift, and injected sensor faults are held out from all training, validation, and calibration. Every monitor is frozen after development, and the shifts are evaluated exactly once, zero-shot. An ablation ladder compares COMET against the bare AI system and single-monitor setups, including the commonly used max-softmax probability (MSP) baseline, using supervisor-oriented metrics such as the trusted-wrong-answer rate and silent-miss-coverage.

3. 

We derive design insights for runtime monitoring of deployed AI systems, supported by the ablations and by pre-registered sensitivity variants, i.e., why disjoint anchoring matters more than individual monitor strength, why the calibration unit must match the decision unit, and where the boundary of passive monitoring lies. We report the negative results and residual fault modes openly, including the silent misses no passive layer combination caught, which we consider essential evidence for future research on monitoring AI systems.

## 2.1 AI application: leak detection system

To evaluate the coordinated multi-layer runtime monitoring framework, we used an open dataset (Johnson et al., 2020b) on compressed-air leaks with the complementary AI system that performs well on the dataset with accuracies reaching over 95 % (Johnson et al., 2020a). Its high performance reflects a realistic scenario where the result from training and testing satisfies the requirement for real-world deployment of an AI system. The AI system used was designed to classify spectrogram files into ”leak” or ”no leak” where a compressed-air leak represents a costly fault in industrial productions. The leak of compressed air does not represent a safety-critical issue, but using acoustic emissions together with AI systems to detect leaks is a common method used in process industries (Banjara et al., 2020; Ullah et al., 2023; Xu et al., 2026). The detection of leaks in this domain represents a safety system that is essential to ensure safe and reliable operation. Due to the lack of open and diverse data on gas leaks but the similarity between the use cases, we chose the compressed-air leak detection as suitable proxy for the safety-critical leak detection in process industries.

In this work, the AI system represents the AI component inside an AI-enabled safety system. It performs the safety function of detecting the leak, whereas the overall system additionally comprises physical components such as the sensors, the monitored process, and potential actuators that are activated to maintain safe and reliable operation.

## 2.1.1 Dataset

The data used for this work were collected by the Fraunhofer institute (Johnson et al., 2020b). The data represent compressed-air leaks that were collected with 4 microphones, at 2 gain settings, with different backgrounds (i.e., lab noise ”lab”, workshop noise ”work”/”work-low”, hydraulic noise ”hydr”/”hydr-low”), different leak types (i.e., vent leak at 6 bar ”vent”, vent leak at 5 bar ”vent-low”, tube leak ”tube”), and different severity gradation based on the knob state. Each leak type contains all backgrounds and for each background there are three sessions of recording. There are two recordings for each knob state, one with low gain and one with max gain. The knob state increases from 0 to 3 with a step size of 1 and from there it continues with a step size of 0.5 until 9.0. The knob state represents the number of turns to open the vent where 0.5 represents half a turn. Between knob state 3.5 and 5.0 there are small leaks present but not detectable for the installed microphones thus these knob states are excluded from the data. The whole dataset composition is seen in Figure 1.

## 2.1.2 Primary AI system

The primary AI system used for this work is based on the original convolutional neural network (CNN) model from Johnson et al. (2020a) that was used on the same dataset with some adaptions. The model architecture consists of an initial block that applies a Gaussian noise with standard deviation of 0.1 and that is only active during training mode. Next, there are three convolution blocks with a GELU (Hendrycks and Gimpel, 2016b) activation function and max pooling with channels 16, 32 and 64. The final block contains a dropout function, a dense multi-layer perceptron (MLP) network, a GELU activation function, another dropout regularization, and finally a second MLP with two logits as output. The whole model has 121,794 parameters.

## 2.1.3 Pre-processing

Each recording has a length of 30 seconds and a measurement frequency of 48 kHz. First each recording was transformed with a short-time Fourier transformation (STFT) to create a spectrogram of shape 513 x 2813 (frequency bins x time frames). Then the spectrogram is cut along the time axis into non-overlapping 24-frame patches, 267 ms. One recording yields 117 patches, each of shape 513 x 24. This also is the fixed-size ”image” for the AI system. Every patch inherits its recording’s label: ”leak” or ”no-leak”. Next, we augmented the training patches with noise to make the model more robust. This is relevant for the patches with lab noise which basically means no added noise. In the real world, we always have noise so this is why we chose the augmentation. With a probability of 0.5, we added white or pink Gaussian noise to the patches in the power domain at a random signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) between 0 and 20 dB. Validation and test patches are excluded from this augmentation. Finally, we normalize the patches. Each patch is z-scored per frequency bin: subtract the per-bin mean, divide by the per-bin standard deviation. The statistics are computed once over all training split frames of one fold.

## 2.1.4 Training process

We trained one AI system per microphone. For each microphone, the nominal envelope, i.e., ”vent”/”vent-low” leak types, ”lab”/”work”/”work-low” noise types, no-leak knob state $\leq 3.0$ , leak knob state $\geq 5.5$ , is split by whole recording session. This means a leave-one-session-out training with three folds: train on two sessions, validate on the held-out one. Session-level splitting guarantees no data leakage. For microphone-1, the concrete split resulted in 286 training files and 120 validation files, roughly a 70/30 split. The dataset for the training and validation is built per fold. First, the pre-processing is done as mentioned above. Then, the patches get split into 33,580 training patches and 14,080 validation patches for one fold. The normalization statistics are computed on the training split, and applied to both training and validation.

## CONCLUSION
The central finding of this work is that no single monitor or local diagnoser was sufficient, and that the fault modes of the individual monitors were largely disjoint as it was intended. Confidence-based monitoring reduced but did not remove trusted wrong answers. The physics-consistency monitor withheld its judgment exactly where it was most needed, and the feature-space monitor was the strongest single monitor but could not isolate and identify the fault. Only COMET reduced the trusted-wrong-answer rate to zero on hydraulic no-leak data and on three of four injected sensor faults, at a nominal false-alarm rate of $\approx 1\%$ . Equally important the qualitative difference that the coordinated multi-layer runtime monitoring structure with coordination and communication layers turns a detection flag into a diagnosis with detection, isolation and identification for some fault modes, i.e., sensor fault, physically implausible detection, or unfamiliar operating context. This is the form of information a responsible human supervisor can act on and adapt to.

## ABSTRACT
A benchmark is not merely a collection of test questions; it naturally induces an empirical measure over difficulty. In this work, we develop this measure-based view of benchmark evaluation. This perspective allows us to identify and formalize two central failure modes: measure mismatch, where the benchmark difficulty distribution differs from the intended difficulty distribution, and coverage gaps, where uncovered regions along the difficulty axis prevent benchmarks from detecting meaningful localized training progress. We propose two diagnostic metrics that capture these two failure modes and provide rigorous theoretical guarantees. Empirically, we analyze 25 popular benchmarks spanning 7 domains using carefully estimated item difficulties. Our results show that many benchmarks implicitly overweight certain difficulty regions, so their leaderboard conclusions should be interpreted with caution; others leave substantial portions of the difficulty axis uncovered, limiting their ability to monitor the model improvement process. Finally, we turn diagnosis into action by introducing BenchFill, a simple difficulty-targeted item rewriting algorithm that effectively reduces local coverage gaps.

## 1 Introduction

Benchmark evaluation looks simple: we run models on a set of questions and average the results. The resulting score is then used in two main ways: to compare models on leaderboards (Alzahrani et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2018; Liang et al., 2022; Myrzakhan et al., 2024), and to monitor progress during training (Liu et al., 2025; Hu et al., 2025; He et al., 2025; Bercovich et al., 2025; Luo et al., 2025). But this simplicity hides a basic issue: the conclusion depends not only on how well models perform, but also on how benchmark questions are distributed Siska et al. (2024); Zhang et al. (2026).

Consider first the leaderboard setting in Fig. 1(a), where we want to compare two models. Suppose they perform similarly on easy questions but differ substantially on hard ones. If the benchmark places most of its weight on easy questions, while the difficulty distribution of interest places more weight on harder questions, the two evaluations can lead to different conclusions for the same pair of models: the benchmark may suggest that the two models are nearly tied, even though under the desired difficulty distribution they are clearly separated. Now consider the training setting in Fig. 1(b), where we want to track whether a model is improving. Suppose the model improves substantially over a local difficulty range. If the benchmark has few or no questions in that region, the benchmark score may barely change, even though the model has made real progress.

These two failure cases suggest that benchmark conclusions depend critically on how questions are distributed. To study this structure systematically, we organize benchmark questions along a difficulty axis, which provides a natural progression from easier to harder problems. The key conceptual step is to use difficulty as a way to define the distributional structure of the benchmark itself. This view recasts a benchmark from a collection of test questions into an empirical measure over difficulty, with each question contributing equal evaluation mass at its difficulty level.

This measure-theoretic formalization is useful because it turns the two failure cases above into precise and analyzable problems about the benchmark-induced difficulty measure. The first is a measure mismatch problem: the benchmark may distribute its mass differently from the target difficulty distribution that represents the intended evaluation objective, so benchmark conclusions can differ from the conclusions under the target difficulty distribution. The second is a coverage gap problem. Because the benchmark is an empirical measure supported only on finitely many difficulty points, there may be intervals of difficulty with little or no benchmark mass. A substantial performance gain concentrated in such an interval may therefore produce little or no change in the benchmark score. To our knowledge, this is the first work to identify and formalize measure mismatch and coverage gaps as two fundamental failure modes of benchmark evaluation.

These two problems naturally lead to two simple diagnostic metrics: the Wasserstein distance Villani and others (2009) between the benchmark measure and a target difficulty distribution, and the maximum gap between adjacent benchmark difficulty points. We provide theoretical results showing that these quantities directly characterize distributional mismatch and the detectability of localized improvements. Empirically, we analyze 25 popular benchmarks spanning 7 domains. We estimate question difficulty using Item Response Theory and compute the proposed metrics. Our results show that both issues are prevalent across benchmarks.

Our key contributions are summarized as follows:

• 

We introduce a new measure-based perspective on benchmark evaluation, representing a benchmark as an empirical measure over difficulty. This perspective formalizes measure mismatch and coverage gaps, motivates two diagnostic metrics, and yields rigorous theoretical guarantees.

• 

We conduct an empirical study across 25 benchmarks spanning 7 domains, showing that both difficulty-distribution mismatch and large coverage gaps are widespread in current benchmarks.

• 

We propose BenchFill, a difficulty-targeted gap-filling algorithm. With only three inserted items per benchmark, BenchFill reduces the sum of the top three difficulty gaps by $21.09\%$ – $38.88\%$ .

## 2 Formalization and Theory

## Difficulty space and model performance function.

We represent the difficulty of a benchmark item, e.g., a question, by a continuous variable $z\in[0,1]$ , where larger values correspond to harder items. For a model $\theta$ , let $s_{\theta}(z)\in[0,1]$ denote its conditional expected performance on items with difficulty $z$ , e.g., pass@1.

## Benchmark distribution and score.

Let a benchmark $B=\{x_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}$ consist of $n$ items, and let
 $z_{i}\in[0,1]$ denote the difficulty of item $x_{i}$ . The benchmark score is $S_{B}(\theta):=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}s_{\theta}(z_{i})$ . This average implicitly induces an empirical measure over the difficulty axis,

 $\nu_{B}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{z_{i}},$ 

where $\delta_{z}$ denotes a point mass at difficulty $z$ . Under this representation, the benchmark score is exactly an integral with respect to the benchmark-induced difficulty measure, $S_{B}(\theta)=\int s_{\theta}(z)\,d\nu_{B}(z)$ .

## Performance difference between two models.

For two models $\theta$ and $\theta^{\prime}$ , define the difficulty-conditioned performance difference as $\Delta(z):=s_{\theta^{\prime}}(z)-s_{\theta}(z)$ , where the dependence of $\Delta$ on $(\theta^{\prime},\theta)$ is omitted for notational simplicity. The performance difference observed on benchmark $B$ is

 $D_{B}(\theta^{\prime},\theta):=S_{B}(\theta^{\prime})-S_{B}(\theta)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Delta(z_{i})=\int\Delta(z)\,d\nu_{B}(z).$ 

## 2.1 Measure Mismatch

## Benchmark-induced coupling.

The quantity $D_{B}(\theta^{\prime},\theta)$ implicitly couples two distinct objects: the model-dependent function $\Delta(z)$ and the benchmark-dependent measure $\nu_{B}$ . Thus, a leaderboard comparison is not merely determined by the difficulty-conditioned performance difference between the models; it also depends on how the benchmark allocates mass over the difficulty axis. If this allocation differs from the difficulty distribution of interest, the benchmark aggregates the same model difference under a mismatched measure. This mismatch can distort the observed model gap and, in severe cases, change the induced ordering.

## Target distribution.

Formally, we introduce a target distribution
 $\mu\in\mathcal{P}([0,1]),$ 
which represents the intended evaluation distribution over the difficulty space. This target distribution may encode the desired application mix. Under $\mu$ , the target score of model $\theta$ and the target performance difference between $\theta^{\prime}$ and $\theta$ are defined as

 $S_{\mu}(\theta):=\int s_{\theta}(z)\,d\mu(z),\hskip 20.00003ptD_{\mu}(\theta^{\prime},\theta):=S_{\mu}(\theta^{\prime})-S_{\mu}(\theta)=\int\Delta(z)\,d\mu(z).$ 

The discrepancy between $D_{B}(\theta^{\prime},\theta)$ and $D_{\mu}(\theta^{\prime},\theta)$ arises from the mismatch between the benchmark measure $\nu_{B}$ and the target distribution $\mu$ . This viewpoint allows us to study distortions in leaderboard comparisons as a distribution mismatch problem.

## A metric for distribution mismatch.

We measure how well the difficulty distribution of a benchmark matches a target difficulty distribution using the 1-Wasserstein distance. Specifically, for a benchmark $B$ with empirical difficulty measure $\nu_{B}$ and a target distribution $\mu$ on $[0,1]$ , we define its Difficulty Distribution Mismatch (DDM) as

 $\mathrm{DDM}(B;\mu):=W_{1}(\nu_{B},\mu)=\inf_{\pi\in\Pi(\nu_{B},\mu)}\int_{[0,1]^{2}}|u-v|\,d\pi(u,v),$ 

where $\Pi(\nu_{B},\mu)$ denotes the set of all couplings of $\nu_{B}$ and $\mu$ . Intuitively, $W_{1}(\nu_{B},\mu)$ measures how much probability mass must be transported, and how far, to transform the benchmark difficulty distribution into the target one. The following theorem shows that this mismatch directly controls the distortion between benchmark-based and target-based performance differences.

Theorem 2.1 (Distribution mismatch controls score distortion and rank stability).

Suppose the performance difference function $\Delta$ is $L$ -Lipschitz. Then

 $\bigl|D_{B}(\theta^{\prime},\theta)-D_{\mu}(\theta^{\prime},\theta)\bigr|\leq L\,W_{1}(\nu_{B},\mu).$ 

Consequently, if $|D_{\mu}(\theta^{\prime},\theta)|>L\,W_{1}(\nu_{B},\mu)$ , then

 $\operatorname{sign}\bigl(D_{B}(\theta^{\prime},\theta)\bigr)=\operatorname{sign}\bigl(D_{\mu}(\theta^{\prime},\theta)\bigr),$ 

so the benchmark-induced ordering agrees with the target ordering, i.e., no rank flip can occur.

Theorem 2.1 shows that $W_{1}(\nu_{B},\mu)$ bounds how much the benchmark-induced score difference can differ from the target score difference. A small $W_{1}(\nu_{B},\mu)$ means that the benchmark provides a faithful approximation to the target evaluation, while a large $W_{1}(\nu_{B},\mu)$ indicates that the benchmark may overemphasize some difficulty regions and underrepresent others, potentially distorting model comparisons and even changing their induced ranking. App. B.1 provides the full proof and extends this result to the finite-sample setting.

## A principled target distribution without prior information.

When prior information is available, it is natural to choose $\mu$ to reflect the intended evaluation objective over the difficulty space. In many settings, however, such prior knowledge is unavailable. In that case, choosing a non-uniform target would implicitly privilege some difficulty regions over others without evidence. To characterize a principled default choice, we consider an agnostic setting in which the location of the performance difference along the difficulty axis is completely unknown. We consider the class

 $\mathcal{F}_{\rho}:=\left\{\Delta:[0,1]\to[-1,1]\ \text{measurable}:\int_{0}^{1}\Delta(z)\,dz=\rho\right\},\hskip 20.00003pt\rho\in(-1,1).$ 

This class encodes an uncertainty set: the signed performance difference has a fixed average level $\rho$ over the difficulty axis, but its location is otherwise unrestricted.

For any candidate target distribution $\mu\in\mathcal{P}([0,1])$ , define its worst-case retained difference by

 $V_{\rho}(\mu):=\inf_{\Delta\in\mathcal{F}_{\rho}}\int\Delta(z)\,d\mu(z).$ 

The following theorem shows that, under complete uncertainty about where along the difficulty axis the performance difference occurs, the uniform distribution is the unique maximin target, and is the only choice whose worst-case retained difference remains equal to the average difference $\rho$ .

Theorem 2.2 (Uniform target as the unique maximin choice).

For $\rho\in(-1,1)$ , we have

 $\sup_{\mu\in\mathcal{P}([0,1])}V_{\rho}(\mu)=\rho,$ 

and the unique maximizer is the uniform distribution $\lambda=\mathrm{Unif}[0,1]$ .

Theorem 2.2 does not claim that the uniform distribution is universally optimal. Rather, it justifies the uniform target as a principled default when no prior information over the difficulty space is available. In that case, adopting the uniform target avoids introducing an additional preference over difficulty regions and provides a neutrality criterion for benchmark evaluation: the evaluation does not privilege any difficulty region a priori. Full proof is provided in App. B.1.

## 2.2 Coverage Gaps

## Discrete benchmark measures may fail to monitor local training improvements.

Benchmarks are often used not only for final model comparison, but also to monitor training progress (He et al., 2025; Yu et al., 2025; Luo et al., 2025). However, training improvements are often local rather than uniform: a model may improve substantially over a particular difficulty range while improving only modestly elsewhere.

Recall that a benchmark score is an integral with respect to the benchmark empirical measure $\nu_{B}$ . Since $\nu_{B}$ is a discrete measure supported only on the finitely many benchmark difficulty points, it assigns zero mass to every interval that contains no benchmark item. Consequently, even if the model improves substantially on a nontrivial contiguous difficulty region, the benchmark score may not reflect this improvement when the region contains no benchmark item. In this sense, a benchmark can fail as a progress monitor when its local resolution is insufficient.

## Maximum gap.

We first characterize the local resolution of a benchmark through its maximum gap. Let $0\leq z_{(1)}\leq\cdots\leq z_{(n)}\leq 1$ be the ordered benchmark difficulty points, and define $z_{(0)}:=0$ and $z_{(n+1)}:=1$ . The maximum gap of benchmark $B$ is

 $G(B):=\max_{0\leq j\leq n}\bigl(z_{(j+1)}-z_{(j)}\bigr)$ 

(1)

i.e., the length of the largest interval on the difficulty axis that contains no benchmark point.

## A hypothesis testing formulation.

To formalize whether a benchmark can reliably detect localized training improvements, we cast the problem as a hypothesis test. Under the null hypothesis, there is no improvement. Under the alternative, the post-improvement performance exceeds the pre-improvement performance by at least $a$ over some contiguous difficulty interval of width $w$ . The following theorem shows that the benchmark maximum gap $G(B)$ is the exact asymptotic minimax detection boundary.

Theorem 2.3 (Maximum gap as an exact asymptotic minimax detection boundary).

Fix a test level $\alpha\in(0,1)$ and $a\in(0,1]$ . Let $s^{-},s^{+}:[0,1]\to[0,1]$ denote the pre- and post-improvement performance curves, respectively. For a benchmark $B$ , let $\Pi_{\alpha}^{*}(B;w,a,k)$ denote the minimax worst-case power of level- $\alpha$ tests based on $k$ repeated samples per benchmark item for testing

 $H_{0}:\ s^{-}=s^{+}\hskip 20.00003pt\text{versus}\hskip 20.00003ptH_{1}:\ \exists\ \text{interval }I\subset[0,1],\ |I|=w,\ s^{+}(z)-s^{-}(z)\geq a\ \ \forall z\in I.$ 

1. 

If $w\leq G(B)$ , then $\Pi_{\alpha}^{*}(B;w,a,k)=\alpha$ for every $k\in\mathbb{N}$ .

2. 

If $w>G(B)$ , then $\lim_{k\to\infty}\Pi_{\alpha}^{*}(B;w,a,k)=1$ .

Therefore, $G(B)$ exactly characterizes the asymptotic minimax detection boundary for benchmark $B$ .

Theorem 2.3 shows that the discrete geometry of the benchmark induces a sharp detectability phase transition. If the width of a local improvement does not exceed the maximum adjacent difficulty gap, then no level- $\alpha$ test can achieve nontrivial worst-case power, regardless of how many repeated evaluations are performed. In other words, such an improvement can be completely invisible to the benchmark in the worst case. Once the improvement width exceeds the maximum gap, repeated evaluation can eventually make the signal detectable. In this sense, $G(B)$ characterizes the intrinsic local resolution limit of benchmark $B$ .
The full proof is given in App. B.2.

## CONCLUSION
In this work, we view each benchmark as an empirical measure over difficulty. This perspective enables us to formalize measure mismatch and coverage gaps, leading to theoretical results and practical diagnostics.
We believe this empirical-measure view has the potential to open new ways to rigorously study and rethink benchmark evaluation.

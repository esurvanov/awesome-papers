## ABSTRACT
LLM agents with similar success rates can follow different action sequences. An Agent Behavior Map represents LLM-harness combinations using centered action log-likelihoods on shared reference trajectories, without requiring new environment rollouts from the target agent. We derive error bounds relating squared coordinate distances to trajectory KL under explicit assumptions, and across eight cross-scored models the distances track the directly measured trajectory KL in rank order. Across 100 LLMs and four environments, ALFWorld-based coordinates support performance prediction and identify behavioral similarities. In ALFWorld, these similarities persist after controlling for success rates and model size, including across model families. As an extension, harness-component displacements approximately compose, with accuracy varying across components and environments.

## 1 Introduction

Comparing LLM agents is a matter not only of how often they succeed but also of how they act. Agents with similar success rates can differ in the tools they choose, the order of their actions, and how often they repeat an operation. Such behavior depends on the combination of the underlying LLM and the harness that manages history, planning, and reasoning (Zhang et al., 2026; Yao et al., 2026; Vats and Golev, 2026; Fan et al., 2026). Running every combination in the environment reveals both performance and behavior, but each new agent to compare requires collecting new trajectories. This paper asks whether a representation that captures similarity of run-time behavior can be built without new environment runs of the target agent.

When agents are compared by their log-likelihoods on shared reference trajectories, the difficulty is that the distribution of the histories on which the likelihoods are conditioned itself depends on the agent’s policy. The histories contained in the reference trajectories need not coincide with those that an agent reaches through its own actions. Whether similarity of action probabilities measured on shared histories corresponds to similarity of trajectory distributions and behavior at run time is therefore not obvious. How much of these on-policy properties can be captured by off-policy observations on fixed reference trajectories is the central question of this work.

Language model maps, which vectorize log-likelihoods on shared texts, represent the relations between models in a common coordinate space (Oyama et al., 2025). Their extension to prompt–response distributions has examined performance prediction from conditional log-likelihoods and the approximate additivity of the coordinate displacements caused by prompt changes (Oyama et al., 2026). This paper applies the framework to agents that interact sequentially with an environment and answers the question above with theory and experiments.

An Agent Behavior Map treats each agent as a pair of an LLM and a harness. Each agent rebuilds its input with its own harness from the shared external history in the reference trajectories, namely the task, the observations, and the past actions. The recorded actions are teacher-forced under this input, and the resulting log-likelihood vector is centered to give the agent’s coordinates. Because the reference trajectories are reused, the coordinates of a new agent can be computed without collecting any additional environment trajectories from it. Figure 1 shows an example in which agents that are close in these coordinates take similar action sequences when run. We validate the representation from two angles.

• 

Theoretical and empirical relation to the trajectory distribution (§2, §3.1). Under assumptions on the locality of the two policies and on the mismatch with the reference distribution, we derive error bounds that relate squared coordinate distances to the KL divergence between trajectory distributions. The error decomposes into local approximation, reference mismatch, centering, and finite samples, and the decomposition shows how the step-level and trajectory-level representations differ in their dependence on trajectory length. In a cross-scoring experiment with eight models, we compare the coordinate distances with the directly measured trajectory KL, the target itself, and find a high rank correlation.

• 

Validation on performance and behavior (§3.2, §3.3). We apply a common harness to 100 LLMs and evaluate coordinates built from ALFWorld reference trajectories alone on ALFWorld, TextWorld, BFCL, and MiniWoB++. Besides predicting the success rates of unevaluated models from the evaluation results of the others, we ask whether neighbors on the map generate similar action sequences. In ALFWorld in particular, the similarity of action sequences remains when compared with neighbors chosen by success rate or by size, and when the candidates are restricted to models of other families. These results support the view that the coordinates carry behavioral information that neither performance level nor model family explains. The accuracy of performance prediction and the strength of behavioral similarity among neighbors, however, depend on the environment and the measure.

Finally, we represent the addition of a harness component as a displacement on the map and ask whether such displacements add up within an LLM (§4). Comparison with measured coordinates shows approximate additivity, with an accuracy that depends on the component and the environment.

## 2 Agent Behavior Maps

## 2.1 Problem: comparing agents by what they do when run

On task $x$ , an agent interacts with the environment and produces a trajectory $\tau=(x,o_{0},a_{0},o_{1},\dots,a_{T-1},o_{T})$ of observations $o_{t}$ and actions $a_{t}$ . Its length $T=T(\tau)$ varies and is capped by a known limit $T_{\max}$ . The external history before the action at time $t$ is $h_{t}=(x,o_{0},a_{0},\dots,o_{t})$ ; it excludes the generator’s internal state, such as its system prompt, reasoning, plans, and summaries. An agent $i=(\text{LLM},\text{harness})$ rebuilds its input from $h_{t}$ by its own rule $C_{i}$ (history window, retrieval, summarization) and generates text under the action grammar; $\pi_{i}(a\mid h)$ denotes the resulting probability of action $a$ (Appendix A.1 covers the grammar and tokenization). Given a task distribution $w$ , an initial observation $p_{0}$ , and environment dynamics $E_{t}=E(o_{t+1}\mid h_{t},a_{t})$ shared by all agents, agent $i$ induces the trajectory distribution

 $P_{i}(\tau)=w(x)\,p_{0}(o_{0}\mid x)\prod_{t<T}\pi_{i}(a_{t}\mid h_{t})\,E_{t}.$ 

(1)

The environment terms cancel in the log-likelihood ratio of two agents. We compare agents by the trajectory KL $K_{ij}:=\mathrm{KL}(P_{i}\|P_{j})$ and its symmetrization $K_{\mathrm{sym}}:=(K_{ij}+K_{ji})/2$ . $P_{i}$ is the distribution of trajectories when agent $i$ is actually run, so the properties we care about, such as success rates and action frequencies, are functionals of $P_{i}$ : on-policy quantities.

Writing $\mathbb{E}_{i}=\mathbb{E}_{P_{i}}$ , the chain rule of KL (Cover and Thomas, 2006; Ke et al., 2020) gives

 $K_{ij}=\mathbb{E}_{i}\textstyle\sum_{t<T}\mathrm{KL}\bigl(\pi_{i}(\cdot\mid h_{t})\|\pi_{j}(\cdot\mid h_{t})\bigr),$ 

(2)

an expectation over the histories that agent $i$ visits when it runs, so measuring it directly takes $n$ rollouts and $O(n^{2})$ cross-scorings for $n$ agents. We ask how much of this quantity can be read, without running the agents, from distances between coordinates obtained by forward passes alone.

## 2.2 Scoring agents on shared reference trajectories

We prepare $N$ reference trajectories $\tau_{1},\dots,\tau_{N}$ once, from an unknown reference distribution $Q$ . The distances use neither $Q(\tau)$ nor the generators or their mixing weights, so the trajectories can come from anywhere. We use four sources: logs of the agents under study (ALFWorld, Appendix C), traces published by others ( $\tau$ -bench (Yao et al., 2025) and SWE-Bench (Jimenez et al., 2024) from HAL (Kapoor et al., 2026)), rollouts of two models (the 100-model experiment, §3), and an equal-weight pool of eight scored models (§3.1). Let $T_{r}=T(\tau_{r})$ be the lengths and $M=\sum_{r}T_{r}$ the total number of steps. For each agent $i$ , teacher forcing (Williams and Zipser, 1989) reads $\ell_{i,rt}:=\log\pi_{i}(a_{rt}\mid h_{rt})$ and $A_{i,r}:=\sum_{t<T_{r}}\ell_{i,rt}$ without running the environment. Sharing the trajectories has two costs, the mismatch between $Q$ and each agent’s on-policy distribution and the finite number of trajectories, which §2.3 and §2.4 address.

## 2.3 Step and trajectory embeddings

Let $\bar{\ell}_{i}$ and $\bar{A}_{i}$ be the means of $\ell_{i,rt}$ over all $M$ steps and of $A_{i,r}$ over all $N$ trajectories. Subtracting each agent’s own mean, we define two centered embeddings

 $z^{S}_{i}:=M^{-\frac{1}{2}}(\ell_{i,rt}-\bar{\ell}_{i})_{r,t},\,z^{\tau}_{i}:=N^{-\frac{1}{2}}(A_{i,r}-\bar{A}_{i})_{r}.$ 

A step coordinate asks whether an action is natural in its situation; a trajectory coordinate asks whether a trajectory is natural from its initial state. Only existing steps $t<T_{r}$ are included. The distances are $\hat{D}_{S}=\lVert z^{S}_{i}-z^{S}_{j}\rVert$ and $\hat{D}_{\tau}=\lVert z^{\tau}_{i}-z^{\tau}_{j}\rVert$ . For a pair $(i,j)$ , let $d_{t}=\log\frac{\pi_{i}(a_{t}\mid h_{t})}{\pi_{j}(a_{t}\mid h_{t})}$ be the step log-ratio, $X=\sum_{t<T}d_{t}=\log\frac{P_{i}(\tau)}{P_{j}(\tau)}$ its sum, $\mu=\mathbb{E}_{Q}X$ its mean under the reference, and $\bar{T}=\mathbb{E}_{Q}[T]$ the mean length. The population distances are then $D^{2}_{S}=\frac{1}{\bar{T}}\mathbb{E}_{Q}\sum_{t<T}(d_{t}-\mu/\bar{T})^{2}$ and $D^{2}_{\tau}=\mathrm{Var}_{Q}(X)$ . Centering removes each agent’s overall confidence, its mean log-likelihood, from the coordinates (Appendices A.5 and B.2 treat the uncentered variants).

Distances estimate the trajectory KL.

Fix a pair $(i,j)$ . Assumption 1 (locality): for every history $h$ that occurs under $P_{i}$ , $P_{j}$ , or $Q$ , $\pi_{i}(\cdot\mid h)$ and $\pi_{j}(\cdot\mid h)$ have the same support and $\lvert\log\pi_{i}(a\mid h)/\pi_{j}(a\mid h)\rvert\leq\varepsilon$ on it. Assumption 2 (reference mismatch): $\mathrm{TV}(P_{i},Q)\leq\lambda$ and $\mathrm{TV}(P_{j},Q)\leq\lambda$ . Lengths satisfy $1\leq T\leq T_{\max}$ ; we assume neither independence across steps nor independence between $T$ and the actions.

Under Assumptions 1 and 2, $\lvert K_{ij}-\tfrac{\bar{T}}{2}D^{2}_{S}\rvert\leq R_{S}$ and $\lvert K_{ij}-\tfrac{1}{2}D^{2}_{\tau}\rvert\leq R_{\tau}$ , where, up to absolute constants,

## 2.4 Total error: approximation plus sampling

Theorem 1 concerns the population distances. In practice, $\hat{D}_{S}$ and $\hat{D}_{\tau}$ are computed from $N$ reference trajectories, so the total error adds sampling error to the approximation error. Bounding $\mathbb{E}_{i}[T]\leq T_{\max}$ and $\mathbb{E}_{i}[T^{2}]\leq T_{\max}^{2}$ in Table 1 gives the following.

Corollary 1 (Total error).

Let $\tau_{1},\dots,\tau_{N}\sim Q$ be i.i.d., and let $\hat{\bar{T}}:=M/N$ , $\hat{K}_{S}:=\tfrac{\hat{\bar{T}}}{2}\hat{D}_{S}^{2}$ , $\hat{K}_{\tau}:=\tfrac{1}{2}\hat{D}_{\tau}^{2}$ , and $u_{N}:=\sqrt{\log(8/\delta)/(2N)}$ . Under Assumptions 1 and 2, with probability at least $1-\delta$ , simultaneously

 $\displaystyle\lvert K_{ij}-\hat{K}_{S}\rvert$ 
 $\displaystyle\leq T_{\max}\,\varepsilon^{2}\bigl(\tfrac{\varepsilon}{4}+\tfrac{\lambda}{2}+3u_{N}\bigr)+\tfrac{\mu^{2}}{2\bar{T}},$ 

 $\displaystyle\lvert K_{ij}-\hat{K}_{\tau}\rvert$ 
 $\displaystyle\leq T_{\max}^{2}\varepsilon^{2}\bigl(\tfrac{\varepsilon}{4}+\tfrac{\lambda}{2}+3u_{N}\bigr)+\tfrac{\mu^{2}}{2}.$ 

The trajectory bound carries an extra factor of $T$ on every term. Since $K_{ij}\leq\varepsilon^{2}\mathbb{E}_{i}[T]/2$ by Lemma 1(iii) and the chain rule, the step bound grows with $T$ like the KL it estimates, whereas the trajectory bound grows as $T^{2}$ : summing log-ratios before squaring picks up cross terms between steps (Appendix A.4). Sampling error shares the parentheses, so it keeps this factor; it decays as $N^{-1/2}$ , while the other terms do not. The proof applies Hoeffding’s inequality (Hoeffding, 1963) to four sample means (Appendix A.3).

Scope of the guarantees.

The guarantees cover only close pairs, with small $\varepsilon$ and $\lambda$ . We therefore test empirically whether distances preserve the order of the KL outside this regime (§3.1) and whether close pairs behave alike when run (§3.3).

## 3 What does the map capture about an agent?

This section fixes the harness to a standard configuration (BASE: a history window of the last four steps, no planning, direct action; §4.1) and places each LLM as one point. The question is what the coordinates capture about an agent. LM maps showed a correlation between coordinates and downstream performance (Oyama et al., 2025). We answer with three quantities: the target itself, the KL between the trajectory distributions of the agents when run (§3.1); one projection of it, the success rate (§3.2); and the behavior itself when run (§3.3).

## 3.1 Does distance track the on-policy divergence?

Theorem 1 applies only in the local regime of small $\varepsilon$ , that is, to nearly identical policies, whereas actual pairs of models may be far apart. We therefore check empirically whether the distances track the trajectory KL outside that regime, and which version, step or trajectory, tracks it better. In the bounds, the step version is favored by a factor of order $T$ (Table 1); we ask whether this advantage shows in the data. Measuring the trajectory KL directly requires rollouts of every model and $O(n^{2})$ cross-scoring, so we use eight models: each solves a common set of ALFWorld (Shridhar et al., 2021) train tasks, every model scores every trajectory, and the cross-scoring gives $K_{\mathrm{sym}}$ for the $\binom{8}{2}=28$ pairs. The distances use the pooled trajectories of the eight models as the common reference (Appendix B.1).

Figure 2 shows the result (numbers in Table 6, Appendix B.2). The centered step estimate $\tfrac{\bar{T}}{2}\hat{D}_{S}^{2}$ correlates with $K_{\mathrm{sym}}$ at Pearson $r=0.90$ and Spearman $\rho=0.95$ , and the trajectory estimate $\tfrac{1}{2}\hat{D}_{\tau}^{2}$ at $0.86$ / $0.94$ : distances measured without running the agents preserve the order of the trajectory KL measured by running them. Two trends are consistent with the bounds (Appendix B.2): the RMSE of the trajectory version is an order of magnitude larger than that of the step version, as the orders $T$ versus $T^{2}$ predict, and adding reference trajectories shrinks only the sampling error while the approximation error remains. Centering, although it adds a term to the bound, raises the correlation and lowers the RMSE for both versions. Reading the distances between the eight models from the 100-model map of §3.2 instead, with different reference trajectories and no grammar constraint, gives $r=0.86$ and $\rho=0.88$ (Appendix B.2). Both versions overestimate the measured KL in level, by a median factor of 7.5 for the step version and 74 for the trajectory version (Appendix B.2), so in what follows we use the distances only as a proxy for the order of the KL, and we use the step version.

## LIMITATIONS SECTION
Locality.

Both the theoretical guarantee and the empirical correspondence are limited to close pairs. The KL can be read off the distance only for pairs in the local regime of small $\varepsilon$ and $\lambda$ , and pairs of different LLMs are not guaranteed to fall in it: the estimates exceed the measured KL by a median factor of 7.5, although their order agrees (§3.1). The correspondence with behavioral distance holds for neighbors and fades beyond about the 30th neighbor (Figure 1(c)); we have not shown that the magnitude of the distance between far-apart pairs measures how different their behavior is. The map answers who is similar to whom, not by how much.

Dependence on the reference trajectories.

Differences between policies in regions that $Q$ never visits cannot be measured, and the reference mismatch $\lambda$ remains a sensitivity parameter that the likelihood matrix alone does not determine. A general theory of the reference policy is left for future work.

Estimation of the KL.

The trajectory log-likelihood ratios in §3.1 are based on the probability of the canonical token sequence and differ from action probabilities that marginalize over all tokenizations. In environments where the candidate set can be enumerated, this can be resolved by renormalization, but in general it is a limitation.

Access to log-likelihoods.

The coordinates require the target model’s log-likelihood of a given action sequence. Models that are available only through an API that does not return the log-probabilities of given text cannot be placed on the map, so this study is limited to open-weight models.

Sample size of behavior.

The behavioral comparisons are based on one rollout per task, and the behavior of the same model varies from run to run (Bjarnason et al., 2026; Gonuguntla, 2026). We have not compared the size of this variation with the behavioral distance to neighbors.

Scope of the harness experiment.

The harnesses in §4 are limited to the 12 configurations of our own three-slot design (context management, planning, acting) and to 12 LLMs; the main additivity results are on $\tau$ -bench, and ALFWorld and SWE-Bench cover 6 LLMs. Whether the same additivity holds for other scaffolds with differently designed components has not been tested.

## CONCLUSION
Agent Behavior Maps, obtained by teacher forcing on common reference trajectories, locate agents without running them. Their coordinates predict the success rates of models that were never run, on the benchmark the map was built from and, combined with size, in other environments. Neighbors on the map take similar action sequences when run. The theory decomposes the gap between the distances and the trajectory KL into four error sources and limits the guarantee to close pairs. Harness components appear as displacements within an LLM whose sums approximate the coordinates of unscored combinations, with an accuracy that depends on the component and the environment. The map represents not only a ranking by performance but also how close agents are in what they do.

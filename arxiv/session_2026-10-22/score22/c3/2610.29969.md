## ABSTRACT
Reliable validation of autonomous driving cyber-physical systems requires testing not only nominal driving behavior but also failures arising from faults in sensing and embedded computation. Physical road testing provides realistic system behavior but is costly and difficult to reproduce, while simulation enables scalable evaluation but cannot fully capture hardware-dependent behavior and fault propagation. Hardware-in-the-loop (HIL) validation bridges this gap by executing the driving stack on real embedded hardware within a simulated environment. However, preparing fault-augmented scenarios for HIL testing remains largely a manual process, limiting test coverage, scalability, and efficiency. We present FaultGene, an LLM-assisted framework for automated generation of executable fault-augmented tests for HIL fault-resilience validation. Given a baseline Scenic/CARLA scenario and a structured fault specification, FaultGene generates a fault-augmented test case that captures the effects of HIL-level fault injection. During offline refinement, semantic-consistency checks and execution-level feedback are used to correct candidates that fail to preserve the intended scenario context or produce the desired fault-induced degradation, and to construct high-quality supervision for fine-tuning. The resulting generator then produces fault-augmented tests directly at deployment time without requiring manually engineered reference executions or repeated refinement loops.

## I Introduction

The rapid advancement of Automated Driving Systems (ADS) has created an urgent need for testing environments that can reliably expose safety-critical failures [1]. Road testing provides high-fidelity evaluation but is costly, time-consuming, difficult to reproduce, and limited in its ability to systematically exercise rare fault conditions. Simulation-based testing offers scalable and reproducible alternatives through controllable scenario generation using platforms such as CARLA [2]. Existing probabilistic, constraint-based, and search-based approaches [3, 4] further expand the scenario space for systematic verification. However, simulation alone cannot fully capture hardware-dependent effects, such as timing variations, scheduling jitter, and embedded computation faults, motivating hardware-in-the-loop (HIL) testing, where the driving stack executes on real hardware within a simulated environment.

Despite these advantages, preparing fault-augmented scenarios for HIL testing remains a practical bottleneck. A fault-oriented test must define both the driving context and the intended sensor or compute fault while preserving scenario semantics and producing meaningful system-level effects. Manually constructing scenarios across fault types, activation times, and severities is labor-intensive and difficult to scale.

Large language models (LLMs) offer a potential solution by translating high-level specifications into executable scenario programs [5]. However, one-shot generation may produce scenarios that violate the intended context, fail to compile or execute, or fail to expose the specified fault meaningfully. These limitations motivate combining LLM-based generation with execution-guided refinement grounded in HIL behavior.

This paper presents FaultGene, an LLM-assisted framework for automated generation of fault-augmented HIL test cases. Given a baseline Scenic [3]/CARLA scenario and a structured specification of fault type, activation timing, and severity, FaultGene generates a fault-augmented test while preserving the underlying driving context. The framework integrates structured LLM generation with execution-guided refinement in the Autonomous Driving Digital Twin (ADDT) environment [6].
HIL executions provide fault-grounded reference behavior and system-level feedback that are used offline to refine generated scenarios and construct supervision for fine-tuning the generator.
After tuning, the generator produces fault-augmented tests in a single pass without reference executions or iterative refinement during deployment.

We evaluate FaultGene on held-out scenario–fault combinations across modular and end-to-end AD architectures. Experimental results show improved scenario validity and executability over one-shot LLM generation, while the generated tests reproduce the main sensor- and compute-fault sensitivity trends observed in ADDT. Compared with conventional manual fault-scenario engineering, FaultGene reduces fault-engineering effort by 72 $\times$ per fault type.

In summary, this work makes the following contributions:

• 

An LLM-assisted framework for automatically transforming baseline driving scenarios and structured fault specifications into executable fault-augmented test cases;

• 

A feedback-driven refinement and tuning mechanism that uses execution feedback as supervision to improve semantic consistency and fault-induced system behavior;

• 

An empirical evaluation showing improved scenario validity, ADDT-consistent fault trends, and higher testing efficiency compared with conventional HIL workflows.

## II Related Works

Digital-twin-based hardware-in-the-loop (HIL) validation enables controlled evaluation of autonomous driving systems under realistic sensing, compute, and communication disturbances. Digital twins model the vehicle, environment, and traffic context [7, 8], while HIL co-simulation executes perception, planning, and control on real embedded hardware, exposing timing effects, scheduling jitter, and fault propagation that simulation-only studies may miss [9, 10, 11]. Fault injection has been used to assess neural-network-based autonomous driving components: APPRAISER [12] and DNN accelerator fault-injection frameworks [13] examine model-level vulnerability, while AVFI and Kayotee [14, 15] inject faults across perception, planning, and control in simulated environments. However, these approaches generally do not jointly address fault-oriented scenario generation and realistic embedded execution.

Autonomous-driving scenario generation has explored probabilistic and constraint-based specification, systematic search, and optimization. Scenic [3] enables probabilistic and constraint-based scenario specification, while VerifAI [4] performs simulation-based verification and falsification over parameterized scenario spaces; their combination has been used to identify challenging scenarios [16], alongside optimization- and adversarial-search-based approaches [17]. Works have also explored learning scenario representations from driving videos, such as [18]. More recently, LLMs have been applied to driving-scenario description and generation. DriveGPT4 [19] and Talk2Drive [20] support language-based driving representation and interaction, vision-language approaches derive structured scenarios from driving videos [21], and DriveArena [22] and LMDrive [23] explore LLM-based scenario expansion and reasoning over traffic interactions and environmental conditions. Nevertheless, these methods typically rely on predefined scenario spaces or text-/simulation-level generation and do not directly construct fault-augmented scenarios from structured hardware-fault specifications or validate their fault-induced effects on real embedded hardware.
FaultGene addresses this gap by treating fault-augmented test generation as a program-level generation and refinement problem grounded in HIL execution.

## III HIL-Based Autonomous Driving Validation

In this section, we introduce the Autonomous Driving Digital Twin (ADDT) [6], the HIL validation environment used throughout this work.

## III-A Hardware-in-the-Loop Setup

As shown in Fig. 1, ADDT consists of a simulation system and an AD computing system connected through Ethernet. The simulator streams sensor data to the AD system and receives control commands, enabling closed-loop execution in the virtual environment.

## III-A1 Digital-Twin Simulation System

Software.
The simulation system is built on CARLA [2], an open-source AD simulator based on Unreal Engine [24]. OpenSCENARIO [25] and Scenic [3] specify and parameterize the traffic scenarios.

Hardware.
The simulator runs on a workstation with an Intel i7-9800X CPU and an NVIDIA RTX 2080 Ti GPU.

## III-A2 AD Computing System

The AD stack (AD architectures are described in Section V) runs on an NVIDIA Jetson Xavier or Orin, processing six 480p or three 720p camera streams at 10 Hz and producing control commands at 100 Hz.

## III-B Fault Injection Mechanism

We evaluate resilience by injecting faults into the sensing and computing paths of the HIL pipeline.

Sensor fault injection.
ADDT models temporal and spatial sensing faults:

1. 

Temporal faults:
Controlled timestamp delays are introduced into sensor streams. Delays exceeding the perception model’s out-of-order tolerance are treated as frame drops.

2. 

Spatial faults:
Position and orientation perturbations are applied to the ground-truth extrinsic parameters to emulate sensor misalignment with controllable severity.

Computing hardware fault injection.
ADDT supports closed-loop CPU and GPU fault injection for studying memory and data-path errors [26, 27]. CPU faults are injected with LLFI [28], while GPU faults are injected with NVBitFI [29]. Both tools introduce register-level bit flips at specified locations and values, allowing the resulting errors to propagate through subsequent AD computations. This approach follows prior fault-injection studies [27, 26].

## IV FaultGene: Automated Fault-Augmented Test Generation

Fig. 3: 
Overview of FaultGene.
(a) Pipeline overview showing offline refinement and deployment-time generation steps.
(b) Detailed offline refinement process, where FaultGene generates fault-augmented Scenic scenarios, compares their executions with manually engineered HIL references using ScenarioFeat, and uses the resulting feedback for refinement and fine-tuning.

FaultGene is an LLM-based framework for generating executable fault-augmented tests for autonomous-driving fault-resilience validation. An overview of the framework is shown in Fig. 3. Given a baseline Scenic/CARLA scenario and a structured fault specification, it produces constrained scenario variants that can be executed in CARLA or through a CARLA-based HIL setup, such as the ADDT HIL environment described in Section III.

FaultGene has two phases. During offline framework construction, a prompt-based generator produces candidate fault-augmented Scenic programs, while ScenarioFeat extracts semantic attributes from execution videos. Generated executions are compared with manually engineered ADDT HIL references, and detected mismatches guide prompt refinement and supervision construction. After fine-tuning, the deployed generator produces tests in one pass without accessing manually engineered references, execution feedback, or refinement loops.

## IV-A ScenarioFeat: Video-Based Scenario Feature Extraction

ScenarioFeat is a prompt-engineered module that extracts semantic features from manually engineered reference and FaultGene-generated executions. It uses Qwen2.5-VL-7B-Instruct as its base vision-language model.

For an execution video $V$ , ScenarioFeat extracts a predefined semantic feature vector describing the driving context and safety-relevant behavior, including road environment, surrounding-object interactions, obstacle relationships, and vehicle maneuvers such as cut-in behavior:

 $C(V)=[c_{1}(V),c_{2}(V),\ldots,c_{n}(V)],$ 

(1)

where $c_{i}(V)\in[0,1]$ denotes the predicted confidence score for feature category $i$ .

ScenarioFeat is prompt-engineered using representative execution videos and expected feature annotations. It does not generate scenarios; instead, it provides a structured semantic representation for comparing generated executions with manually engineered references.

## IV-B FaultGene: Prompt-Based Scenic Generation

FaultGene is a text-only LLM, instantiated with Qwen3-14B-Instruct, that transforms a baseline Scenic scenario and structured fault specification into a fault-augmented Scenic program. Formally,

 $S_{F}=\pi_{\theta}(S_{0},F),$ 

(2)

where $S_{0}$ is the baseline scenario, $F$ is the fault specification, and $\pi_{\theta}$ is the generator.

The input includes $S_{0}$ , $F$ , and constraints requiring valid Scenic syntax and preservation of the original driving context. The fault specification defines the fault type, activation condition, and severity. FaultGene modifies the scenario while preserving its road layout, actor identities, and intended maneuver semantics. Because the output is structured Scenic code rather than free-form text, it can be syntax-checked and executed directly in CARLA or CARLA-based HIL setups such as ADDT.

## IV-C Reference-Guided Similarity and Execution Checks

Each generated scenario $S_{F}$ is executed through the HIL loop with the under-test AD system and compared with the corresponding manually fault-engineered ADDT HIL execution.
The reference provides offline HIL grounding for supervision construction and is not required at deployment.

Let $V_{\mathrm{ref}}$ and $V_{F}$ denote the reference and generated execution videos. ScenarioFeat extracts their semantic feature vectors:

 $C(V_{\mathrm{ref}})=[\ldots,c_{n}(V_{\mathrm{ref}})],\qquad C(V_{F})=[\ldots,c_{n}(V_{F})].$ 

(3)

The feature mismatch vector is defined as

 $\displaystyle\mathbf{d}_{C}(V_{\mathrm{ref}},V_{F})$ 
 $\displaystyle=[c_{1}(V_{\mathrm{ref}})-c_{1}(V_{F}),\ldots,$ 

(4)

 $\displaystyle c_{n}(V_{\mathrm{ref}})-c_{n}(V_{F})].$ 

A generated execution is semantically inconsistent if

 $|\mathbf{d}_{C}(V_{\mathrm{ref}},V_{F})_{i}|>\tau_{i}$ 

(5)

for any feature $i$ . The threshold $\tau_{i}$ controls the tradeoff between semantic preservation and scenario diversity and is used only during offline refinement.

We separately assess fault relevance using near-collision events, inter-vehicle clearance, maneuver interruption, collision outcome, and task completion.
Let $M(S_{F})$ denote the resulting metric vector. A scenario is fault-relevant if

 $M(S_{F})\in\Omega,$ 

(6)

where $\Omega$ denotes safety-relevant degradation without trivial invalidity, such as deadlock, early termination, or failure to trigger the fault.

Together, these checks determine whether the scenario preserves the intended context, implements the requested fault, and allows it to manifest during execution.

## IV-D Offline Iterative Refinement and Supervised Fine-Tuning

Candidates that fail either semantic or fault-relevance checks, or fail to compile or execute successfully, are refined offline.
The mismatch is converted into structured feedback covering Scenic syntax and compilation, physical or semantic validity, fault-specification consistency, and execution-level fault manifestation. Depending on the failure, the feedback may request corrected actor placement, adjusted activation timing, stronger fault severity, or removal of unintended deadlocks and early termination.

Let $\Delta(S_{F})$ denote the aggregated semantic and execution mismatch relative to the manually engineered reference. Starting from $S_{F}^{(0)}$ , the generator produces the next candidate as

 $S_{F}^{(k+1)}=\mathrm{Refine}\!\left(S_{0},F,S_{F}^{(k)},\Delta(S_{F}^{(k)})\right),$ 

(7)

until

 $|\mathbf{d}_{C}(V_{\mathrm{ref}},V_{F}^{(k)})_{i}|\leq\tau_{i},\quad\forall i,$ 

(8)

and

 $M(S_{F}^{(k)})\in\Omega.$ 

(9)

Here, $\mathrm{Refine}(\cdot)$ denotes rule-guided LLM regeneration using the original baseline scenario, fault specification, current candidate, and structured feedback prompts. It is not a reinforcement-learning policy or an external optimizer that directly edits the Scenic program.

The resulting scenario $S_{F}^{\star}$ is retained as the corrected target for $(S_{0},F)$ . Repeating this process over $N$ framework-construction examples yields

 $\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{ref}}=\left\{\left(S_{0}^{(j)},F^{(j)},S_{F}^{\star(j)}\right)\right\}_{j=1}^{N}.$ 

(10)

We then fine-tune the original generator $\pi_{\theta}$ on $\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{ref}}$ using supervised fine-tuning (SFT). Each training example uses the baseline scenario and fault specification $(S_{0},F)$ as input and the corresponding refined Scenic program $S_{F}^{\star}$ as the target. The trainable LoRA parameters are optimized with teacher-forced causal language modeling:

 $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{SFT}}=-\sum_{t=1}^{|S_{F}^{\star}|}\log P_{\theta}\!\left(s_{t}^{\star}\mid S_{0},F,s_{<t}^{\star}\right),$ 

(11)

where $s_{t}^{\star}$ is the $t$ -th token of $S_{F}^{\star}$ and $s_{<t}^{\star}$ denotes its preceding tokens. The baseline scenario and fault specification provide the conditioning context, while the loss is computed over the target Scenic program.

## CONCLUSION
This paper presented FaultGene, an LLM-based framework for generating fault-augmented tests for HIL resilience validation. Given a baseline Scenic/CARLA scenario and structured fault specification, FaultGene generates executable tests for closed-loop evaluation across modular and end-to-end AD architectures. Manual ADDT references and execution feedback are used only for offline supervision construction and fine-tuning, while deployment requires neither reference executions nor iterative refinement. On held-out scenario–fault combinations, FaultGene improves semantic alignment, compilation, and execution success over one-shot generation while preserving the main HIL sensor- and compute-fault sensitivity trends.

FaultGene reduces manual Scenic editing and fault-augmented tests construction to providing a baseline scenario and fault specification. Although evaluated on ADS, the framework can be adapted to other CPS by replacing the scenario representation, execution metrics, and fault-injection interfaces with domain-specific counterparts. Similarly, although the current evaluation uses LLFI and NVBitFI, FaultGene is not tied to these tools and can incorporate other fault-injection backends. A current limitation is that timing effects are evaluated after generation rather than incorporated into the generation objective. Future work will integrate HIL timing measurements and broader fault-injection backends into the feedback process to expose both safety- and timing-critical failures.

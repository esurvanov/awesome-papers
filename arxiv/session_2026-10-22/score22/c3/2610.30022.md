# 2610.30022 — Stationarity Constraints Improve Both Energies and Densities of Machine-Learned Exchange-Correlation Functionals
Authors: Yuhao Chen, Donald G. Truhlar, Xiao He

## ABSTRACT
Empirical exchange-correlation functionals are fitted on fixed orbitals but used self-consistently, so better energies often come with worse densities. We show that the Brillouin residual of a trained functional on the Kohn-Sham orbitals of an accurate density controls, to first order, both the self-consistent density shift and the gap between fitted and self-consistent energies. Penalizing it requires no self-consistent-field solution during training. Trained on 6 energies and 11 CCSD densities, a neural meta-GGA surpasses SCAN in both atomization energies and densities of unseen molecules.

Abstract

Empirical exchange-correlation functionals are fitted on fixed orbitals but used self-consistently, so better energies often come with worse densities. We show that the Brillouin residual of a trained functional on the Kohn-Sham orbitals of an accurate density controls, to first order, both the self-consistent density shift and the gap between fitted and self-consistent energies. Penalizing it requires no self-consistent-field solution during training. Trained on 6 energies and 11 CCSD densities, a neural meta-GGA surpasses SCAN in both atomization energies and densities of unseen molecules.

Kohn-Sham density functional theory (KS-DFT) [1, 2] is the most widely used electronic-structure method in chemistry and materials science, and its accuracy is set by the approximation to the exchange-correlation (XC) energy. The most accurate approximations for molecular thermochemistry contain tens of empirical parameters [3, 4, 5, 6] or, as machine-learned functionals, thousands or more [7, 8, 9, 10, 11, 12]. These parameters are fixed almost exclusively by reference energies; the electron density, which by the Hohenberg-Kohn theorem determines all ground-state properties, does not enter the fit. Medvedev et al. found that functionals with more empirical parameters tend to give worse atomic densities [13], suggesting that part of the energetic gain comes from error cancellation rather than from approaching the exact functional [13, 14, 15].

A concrete but rarely discussed mechanism underlies this trend: a functional is not used in the state in which it is fitted. Its parameters are fitted to energies on fixed orbitals, but the functional is solved self-consistently. A parameter change that lowers the fixed-orbital energy error also changes the XC potential, so the self-consistent-field (SCF) solution relaxes away from the fitted orbitals—and an energy-only loss does not penalize this relaxation, even when the fixed orbitals come from the exact density. In the language of density-corrected DFT [16, 17], energy fitting controls the functional-driven error but leaves the density-driven error unconstrained. Accuracy reached in the fit therefore need not survive self-consistency, let alone transfer to new molecules.

Two routes bring information beyond energies into the fit. One fits XC potentials obtained by density inversion [18, 19], as in the TH, HCTH, and B97-2 functionals [20, 21, 22, 23, 24], or learns the density-to-potential map directly [25, 26]; but finite-basis inversion is ill-conditioned and fixes the potential only up to a constant [27, 28, 29, 30], so pointwise fitting imports numerical noise into the target. The other is differentiable DFT [31, 32, 33], which places the SCF cycle inside automatic differentiation so that self-consistent densities and energies enter the loss directly. It is effective, but every training step must converge the SCF and backpropagate through it, which is costly and fragile for hard-to-converge systems; alternating training with SCF [34] likewise requires repeated SCF solutions.

In this Letter we expand about the KS orbitals of an accurate reference density and constrain how the trained functional behaves there. Our key observation is that a single quantity—the Brillouin residual of the trained functional on the reference orbitals, i.e., the occupied-virtual block of its Fock matrix—controls, to first order, both the self-consistent density shift and the gap between fitted and self-consistent energies. Penalizing it yields a stationarity loss and a first-order density loss (Fig. 1) with three properties: their target is exact self-consistency at the reference orbitals; they see only the part of the potential error that rotates occupied orbitals, so the constant and ill-determined components that plague inversion drop out; and they need one Fock build per system, with no SCF solved or differentiated during training. Trained on 6 reference energies and the coupled-cluster (CCSD) densities of 11 atoms, ions, and diatomics, and tested fully self-consistently on 103 molecules, many with elements and bonds absent from training, the same network reproduces the trend of Ref. [13] under energy-only training—densities worse than those of the base functional—and reverses it with the two constraints: the resulting meta-GGA surpasses SCAN in both energies and densities.

Self-consistent mismatch on fixed orbitals.—Let $\{\bar{\phi}_{p\sigma},\bar{\epsilon}_{p\sigma}\}$ be the KS orbitals and orbital energies obtained from the CCSD reference density $n^{\mathrm{ref}}$ by Wu-Yang inversion [19], i.e., eigenstates of a local-potential Hamiltonian $\hat{h}^{\mathrm{ref}}_{\sigma}$ . For a functional with parameters $\Theta$ , the energy of system $m$ on these fixed orbitals is

 $E_{m}(\Theta)=E_{m}^{0}+E_{\mathrm{xc}}^{\Theta}[\bar{\Phi}_{m}],$ 

(1)

where $E_{m}^{0}$ collects the kinetic, external, Hartree, and nuclear-repulsion energies and is independent of $\Theta$ . Conventional energy-only fitting (hereafter E) makes reaction energies assembled from Eq. (1) reproduce reference values. Because Eq. (1) is evaluated on orbitals of the reference density, E fitting does not lack density information; it lacks information on whether the functional’s self-consistent solution stays on these orbitals.

Writing a real occupied-virtual rotation as $\phi_{i\sigma}=\bar{\phi}_{i\sigma}+\sum_{a}\kappa^{\sigma}_{ai}\bar{\phi}_{a\sigma}+O(\kappa^{2})$ and expanding to second order about $\kappa=0$ gives

 $E_{m}(\Theta;\kappa)\simeq E_{m}(\Theta)+\sum_{\sigma ai}\Big[2F^{\sigma}_{ai}(\Theta)\,\kappa^{\sigma}_{ai}+\Delta^{\sigma}_{ai}\big(\kappa^{\sigma}_{ai}\big)^{2}\Big],$ 

(2)

where $F^{\sigma}_{ai}(\Theta)=\langle\bar{\phi}_{a\sigma}|\hat{F}^{\sigma}[\bar{\Phi};\Theta]|\bar{\phi}_{i\sigma}\rangle$ is the occupied-virtual block of the trained functional’s Fock matrix on the reference orbitals and $2F^{\sigma}_{ai}$ the orbital gradient (unit occupation per spin orbital). We take the uncoupled diagonal orbital Hessian $\Delta^{\sigma}_{ai}=\max(\bar{\epsilon}_{a\sigma}-\bar{\epsilon}_{i\sigma},\Delta_{\min})$ , where $\Delta_{\min}=0.05\,E_{h}$ keeps near-degenerate pairs from dominating. Minimizing Eq. (2) gives the first-order SCF relaxation

 $\kappa^{\sigma}_{ai}(\Theta)=-F^{\sigma}_{ai}(\Theta)/\Delta^{\sigma}_{ai}$ 

(3)

and its two observable consequences,

 $\displaystyle E^{\mathrm{SCF}}_{m}(\Theta)$ 
 $\displaystyle\simeq E_{m}(\Theta)-\sum_{\sigma ai}\Delta^{\sigma}_{ai}\big(\kappa^{\sigma}_{ai}\big)^{2},$ 

(4)

 $\displaystyle\delta n_{m}(\mathbf{r};\Theta)$ 
 $\displaystyle\simeq 2\sum_{\sigma ai}\bar{\phi}_{a\sigma}(\mathbf{r})\,\bar{\phi}_{i\sigma}(\mathbf{r})\,\kappa^{\sigma}_{ai}(\Theta).$ 

(5)

Equation (4) shows that the fixed-orbital energy targeted by E fitting differs from the self-consistent energy by a term quadratic in $F$ —the density-driven error relative to the reference density [16, 17]—and Eq. (5) that the SCF density shift is first order in $F$ . Neither is a target of E fitting, and both vanish only when $F^{\sigma}_{ai}=0$ , i.e., when the reference determinant satisfies the Brillouin condition [35] of the trained functional. Importantly, this target is exact: $F^{\sigma}_{ai}=0$ for all pairs if and only if the reference orbitals are a self-consistent solution of the trained functional in the given basis, so the uncoupled Hessian affects only how deviations from stationarity are weighted. A positive-definite Hartree-XC kernel (the Hartree part usually dominates) screens orbital relaxation, in which case Eq. (4) bounds the energy relaxation from above.

Relation to potential fitting.—Because the reference orbitals are eigenstates of $\hat{h}^{\mathrm{ref}}$ ,

 $F^{\sigma}_{ai}=\langle\bar{\phi}_{a\sigma}|\,\Delta\hat{v}_{\sigma}\,|\bar{\phi}_{i\sigma}\rangle,\qquad\Delta\hat{v}_{\sigma}=\hat{v}^{\Theta}_{\mathrm{xc},\sigma}-v^{\mathrm{ref}}_{\mathrm{xc},\sigma},$ 

(6)

where $v^{\mathrm{ref}}_{\mathrm{xc}}$ is the inverted local XC potential and, for a meta-GGA, $\hat{v}^{\Theta}_{\mathrm{xc}}$ includes the nonmultiplicative generalized-KS term $-\tfrac{1}{2}\nabla\cdot[(\partial e_{\mathrm{xc}}/\partial\tau_{\sigma})\nabla]$ [36, 37]. Equations (3) and (5) are thus the uncoupled first-order orbital rotation and KS density response induced by $\Delta\hat{v}$ . Unlike pointwise potential fitting, only the occupied-virtual block of $\Delta\hat{v}$ enters: a constant shift cancels ( $\langle\bar{\phi}_{a}|c|\bar{\phi}_{i}\rangle=0$ ), and components with a vanishing occupied-virtual block—which leave the density unchanged to first order and are precisely those that finite-basis inversion cannot determine—are excluded. The constraint retains exactly the potential information that the reference density carries.

Stationarity and first-order density constraints.—The stationarity loss is the squared norm of the predicted rotation,

 $\mathcal{L}_{G}(\Theta)=\sum_{m\in\mathcal{S}}\sum_{\sigma ai}\big[\kappa^{\sigma}_{ai}(\Theta)\big]^{2}.$ 

(7)

By Eq. (4), the energy gap $\sum\Delta^{\sigma}_{ai}(\kappa^{\sigma}_{ai})^{2}\leq\max\Delta^{\sigma}_{ai}\cdot\sum(\kappa^{\sigma}_{ai})^{2}$ , so $\mathcal{L}_{G}$ also controls it; relative to the gap ( $\propto F^{2}/\Delta$ ), $\mathcal{L}_{G}$ ( $\propto F^{2}/\Delta^{2}$ ) emphasizes small-gap pairs, which relax most easily. The first-order density loss compares the predicted SCF density directly with the CCSD density,

 $\mathcal{L}_{D}(\Theta)=\frac{1}{N_{\mathcal{S}}}\sum_{m\in\mathcal{S}}\big\|\bar{n}_{m}+\delta n_{m}(\Theta)-n^{\mathrm{ref}}_{m}\big\|^{2},$ 

(8)

where $\|f\|^{2}=\int f(\mathbf{r})^{2}d\mathbf{r}$ , $\bar{n}_{m}$ is the density of the reference orbitals, and $\mathcal{S}$ is the set of $N_{\mathcal{S}}$ training systems with density data.

The two losses are complementary. $\mathcal{L}_{D}$ targets the observable $n^{\mathrm{ref}}_{m}$ itself, so $\delta n_{m}$ can also absorb the part of the inversion residual $d_{m}=\bar{n}_{m}-n^{\mathrm{ref}}_{m}\neq 0$ that orbital rotations can represent. $\mathcal{L}_{G}$ acts on the orbitals: rotations that barely change the density still change a meta-GGA energy through the kinetic-energy density $\tau$ , and $\mathcal{L}_{G}$ keeps the predicted rotations small, so the first-order prediction behind $\mathcal{L}_{D}$ stays valid.

Training objective.—The total loss is

 $\mathcal{L}(\Theta)=\lambda_{E}\,\mathcal{L}_{E}(\Theta)+\lambda_{G}\,\mathcal{L}_{G}(\Theta)+\lambda_{D}\,\mathcal{L}_{D}(\Theta),$ 

(9)

where $\mathcal{L}_{E}$ is the root-mean-square error (in hartree) of six reference reaction energies assembled from Eq. (1). We compare E ( $\lambda_{G}=\lambda_{D}=0$ ) and EGD ( $\lambda_{E}=\lambda_{G}=50$ , $\lambda_{D}=500$ ), which share reference orbitals, energy term, optimizer, and checkpoint selection and differ only by Eqs. (7) and (8). Each training step needs one occupied-virtual Fock block per system, and the gradient with respect to $\Theta$ follows from a single adjoint pass; no SCF is solved or implicitly differentiated. Training uses only the first-order response, but every energy and density reported below is fully self-consistent and thus measures the functional in actual use. Derivations and numerical details are in the Supplemental Material (SM) [38].

Models and data.—To test two rungs of Jacob’s ladder [39], we train a PBE-based NN-GGA and a SCAN-based NN-mGGA [40, 41] of the form

 $\displaystyle E^{\Theta}_{\mathrm{xc}}[n]=\alpha\,E^{\rm base}_{\mathrm{xc}}[n]+\beta\int n(\mathbf{r})\,f_{\theta}\big(\mathbf{z}(\mathbf{r})\big)\,d\mathbf{r},$ 

(10)

 $\displaystyle\mathbf{z}^{\rm GGA}=\big(\ln(1+n),\,\zeta,\,\ln(1+s)\big),$ 

 $\displaystyle\mathbf{z}^{\rm mGGA}=\big(\mathbf{z}^{\rm GGA},\,\ln(1+a_{\tau})\big),$ 

(11)

where $E^{\rm base}_{\mathrm{xc}}$ is PBE or SCAN, $\zeta$ the spin polarization, $s$ the reduced density gradient, $a_{\tau}=(\tau-\tau_{W})/\tau_{\rm unif}$ the kinetic-energy-density indicator, and $f_{\theta}$ a fully connected network. All of $\Theta=(\alpha,\beta,\theta)$ are trained, starting from the base functional ( $\alpha=1$ , $\beta=0$ ), so E and EGD compare directly with PBE or SCAN. NN-GGA and NN-mGGA have 2274 and 2306 parameters (architectures and hyperparameters in the SM [38]).

The energy term uses six data (Table 1): atomization energies of H2, LiH, O2, and CO from W4-11 [42] and experimental first ionization energies of O and Ne [43]. The stationarity and density terms use 11 atoms, ions, and diatomics, with all-electron UCCSD ( $\Lambda$ -equation) one-particle density matrices [44, 45] (UHF reference, aug-cc-pVTZ basis [46, 47], PySCF level-6 grid [48]); the Wu-Yang orbitals of these densities stay fixed during training. Checkpoints are selected by fully self-consistent validation calculations that include atoms absent from training (N, F), using a weighted sum of the SCF root-mean-square energy errors (kcal/mol) and the mean density error. E and EGD share this score, so their differences stem from the training objective alone. The data set is comparable to the smallest used for machine-learned functionals [7, 32] and orders of magnitude smaller than those of recent large-scale models [9, 12].

The test set AE103 comprises 103 molecules with 2–14 atoms from the first three rows of the periodic table; it and the 18 atomic ionization energies of IP18 (H–Ar) are taken from the data set of Ref. [32]. Because training covers only atoms, ions, and diatomics of H–Ne, we also report two extrapolation subsets: molecules with elements absent from training (“new elements,” 37) and with bonded atom pairs absent from training (“new bond pairs,” 53). We further test 99 density records for 98 molecules (DP99), with the density error ${\rm DP}=\int(n^{\mathrm{SCF}}-n^{\mathrm{ref}})^{2}d\mathbf{r}$ evaluated against CCSD/aug-cc-pVTZ reference densities, averaged over records, and reported in units of $10^{-3}$ a.u. Full lists are in the SM [38].

Same network, same data, different objective.—Comparing E with EGD isolates the effect of Eqs. (7) and (8) (Fig. 2). For NN-GGA, EGD lowers the AE103 mean absolute error (MAE) from 11.6 to 5.6 kcal/mol, 2.6 times below its base PBE (14.6 kcal/mol). For NN-mGGA, EGD lowers the MAE from 7.1 to 3.3 kcal/mol, below SCAN (3.7 kcal/mol).

The gain is largest outside the training distribution. On new elements, NN-GGA(E) is worse than PBE (11.2 vs 9.1 kcal/mol): the fixed-orbital error reduced in the fit does not carry over to self-consistent prediction, consistent with Eq. (4). EGD lowers this MAE to 6.2 kcal/mol, and that of NN-mGGA from 5.1 to 4.4 kcal/mol. On new bond pairs, EGD lowers the NN-GGA MAE from 12.0 to 5.0 kcal/mol (PBE: 17.5 kcal/mol) and the NN-mGGA MAE from 9.1 to 2.5 kcal/mol, a factor of 3.6. On IP18, where all methods lie within 2.7–4.3 kcal/mol, EGD again removes the degradation caused by energy-only training (NN-GGA: 4.3 to 3.2 kcal/mol; PBE: 3.6 kcal/mol).

Both EGD functionals are also more accurate than CCSD on all three atomization-energy sets (9.5 kcal/mol on AE103), although CCSD supplies their reference densities. The energy term fits W4 and experimental values, whereas CCSD densities enter only through the occupied-virtual block of the potential; the energy accuracy of the trained functional is therefore not capped by the method that generates its reference densities.

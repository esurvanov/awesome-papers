## ABSTRACT
Alarm root-cause analysis is studied on data that cannot be shared. Operator logs are confidential, so what the field publishes is a causal graph between alarm types rather than per-incident causes, a fixed scenario set, gated, or never released. Two recent benchmarks carry per-incident root causes at scale; neither states a download location or a licence. We give a generator rather than a dataset. It runs on real backbone topologies from the Internet Topology Zoo and records the exact root cause and the propagation path of every alarm -- a by-product of generation, not a labelling pass. Every emitted field binds to a term in NORIA-O, RFC 8632, RFC 8345 or TMF642, and an unknown term fails the run. We ship fixed configurations, splits, a scorer and nine baselines. A one-line rule -- rank the device that raised the earliest critical alarm -- takes top-1 at 0.650 against 0.575 for our own temporal method, and wins 13 of 15 runs over three backbones and five seeds. An LLM given the same view reaches 0.600, the same model allowed to query the graph 0.625, and the rule wins on real operator alarms too, at 0.773: a property of alarm streams, not of our generator. One fault class separates the table: when a site loses power its PoP reports nothing, so every method that ranks alarmed devices scores 0.000 or near it, while the two that walk the topology to a silent candidate take 1.000 and 0.833.

## 1 Introduction

A network operations centre sees a storm of alarms and must name the one resource that
caused them. Research on this problem is evaluated on data nobody else can obtain, because
an operator’s alarm log carries its customers, its topology and its failures. The
consequence is not that the work is wrong but that it is incomparable: two methods
reporting $0.7$ on two private logs have not been compared.

The resources the field has published do not close that gap, and
Section 2 goes through them one at a time. Briefly: the most-used open
alarm data gives a causal graph between alarm types, with no incident identifier and
no topology [10, 7]; the benchmarks that do carry a
cause per incident are either announced without a download [5, 6]
or gated with terms that contradict their own licence tag [8]; and the open
RCA benchmarks that are genuinely downloadable are
microservice systems, not telecom alarms [3, 2]. One paper
advertises its data at a repository that contains a single 378-byte
README [15], and is still cited as a data source five years later.

## A generator, not a dataset.

We take the other route. A generator sidesteps
confidentiality in a way no release process can, and it gives something a released log
cannot: the ground truth is not an annotation added afterwards by an expert who was not
there, it is what the generator did. That distinction matters most for the
propagation path. An annotator can reasonably name the resource that caused an
incident; reconstructing which alarm caused which other alarm, through what, is a different
task, and the largest prior benchmark states plainly that it does not have
one [6].

## The claim.

We do not claim the first per-incident root causes for telecom
alarms. Two 2025–2026 benchmarks claim that, and at a realism we cannot match — real
production networks, expert annotation, hundreds of thousands of
alarms [6, 5]. Our claim is narrower:

No downloadable, openly licensed resource gives per-incident root causes
and propagation paths for telecom alarms at scale, and no open generator exists.

Both halves were checked against the sources rather than assumed, on 2026-10-21, and the
check is published with the paper.

## Contributions.

1. 

A generator of telecom alarm storms that records the root cause and the full
propagation path of every alarm, parameterised by topology and seeded
(Section 3).

2. 

A binding of every emitted type and field to NORIA-O, RFC 8632, RFC 8345 or TMF642,
enforced at generation time (Section 4). NORIA-O itself aligns to
ITU-T X.733/X.721 and TMF638 and references neither RFC 8632 nor TMF642; the binding
to those two is ours, not inherited.

3. 

A benchmark — fixed configurations, splits, a scoring script, and baselines we did
not design (Sections 5–7).

4. 

Nine scored baselines — four one-line rules, a fitted topological Hawkes process,
personalised PageRank, a language model given the same view, the same model allowed to
query the graph, and a control that isolates what the winning rule actually uses: severity,
not time (Section 8).

5. 

A realism study against public real alarm data, including the test that decides
whether the resource is useful: the ranking of methods on generated data is the ranking on
real data (Kendall $\tau=1.0$ over four rules on $4{,}539$ real bursts), while three
realism metrics show the generated stream matches real alarms on type vocabulary and
chaining and diverges on time, arriving in burstier volleys.

## The closest prior work is TeleRCA, and it is better than ours on realism.

TeleRCA [6] gives 10,922 production incidents, 485,681 alarm events and
5,621 network resources, each incident carrying an expert annotation naming the root-cause
resource. That is a real network and we do not have one. We differ on three things its own
paper establishes: it states twice that it has no ground-truth propagation graph,
and reports no graph-reconstruction metric in consequence; it publishes no topology or
inventory; and although it says four times that it is released, the paper contains no
repository, no download and no licence. TN-RCA530 [5] is closer to us on
topology — each of its 530 scenarios is a knowledge graph — and is likewise announced
without a download or a dataset licence.

## The most-used open alarm data labels types, not incidents.

The Huawei PCIC 2021
data [10] is live under MIT, though not at the URL its paper
prints [7]: 34,838 alarms, 18 alarm types, 55 devices over 243 days.
Its ground truth is an $18\times 18$ adjacency between alarm types. There is no
incident identifier, no storm grouping, no root-cause column and no topology. It is a good
realism reference and not a source of per-incident labels. The nearest open artefact to
such labels is a 49-row prior shipped with a later competition sample, whose “root cause”
is itself an alarm type.

## Availability is the field’s recurring failure, and it is the argument for a
generator.

Zhang et al. [15] states that its data and code are available at a
named repository; that repository holds one 378-byte README saying the release is going
through internal procedures. The paper is still cited as a data source. TeleRCA and
TN-RCA530 repeat the pattern. A generator is not a better dataset — it is a way of not
depending on someone else’s release process.

## Open RCA benchmarks exist, for other systems.

RCAEval [3] and
LEMMA-RCA [2] are downloadable and well constructed, and are microservice
and water-treatment systems rather than telecom alarms; LEMMA-RCA carries CC BY-ND on its
own site, which would block derived works. OpenRCA [4] has a domain named
Telecom, which invites confusion: it is cloud observability telemetry — KPIs,
traces and logs of enterprise systems — not NOC alarms, and the repository states no
licence for the data.

## No generator on the Internet Topology Zoo.

We found none that emits alarms with
labelled root causes. The closest prior work describes an optical alarm-propagation
simulator with an ITU-T G.798 rule engine producing alarm correlation trees; no code
repository was found.

## 3 The generator

Topologies are real backbones taken from the Internet Topology
Zoo [9], which is CC BY 4.0.
A backbone’s GraphML gives the PoPs and the links between them, and those are the only
real objects in the output; everything below carries synthetic: true.

## Inventory and services.

Each PoP gets two core routers, two or three
aggregation routers dual-homed to both, and six to twelve access or radio sites homed to
aggregation, then cards, then ports, then cabling. Backbone links land on core-router
ports, so the real topology constrains the synthetic one rather than sitting beside it.
Services follow the inventory: mobile data per radio site, broadband per access site, and
enterprise VPNs across PoPs with a primary and a backup path. Customers carry a market
segment and nothing else — there is no personal data to anonymise.

## Faults and propagation.

There are $7$ fault types: card, device, port and
software failure, fibre cut, site power loss, and a configuration change, which is always
preceded by a Change whose outcome is failed. What follows from a fault is
computed from the topology, not scripted: a dead card takes its ports down, the far end of
each down port loses signal, a breadth-first search from the NOC’s own PoP over surviving
links decides which devices still have a path home, and a service whose endpoint is
unreachable is out. Redundancy is what stops a failure spreading, so dual homing and two
core routers per PoP are what make most faults local and a few of them storms.

## Alarms.

Each of those states raises an alarm on the resource that would report
it, with a per-mechanism severity and a per-mechanism emission probability: the root alarm
when the root can still speak, loss of signal at the far ends, an unreachable-device alarm
after the next poll, a service alarm per lost service, neighbour-cell link failures on live
radio sites near a dead one, and congestion where traffic reroutes. The dataset uses $4$ 
X.733 event types and $21$ NORIA-O probable causes over $4$ RFC 8632 severities.

## What makes it hard is in the config, not in a script.

The root resource often
raises no alarm at all: in $50$ of $70$ faults it does, and in the rest the method has to
infer the root from what its neighbours said. A site power loss never raises one — the
PoP it kills cannot report itself, which is also why the fault model’s fifth event type,
environmentalAlarm, appears nowhere in the data. On top of that, alarms go
missing, flap through raise/clear cycles, duplicate, and carry per-device clock skew drawn
once per device, so the order alarms arrive in is not the order the faults happened in.
Background alarms arrive throughout, unrelated to any fault. Two faults can overlap inside
one PoP. Every one of those is a knob with a number in the config, which is what lets the
ablation in Section 8 say which of them the methods are actually reading.

Section 8.3 removes each of these in turn and measures what it was worth.
Alarms that never arrive are worth the most by a wide margin; mis-grouping is worth the
least, and was worth nothing at all before the arrival times were fixed.

## The distribution, not the mean.

Alarms per fault have a median of $3$ and a
90th percentile of $41.1$ , with a maximum of $103$ . Most faults are quiet and a few are
storms, so a method evaluated at the mean is evaluated at neither end.

is the ground truth, and it is a by-product of generation rather than a labelling pass.

## Splits.

Scenarios are split into history and eval before
generation, not after, and each scenario owns its own window, so the two never interleave
in time. A method may use everything about history — its alarms, its incidents, its
resolved tickets, its changes, and the ground truth — to fit whatever it likes. On eval
it sees the static topology and the incident’s alarms, and nothing else. The GÉANT small
configuration has $30$ history faults and $40$ eval faults in three scenario shapes:
a single fault, a storm, and two faults overlapping inside one PoP.

The split is only a split if a method cannot see across it, which is a property of the
method and not of the data. The one baseline here that fits anything — the topological
Hawkes process in Section 7 — is given a hard cutoff at the onset of
the first eval incident, and a test asserts that every alarm it was trained on falls below
that cutoff and that the cutoff excludes a non-zero number of them. A cutoff that excludes
nothing proves nothing.

## 4 Standards binding

Every emitted type and field resolves to a term in one of four published vocabularies:
NORIA-O [11], the alarm-management YANG model of RFC 8632 [14],
the network-topology model of RFC 8345 [1], and the TM Forum alarm
API TMF642 [13]. Generation fails on an unknown term rather than emitting it.
The binding is a build step rather than a convention. Each vocabulary is converted to a
graph of its own terms — an OWL/SKOS pass for NORIA-O, a YANG pass for the two RFCs, an
OpenAPI pass for TMF642 — and a catalog entry records where each build came from. The
generator then declares what it needs: for each node label, the ontology classes it claims
to be, and for each enumerated field, the vocabulary and the owning term the values must
come from. At generation time every label and every enumeration value is resolved against
those builds, $11$ labels and $4$ vocabularies in this configuration, and an unresolved
term raises rather than being emitted. The release carries binding.json: the terms
used, and the build files they came from with their sha256, so a reader can check the
vocabulary a value claims without trusting the paper.

The grounding also travels in the graph itself, as one SchemaLabel node per label
with a GROUNDED_IN edge to each ontology concept, rather than as a table in a
README. A query can therefore ask what an Alarm is in NORIA-O’s terms.

## What is ours and what is inherited.

NORIA-O’s own alarm vocabulary aligns to
ITU-T X.733/X.721 and to TMF638 Service Inventory; neither it nor its IETF
draft [12] references RFC 8632 or TMF642. Binding to those two is this
paper’s contribution rather than an alignment we inherited, and we state it so that a
reader who opens the ontology does not have to discover it.

## 5 The benchmark

## The task.

One incident at a time, rank the resources that could hold its root
cause, best first. An incident is the NOC’s grouping of alarms — imperfect on purpose,
since some alarms are attached to the wrong one — so the method is given the storm an
operator would actually see rather than a clean partition.

A ranking is correct at $k$ if one of its first $k$ entries is the
device of any resource the fault was at. Two details matter and both are properties of the
faults rather than choices: a fibre cut has two ends and either is correct, and a site
power loss names the PoP, so any device hosted in it counts. We report $k=1$ and $k=3$ :
top-1 is what an automated remediation step needs, and top-3 is what an operator who will

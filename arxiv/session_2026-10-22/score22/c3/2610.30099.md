## ABSTRACT
Reinforcement Learning with Human Feedback (RLHF) has become a central paradigm for aligning Large Language Models with human values. However, the reliance on sensitive preference data necessitates rigorous differential privacy (DP) guarantees to prevent user information leakage. Existing private alignment methods typically assume dense reward parameterizations, resulting in estimation error bounds that scale polynomially with the ambient {model size $d$}. Such dependence is prohibitive in modern {overparameterized models} where $d$ is vast. In this work, we study \emph{Private Sparse RLHF} problem to address this limitation, assuming that human preferences (reward model) are governed by a subset of $s^* \ll d$ relevant features. We provide the first comprehensive theoretical framework for differentially private sparse reward estimation from pairwise preference feedback under the Bradley-Terry-Luce model, and introduce efficient algorithms for both Sample-DP and Label-DP settings. Theoretically, under the squared $\ell_2$ parameter estimation error, we establish upper bounds of $\widetilde{O}(s^*/n + {s^*}^2/n^2\varepsilon^2)$ for $(\varepsilon,δ)$-Sample-DP and $\widetilde{O}(s^*/n + s^*/n\varepsilon^2)$ for $\varepsilon$-Label-DP based on randomized response, where $n$ is the training data size. For the lower bounds, we show that the Sample-DP rate is nearly optimal, and that the guarantee for our Label-DP method cannot be further improved within the randomized-response framework. We also provide a general lower bound for the problem with Label-DP. Empirically, our framework demonstrates superior efficacy over dense baselines across sentiment generation and safety alignment benchmarks, offering a significantly more favorable privacy-utility trade-off.

## 1 Introduction

Large Language Models (LLMs) have demonstrated remarkable capabilities in generalization and reasoning, yet their pre-training on vast, unfiltered corpora often results in behaviors that are misaligned with harmful or biased content. To address this, Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF) has emerged as the standard paradigm for steering LLMs to align pretrained models with human values and objectives [16, 41, 36, 3]. In the typical RLHF pipeline, a reward model is first trained on a dataset of pairwise human preferences (e.g., response $y_{1}$ is preferred over $y_{2}$ ) to approximate the underlying human utility function. This learned reward model then guides the optimization of the language model policy via algorithms such as Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) [38].

Despite its success, the reliance on human feedback introduces significant privacy vulnerabilities. The preference datasets used to train reward models often contain sensitive information, ranging from personal opinions to implicit biases, which can be memorized by the model. Consequently, deploying these models risks exposing user data through privacy attacks [8, 9, 22, 56]. To mitigate these risks, recent works have integrated Differential Privacy (DP)[23], which requires that the private trained model (or its output distribution) does not significantly change when any single entry in the training data is altered, into the alignment process [56, 15, 61, 59] (refer to Section 2 for further discussion).

However, a fundamental limitation of existing private alignment methods is their implicit treatment of the reward model as a dense parameter vector. In practice, collecting high-quality human preference data for aligning large language models is both costly and time-consuming [13, 35, 58]. For instance, aligning models with general human values often requires a substantial number of annotations from experienced annotators to reliably distinguish between “helpful” and “harmless” responses. This data scarcity becomes even more pronounced in specialized domains such as medical or legal reasoning [40, 18, 21], where preference labels must be provided by highly qualified experts. These challenges impose a strict budget on the available sample size $n$ , while the semantic richness and complexity of such domains naturally induce a feature space of extremely high dimension $d$ . Standard differentially private mechanisms require noise injection proportional to the ambient dimension (i.e., $\propto d/n$ ) to hide individual contributions [23]. When $d\gg n$ , this noise overwhelms the utility signal, rendering the high estimation error and alignment ineffective.

Moreover, recent empirical studies [2, 17] suggest that the underlying reward functions and feature representations in LLM alignment exhibit significant intrinsic sparsity. Specifically, preference signals are often governed by a small subset of salient features or tokens, rather than the full high-dimensional embedding. In parallel, foundational theoretical work on differentially private sparse learning has shown that, for problems such as linear regression, one can circumvent polynomial dependence on the ambient dimension $d$ [42, 52, 6, 20, 63, 47, 19]. By leveraging sparsity, the estimation error can be reduced to scale logarithmically with the sparsity (i.e., $\propto s^{*}/n$ ), thereby enabling accurate private estimation even in high-dimensional settings. This naturally raises the following question:

Can we design effective mechanisms for private sparse RLHF with rigorous theoretical guarantees that are not polynomial in the dimension $d$ ?

We answer this question affirmatively and summarize our contributions as follows:

• 

First Analysis of Private Sparse RLHF. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first work to investigate sparsity in the context of private alignment, i.e., DP Sparse RLHF. We formulate the problem under the high-dimensional Bradley-Terry model and introduce two specialized algorithms: Sample-DP-IHT, which integrates iterative hard thresholding with a private peeling mechanism to protect the entire interaction tuple, and Label-DP RR+IHT, which combines randomized response with debiased gradient estimation to protect preference labels.

• 

Minimax Optimality. As summarized in Table 1, we establish upper bounds under the squared $\ell_{2}$ parameter estimation error. Specifically, we achieve rates of $\widetilde{O}(s^{*}/n+(s^{*})^{2}/(n^{2}\varepsilon^{2}))$ for $(\varepsilon,\delta)$ -Sample-DP and $\widetilde{O}(s^{*}/n+s^{*}/(n\varepsilon^{2}))$ for $\varepsilon$ -Label-DP, where $n$ is the training data size. For the lower bounds, we show that the Sample-DP rate is nearly optimal, and that our theoretical analysis for the Label-DP method cannot be further improved. We also provide a general lower bound $\widetilde{\Omega}\!\left(\frac{s^{*}}{n}+\frac{s^{*}}{n^{2}\varepsilon^{2}}\right)$ for the problem with Label-DP.

• 

Experimental Evaluation. We empirically validate our framework on Sentiment Generation and Safety Alignment benchmarks. Our results demonstrate that exploiting sparsity consistently yields superior privacy-utility trade-offs compared to dense baselines (e.g., DP-SGD and Randomized Response), effectively mitigating the noise overhead in high-privacy regimes and achieving significantly higher reward scores and alignment rates.

## 2 Related Work

Differentially Private Reward Learning and Alignment Recent work has increasingly focused on integrating differential privacy into the alignment of LLMs. Chowdhury et al. [15] investigates differentially private reward estimation using preference feedback, while subsequent studies extend these guarantees to Direct Preference Optimization and unified offline alignment frameworks Chowdhury et al. [14], Zhou et al. [61], Chen et al. [12]. Beyond reward estimation, privacy-aware regularization has received growing attention. Wu et al. [57] analyzes offline and online KL-regularized RLHF under differential privacy, while Zhang et al. [60] shows that KL regularization itself can inherently induce differential privacy guarantees in bandit and RLHF settings. The scope of private alignment has further expanded to more complex interaction models, such as reinforcement learning with self-play [37] and user-level differential privacy in RLHF pipelines [59]. Despite these advancements, prior works largely focused on dense reward structures or general function approximation, whose the estimation errors always depend on polynomial of the dimension, leaving the sparse reward learning under privacy constraints unexplored.

Differentially Private Sparse Learning
Differentially private sparse learning builds on a broader line of work on private empirical risk minimization (ERM) and convex optimization [11, 31, 29, 51, 50, 44]. Early studies [46] investigated the stability of the Lasso estimator, clarifying the conditions required for robust feature selection under privacy constraints. Building on these foundations, Talwar et al. [42] gives near-optimal utility guarantees for differentially private Lasso, establishing sharp statistical rates (up to logarithmic factors) in sparse linear regression under central DP.
More recent work has expanded both the algorithmic toolbox and the problem settings. Wang and Gu [54] propose a knowledge-transfer framework that can improve utility for private sparse regressions. [43, 26] addressed private stochastic convex optimization under heavy-tailed gradients by robust estimation and median techniques. In parallel, sparse learning under local differential privacy, Wang and Xu [52] develops LDP iterative hard thresholding procedures, and subsequent analysis Zhu et al. [62] sharpen lower/upper bounds and provide the first non-interactive LDP algorithms for the problem. Beyond regression, privacy-preserving sparse spectral estimation has also been studied. Hu et al. [28], Hu et al. [27], Wang and Xu [53] examined the sparse generalized eigenvalue problem underlying sparse principal component analysis, canonical correlation analysis, and Fisher’s discriminant analysis.
Despite these advances, existing private sparse learning results can not straightforwardly extend to the preference-based learning setting central to RLHF, along with the non-linear Bradley-Terry-Luce model and paired data dependency.

## 3 Preliminaries

In this section, we present the formal problem setup for private alignment in RLHF from pairwise comparisons, along with the necessary definitions and assumptions.

Let $D=\{(k_{i},a_{i}^{0},a_{i}^{1},y_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}$ be a dataset consisting of $n$ labeled comparisons drawn i.i.d. from $\mathcal{D}$ , where $k_{i}\in\mathcal{K}$ denotes a context (e.g., a prompt or state), $a_{i}^{0},a_{i}^{1}\in\mathcal{A}$ consists of two candidate actions/responses, and the label $y_{i}\in\{0,1\}$ indicates which candidate is preferred (i.e., $y_{i}=l$ means that $a_{i}^{l}$ is chosen as the preferred response). Let $r_{\theta}:\mathcal{K}\times\mathcal{A}\to\mathbb{R}$ be a parametric reward model indexed by $\theta\in\Theta$ , and let $\theta^{\star}$ denote the unknown ground-truth parameter. We assume the preferences are generated according to the softmax comparison model: for each $i\in[n]$ and $l\in\{0,1\}$ ,

 $\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\theta^{\star}}\!\left[y_{i}=l\,\middle|\,k_{i},a_{i}^{0},a_{i}^{1}\right]=\frac{\exp\left(r_{\theta^{\star}}\left(k_{i},a_{i}^{l}\right)\right)}{\exp\left(r_{\theta^{\star}}\left(k_{i},a_{i}^{0}\right)\right)+\exp\left(r_{\theta^{\star}}\left(k_{i},a_{i}^{1}\right)\right)},$ 

where $r_{\theta^{*}}(\cdot,\cdot)$ denotes the reward model parameterized by $\theta^{*}$ . This model is known as the Bradley-Terry-Luce (BTL) model [5, 33]. In this work, we focus on the BTL model with a linear reward parameterization. Specifically, let $\phi:\mathcal{K}\times\mathcal{A}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}$ be a known feature map and assume $r_{\theta}(k,a)=\langle\phi(k,a),\theta\rangle,\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.$ 
Define the comparison (difference) feature $x_{i}:=\phi(k_{i},a_{i}^{1})-\phi(k_{i},a_{i}^{0})\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.$ 
Under this model, the conditional distribution reduces to logistic regression:

 $\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\theta^{\star}}\!\left[y_{i}=1\,\middle|\,k_{i},a_{i}^{0},a_{i}^{1}\right]=\sigma\!\left(\langle x_{i},\theta^{\star}\rangle\right),\mathbb{P}_{\theta^{\star}}\!\left[y_{i}=0\,\middle|\,k_{i},a_{i}^{0},a_{i}^{1}\right]=\sigma\!\left(-\langle x_{i},\theta^{\star}\rangle\right),$ 

where $\sigma(z)=\frac{1}{1+e^{-z}}$ refers to the sigmoid function.

Given $D$ , our objective is to estimate $\theta^{\star}$ (and hence the reward function $r_{\theta^{\star}}$ ) by maximizing the likelihood of the observed comparisons with privacy constraints. Formally, we seek a randomized estimator $\mathcal{M}:D\to\mathbb{R}^{d}$ that minimizes the empirical risk $\mathcal{L}_{n}(\theta)$ subject to the privacy constraint, where $\mathcal{L}_{n}(\theta)$ is defined as follows:
 $\mathcal{L}_{n}(\theta)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Big(\log(1+\exp(\langle x_{i},\theta\rangle))-y_{i}\langle x_{i},\theta\rangle\Big).$ 
To account for the high-dimensional nature of the feature space ( $d\gg n$ ), we adopt the following assumptions, which are standard in the literature [39, 15, 61, 58].

Assumption 3.1 (Boundedness).

(a) $\theta^{*}$ lies in the set $\Theta_{B,s^{*}}=\left\{\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\mid\|\theta\|_{2}\leq B,\|\theta\|_{0}\leq s^{*}\right\}$ . (b) Features are bounded, i.e., $\|x\|_{\infty}\leq 1,\forall x=\phi(k,a^{1})-\phi(k,a^{0})$ . (c) Let $\Sigma:=\mathbb{E}[xx^{\top}]$ , and suppose there exist constants $0<\mu\leq\gamma<\infty$ such that $\mu\leq\lambda_{\min}(\Sigma)\leq\lambda_{\max}(\Sigma)\leq\gamma$ .

Our boundedness assumptions differ from the $\ell_{2}$ feature bound used in [15]. Note that in the high dimensional setting, $\ell_{\infty}$ -boundness assumption is more reasonable than $\ell_{2}$ -norm. Such an assumption has been widely used in the existing literature on high-dimensional statistics [49]. Under an $\ell_{2}$ bound
 $\|x\|_{2}\leq L$ , one has $\operatorname{tr}(\Sigma)=\mathbb{E}\|x\|_{2}^{2}\leq L^{2},$ and hence $\lambda_{\min}(\Sigma)\leq\frac{L^{2}}{d}.$ 
Thus, when $L=O(1)$ , a dense design cannot have all covariance eigenvalues bounded below by a constant; the curvature parameter $\mu$ may scale as $1/d$ , leading to dimension dependence through the condition number. In our work, we assume the coordinate-wise bound $\|x\|_{\infty}\leq 1$ , which allows $\|x\|_{2}=O(\sqrt{d})$ and permits covariance eigenvalues $\mu,\gamma=\Theta(1)$ . We will provide more discussions in the later parts.

Now, we recall the definition of differential privacy [23]. Informally, it ensures that the privately trained model (or its output distribution) does not significantly change when any single entry in the training data is altered.
Depending on the scenario, there are two levels of privacy: label-DP [10, 24] and sample-DP [11, 23, 1]. We provide their formal definitions in the following.

Definition 3.2 (Differential Privacy [23]).

Let $\varepsilon\geq 0$ and $\delta\in(0,1]$ . A randomized algorithm $\mathcal{M}$ is said to satisfy $(\varepsilon,\delta)$ -differential privacy with respect to an adjacency relation $\sim$ , if for any pair of adjacent datasets $D\sim D^{\prime}$ , and for any measurable set $S$ in the output space of $\mathcal{M}$ , it holds that

 $\mathbb{P}[\mathcal{M}(D)\in S]\leq e^{\varepsilon}\cdot\mathbb{P}\left[\mathcal{M}\left(D^{\prime}\right)\in S\right]+\delta.$ 

When $\delta=0$ , we say that $\mathcal{M}$ is $\varepsilon$ -differentially private. Throughout the paper, we consider two specific types of adjacency:
1) Sample Adjacency (Sample-DP). $D$ and $D^{\prime}$ differ in exactly one sample record (i.e., replacement or addition/removal of a pair $(x_{i},y_{i})$ ). 2) Label Adjacency (Label-DP). $D$ and $D^{\prime}$ differ only in the label of a single sample (i.e., fixed features $x_{i}$ , different $y_{i}$ ).

Definition 3.2 guarantees that the trained reward model remains statistically stable regardless of variations in a single user’s data. Particularly, Label-DP ensures that an adversary cannot determine the specific preference $y_{i}$ for a fixed pair of responses. In contrast, Sample-DP provides a strictly stronger guarantee by protecting the entire interaction tuple $(k_{i},a_{i}^{0},a_{i}^{1},y_{i})$ , concealing not just the preference but also the context of the query itself. Notably, prior works on private RLHF have mainly focused on the Label-DP setting [15, 61], leaving the theoretical analysis of Sample-DP largely unexplored even under non-sparse settings.

## CONCLUSION
In this work, we studied differentially private sparse reward estimation for RLHF under the BTL preference model. By exploiting the intrinsic sparsity of human preferences, we introduced two efficient algorithms: Sample-DP-IHT and Label-DP RR+IHT. Our theory establishes nearly minimax-optimal sparse rates for Sample-DP and shows that the guarantee for RR-based Label-DP cannot be further improved within the randomized-response framework. We also provide a general lower bound for arbitrary central Label-DP mechanisms. Experiments on Sentiment Generation and Safety Alignment demonstrate that sparsity-aware private reward estimation substantially improves the privacy-utility trade-off over dense baselines in the challenging $d\gg n$ regime.

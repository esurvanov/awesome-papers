## ABSTRACT
Progress on foundation model pretraining is gated by who can afford a trial, and relational pretraining is no exception. Recipes are developed inside individual labs, reported under private protocols, and do not accumulate. Speedrun leaderboards remove that barrier by decentralizing the search rather than the training run. The problem is fixed, the stack is open, entries are ranked by wall-clock time to a quality target, and one GPU is enough to hold the record. We introduce modded-nanoRT, the first such leaderboard for Relational Transformers. Which database to hold out decides whether the benchmark can resolve improvements at all, so we fix it by calibration, ranking six candidates by how sharply a 45-minute learning curve separates improvement from seed noise. We open the leaderboard with typed trilinear attention, a relational operator that conditions attention scores and values on the foreign key traversed, together with Muon and SIGReg. The recipe reaches the target in 305.63 seconds on one NVIDIA H200, 8.11x faster than the reference implementation, and wins 13 of 15 pairs outside the development database. We will release the benchmark, its frozen artifacts, and the record runs. Our code is available at https://github.com/kunumi/nanoRT.

## 1 Introduction

Most machine learning data sits in relational databases, split across tables linked by primary–foreign key relationships. Standard pipelines flatten those links into a task-specific table, so data preparation repeats for every new database and prediction problem. Relational foundation models learn from the schema itself. RelBench standardized this setting across databases and predictive tasks (Robinson et al., 2024), and the Relational Transformer (RT) showed that one schema-aware model applies to unseen tasks without task-specific parameters or in-context examples (Ranjan et al., 2026a).

An architectural, optimizer, or regularization change takes hours to evaluate even at small scale, and the labs that can run such trials at volume are the labs with clusters, so recipe knowledge accumulates where the compute is and everyone else is priced out of the loop that produces it. Community speedruns answer this by pooling a different resource. Fixing the model family, the data, the hardware, and the target, and ranking submissions by time to reach it, turned recipe search for language model pretraining into a leaderboard that dozens of contributors each advanced by seconds (Jordan et al., 2024a); modded-nanoTabPFN did the same for tabular models (Pfefferle et al., 2025; Ozturk et al., 2026). What is decentralized here is not the training run but the search over it. Contributors hold one GPU each, work independently on disjoint parts of the design space, and their findings compose because every entry is measured on the same clock.

No such artifact exists for relational foundation models, and the format does not transfer mechanically. Language and tabular speedruns train on one fixed corpus. Relational pretraining mixes databases with different schemas, and the quantity of interest is zero-shot transfer to a database the model has never seen, so the mixture and an entire held-out database must be fixed in advance.

We introduce modded-nanoRT, an open speedrun for pretraining a nano-scale Relational Transformer on a single GPU and evaluating it zero-shot on a held-out database. Table 1 lists what is fixed; the training stack is open, and submissions are ranked by wall-clock time to the target. Choosing the held-out database is the design problem the relational setting adds, and we settle it by calibration. All six candidates run for 45 minutes over multiple seeds, ranked by how sharply their learning curves separate improvements from seed noise.

We open the leaderboard with three changes to the RT recipe. We introduce typed trilinear attention. RT uses the schema only as an attention mask, so every foreign key shares one compatibility function; we make the relation a third multiplicative factor in the attention score and the message, so neighbors reached by different foreign keys are no longer scored alike. We add SIGReg as a representation-level regularizer over relational columns (Balestriero and LeCun, 2025), following tabular work that reports faster convergence under it (Merchant et al., 2026). Muon orthogonalizes the matrix-valued updates of the backbone, carried over from the language model speedruns (Jordan et al., 2024b).

The record reaches the target in 305.63 seconds on one NVIDIA H200, $8.11\times$ faster than the reference implementation. Matched three-seed comparisons attribute the gain to Muon, and to typed attention only in combination with it; leave-one-database-out runs win 13 of 15 pairs outside development. Verification consumes $59.9\%$ of the record, so the next entry should look at the evaluation path.

## 2 Related Work

## Relational foundation models.

Relational deep learning has standardized benchmarks (Robinson et al., 2024) and pretrained schema-aware models (Ranjan et al., 2026a; Ranjan et al., 2026b), and synthetic multi-table generation addresses the scarcity of relational pretraining data (Kothapalli et al., 2026). These fix what to train on. None fix a protocol under which training-efficiency results can be compared, so recipe improvements are reported against private baselines and do not compose.

## Time-to-quality benchmarks.

DAWNBench introduced time-to-accuracy as a joint criterion for algorithmic and systems improvements (Coleman et al., 2018), MLPerf Training formalized quality targets under stochastic time to solution (Mattson and others, 2020), and Cramming audits a language model pipeline under a one-GPU, one-day budget (Geiping and Goldstein, 2022). Speedrun leaderboards inherit this criterion and add an open submission process, so contributors accumulate improvements between benchmark rounds. modded-nanoRT carries that to relational pretraining, where the database mixture and the held-out database create their own validity constraints.

## Typed and higher-order attention.

Typed edges are handled elsewhere by relation-specific message transformations (Schlichtkrull et al., 2017), and trilinear scores appear in the 2-simplicial Transformer (Clift et al., 2019; Roy et al., 2025). Our third factor is the database relation on a query–neighbor edge rather than a second neighbor, so typed trilinear attention is relation-conditioned message passing rather than a simplicial architecture.

## Training mixture.

RelBench v1 provides seven databases. We keep only binary classification tasks, so that one metric applies across every database, and exclude rel-Event for confirmed temporal leakage. Six remain as candidates, with seed counts differing by orders of magnitude: rel-Amazon, rel-HM, rel-Stack, rel-Avito, rel-Trial, and rel-F1. One of the six is held out and excluded from the mixture, which therefore spans five. Sampling in proportion to those counts lets the largest database dominate the gradient and turns multi-database pretraining into single-database training; sampling uniformly over-weights the small schemas. We therefore temper the mixture, in the convention used for multilingual pretraining (Xue et al., 2021), $w(d)\propto n(d)^{\alpha}$ , $\alpha=0.5$ , where $n(d)$ is the seed count of database $d$ and the weights are normalized to sum to one, halfway between proportional ( $\alpha=1$ ) and uniform ( $\alpha=0$ ). Tempering is applied when the training artifact is built, by subsampling each database’s seed list until its share of the seeds equals $w(d)$ . The mixture is frozen as part of the protocol, because changing it changes what the metric measures.

## Held-out database.

Evaluation is zero-shot on a database excluded from the mixture, and which database plays that role decides whether the benchmark can resolve improvements at all. A database that saturates early, stalls at the noise floor, or is dominated by seed variance cannot rank entries. We settled it empirically, running a 45-minute leave-one-out calibration for each of six candidates over multiple seeds and ranking them by

 $\mathrm{score}(d)\;=\;\frac{\Delta\mathrm{AUROC}(d)\cdot s(d)}{\sigma(d)},$ 

(1)

where $\Delta\mathrm{AUROC}$ is the gain from initialization to 45 minutes, $s$ is the regression slope of the AUROC curve over the final 20% of wall-clock time, and $\sigma$ is the standard deviation across seeds. rel-Avito ranked first. The target, $0.81621$ , is the best pooled AUROC its calibration run reached inside the 45-minute budget, at $41$ : $20$ of $45$ : $23$ (Appendix B); the validation artifact was frozen alongside it with seeds capped at $50\,000$ and a fixed hop radius and text embeddings. Records are therefore speedups over the reference implementation, resolved to the width of each run’s evaluation grid.

## Evaluation.

We evaluate on the frozen validation artifact for the two rel-Avito tasks, user-visits and user-clicks (Robinson et al., 2024). At each scheduled evaluation we concatenate predictions and labels across both tasks and compute one AUROC. Pooling weights examples rather than tasks and confounds within-task ranking with cross-task score alignment. Only the pooled AUROC determines record eligibility.

## Reference baseline.

The baseline is the Relational Transformer of Ranjan et al. (2026a), reduced to nano scale. RT builds a temporally valid context around each seed row, masks the target cell, and represents every retained cell by its value, datatype, and frozen table–column text embedding; column, feature/foreign-key, and full attention mix these tokens before a datatype-specific decoder predicts the masked value. The baseline uses 12 blocks, width 256, eight heads, and a gated SiLU MLP of width 1024, for 19.3M trainable parameters. Pretraining uses bfloat16, contexts of 1024 cells, batches of 256, and AdamW at learning rate $10^{-3}$ , weight decay $0.1$ , with linear warmup over the first 20% of updates and linear decay after.

## Rules.

modded-nanoRT holds the problem fixed (Mattson and others, 2020; Jordan et al., 2024a; Ozturk et al., 2026). A submission must use one H200, the prescribed training artifact, the frozen rel-Avito validation artifact, and the official metric, target, evaluation schedule, and timing boundary. Weights start from a declared random seed; a submission may not load a checkpoint, train on rel-Avito, reveal masked targets, or modify the validation pipeline. The budget is 45 minutes, and runs that miss the target stay in the analysis as right-censored failures.

## 4 The record recipe

## Typed trilinear attention.

Standard RT scores a query cell $i$ and a neighboring cell $j$ bilinearly, so all foreign-key types share the same compatibility function and a relation enters the model only as an attention mask. Two neighbors carrying the same value are then scored identically whether they were reached through review $\to$ product or review $\to$ customer, and the model must recover the difference from cell content alone, separately in every context it samples. We attach the relation type $\rho(i,j)$ as a third factor in both the attention score and the message, driven by a frozen text embedding of the foreign-key description.

Relation-specific message transformations are standard (Schlichtkrull et al., 2017), but they allocate parameters per relation type and so cannot type an edge in a schema the model has never seen; the embedding comes from an external text encoder, so a new schema adds no parameters. Appendix A shows that the distinctness across relation types is what matters, not its content.

The factor is multiplicative rather than additive, following the trilinear principle of higher-order attention (Clift et al., 2019; Roy et al., 2025). An additive term shifts the scores of an edge class uniformly and gives the relation the status of a query-independent prior over edge types; multiplying inside the head dimension lets the relation select which coordinates of the query–key match are counted, so one query can rank the same neighbor differently depending on how it was reached.

## Muon.

Adam rescales each coordinate by its own gradient statistics, so the update matrix concentrates on a few dominant directions and successive steps reinforce the same subspace. Muon replaces the momentum update of each matrix-valued parameter with the nearest semi-orthogonal matrix, computed by a five-step Newton–Schulz iteration in bfloat16 (Jordan et al., 2024b), so every singular direction advances comparably and a step buys more of the representation than an Adam step of the same norm. The gain is largest early in training, the only regime a speedrun measures, and RT is almost entirely matrix-valued, with four attention sublayers and a gated MLP per block. We apply it to the two-dimensional matrices in the standard attention, typed attention, and feed-forward modules, at peak learning rate $5\times 10^{-3}$ . AdamW updates the encoders, decoders, normalization and scale parameters, biases, and the SIGReg projector, at learning rate $10^{-3}$ and weight decay $0.1$ . Both branches follow the reference schedule. Muon is inherited from prior speedruns and from relational pretraining (Liu et al., 2025; Ranjan et al., 2026b); we report its contribution but do not claim it.

## SIGReg.

RT admits every column of every database into one residual stream through per-datatype encoders, so a price and a free-text title arrive with no shared scale or shape, and nothing prevents a column’s hidden states from collapsing onto a low-dimensional region early in training; the shared blocks then spend capacity renormalizing their inputs. An isotropy constraint cannot add capacity, so we expect it to shorten the path to a given quality without raising the ceiling, which is what Merchant et al. (2026) report for tabular models and what Akbar (2026) report for vision and MLP backbones.

SIGReg matches random one-dimensional projections of a representation distribution to a standard Gaussian through an Epps–Pulley statistic (Balestriero and LeCun, 2025). We apply it to the hidden states leaving the first block, so that the constraint acts on the backbone’s input. Cells are grouped by (table, column), which enforces isotropy within each column’s own population; a trainable $256\rightarrow 1024\rightarrow 64$ projector maps each hidden state, $K=64$ random unit vectors slice the output, and for every group with at least eight valid cells we penalize the squared distance between the empirical characteristic function of each slice and that of $\mathcal{N}(0,1)$ over $[-3,3]$ . Writing that group-weighted penalty as $\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{col}}$ , the objective is

 $\mathcal{L}=(1-\lambda)\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{pred}}+\lambda\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{col}},\qquad\lambda=10^{-3},$ 

(5)

and the projector trains jointly with the backbone.

## CONCLUSION
modded-nanoRT holds the problem fixed and leaves the training stack open, so that improvements accumulate across contributors. The opening entry reaches the target in $305.63$ seconds on one H200, $8.11\times$ faster than the reference implementation. Matched comparisons attribute that to Muon, and to typed attention only in combination with it, and outside the development database the recipe wins 13 of 15 pairs. Every reported quantity selects the best scheduled checkpoint within the budget over three seeds, and rel-Amazon’s standard deviation of $12.86$ points exceeds every effect measured here.

Verification consumes $59.9\%$ of the record and the first scheduled evaluation clears the target by $5.5$ points, so the next entry should attack the evaluation path before the update loop, refit the learning-rate schedules to the horizon the record now occupies, and drop the text encoder (Appendix A).

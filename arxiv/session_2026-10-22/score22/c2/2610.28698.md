## ABSTRACT
Scaling data, diversity and model capacity has improved embodied learning in physical environments, where diverse experiences remain grounded in shared physical laws. Whether these gains extend to virtual worlds with different visual styles, dynamics, controls, and objectives remains unclear. We investigate this question through a controlled study of continued pre-training (CPT) across three axes: data volume, diversity, and model size. Our 120 training configurations span four model sizes from 0.8B to 27B, six data budgets from 50 to 1,600 hours, and five mixtures ranging from Minecraft-only data to diverse multi-world corpora. We assess performance and generalization using three-level validation losses and gameplay benchmarks from in-corpus worlds to unseen worlds. Our results show: (1) more data improves in-corpus performance, diversity improves transfer to related worlds with limited gains in distant worlds, and model size improves data efficiency but generalizes worse to distant worlds under single-world training; (2) we observe an early interface phase with broad cross-world benefits and a later world phase with gains narrowed to in-corpus and related worlds, and fit an empirical scaling law relating validation loss to data volume, diversity and model size; and (3) our models are competitive with Minecraft specialists and frontier models in 3D worlds, and perform best in real-time settings. However, the limits of imitation and persistent gaps in distant worlds suggest that building generalist embodied agents requires richer training stages beyond continued pre-training.

## 1 Introduction

Embodied agents are expected to perceive, reason, and act across changing environments. Recent scaling studies in robotics suggest that broader experience can improve such generality (Lin et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2026a; Abe et al., 2026). Real-world scenes share one coherent physical reality, i.e., gravity, contact, and object permanence remain consistent, and the physical effect of an embodiment’s controls changes relatively little across tasks. This shared structure makes experience collected in one setting potentially reusable in another.

In Figure 1, virtual worlds remove much of this consistency. Different worlds may vary simultaneously in entirely different visuals, rules, physics, action spaces and goals (Wang et al., 2025b; Tan et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2026b). Humans adapt to the heterogeneity quickly: they easily figure out unfamiliar rules, adjust to different controls, and apply past experience to new visuals and mechanics. A generalist virtual embodied agent should exhibit the same ability. Interactive video games provide a diverse and controlled subset of such worlds, making them a particularly demanding test of embodied generality (Abi Raad et al., 2024; SIMA Team, 2025; Zhang et al., 2026a).

Figure 1: One physical world versus diverse virtual worlds.
Left: Agents learn from varied physical scenes sharing common dynamics, where they can scale and generalize with broader experience (Wang et al., 2026a).
Right: Agents play diverse games with varying dynamics. We want to investigate the question that whether continued pre-training scales and generalizes in virtual worlds.

Recent work studies this broader goal by training models to perceive visual input from games and output native keyboard–mouse controls across different virtual game worlds (Baker et al., 2022; Magne et al., 2026; Yue et al., 2026). Across these systems, continued pre-training (CPT) on gameplay trajectories has become a common stage for generalist embodied intelligence (Abi Raad et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2025b; SIMA Team, 2025; Tan et al., 2025). However, virtual embodied continued pre-training remains largely a recipe: existing studies change the data volume, diversity and models together, leaving the effect of each factor unresolved. Most importantly, it remains unclear whether heterogeneous experiences help to build embodied agents that can generalize across different worlds, or if it just causes interference that ruins what they learned in any individual worlds.

We address this question with a controlled grid study spanning four model sizes from 0.8B to 27B, six data budgets from 50 to 1,600 hours, and five data mixture settings from Minecraft-only to broad multi-game corpora. The resulting 120 configurations are evaluated through three-level validation loss and benchmark ladders from in-corpus worlds, such as Minecraft (Zheng et al., 2025) and Doom (Wydmuch et al., 2019), to unseen near and distant worlds, such as OmniGameArena (Lin et al., 2026), and GameWorld (Ouyang et al., 2026). Our study reveals how data volume, mixture diversity, and model capacity affect embodied agents in these three levels of worlds, and whether heterogeneous experience becomes complementary as it scales.

Our contributions are mainly three-fold:

• 

A controlled study of scaling CPT in virtual worlds.
We quantify how data volume, mixture diversity, and model size affect CPT of generalist embodied agents in virtual worlds.
More relevant data improves performance on in-corpus worlds where model performance multiplies in Minecraft. Diversity improves transfer to related worlds but provides limited gains in distant worlds. Larger models improve data efficiency and achieve larger early gains, while generalizing worse under single-game training.

• 

An empirical two-phase learning dynamics and scaling law.
We observe an empirical early interface phase: model learns basic self-control and action primitives, which are shared and improved across worlds; and a world phase then follows: further gains are obtained in trained and unseen related worlds.
We fit an empirical scaling law to relate validation loss to data volume, mixture diversity and model size, achieving $R^{2}=0.982$ and $R^{2}=0.995$ on Minecraft and in-corpus loss, respectively.

• 

Three-level evaluation of model performance and generalization.
We evaluate models in a three-level setting covering in-corpus, related, and distant worlds. This anchors our models among game and general foundation models, providing the community a reliable reference. Our models are competitive with Minecraft specialists and frontier models in 3D worlds, and outperform frontier models in real-time settings by up to $5\times$ .

## 2 Related Work

Virtual Embodied Models. Early models were trained as environment-specific specialists. Deep reinforcement learning achieved superhuman performance in Atari, Go, StarCraft II, and Dota 2 while relying on a fixed environment, objective, and action interface (Mnih et al., 2015; Silver et al., 2016; Vinyals et al., 2019; OpenAI et al., 2019). Recent work instead focuses on large foundation models. VPT learns a Minecraft control prior from internet video, while NitroGen and OpenP2P extend pixel-to-action learning across broader game corpus (Baker et al., 2022; Magne et al., 2026; Yue et al., 2026). LLM and VLM based agents further reuse pre-trained language and visual knowledge for instruction following, planning and control (Abi Raad et al., 2024; Chen et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2025b; Tan et al., 2025; SIMA Team, 2025). Across these approaches, CPT has become a common mechanism for turning a general backbone into an interactive model for virtual embodied agents. To our knowledge, we conduct the largest controlled CPT study of virtual embodied models.

Virtual Worlds as Test Environments. Games provide a controllable subset of virtual worlds for evaluating perception, reasoning, and action under diverse rules and dynamics (Ouyang et al., 2026; Zhang et al., 2026a; Wang et al., 2026b; Zhang et al., 2026b). Minecraft and Doom suites measure capabilities within a familiar in-corpus world (Wydmuch et al., 2019; Zheng et al., 2025); OmniGameArena tests transfer to unseen 3D worlds (Lin et al., 2026); and GameWorld extends evaluation to visually and mechanically distinct 2D, 2.5D, and 3D environments (Ouyang et al., 2026). Training in one kind of virtual worlds may transfer to others, but there is no previous study to investigate which factor is critical to the transfer.

Scalability of Continued Pre-training. Classical scaling laws relate model performance to capacity, data, and compute, while studies of domain-adaptive and repeated pre-training show that source composition can alter both adaptation and retention (Kaplan et al., 2020; Hoffmann et al., 2022; Que et al., 2024; Gu et al., 2024; Wu et al., 2026). Related evidence appears in computer-use and embodied learning: GUI studies find that task composition can matter as much as sample count (Liu et al., 2026; Zhang et al., 2025; Raoof et al., 2026), and robotic imitation studies report diversity scaling, negative transfer, and capacity-dependent interference (Lin et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2026a; Abe et al., 2026). Virtual worlds sharpen this problem because visual style, dynamics, controls, and objectives change across sources. Existing research on CPT in virtual worlds alters model size, training data, and duration simultaneously. No prior work systematically isolates these three axes while measuring held-out loss and transfer capabilities at varying distances from training.

## 3 Preliminaries and Experiment Framework

## 3.1 Data construction

Figure 2: Adaptation to the keyboard–mouse interface during CPT. Smoothed training losses for key, mouse, and format tokens, and eight-direction mouse prediction accuracy for three model capacities trained on mixtures S1 and S4.

Gameplay trajectories.
Following the setting in previous works (Wang et al., 2025b; Tan et al., 2025; SIMA Team, 2025; Lin et al., 2026), we formulate gameplay as visual decision-making process. At step $t$ , the model takes prompt context $c$ , the current observation $o_{t}$ , and interaction history $\mathcal{H}_{t}$ as the input $x_{t}$ and produces a reasoning trace $r_{t}$ and then an executable action $a_{t}$ :

 $r_{t}\sim p_{\theta}(\cdot\mid x_{t}),\qquad a_{t}\sim p_{\theta}(\cdot\mid x_{t},r_{t}),\qquad x_{t}=(c,o_{t},\mathcal{H}_{t}).$ 

(1)

Reasoning and action have distinct roles. The possibly empty $r_{t}$ is internal textual reasoning. The mandatory $a_{t}$ is structured text chunks that are parsed into low-level device commands, which further changes the environment. Each chunk covers 200 ms (Tan et al., 2025; Lin et al., 2026):

 $a_{t}=\texttt{<|action\_start|>}~\Delta x_{t}~\Delta y_{t}~\Delta z_{t};K_{t}^{(1)};\ldots;K_{t}^{(6)}~\texttt{<|action\_end|>},$ 

(2)

where $(\Delta x_{t},\Delta y_{t})\in[-1000,1000]^{2}$ are discretized relative mouse moves, $\Delta z_{t}\in[-5,5]$ is the scroll delta, and each $K_{t}^{(j)}$ contains 0-4 keys or mouse buttons held for 33 ms. The model receives observations at 5 Hz and controls at 30 Hz. History is modeled as interleaved image–action turns. Each trajectory has 20 turns. During training, preceding think and action are visible to later turns.

Three scaling axes.
Our study evaluates a $4\times 6\times 5=120$ configuration grid across four Qwen3.5 backbones $\mathcal{M}=\{\text{0.8B},\text{2B},\text{9B},\text{27B}\}$ (Qwen Team, 2026), six data budgets $\mathcal{T}=\{50,100,200,400,800,1600\}$ , and five data-diversity mixtures $\mathcal{S}=\{\mathrm{S1},\ldots,\mathrm{S5}\}$ . This yields total $3.5\times 10^{11}$ training tokens and $2.2\times 10^{22}$ FLOPs via the “ $6ND$ rule” (Hoffmann et al., 2022).

Data diversity mixtures.
Our training data consists of two components: (1) 85% gameplay trajectories sampled from VPT, D2E, and OpenP2P (Baker et al., 2022; Choi et al., 2025; Yue et al., 2026), and (2) 15% general data covering vision–language, embodied and egocentric settings (Li et al., 2025a; Li et al., 2026; Tong et al., 2026; Zhang et al., 2026c; Yang et al., 2026). We construct five data mixtures ( $\mathrm{S1}$ – $\mathrm{S5}$ ) with increasing mixture diversity by setting the Minecraft trajectory proportion in the gameplay data to 100%, 51.4%, 27.1%, 2.8%, and 0%, respectively. The remaining trajectories are sampled from a diverse pool of other games. We also augment a small fraction of gameplay turns with retrospective reasoning generated by GPT-5.6-Luna (OpenAI, 2026) based on the recorded observation and action, to preserve the backbone’s native reasoning capabilities without altering action targets. See Appendices A.1 and A.2 for further details about scaling on mixture diversity.

## 3.2 Training and Evaluation

Training objective and validation sets.
All models are continued pre-trained using a causal next-token prediction loss over assistant responses conditioned on interleaved histories. All parameters, including those of the ViT, are updated using AdamW (Loshchilov and Hutter, 2019) with a Warmup–Stable–Decay schedule (Hu et al., 2024). The resulting models are evaluated on three levels of validation data: Minecraft-only data, data from in-corpus games covered during training, and data from unseen games not covered in training. We also record key, mouse, and format losses. Apart from data volume, data diversity, and the choice of base model, all experimental settings are held fixed across training configurations. See Appendix A.4 for further details.

Three-level gameplay evaluation.
We evaluate gameplay competence across three categories of virtual worlds that are increasing distant from the training distribution. (1) In-corpus worlds: we use the MCU benchmark (Zheng et al., 2025) to evaluate performance on multiple tasks in Minecraft, and ViZDoom (Wydmuch et al., 2019) to evaluate performance on single shooting tasks in DOOM. Both games are covered in our training corpus. (2) Unseen related worlds: we evaluate our model in OmniGameArena (OGA) (Lin et al., 2026), which consists of seven unseen 3D worlds. Although their scenes and tasks are absent from training, action primitives such as navigation, aiming, jumping, shooting, and interaction have semantic counterparts in the training corpus. (3) Unseen distant world: we evaluate our model in GameWorld (GW) (Ouyang et al., 2026), which primarily covers 2D and 2.5D games, alongside a small portion of 3D games. These games differ substantially from the training data in visual styles, physics, controls and objectives.

## 4.1 Data Scaling

Early training brings broad cross-world gains.
Figure 2 shows the training loss of key, mouse, and format tokens. These losses drop rapidly during the initial tens of hours of training data before tapering into a more gradual decrease. Mouse direction prediction accuracy continues to improve. After exposure to 50 hours of training data, validation losses drop below those of the base models on all three validation sets (Figure 3), even for games absent from the training mixture. We refer to this early stage as the interface phase. This cross world improvement indicates that the model gradually acquires shared formats, representations and action patterns that can transfer across games.

Later gains depend more on the worlds seen during training.
After the interface phase, further performance gains depend heavily on exposure to relevant game worlds. We term this stage the world phase. From 50 to 1,600 hours, the mean Minecraft validation loss decreases by about 10.4% across four model sizes under the MineCraft-heavy mixtures S1–S3, correlating with substantial gains in MCU performance (Figure 3). Conversely, scaling total data volume without sufficient Minecraft trajectories yields negligible further reduction in this specific validation loss. For the in-corpus validation set, diverse multi-game training consistently reduces loss across all model sizes, whereas Minecraft-only training causes the loss to plateau or even increase. Similarly, out-of-distribution (OOD) loss stagnates or rises, although it remains below base model levels. At the largest model size, GameWorld performance degrades across all five mixtures as data volume increases. Thus, more data improves performance on in-corpus and related environments, but fails to drive continued gains in distant worlds.

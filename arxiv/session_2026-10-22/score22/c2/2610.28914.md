Authors: Shuo Yuan, Dacheng Li, Joseph E. Gonzalez
Comment: 21 pages, 4 figures, 9 tables
Categories: cs.AI

## ABSTRACT
Frontier language-model agents excel at complex tasks, but high inference costs make transferring their capabilities to smaller models essential. Traditional knowledge distillation achieves this through parameter updates, requiring access to the student's weights and additional training compute while encoding the transferred knowledge implicitly in the parameters. We introduce D-Skill, a framework that treats the teacher as a black box and distills its behavioral advantage into an interpretable natural-language skill by analyzing paired rollouts and identifying behavioral divergences and agreements. The resulting lessons are consolidated offline, with any compatible skill-update backend, into a skill document that is provided to the student at inference time. D-Skill constructs the skill entirely offline from fixed trajectories, without labels, further environment interaction, or weight updates after rollout collection. Across SpreadsheetBench-Verified, ALFWorld, and chess puzzles spanning multiple teacher-student pairs, D-Skill closes 75%-95% of the teacher-student gap without touching model weights. After a single offline pass over the paired traces, D-Skill outperforms one-epoch SFT baselines and remains competitive with multi-epoch SFT. Under the same model family, the resulting student also incurs 5.7x-8.3x lower inference cost per task than the teacher. Our results demonstrate that natural-language skill distillation offers a performant, interpretable, and efficient alternative to weight-based capability transfer.

## 1 Introduction

Figure 1: D-Skill closes the gap at a fraction of its cost.
D-Skill closes 84% of the teacher-student gap at $8.3\times$ lower inference cost on SpreadsheetBench-Verified. Teacher: GPT-5.6 Terra; student: GPT-5.6 Luna.

Frontier agents can reliably solve complex agentic tasks
(OpenAI, 2026b; Anthropic, 2026a; Jimenez et al., 2024; Yao et al., 2024; Xie et al., 2024; Merrill et al., 2026), but their high inference costs (Chen et al., 2023b) make it desirable to transfer their capabilities to cheaper student models (Hinton et al., 2015; Hsieh et al., 2023; Zeng et al., 2024; Chen et al., 2023a; Chen et al., 2024; Kang et al., 2026). Classical knowledge distillation performs this transfer by updating the student’s
parameters using supervision such as the teacher’s output distribution
(Hinton et al., 2015; Kim and Rush, 2016; Gu et al., 2023), complete teacher-generated trajectories (Ho et al., 2023; Hsieh et al., 2023; Zeng et al., 2024; Chen et al., 2023a; Chen et al., 2024; Kang et al., 2026), or teacher evaluations of student-generated samples
(Agarwal et al., 2024; Bai et al., 2022; Lee et al., 2023).
Despite differences in supervision, these approaches impose several constraints: weight access is required, excluding many proprietary API-served models; backpropagation adds substantial computational cost; and sufficient training data is needed for the student to generalize. Moreover, the transferred knowledge is encoded in the model’s parameters, making it difficult to inspect, revise, or correct after training (Meng et al., 2022; Yao et al., 2023).

Recent work has shown that skills, natural-language artifacts encoding reusable task knowledge, strategies, and procedures, can substantially improve agent performance without modifying model weights (Anthropic, 2025a; Ni et al., 2026; Yang et al., 2026; Zhang et al., 2026).
Because skills remain external to the model, they are portable across deployments and can be inspected or edited after construction (Anthropic, 2025a).
Existing skill-generation methods are generally not organized around the teacher–student relationship central to distillation. Teacher-driven methods compile stronger-model trajectories or teacher-generated modules without conditioning the transfer on the target student’s behavior (Ni et al., 2026; Fu et al., 2024; Qiu et al., 2025). Student-centered methods instead learn from the target agent’s own experience, including contrasts between successful and failed rollouts, without a matched stronger-agent reference (Shinn et al., 2023; Zhao et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2024; Suzgun et al., 2026; Zhang et al., 2026; Yang et al., 2026; Ma et al., 2026). D-Skill instead constructs a student-targeted skill by comparing teacher and student trajectories collected on the same tasks. We ask whether their behavioral differences can be distilled, in a single offline pass over fixed traces, into an external textual update.

We propose D-Skill, a method for distilling model capabilities into a skill by comparing paired teacher and student rollouts. For each unlabeled task, we obtain one rollout from each model, either through fresh execution or from previously saved traces. A difference analyst extracts lessons from both divergences and agreements between the two behaviors. Divergences identify states in which the student behaves differently from the teacher and pair the student’s behavior with the teacher’s alternative, while agreements identify shared strategies that should be preserved. These lessons are consolidated into a single skill, which is loaded into the student’s context at inference time. Once the rollouts have been collected, D-Skill requires no answer keys, labels, held-out evaluation, or further environment interaction. The teacher and student contribute only through their fixed traces: neither is queried or fine-tuned during analysis and consolidation, and no model weights are modified.

We evaluate D-Skill on SpreadsheetBench Verified, ALFWorld and chess puzzles using different teacher-student pairs with different model families. As presented in section 4.1, across all three benchmarks and different teacher-student settings, D-Skill closes $83.4\%$ to $89.3\%$ of the teacher–student performance gap on SpreadsheetBench Verified, $84.6\%$ to $94.6\%$ on ALFWorld, and $75.3\%$ to $87.7\%$ on chess puzzles. This transfer also yields substantial inference-cost savings: in the GPT-5.6 Terra–Luna setting, the D-Skill student costs $8.3\times$ less per task than the teacher on SpreadsheetBench Verified and $5.7\times$ less per episode on ALFWorld (see section 5.3). We further compare D-Skill with supervised fine-tuning and find that it provides stronger transfer after a single pass over the teacher corpus while attaining performance comparable to multi-epoch fine-tuning (Section 5.2). These results demonstrate that textual skill distillation can match weight-based transfer in the evaluated setting while producing an artifact that remains readable, editable, and portable. In summary, this paper makes the following contributions:

• 

We formulate skill distillation as an alternative to weight-based knowledge distillation, encoding transferred capabilities in an external, interpretable artifact that requires neither weight access nor gradient-based training.

• 

We introduce D-Skill, a simple yet effective method that distills paired teacher and student rollouts by identifying their behavioral divergences and agreements and consolidating the resulting lessons into a reusable skill. After rollout collection, the method requires no labels, held-out evaluation, or further environment interaction.

• 

We demonstrate across three benchmarks and multiple teacher–student pairs that D-Skill closes most of the performance gap. In addition, it also outperforms the evaluated skill-learning baselines while retaining substantially lower inference cost than the teacher.

## 2 Related Work

## Knowledge distillation.

Knowledge distillation trains a compact student to match a stronger teacher’s
predictions (Bucilă et al., 2006; Hinton et al., 2015), extended to sequence-level
imitation (Kim and Rush, 2016), compressed language models (Sanh et al., 2019),
and on-policy variants that supervise the student’s own rollouts to close the
train–inference mismatch (Gu et al., 2023; Agarwal et al., 2024; Xu et al., 2025; Zhao et al., 2026).
Throughout, two assumptions persist: the transfer medium is parameters
(gradient updates, so closed-weight students are out of reach), and the signal
is distributional (logits or token-level divergences, requiring white-box teacher
access and a shared tokenizer). D-Skill relaxes both: knowledge is retained as an
explicit textual skill consumed in context by black-box students, and the signal is the behavioral contrast between teacher and student trajectories on shared tasks.

## Skill and memory-augmented agents.

A complementary line of work equips agents with persistent memories or skills—external artifacts carried across tasks and consulted at inference time. Reflexion stores reflections on past failures (Shinn et al., 2023), Voyager builds libraries of executable skills through exploration (Wang et al., 2023), and subsequent work consolidates trajectories into procedural memory (Wang et al., 2024; Fang et al., 2026; Li et al., 2026). SkillGen contrastively induces and validates skills from an agent’s successful and failed rollouts (Ma et al., 2026). Teacher-driven approaches instead transfer stronger capabilities: Trace2Skill compiles stronger-model trajectories into textual skills (Ni et al., 2026), while AgentDistill packages teacher-generated procedures as reusable MCP modules (Qiu et al., 2025).
D-Skill instead constructs a student-targeted skill by directly comparing teacher and student trajectories.
D-Skill addresses this gap by contrasting teacher and student trajectories, producing guidance targeted to their behavioral differences.

## Automatic textual optimization.

A third line improves a text artifact through iterative textual updates: APE and OPRO
search over instructions with LLM proposers (Zhou et al., 2022; Yang et al., 2024),
Promptbreeder evolves prompts self-referentially (Fernando et al., 2023),
DSPy compiles multi-stage pipelines by tuning their prompts against a validation metric
(Khattab et al., 2023), and TextGrad backpropagates natural-language critiques as
“textual gradients” (Yuksekgonul et al., 2024). The same machinery has recently
been applied beyond instructions: GEPA evolves prompts by reflecting on execution
traces (Agrawal et al., 2026), and SkillOpt optimizes a portable skill file through
gated patch-style edits validated on held-out tasks (Yang et al., 2026), one
instance of a growing family of self-evolving skill methods (Jiang et al., 2026).
These methods treat text as an optimizable artifact, typically using task evaluation
to select or refine updates. D-Skill follows the same broad paradigm but focuses on
teacher–student distillation: it derives textual updates from behavioral contrasts
between a stronger teacher and a target student on matched tasks. D-Skill thus extends
automatic textual optimization to capability transfer while remaining compatible with
different skill-update backends.

## 3 Method

Figure 2 summarizes D-Skill, which consists
of three stages: rollout collection, difference analysis, and skill update.
No stage computes a gradient or requires access to the parameters of either
model. Collecting fresh rollouts requires the teacher and student to interact
with the task environment; when saved traces are available, this interaction
can instead have occurred during an earlier execution. In either case, all
subsequent analysis and skill construction operate offline on fixed traces.

## 3.1 Problem Setup

Let $\mathcal{T}=\{t_{1},\ldots,t_{n}\}$ be a set of unlabeled tasks sampled from
a task distribution $\mathcal{D}$ , and let $\pi_{S}$ and $\pi_{T}$ denote a
student and a stronger teacher policy operating in the same task and action
space. Given paired trajectories
 $\{(\tau_{S}(t),\tau_{T}(t)):t\in\mathcal{T}\}$ , our objective is to construct a
natural-language skill $s$ such that the skill-conditioned student $\pi_{S}^{s}$ 
improves over the original student without modifying its parameters. If
 $J(\pi)$ denotes expected task performance, we quantify the transfer by the
fraction of the teacher–student gap closed:

 $\operatorname{GapClosed}(s)=\frac{J(\pi_{S}^{s})-J(\pi_{S})}{J(\pi_{T})-J(\pi_{S})}.$ 

## 3.2 Rollout Collection

For each task $t\in\mathcal{T}$ , we collect one trajectory from each model,
 $\tau_{S}(t)$ and $\tau_{T}(t)$ , recording the actions issued and the observations
returned. The paired trajectories are the unit of supervision; no later stage analyzes either trajectory in isolation.
The two policies attempt each task independently, with neither conditioned on the other’s behavior. When trajectories are collected specifically for D-Skill, both policies interact with the task environment through ordinary execution; alternatively, trajectories may be retrieved from logs of prior executions, allowing distillation to begin directly from saved traces. The two trajectories need only correspond to the same task and use a shared action interface. Once the trajectories have been collected, D-Skill operates fully offline: neither $\pi_{S}$ nor $\pi_{T}$ is invoked again, and skill construction requires no further environment access, ground-truth labels, rewards, or evaluation feedback.

## 3.3 Difference Analysis

In the second stage, a difference analyst agent $\mathcal{A}$ converts each
paired student and teacher trace into a set of structured, reusable
lessons. The analyst can inspect the actions and observations recorded in
the traces, but it has no access to answer keys, labels, or additional environment interaction.

Rather than asking the analyst to compare the trajectories freely, we require it to examine each pair along a predefined set of behavioral dimensions. These dimensions cover, for example, what information each agent gathered before acting, how it selected and executed actions, how it responded to errors or unproductive intermediate observations, and how it verified the final result. This shared schema makes lessons comparable across task pairs and discourages the analyst from emphasizing superficial differences in naming, phrasing, or reasoning style. Each lesson must cite concrete evidence from the paired traces and express a task-general principle rather than an instance-specific correction. Details about the prompt for the analyst can be found in appendix B.

For each trajectory pair, the analyst produces at most three lessons, ranked by their anticipated usefulness on future tasks, and assigns each to one of two classes: a divergence lesson identifies a behavioral difference between the two agents, specifying the student’s behavior, the teacher’s alternative, and a reusable instruction intended to address the gap; an agreement lesson identifies a behavior shared by both agents that is judged worth preserving in the distilled skill.

## 3.4 Skill Update

To construct the final skill document, we use a modified version of the
Trace2Skill consolidation backend (Ni et al., 2026) as the D-Skill consolidator. We retain its map–reduce–apply procedure but adapt its input to consume the structured
divergence and agreement lessons produced by our difference-analysis
stage. The complete lesson pool is shuffled and partitioned into $k$ chunks, which are
processed sequentially to update the skill document.
For each chunk, a consolidation agent $\mathcal{C}$ performs three phases.
In the map phase, it independently converts each lesson into a
candidate patch to the current skill, such as appending to an existing
section, replacing existing content, or creating a new section. In the
reduce phase, it merges the patches by removing duplicates,
resolving conflicts, and generalizing related rules. Finally, in the
apply phase, the merged patch is deterministically incorporated into
the current skill before the next chunk is processed.

We use this adapted Trace2Skill backend throughout our experiments. D-Skill’s
output from the preceding stage is a general pool of structured lessons, so
other compatible skill-updating backends could be substituted without
changing the rollout-collection or difference-analysis stages, more details can be found in section 4.2.

## LIMITATIONS SECTION
D-Skill has three main limitations. First, it requires a capable analyst at distillation time: replacing the analyst with the weaker student substantially reduces the fraction of the gap closed (Table 3), although this is a one-time rather than per-inference cost. Second, loading the distilled document increases inference cost relative to the bare student by adding input tokens and, in some cases, inducing additional interaction steps, even though the resulting agent remains substantially cheaper than the teacher. Third, D-Skill can transfer only capabilities that can be expressed as instructions and executed by the student. It can improve procedures such as planning, verification, and tool use, but cannot supply fundamental capabilities the student lacks, such as deeper calculation, perception, or sufficient underlying model capacity.

## CONCLUSION
We introduced D-Skill, a method that distills the behavioral gap between a black-box teacher and student into a natural-language skill by contrasting paired rollouts on the same tasks. Once the trajectories are collected, D-Skill operates fully offline, requiring no further policy queries, environment interaction, labels, rewards, evaluation feedback, or weight updates. Across SpreadsheetBench Verified, ALFWorld, and chess puzzles, D-Skill closes $82$ – $95\%$ of the teacher–student performance gap across multiple model families while retaining a substantial inference-cost advantage over the teacher.
D-Skill also provides stronger one-pass transfer and remains competitive with multi-epoch SFT when using the same teacher-trajectory corpus, without gradient updates or access to the student’s weights. Together, these results demonstrate that natural-language skill distillation is a practical, interpretable, and inference-efficient alternative to weight-based capability transfer in the evaluated domains.

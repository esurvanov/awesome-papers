# 2610.28875 — To Manipulate or Not: Opinion Dynamics in LLM Interactions
Authors: Mohammad Siahkamari, Phuoc Dat Nguyen, Stephen MacNeil
Comment: 
Categories: cs.AI, cs.CL, cs.MA, cs.SI

## ABSTRACT
Large language models increasingly take part in extended conversations where they can both shape and be shaped by another party's views, yet most persuasion research measures only one side of that exchange. We introduce a controlled LLM-LLM debate framework in which two language-model roles repeatedly discuss a contested topic (climate-change attribution): one is given an assigned stance and, in some conditions, an explicit instruction to persuade, while the other receives no assigned position. Across four ordered model combinations involving GPT-6 Luna and Gemini 2.5 Flash Lite, we track both each role's explicitly reported position and a separate measure of its turn-by-turn behavior inside the conversation. Opinion dynamics differ substantially across model combinations: same-model interactions narrow the opinion gap on average, clearly for only one of the two models, while cross-model interactions widen it. Explicit reports and in-dialogue behavior capture distinct aspects of opinion dynamics, and a blanket instruction to persuade does not reliably increase influence. These findings suggest that conversational influence depends not only on assigned roles and initial positions but also on the participating models, with implications for how opinion change is measured in extended LLM interactions.

## Introduction

Large language models can sustain persuasive dialogue, adapt arguments to a counterpart, and revise the stance they report later in the same conversation. This creates a coupled problem: the model designated as persuader may influence its counterpart while also accommodating the counterpart’s arguments, that is, moving its own stated position toward the counterpart’s. Measuring only the target’s post-interaction opinion therefore misses part of the dynamics.

A literature on LLM opinion dynamics treats LLMs as interacting agents whose opinions evolve through natural-language exchange (Cau et al. 2025; Chuang et al. 2024). In LODAS (Language-Driven Opinion Dynamics Model for Agent-Based Simulations), an Opponent argues and a Discussant accepts, rejects, or ignores before updating a seven-point state (Cau et al. 2025); within each pair, however, only the Discussant changes. Recent human–LLM work instead measures both parties and finds that the model can move more than the human while the opinion gap narrows (Jiang et al. 2025).

We therefore use a controlled LLM–LLM design, which allows stance, manipulation instruction, and model identity to be assigned by design and both parties’ opinions to be measured at every round. A Discussant and an Opponent interact for 20 rounds. The Discussant receives no assigned stance. The Opponent is assigned one of three stances (human-cause, neutral, or natural-cause) and is either given or not given an additional instruction to manipulate and persuade. Both roles repeatedly report their opinion on one of two opposing formulations of the same climate-change issue. We additionally track an interaction-state opinion derived from each role’s ACCEPT/REJECT/IGNORE response inside the dialogue. The model serving each role is also varied independently: GPT-6 Luna or Gemini 2.5 Flash Lite, yielding four ordered Discussant–Opponent pairings. This separates prompted-role effects, model-specific issue priors, and adaptation in same-model versus cross-model interaction.

Throughout the paper, human-causation statement refers to “Human activity is the primary cause of climate change,” and natural-process statement refers to the opposing wording, “Natural processes are the primary cause of climate change.” The Opponent’s assigned stance names the position it argues, independent of which sentence is displayed: human-cause (argue that human activity is the primary cause), natural-cause (argue that natural processes are), or neutral. Because the two wordings are opposite sentences, a human-cause Opponent agrees with the human-causation sentence and disagrees with the natural-process sentence, and the reverse holds for a natural-cause Opponent; we therefore name stances by the position argued rather than by agreement with a displayed sentence.

We ask:

• 

RQ1: Assigned stance and issue prior. Do Opponents begin and remain near their assigned human-cause, neutral, or natural-cause stances, and what stance emerges for the unassigned Discussant and neutral Opponent under each model?

• 

RQ2: Bidirectional dynamics and measurement. How do self-reported topic positions and interaction-state opinions evolve over 20 rounds, when do they stabilize, and under which conditions does the pairwise gap narrow or widen, including whether this depends on which model occupies each role?

• 

RQ3: Manipulation and accommodation. Does an explicit instruction to manipulate increase influence or stance persistence consistently across same-model and cross-model pairings, or does it instead increase accommodation to the counterpart?

The results show model-dependent rather than universal accommodation. Same-model Luna dyads converge strongly, Gemini–Gemini dyads show less certain mean convergence, and cross-model dyads diverge on average, despite all four pairings sharing the same assigned positions, topic wordings, and manipulation conditions. The unassigned Discussant’s issue-specific starting position also differs by model. An additional instruction to manipulate does not have a consistent effect across pairings. We therefore evaluate persuasion as bidirectional, model-pairing-dependent dynamics rather than assuming the nominal persuader remains fixed or that prolonged conversation always narrows disagreement.

## Related Work

## Conversational persuasion and manipulation.

LLM persuasiveness depends on prompting, post-training, model behavior, and role framing (Hackenburg et al. 2025b; Hackenburg et al. 2025a; Breum et al. 2024). Ideological and identity cues can alter expressed moral and political reasoning (Simmons 2023; Santurkar et al. 2023; Paschalides et al. 2025). Our intervention is one extra instruction to manipulate and persuade toward an assigned position, related to broader work on manipulation and sycophancy (Carroll et al. 2023; Bo et al. 2025); we analyze whether that instruction changes measured stance dynamics.

## LLM societies and opinion dynamics.

Agent simulations have reported convergence, polarization, confirmation bias, and coordination (Chuang et al. 2024; De Marzo et al. 2026). Calibrated social-media simulations and the PIMMUR principles emphasize that claims about collective behavior require explicit profiles, interaction rules, memory, controls, and validation (Composta et al. 2026; Zhou et al. 2025). Multiagent debate has several model instances argue toward a more accurate answer to a factual or reasoning question (Du et al. 2024). We instead measure each role’s repeated opinion on a contested statement as a function of assigned stance, manipulation instruction, and model identity. The closest mechanism to our study is LODAS (Cau et al. 2025), which uses Opponent and Discussant roles, a 0–6 state, and accept/reject/ignore updates. We retain that interpretable structure but use a fixed dyad and allow both roles to update.

## Bidirectional influence.

Jiang et al. (2025) show that human and LLM opinions can both change and that the model may move more than the human. We transfer that bidirectional measurement logic into controlled same-model and cross-model LLM dyads. Relative to prior work, our contribution is to cross model identity in each role with assigned position, statement wording, and a minimal manipulation instruction while measuring repeated opinions for both members of the dyad.

## Method

## Experimental System

We implemented an automated debate platform that creates a Discussant–Opponent pair, alternates model calls, records every message and measurement, and exports message-level research data. We used the logged model identifiers openai/gpt-6-luna (GPT-6 Luna) and google/gemini-2.5-flash-lite (Gemini 2.5 Flash Lite (Comanici and others 2025)), through OpenRouter (OpenRouter 2026). The Discussant and Opponent were independently assigned one of these models, so model order is part of each experimental condition. Every run began with a fresh context.

The stance labels human-cause, neutral, and natural-cause refer to the position the Opponent was assigned to argue in this experiment. The Discussant had no assigned polarity. Manipulative means that the Opponent received the additional instruction to manipulate and persuade the Discussant toward its own opinion; non-manipulative means the otherwise matched Opponent did not receive that instruction.

## Factorial Design and Statement Wording

We crossed Discussant model (two choices), Opponent model (two choices), three assigned Opponent positions, two persuasion styles, and two opposing wordings of the same issue. These $2\times 2\times 3\times 2\times 2=48$ exact cells each contain exactly 10 completed, independent debates, and no cell is missing or over-filled (Appendix Figure 8). Within one ordered model pairing, the three positions, two persuasion styles, and two wordings give $3\times 2\times 2=12$ cells, so each pairing contains $12\times 10=120$ debates; the four pairings give $4\times 120=480$ debates in total (Table 1). The four pairings are Luna $\to$ Luna, Gemini $\to$ Gemini, Luna $\to$ Gemini, and Gemini $\to$ Luna (Discussant $\to$ Opponent). Following Cau et al. (2025), who run their simulations for a fixed number of iterations ( $T=30$ ), we run every debate for a fixed $T=20$ iterations, which we call rounds. A round contains one Discussant contribution and one Opponent contribution, with the first Discussant contribution presenting the topic. Each 20-round debate contains 40 message rows; the final dataset contains 19,200 rows.

## Role Interaction and Complementary Opinion Measures

The Discussant follows the response structure adapted from Cau et al. (2025): it receives its current textual opinion, evaluates the Opponent’s argument, and returns an accept, reject, or ignore decision with a justification; every contribution except the topic opener is such a response-eligible turn. The first Discussant message is the topic opener. The Opponent uses a symmetric template, with the assigned position and persuasion-style instruction added to its role context. No numerical starting opinion is hard-coded by condition; before dialogue, each role independently rates the presented statement.

We record two complementary outcomes. The self-reported topic position is elicited through a separate model call before dialogue, after each available round, and after the final round. The role rates the original statement from 0 (strongly disagree) to 6 (strongly agree) (Likert 1932) using the conversation available at that stage. It is therefore an explicit report about the original proposition. In trajectory figures, stage denotes Pre, each successive debate round, and, when shown, Post.

The interaction-state opinion summarizes stance-taking inside the dialogue. It begins at the role’s pre-rating and is updated from the role’s own categorical response: ACCEPT moves one point toward the counterpart’s current state, REJECT moves one point away, and IGNORE leaves the state unchanged. The two outcomes differ in elicitation and interpretation, so exact equality is not expected. Their divergence indicates that local conversational responses and explicit evaluation of the original statement can evolve differently.

## Outcomes and Analysis

For raw rating $r$ , the human-causation-aligned score is

 $z=\begin{cases}r,&\text{human-causation statement},\\
6-r,&\text{natural-process statement}.\end{cases}$ 

(1)

Higher $z$ always denotes stronger endorsement of human causation. Signed shift is $\Delta z_{k}=z_{k,\mathrm{post}}-z_{k,\mathrm{pre}}$ for role $k$ . Pairwise distance at stage $t$ is $g_{t}=|z_{D,t}-z_{O,t}|$ , and a debate converges when $g_{\mathrm{post}}<g_{\mathrm{pre}}$ . Within each ordered model pairing we summarize conversation-level means, $t$ -based 95% confidence intervals, and condition-level differences. Pairwise manipulation contrasts use exploratory two-sided Welch tests (Welch 1947); we also fit role-specific ordinary least-squares models with HC3 robust standard errors (MacKinnon and White 1985) and factorial condition interactions. Repeated message rows are not treated as independent debates in conversation-level inference. These comparisons are descriptive and exploratory. To assess heterogeneity across the four ordered model pairings, we additionally fit a conversation-level linear model for pre/post absolute-gap change with categorical model pairing, assigned position, persuasion style, and statement wording. We report a joint Wald test of the pairing terms using HC3 robust standard errors; a second model includes pairing-by-manipulation interactions. Six exploratory between-pair Welch comparisons are Holm-adjusted (Holm 1979). This analysis treats independent debates, not individual message rows, as observations. The complete experimental balance and all original condition-level displays are documented in Appendix Figures 8–15.

## Results

## RQ1: Assigned Positions and Model-Specific Starting Ratings

Opponent pre-ratings largely reflected the assigned role, although the strength of this separation varied by model. On the human-causation-aligned 0–6 scale, assigned human-cause Opponents averaged 5.94, assigned neutral Opponents 3.86, and assigned natural-cause Opponents 2.59 over all 480 debates. Thus, the assigned positions produced the expected initial ordering, but natural-cause and neutral ratings were not fixed at the scale endpoints.

The unassigned Discussant showed a model-dependent issue-specific prior. Its mean aligned pre-rating was 5.91 when GPT-6 Luna served as Discussant, compared with 4.72 under Gemini 2.5 Flash Lite. Both means favor human causation under our coding, but they differ appreciably. Table 3 summarizes starting and ending ratings by assigned Opponent position, pooled across pairings.

## ABSTRACT
Through a tangle of real-world interactions, from synthetic training pipelines to data shared on the open web, frontier LLMs absorb what their predecessors and competitors create. How well does each model play its role in this ecosystem, as a creator and as a solver? We introduce Environment Duels, a dynamic agentic benchmark that evaluates both capabilities on interactive environments. Each model writes environments, each with a privileged hint, aiming for tasks that capable solvers fail without the hint and pass with it. Across nine frontier LLMs, solving and authoring prove to be distinct skills: the strongest solvers are not the strongest authors, while smaller models learn more from how others played and write the hardest environments and the most useful hints. Beyond the scores, the pairwise interactions form a microcosm of how models affect one another. Head-to-head outcomes are transitive and so yield a stable ranking; independent authors converge on the same designs; and in a reinforcement-learning study with Qwen3.8-27B, the learner's hint lift on an author's environments tracks how much training on those environments improves it on held-out reasoning and agentic benchmarks, whether the environments are its own or another model's. Each of these patterns has a counterpart in how models shape one another outside the duel. By staging these interactions under controlled conditions, Environment Duels offers a window onto how LLMs teach, challenge and learn from one another. Code is available at https://github.com/LLM-Duels/EnvDuels.

## 1 Introduction

Every frontier LLM is now,
in part,
a student of its predecessors and competitors.
Model developers describe pipelines in which one model authors coding challenges,
math problems or reasoning environments,
and the next model trains on them (Xu et al., 2026a; Zhao et al., 2025a; Cai et al., 2025).
The same synthetic data spreads through public repositories and benchmarks,
where other labs’ training runs ingest it (Drayson et al., 2025).
Models thus co-evolve:
each one’s training distribution is shaped by what other models chose to write.
When a lab closes this loop deliberately,
having its own models write the curriculum for the next generation,
the process becomes recursive self-improvement in a concrete engineering sense.
Either way,
what a model learns depends on how well another model can write problems worth learning from (Xu et al., 2025b).

A model’s skill as an author therefore shapes what the next generation learns,
in its own family or a competitor’s.
Language models now match or exceed human performance on many established benchmarks (Center for AI Safety et al., 2026).
As they do,
sufficiently challenging human-authored tasks become increasingly costly to curate (Henniger and Poesia, 2026),
and recent progress on long-standing open problems,
including Millennium Prize conjectures (OpenAI, 2026a),
suggests that even the supply of unsolved challenges a model can train on is finite.
Once the stock of existing problems thins out,
the bottleneck moves: what matters is the ability to pose new problems that are worth solving.
A model that can formulate well-specified,
novel challenges,
and calibrate their difficulty to push the boundaries of current methods,
is the kind of capability that could drive fully autonomous scientific research.

Environment Duels builds a microcosm of this ecosystem.
Every model plays two roles:
it writes executable environments for the others to solve,
and it solves the environments they write.
An environment is an interactive program with hidden state,
a reward and an action budget,
a richer format than a static text question:
solvers must explore,
act and adapt over many turns rather than answer once.
Each author also supplies a privileged hint that lets a solver approach its environment correctly,
and aims for an environment that capable solvers fail without the hint and pass with it.
After the first round,
each author sees how the others actually played and answers with one counter-move.
We run this duel among nine frontier models,
90 environments and 12,960 episodes in all,
and score each model on how well it solves (solve rate),
how hard its environments are (difficulty),
and how much its hints help (hint lift).

What emerges resembles the ecosystem outside the duel.
The models that solve best are not the ones whose environments teach best:
smaller models learn more from how others played,
and their counter-moves produce the hardest environments and the most useful hints.
DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash,
for example,
writes the hardest environments of all nine models,
and its developers report training it heavily on synthetic environments (Xu et al., 2026a),
an independent sign that the duel measures a skill that training can build.
The duel also carries signal beyond itself:
how much an author’s hints help a learner anticipates how much that learner gains from training on the author’s environments.

Our contributions are as follows.

1. 

Environment Duels is an agentic benchmark,
and a source of RL environments,
that refreshes itself:
because the models under evaluation write every environment,
its ceiling rises with the strongest author at the cost of tokens rather than human annotation.

2. 

Nine frontier models write,
solve and respond to one another’s environments,
and the resulting duel dynamics reflect how the wider model ecosystem behaves outside the duel.

3. 

A reinforcement-learning experiment shows that a learner’s hint lift on each author’s environments tracks how much training on them improves it on held-out benchmarks.

4. 

All environments,
the pipeline that generates them,
the solver trajectories and the analysis code will be released,
and Environment Duels will be updated as new models enter.

## 2 Methodology

In Environment Duels,
every evaluated model is both an author and a solver.
A designer writes an executable environment together with a privileged hint; every other model then plays it twice on identical instances,
once with the hint in its context and once without.
Measured outcomes then return to the designer,
which answers with one counter-move,
a revised environment shaped by how the other models played.
Figure 1 summarises the framework.
We describe construction (Section 2.1),
then the duel and its scores (Section 2.2).

## 2.1 Constructing an environment

Each designer is assigned a domain from a fixed set of five
(symbolic reasoning,
planning and optimization,
causal diagnosis,
adversarial strategy,
dynamic control).
The domain determines the mechanism the environment must exercise.
Within that domain the designer authors an environment that capable solvers should fail without the hint and pass with it,
working through four stages:

PlanImplementValidateReviewadmitted $\leq$ 2 $\times$ repair

## Plan.

Before writing any code,
the author produces a design specification,
the exact hint that solvers will receive,
and a private feasibility analysis:
a proposed hinted strategy with turn and resource accounting.
This analysis is written reasoning rather than an executable action sequence,
and neither it nor the plan is ever shown to solvers.
A dedicated planning call reserves the author’s full reasoning budget for task design:
working out the core difficulty,
the conditions under which the hint resolves it,
and an adversarial check against simple bypass strategies.
This keeps the design effort separate from implementation effort,
so that the environments are harder and better calibrated.

## Implement.

A second call receives the locked plan and hint and writes the environment source,
which must not reveal the hint or give away its strategic insight in any observation.
Formally,
an environment $e=(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{O},\mathcal{A},\mathrm{reset},\mathrm{step},T)$ has hidden states $\mathcal{X}$ ,
text observations $\mathcal{O}$ and actions $\mathcal{A}$ ,
and an action budget $T$ .
A seed $s$ fixes the instance,
each action advances it deterministically,
and a play succeeds when the environment ends it with positive reward within the budget:

 $\mathrm{reset}(s)=(x_{0},o_{0}),\quad\mathrm{step}(x_{t},a_{t})=(x_{t+1},o_{t+1},r_{t+1},d_{t+1}),\quad y=\mathbf{1}[\tau\leq T,\ r_{\tau}>0],$ 

with reward $r$ ,
termination flag $d$ and $\tau$ the first step with $d_{\tau}=1$ .
The solver never sees $x_{t}$ and acts on its history $(o_{0},a_{0},\dots,o_{t})$ ,
preceded by the hint $h$ in the hinted condition.
In code,
 $e$ is a single Python class exposing reset(seed) and step(action),
restricted to the standard library,
with deterministic replay and $T=24$ .
The implementation call focuses entirely on translating the plan into correct code;
all design decisions have already been made.

## Validate and review.

The pair $(e,h)$ must pass both a programmatic execution check and a self-review by the author in a fresh session before $e$ is admitted to the duel.
A programmatic execution check runs $e$ in an isolated sandbox
(no file-system or network access, fixed time and memory limits),
resets it on two seeds and plays a few probe actions to confirm that observations,
rewards and termination flags are well formed.
For the self-review, the author examines the anonymised $(e,h)$ in a fresh session for reachability within $T$ ,
instruction consistency,
information sufficiency and hint correctness. If the self-review rejects $(e,h)$ as invalid,
the workflow returns to planning:
the author revises the plan and hint before generating a fresh implementation.
After the duel runs,
we audit environment–seed instances for which no evaluated model succeeds in at least one hint condition.
Each flagged instance undergoes a white-box LLM audit with access to the environment source code and solver trajectories,
and a separate manual review.
We exclude flagged instances judged invalid by both audits from downstream metrics;
the other seeds of the same environment remain eligible.
Appendix A reports the audit procedure and results.

## Repair.

A failed execution check returns the error to the implementation call.
A review rejection returns to planning instead:
the author receives the verdict and the failing source,
revises the plan and hint,
and a fresh implementation is generated from the updated plan.
Each environment gets two such repairs.
If both fail,
the author discards the design and starts again from a new plan,
at most twice,
and an environment that still fails is dropped.
In practice the budget is rarely exhausted.
The full prompts for each stage are given in Appendix F.

## 2.2 The duel

The duel measures how well each model solves each environment,
with and without the author’s hint.
Let $\mathcal{M}$ be the set of models and $\mathcal{E}$ the set of environments,
with $\mathcal{E}_{a}\subseteq\mathcal{E}$ those authored by model $a$ and $a(i)$ the author of environment $i$ .
Every model $j\in\mathcal{M}$ plays every environment $i\in\mathcal{E}$ ,
including its own,
on the same $n$ seeds in two conditions:
without the hint ( $c=0$ ) and with it ( $c=h$ ).
The solver sees only the environment’s text observations,
never its code or any other play.
A play in condition $c$ has outcome $y^{c}$ as defined in Section 2.1.
The two conditions differ only in whether the hint is in the solver’s context,
so each seed yields a matched pair $(y^{0},y^{h})$ .

Let $k^{c}_{ij}$ be the number of successes of solver $j$ on environment $i$ out of $n$ seeds,
and $p^{c}_{ij}=k^{c}_{ij}/n$ .
The duel thus produces two matrices $K^{0}$ and $K^{h}$ ,
with one cell $(i,j)$ per environment–solver pair.
The author’s goal is a cell where $k^{0}_{ij}$ is low and $k^{h}_{ij}$ is high:
the task defeats capable solvers unless they have the hint.
We score models on three axes (Table 1),
each averaging a cell quantity over solvers and then over environments;
we report them separately and form no composite.
Every model is scored on every cell,
including those of its own environments.

## Hint lift

measures how much an author’s hints help.
Because the two conditions share environment,
seed and budget,
each seed gives a matched pair,
and the lift of a cell splits into the plays the hint fixes minus the plays it breaks:

 $p^{h}_{ij}-p^{0}_{ij}\;=\;\underbrace{\tfrac{1}{n}\,\#\{y^{0}{=}0,\,y^{h}{=}1\}}_{\text{fixed: fails without hint, solves with it}}\;-\;\underbrace{\tfrac{1}{n}\,\#\{y^{0}{=}1,\,y^{h}{=}0\}}_{\text{broken: solves without hint, fails with it}}\,.$ 

A hint can therefore score zero either by changing nothing or by breaking as many plays as it fixes.
Hint lift scores the author as a teacher;
a solver’s ability to use hints is reported separately as rescue $P(y^{h}{=}1\mid y^{0}{=}0)$ and harm $P(y^{h}{=}0\mid y^{0}{=}1)$ .

## Counter-move.

This is where authors and solvers co-evolve.
An author designs its environment blind;
once every play has returned,
it sees how the other models actually played and answers with one revised version.
Alongside anonymised outcome statistics,
it receives three matched trajectory pairs,
each showing one solver’s full attempt on one seed without and with the hint,
which reveal shortcuts the solvers found and hints they ignored or misread.
The author may change the environment,
the hint or both,
but must preserve the task,
interface,
reward contract,
action budget and seed-to-instance mapping,
so each seed poses the same instance before and after.
The revised version passes through the same construction stages,
is played again by every model in both conditions,
and is the version all reported scores use.

The counter-move is one round of co-evolution:
authors adapt to the solvers they face,
and a single round raises both difficulty and hint lift,
with the no-hint pass rate falling from 84.9% to 78.5%
and hint lift rising from 6.3% to 13.4%.
Authors can thus take feedback from other models and turn it into harder,
better-calibrated environments.
Such loops are increasingly common outside the duel.
A user who consults an LLM council sends one question to several models,
which review and rank one another’s answers before one of them writes the final response (Karpathy, 2025);
related systems let models debate or build on each other’s outputs (Du et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2025; Zhao et al., 2025b).
The counter-move isolates one such loop under controlled conditions;
Section 3.2 and Appendix E analyse its effect further.

## 3 Results

## 3.1 Experimental setup

We run environment duels with nine frontier models:
Claude-Opus-5 (Anthropic, 2026),
GPT-5.6-Sol (OpenAI, 2026b),
DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash (Xu et al., 2026a),
GLM-5.3 (Zeng et al., 2026a),
Kimi-K3 (Moonshot AI, 2026),
Inkling-Small (Thinking Machines Lab, 2026),
Qwen3.8-27B (Qwen Team, 2026),
Grok-4.6 (xAI, 2026)
and Gemini-3.8-Flash (Google DeepMind, 2026).
Every model call at every stage (planning,
implementation,
repair,
review,
solving and counter-move) runs inside that model’s own agent harness at the highest reasoning effort it supports. (Appendix B)
Each call starts in an isolated workspace containing only that task’s inputs,
and the agent keeps its native tool loop,
shell and context management,
so authoring and solving are measured under the agentic conditions in which these systems are deployed.

Each model authors ten environments,
two in each of the five domains,
for 90 environments in total.
Every solver plays every environment on four seeds,
with and without the hint,
under a 24-action budget,
both before and after the counter-move,
for 12,960 episodes in total.
Section 3.2 ranks the models as solvers,
authors, and teachers,
and Section 3.3 examines individual entries of the duel matrix,
which motivate the reinforcement-learning experiment of Section 4.
Appendix B lists each model’s harness version and thinking setting,
and Appendix F gives the prompts.

## CONCLUSION
Environment Duels has every model author executable environments with a privileged hint,
solve those of every other model,
and answer with a counter-move after watching how others play,
a round of co-evolution between authors and solvers.
Because the models under evaluation write the environments,
the benchmark rises with its strongest author rather than saturating.
Authoring and solving rank the models differently:
compact models write the hardest environments and the most useful hints,
hints help most when author and learner are alike,
and head-to-head duels give a stable ranking of their own.
A learner’s hint lift on an author’s environments anticipates how much training on them helps,
and the environments themselves show where models’ design habits converge.
Small as it is,
the duel is a microcosm of an ecosystem in which LLMs increasingly teach,
challenge and learn from one another,
and a place to watch that ecosystem change as new models enter.

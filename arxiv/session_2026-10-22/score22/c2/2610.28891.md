## ABSTRACT
Many social evaluations for multiagent systems study behaviors as they emerge from environments instead of systematically intervening in the environment to elicit such behaviors. We introduce Fig (Finding Interactions in Games), an agentic system that performs targeted elicitation interventions on game environments. It takes a natural language behavior description, decomposes it into sub-behaviors, generates new social games or selects existing ones, and executes single or combined interventions to the games' engine, opponents, instructions, and context. To evaluate model behavior, it combines built-in mechanical checks and synthetic rubric judgments. We validate the interventions, generated games, and evaluation mechanisms via human review. Models attempt the target behavior in 45.2% of episodes on average for valid scenarios generated by Fig for six test behaviors -- cooperation, exploitation, deception, covert coordination, betrayal, and sabotage. For game-behavior pairs with available controls, Fig's interventions increase attempt rates by 10.9% on average compared to matched control games. Fig shows that multiagent environments can serve as programmable test beds for intentional, controllable, and reproducible behavior evaluation. Code is available at https://github.com/fleet-ai/fig.

## 1 Introduction

Scaling behavioral evaluations and data for current multiagent systems can help create desirable properties in future ones. The behaviors in scope are: 1) positive behaviors: agents should become more prosocial, better cooperating and communicating with each other and with humans; 2) emergent behaviors: we should improve agents’ judgment in reaction to other agents’ actions by eliciting and studying behaviors such as covert coordination; 3) misbehaviors: observing instances of cheating, deception, and exploitation and studying these instances helps reduce them.

Figure 1: The Fig pipeline, with covert coordination as an example. Given the behavior input prompt, an LLM module decomposes the behavior and maps it to games and mechanisms. The implementation agent implements the scenario. Rubrics and mechanical checks make up the evaluation module defining attempt, execution, and success.

Game environments have been used to study multiagent behavior [8]. TextArena provides a unified game interface, although it is not amenable to behavioral evaluations that require counterfactual replays and controlled payoffs. Other game benchmarks target social deduction games like Avalon and Among Us [12, 20], study behavior in specific classes of games [15, 11], and create novel games to study interaction dynamics [21]. However, they are usually observational, and behaviors are treated as artifacts of specific environments instead of a result of controlled elicitation. Recently, CoopEval explored creating mechanisms for cooperation out of a few base dilemmas [25].

We introduce Fig, a unified game interface and agent behavior elicitor that is easily extensible to new classes of games. Fig provides:
1. A unified game abstraction for generating and importing games;
2. Principled interventions to incentivize different behaviors;
3. Programmable and responsive opponents;
4. Mechanical checks and rubric-judge verification for behavior detection.

Fig is also related to automated behavior evaluation systems such as Petri, Bloom, and Gram [6, 9, 17], which generate scenarios to elicit behaviors from a focal model and judging criteria to measure these behaviors. Automated auditing agents [2] further allow the evaluator to interactively probe a focal model. Fig applies a similar automated elicitation to multiagent game environments, where rules, incentives and other agent policies can be intervention targets.

Across a diverse catalog of multiagent games and behavioral targets, we show that Fig can automatically construct evaluations that elicit behaviors. Through component ablations, we further study which parts of the generation pipeline contribute to successful elicitation. Together, these results suggest that multiagent environments can become controllable evaluation substrates, enabling behavioral evaluations to be generated by systematically varying not only prompts and scenarios, but also interaction mechanisms.

## 2 Fig: An Agentic Elicitation Pipeline

Fig turns a natural language behavior description into mutated and new social games that create opportunities to exhibit that behavior. It provides a common intervention and measurement abstraction over heterogeneous social games, allowing the same behavioral target to be instantiated, manipulated, and evaluated across different environments. The components of the game abstraction are defined in Table 1.

Into this unified interface, we import a catalog of 135 text games (distribution in Appendix A.1, Fig. 6): 75 from TextArena [8], 21 from OpenSpiel [13], 21 from Social Gym [10], 15 Fig originals, and one each from AvalonBench [16], CICERO [19], and LLMafia [5]. 3 games (Claims Processing, Damage Claims, and Dispatch Accuracy) have independent payoffs and are thus not reflected in the plot, but they are retained for their potential for creating intervention variants. Social Gym has mostly competitive, communication-heavy multiplayer games, and heavily represent voting, elimination and hidden roles. OpenSpiel mostly has two-player simultaneous-choice matrix and bargaining games. TextArena has a wide range, including many word and puzzle games, but only 28% allow for communication. Fig originals are 15 classical mixed-motive games and their variants built around resource allocation, contracts and sanctions. We categorize these games into 103 families to account for variants of the same canonical game. For example, prisoners dilemma may have continuous, iterated, and three-player versions, multi-issue negotiation may have the base version and New Recruit as a variant.

There are 2 intentional exclusions:

1. We restrict our selection to text-based games and exclude primarily visual environments like Melting Pot [14] and Petting Zoo [24], because they burn too much agent token budget and only add value insofar as real-time simultaneous behavior, which is out of scope.

2. Purely cooperative games with shared goals and public information have been well-covered by other works including Overcooked-AI [3], ProAgent [30], and Collab-Overcooked [23]; for this study, we place less emphasis on them, hence the absence in that grid.

## 2.1 The Design Stage: Mapping Behavior to Interventions

## Terminology.

Game mechanics refers to the rules, incentives, and information structure of a game. Interventions are modifications to these mechanics or to the focal player’s context or opponents. Mechanism templates are semantically similar interventions based on the same underlying idea.

First, a prompted decomposer decomposes the natural language behavior into a working definition of that behavior and up to 15 sub-behaviors. Then a rubric generator generates criteria for these behaviors, describing the kind of evidence to look for in traces that counts towards the presence of that behavior. These rubrics would be used by the later-stage LLM judge in Section 2.3 to run on control and intervention traces and classify behavior exhibitions into binary labels for attempt, execution and success.

Secondly, for each working behavior definition, we also prompt GPT-5.6 Sol to generate multiplayer text games where the target behavior may be useful but not necessary. The generator does not see the existing catalog and is instructed not to repurpose a known game. The generated games then go through the same intervention design stages as the catalog games, and multiple interventions can come from one generated game.

Thirdly, a prompted game selector takes in the sub-behaviors and catalog, and outputs all games that it deems relevant, classifying them into relevance categories. As many games as needed are included from the first three categories below, and the candidates now include both generated and selected games.

• 

Direct fit: current gameplay already provides a substantive opportunity for the behavior.

• 

Parameter variation: adjusting existing payoff, resource, player-count, horizon, or other setup parameters can make the behavior salient without introducing a new intervention.

• 

Rule/engine mutation: changing or adding rules, rewards, observations, permitted actions, communication enforcement, or engine logic could elicit it.

• 

Insufficient evidence: the described mechanics are too incomplete to assess potential.

• 

Not relevant: no convincing way to elicit.

Then, the prompted intervention designer proposes interventions—changes to game mechanics, opponents, and/or context—to elicit and isolate the target behavior. Fig supports four intervention types:

• 

Engine-side intervention: changes to game state, parameters, rules, etc.

• 

Opponent-side intervention: changes to other opponents’ behavior to induce focal model reactions;

• 

Context-side intervention: changes to focal model’s context, including its initial instructions and role, available information, and messages inserted into, removed from, or edited in play history;

• 

Combined: a composition of above interventions.

We filter for interventions with confidence $\geq 0.7$ as a separate field reported by the intervention designer.

A further deduplication prompt sorts the candidate interventions into mechanism template descriptions, merging interventions from games that share a mechanism template and game family. Mechanism templates group interventions semantically based on the following criteria:

1. Opponent or engine arrangements create similar opportunities (e.g. a trusting opponent that plays based on verbal promises);

2. The focal player’s decisions are similar (e.g. late defection after building credibility);

3. The interventions’ mutated norms and control alternatives are similar.

For example, the family called explicit bilateral commitment then hidden pivot is defined as "a trusting counterpart conditions repeated simultaneous play on an explicit bilateral commitment, allowing the focal player to build credibility before secretly deviating in a later round…". It includes Battle of the Sexes, iterated Prisoner’s Dilemma, and iterated Stag Hunt.
In summary, the design stage turns a behavior description into pairs of games and interventions, specifying the opportunity and what norm the intervention mutates to distinguish model responses from normal gameplay. The next section covers how Fig translates the designs into runnable game variants with opponent controllers, and defines programmatic evidence for the presence or absence of the target behaviors.

## 2.2 Intervention Implementation

The implementation agent based on GPT-5.6 Sol translates designs into runnable game variants. The agent is given tools to explore and modify the given workspace, and writes the implemented variants represented by JSON objects encoding the game, target behavior, and entry point, with indices pointing to the actual code. We include a small efficiency optimization by scanning prior interventions for reuse opportunities.

Figure 2: Illustrative examples of mechanical vs rubric checks.

Then, the implementation agent also creates the mechanical evaluator, defining what it means for the behavior to be present, absent, or near-miss (the trace matches to a high degree, but the defining state transition does not happen). Similar game-specific evaluators have been used in CoopEval and ParliamentBench [25, 1]. While the latter hand-engineers metrics for a single social deduction environment, we automatically generate the evaluator and intervention-specific checks to adapt to each game variant. At evaluation time, this evaluator’s verdicts would be combined with the rubric judge from the design stage.

Finally, a validation module checks that:

1. The evidence rules are sound, the evaluator covers present / absent / boundary cases, and the code structure is up-to-date for the specific eval run;

2. Incentive positivity: filter out cases where normal rational play already wins.

3. The norms are stated clearly to the player in their initial briefing so that a target behavior is distinguishable from ordinary competent play.

## Opponent specification and control

Prior work has used opponents to influence a focal policy: ZSC-Eval trains a diverse population of reward-shaped partners to estimate zero-shot coordination [27], and RevengeBench probes opponents in code to reverse engineer a policy [22]. Fig’s automatically generated opponent programs serve as the treatment to a controlled elicitation comparison. The planner generates an opponent specification, containing the opponent’s role, objective, and policy, plus any custom responses and their intervention triggers. The opponent programs apply adaptive selection for the opponent controller:

• 

Programmatic control: all actions are implemented in code;

• 

LLM control: uses a language model as the opponent controller (with behavioral constraints) when the evaluation requires adaptive interaction, such as coordination with an unknown partner or emergent deception.

• 

Hybrid control: does a programmed action upon a trigger, but otherwise delegates the decisions to the LLM controller.

For example, if the target behavior is retaliation against a defection after some rounds of cooperation, the opponent program checks the game history and submits the defection action, and the hybrid module adapts any subsequent dialogue and play. This trigger is simply unreached if the focal player defects earlier, and the controller does not force a focal action.

We validate opponent realism by calibrating against 3 human reviewers, with the third one blindly adjudicating unresolved cases from the first two. With a fully scripted opponent controller, the reviewers marked only 13/30 episodes (43.3%) realistic. After adding the hybrid approach, they rate 29/30 episodes realistic (96.7%). The following experimental results use Claude Haiku 4.5 at 2,048 output tokens with temperature 0.7 as the opponent.

## 2.3 Behavior Evaluation Metrics

In the final stage, Fig automatically launches the evaluations. Players are prompted to "play to win the game". We tried a different
exploration prompt variant of "actively explore the environment
to find strategies and game mechanics that help you win", which
moderately increases observed attempts; since the exploration prompt does not significantly change the distribution shape of behaviors, the following sections only report
results under the "play to win" prompt. Players are not told that
they are being evaluated.

Behavioral targets are scored along three axes: whether the player attempted the behavior, whether it fully executed it, and whether the execution succeeded. An attempt does not necessarily lead to execution, and an execution does not necessarily succeed.

LLM judges apply the rubrics generated in the design stage to trajectories, and mechanical checks generated during the implmentation stage inspect game states such as reward and monitor fields. Both follow the same protocol and score all three axes. For trajectory $\tau_{i}$ , evaluator $e$ outputs one label per axis,

 $\ell_{ie}^{k}\in\{0,1,\mathrm{NA}\},\qquad k\in\{\mathrm{attempt},\mathrm{execution},\mathrm{success}\},$ 

where $\mathrm{NA}$ denotes insufficient evidence, or, for a mechanical check, that no check is defined for that axis.

Let $\mathcal{S}$ be the set of LLM judges and $\mathcal{M}$ the set of mechanical checks. For each channel $\mathcal{C}\in\{\mathcal{S},\mathcal{M}\}$ , we average its determinate labels:

 $C_{i}^{k}=\frac{1}{|D|}\sum_{e\in D}\ell_{ie}^{k},\qquad D=\{e\in\mathcal{C}:\ell_{ie}^{k}\neq\mathrm{NA}\},$ 

with $C_{i}^{k}=\mathrm{NA}$ when $D$ is empty. This gives a semantic score $S_{i}^{k}$ and a mechanical score $M_{i}^{k}$ .

# 2610.28909 — DiffResearch: Accelerating Deep Research with Diffusion Language Models
Authors: Alan-Barsag Gazzaev, Alexey Gavrilov, Mikhail Mozikov, Sergey Muravyov
Comment: Accepted to EMNLP 2026 Industry Track
Categories: cs.AI

## ABSTRACT
Deep research systems iteratively plan, gather evidence, synthesize, and revise, making end-to-end latency a key bottleneck for interactive use. Almost all production systems are built on autoregressive (AR) language models and inherit left-to-right decoding in planning and long-form writing. We present DiffResearch, an open multi-agent deep research framework that places a diffusion language model (dLLM) at the core of every stage, and measure how a diffusion backbone changes latency and report quality. Holding the agentic scaffold, retrieval code, prompts, and evaluation fixed, we swap the backbone between an open diffusion model and a comparable AR model. Across 100 Deep Research Bench tasks, the diffusion backbone is roughly 6 times faster end-to-end, driven by order-of-magnitude reductions in planning and writing. We further introduce dFast, a decomposition mode that reads intermediate denoising states and extracts subqueries before generation finishes, providing a 1.99 times median speedup for decomposition in isolation with no loss of report quality. On the benchmark's RACE rubric, dFast obtains the best overall score among the three configurations. Finally, in a public system-level leaderboard snapshot, an instance built on a stronger diffusion model ranks above several commercial systems. We release DiffResearch under an Apache-2.0 license.

## 1 Introduction

The past two years have seen deep research (DR) systems emerge as a flagship application of large language models (LLMs). Commercial products such as OpenAI Deep Research (OpenAI, 2025), Gemini Deep Research (Citron, 2024), and Perplexity Deep Research (Perplexity, 2025), alongside a growing body of open-source frameworks (The LangChain Team, 2025; Zheng et al., 2025), autonomously orchestrate multi-step web exploration, targeted retrieval, and higher-order synthesis to turn large amounts of online information into citation-rich reports. Among the tasks these systems perform, scientific literature review is one of the most demanding: a review requires balanced thematic coverage, faithful attribution, the identification of competing methodological camps, and a coherent narrative that situates each work within the broader landscape. As Deep Research Bench (Du et al., 2026) and similar evaluations (Coelho et al., 2026; Yifei et al., 2026) have shown, the strongest agents now approach the quality of human analysts on PhD-level tasks across dozens of fields.

Despite this progress, almost all production DR agents share one architectural backbone: they are built on autoregressive LLMs (Brown et al., 2020; Touvron et al., 2023; OpenAI, 2023) and inherit the same left-to-right, single-pass decoding that governs their token generators. This shapes both how reviews are written and how fast they are produced. A writing agent drafts each section sequentially, with limited ability to revise earlier passages once new evidence arrives later, so that errors in early structural decisions can propagate through the rest of the review. The same sequential bottleneck constrains the orchestration layer: when an agent must emit several search queries to fan out retrieval over distinct facets of the literature, autoregressive decoding produces them one token at a time, and downstream retrieval is dispatched only after generation completes. For an interactive research assistant, every one of these stages is on the critical path, and their latencies add up.

Diffusion language models (Li et al., 2022; Austin et al., 2021; Lou et al., 2024) offer a complementary generation paradigm: text is refined through iterative denoising that updates an entire sequence in parallel across a small number of steps. Recent systems such as LLaDA (Nie et al., 2025), Dream (Ye et al., 2025), and Mercury (Inception Labs et al., 2025) suggest that diffusion LLMs can approach the quality of autoregressive models while offering advantages in generation speed and global coherence. Yet diffusion LLMs have largely been evaluated in standalone settings such as chat and coding; their integration into agentic pipelines that interleave planning, retrieval, and iterative refinement remains underexplored, and the practical latency consequences of that integration have not been measured directly.

## 2 Related Work

## Deep research agents.

Commercial systems (OpenAI, 2025; Citron, 2024; Perplexity, 2025) and open-source frameworks (The LangChain Team, 2025; Zheng et al., 2025) integrate multi-step reasoning, retrieval, and synthesis to produce long-form, citation-grounded outputs. Benchmarks such as Deep Research Bench (Du et al., 2026), DeepResearchGym (Coelho et al., 2026), and ResearchQA (Yifei et al., 2026) evaluate these systems on complex, multi-domain tasks. Most rely on autoregressive LLMs (Brown et al., 2020; Touvron et al., 2023; OpenAI, 2023) and inherit the constraints of sequential decoding. Prior work has explored agentic scaffolds that decompose tasks into modular planning, retrieval, and evaluation components (Wang et al., 2024; Xi et al., 2025), judge models that assess output quality (Zheng et al., 2023), and retrieval-augmented generation to ground outputs in external sources (Lewis et al., 2020; Gao et al., 2023b).

## Diffusion language models.

Diffusion-based text models (Li et al., 2022; Austin et al., 2021; Lou et al., 2024) generate through iterative denoising, refining an entire sequence in parallel across a few steps, and naturally support structure-first generation and iterative error correction (Li et al., 2022; Gong et al., 2023). Recent open models such as LLaDA (Nie et al., 2025) and Dream (Ye et al., 2025), and the Mercury family (Inception Labs et al., 2025), show that diffusion LLMs can approach autoregressive quality while offering speed and global-coherence advantages. To our knowledge, no prior work has measured how diffusion decoding affects the end-to-end latency and report quality of an agentic deep research pipeline under a controlled backbone swap.

## Efficient generation.

A large literature improves generation efficiency through speculative decoding, caching, distillation, and parallel decoding. Our fast decomposition mode is complementary and is not speculative decoding: it does not use a draft model, but instead reads the diffusion model’s own intermediate denoising states and stops once a valid structured output is available.

## 3 System Design

## 3.1 Multi-Agent Diffusion Pipeline

DiffResearch is a multi-agent framework in which every agent is an instance of a diffusion LLM. The system is organized as a small, explicit pipeline rather than a monolithic chain; we deliberately avoid heavy orchestration abstractions (Chase, 2022) that obscure data flow and make latency budgets and retry behavior hard to reason about. Each agent is a Python class that inherits from a common backbone and implements a single generate method. The backbone is interchangeable: a Hugging Face diffusion runtime serving google/diffusiongemma-26B-A4B-it for diffusion-native deployment, or a Hugging Face autoregressive runtime serving google/gemma-4-26B-A4B-it for the baseline. The controlled comparison in Section 5 relies on this: changing the backbone leaves every prompt, retrieval call, and evaluation step unchanged.

Nine agents cover query formatting, planning, plan-checking, relevance filtering, information extraction, summarization, complexity assessment, query decomposition, and judging. Outputs are kept minimal and machine-parseable: binary flags for routing decisions, newline-separated query lists from the decomposition and judge agents, and free-form text only where downstream consumption requires it. Per-agent decoding budgets are exposed through the constructor; for example, the relevance and complexity agents use a short budget appropriate to their binary outputs, while the summarization agent uses a substantially larger budget to accommodate full review drafts.

The base pipeline has five core stages (intent classification, query reformulation, planning, retrieval, and writing), with judging as an optional sixth stage. The intent agent routes a query to a web channel or an academic channel and emits a label with a logged-but-not-propagated rationale. The reformulation agent rewrites the query into retrieval-friendly search strings (Wang et al., 2023; Jagerman et al., 2023) while preserving the original information need verbatim. The planning agent (Wei et al., 2022; Yao et al., 2023) produces a structured outline: main sections, two-to-four sub-points each, and an explicit list of key concepts to cover. The outline serves both as the writing target and as the reference for plan-checking. The writing agent drafts the review conditioned on the plan and the retrieved corpus through diffusion-style refinement. The judge (Zheng et al., 2023) compares the review against the plan and corpus along plan adherence, citation faithfulness (Gao et al., 2023a; Liu et al., 2023), and coverage completeness.

## 3.2 Retrieval Backends

Retrieval grounds every claim and is treated as a first-class component. The web channel issues queries through the Serper API (Serper, n.d.) and extracts page content into plain text. The academic channel issues parallel calls to the Semantic Scholar API (Kinney et al., 2023), for broad peer-reviewed coverage and citation-graph signals, and the arXiv API (arXiv, n.d.), for timely preprints; results are deduplicated by DOI and arXiv identifier and merged into a single ranked list. In all cases retrieval is performed by deterministic API calls with no LLM in the loop, which keeps it fast, cacheable, and easy to ablate. For the controlled benchmark runs in this paper, retrieval is restricted to the Semantic Scholar channel with results_per_query $=2$ , so these parallel academic calls are not exercised; retrieval is then a single rate-limited source, identical across backbones.

## 3.3 Model Serving and Decomposition

Both models are run through Hugging Face runtimes. The same pipeline code drives all three configurations (diffusion full, diffusion fast, autoregressive), and only the backbone and the decompose mode change. The decompose call accepts a mode of full or fast, and its output is accepted only when it parses as JSON with exactly the requested number of non-empty subqueries, which gives both modes the same correctness criterion.

## 3.4 Operating Modes

## Full mode.

Full mode is ordinary diffusion generation. The decomposition prompt is built and the model is run to completion; the final text is parsed as JSON and accepted if it contains exactly the requested number of subqueries. This is the correctness-oriented configuration.

## Fast mode.

Fast mode extracts the decomposition before final generation completes. The same prompt is issued, but a custom streamer inspects each intermediate denoising state: the draft is decoded, stripped of thinking-channel artifacts, scanned for a JSON object containing a subqueries list, and accepted as soon as a valid exact-count object appears. When early stopping is enabled, the streamer raises an exception that the decompose routine catches and returns the early subqueries instead of waiting for the final pass. This is not speculative decoding: the model is unchanged, and the only difference is that we read its own intermediate denoised states. The tradeoff is that an early draft may contain duplicated fragments or rough wording, but for retrieval, topical coverage matters more than exact phrasing.

## 4 Experimental Setup

We evaluate on Deep Research Bench (Du et al., 2026), a benchmark of 100 PhD-level research tasks (50 Chinese, 50 English) spanning 22 fields, calibrated against a statistical analysis of over 96,000 real-world user queries. We follow the benchmark’s official RACE evaluation framework, which scores reports along comprehensiveness, insight, instruction-following, and readability, and reports an aggregate overall score; here RACE is run with cleaning, using Gemini 2.5 Pro as the evaluator. RACE scores in our controlled runs are reported on a $0$ – $1$ scale.

For the controlled comparison we use two backbones of comparable size in the same pipeline: the diffusion model google/diffusiongemma-26B-A4B-it (denoted dFull and dFast for its full and fast decomposition modes) and the autoregressive model google/gemma-4-26B-A4B-it (AR). Both are run through Hugging Face runtimes on an H200 pod, with retrieval performed locally. All three configurations share the same agent code, retrieval (Semantic Scholar only, subquery_count $=4$ , results_per_query $=2$ , one request per second, per-request timeout), and RACE evaluation; only the generator backbone and the decompose mode differ. AR uses standard left-to-right decoding with no draft-state stream. We additionally report a separate isolated study of the decomposition stage on 100 synthetic-but-realistic research queries, instrumented directly through the diffusion streamer with model loading excluded from all timings.

We additionally analyze the stored run artifacts without regenerating reports, repeating retrieval, or calling the evaluator. For every successful report we measure total non-whitespace characters, numeric inline citations, and Markdown content headings, and split these diagnostics and saved RACE scores by language. We also conduct a configuration-blinded manual source-grounded audit of 10 reports from common-success tasks (5 English, 5 Chinese; dFull $n=3$ , dFast $n=3$ , AR $n=4$ after unblinding). Prompt requirements are fixed before reading reports; five material claims per report and all inline citation mentions are checked against the exact retrieved evidence available to the writer. This diagnostic is not expert human evaluation or an open-world factuality test.

## LIMITATIONS SECTION
The end-to-end speedups are measured with the model served on an H200 pod and retrieval kept local. On hardware or model configurations where local diffusion inference becomes the bottleneck, or where retrieval cost is small relative to generation, the absolute latency gap will differ; we have not benchmarked under tight consumer-grade latency budgets. Fast mode is deliberately scoped to decomposition and does not reduce observed end-to-end latency relative to dFull in the current setup: the first exact-count draft rarely matches the final string exactly (1 of 100 in our probe), so it is appropriate only where downstream consumers tolerate wording noise, as retrieval does, and not for the final answer. The controlled comparison uses two open models of comparable size; while it holds the scaffold, prompts, retrieval, and evaluation fixed, it is a single backbone pair, and a broader sweep across model families and sizes would strengthen the attribution. Finally, the leaderboard comparison is against full systems rather than writer-isolated variants and reflects a snapshot in time; it should be read as a system-level result, not a causal attribution to diffusion decoding alone. Retrieval in these runs used Semantic Scholar only, which for some benchmark tasks did not return the sources the task needed; the academic channel can also use arXiv and the web channel, but both were disabled here. Parallel retrieval dispatch, which single-pass subquery generation enables in principle, is not evaluated here because retrieval is rate-limited to one request per second; quantifying it under a higher-throughput retrieval backend is left to future work.

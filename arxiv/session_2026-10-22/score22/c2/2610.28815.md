# 2610.28815 — Forging More Tasks into Better Harnesses: Agentic Harness Evolution via Successive Scale Racing
Authors: Jingzhe Liu, Jiliang Tang, Tong Zhao, Neil Shah, Clark Mingxuan Ju
Comment: 
Categories: cs.AI

## ABSTRACT
Agentic harness evolution optimizes an agent's instructions, tools, and control flow by using its task execution experiences as evolving evidence. While more evolution tasks should intuitively improve performance, our preliminary study reveals a paradoxical failure mode: the performance of existing methods degrades as the task pool expands. Our further analysis demonstrates that this degradation stems from suboptimal evidence selection during optimization, as different evidence combinations lead to substantially different evolution outcomes. To address this issue, we propose Successive Scale Racing (SSR), a framework that adaptively searches for optimal evidence combinations in each evolution round. SSR constructs diverse evidence combinations based on the corresponding task quality and coverage, and efficiently races the resulting candidate harnesses under a small compute budget. Empirically, across three distinct benchmarks, SSR achieves state-of-the-art results in all six configurations. Notably, as the task pool grows from 40 to 200, SSR steadily improves the pass rate from 64.3% to 68.3%, while the baselines' performances all drop. Furthermore, its racing mechanism identifies near-optimal candidates while reducing validation executions by 60%.

## 1 Introduction

An agent harness governs a language model’s interaction with its environment through instructions, tool interfaces, context management, and control flow (Ning et al., 2026; Pan et al., 2026a). By determining what the model observes, remembers, and can do (Packer et al., 2023), it shapes how model capabilities are used on real-world tasks. Harness engineering has relied heavily on human design and implementation, such as manually refined interfaces (Yang et al., 2024) and coordination protocols (Hong et al., 2023; Qian et al., 2023). Recent work automates this process, evolving agent designs and workflows from execution experience while keeping model weights fixed (Hu et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2024a; Zhang et al., 2026).

Current frameworks for agentic harness evolution commonly follow an execution–reflection–optimization paradigm (Lee et al., 2026; Lin et al., 2026; Wang et al., 2026; Liu et al., 2026). The agent first solves assigned tasks, producing execution trajectories. An agent acting as the optimizer then distills lessons, which we refer to as evidence, from these trajectories, and uses them to guide improvements to the harness. This process repeats until a stopping criterion is met or the execution budget is exhausted.

Figure 1: Many existing methods evolve on large, fixed task pools, which can induce noisy or redundant signals. SSR searches over ordered evidence groups.

In machine learning, expanding training task pools generally improves generalization and system capabilities (Kaplan et al., 2020; Hoffmann et al., 2022). However, whether agentic harness evolution similarly benefits from larger task pools remains underexplored.
To address this gap, we first evaluate existing state-of-the-art methods (Lin et al., 2026; Wang et al., 2026; Liu et al., 2026; Pan et al., 2026b) by enlarging the set of training tasks used for evolution. Surprisingly, our preliminary study in Section 3 shows that larger task pools do not consistently yield better harnesses with existing methods. AHE (Lin et al., 2026), for example, initially improves but drops from 63.0% at 80 tasks to 57.7% at 200 tasks. Based on this observation, we pose our core research question:

How can we leverage experience from more tasks to evolve better harnesses?

To provide further insights into this problem, we investigate how different trajectory selections impact harness updates. We find that a set of
tasks selected by a simple LLM judge can generate better harnesses than either an equally sized alternative subset or the much larger full task pool.
This reveals a key insight: more task experiences, while providing more information, might also introduce noise or redundant signals. Feeding all trajectories into the optimizer could blur the focus of individual modifications. Thus, harness performance depends not on the total volume of available evidence, but on selecting the appropriate combinations of evidence for each evolution step.

However, searching for effective evidence combinations presents two key challenges: (1) The space of possible trajectory combinations is combinatorial. Generating every candidate harness and thoroughly evaluating each on validation tasks is computationally prohibitive. (2) The ideal evidence scale is not static; it shifts as the parent harness evolves and handles tasks differently across iterations.

To address these challenges, we propose Successive Scale Racing (SSR) (Figure 1), a framework that adaptively searches over evidence combinations under a fixed execution budget. SSR addresses the two challenges in the following ways: (1) Instead of enumerating combinatorial subsets, SSR introduces Representative Evidence Ranking (RER) to order task evidence by quality and coverage. It generates candidates from a selected set of evidence combinations based on the rankings. To minimize validation costs, SSR employs a cumulative performance racing mechanism that evaluates candidates on shared task batches and progressively prunes weaker candidates early. (2) In every evolution round, SSR refreshes execution trajectories based on the incumbent harness and dynamically re-ranks evidence subsets, allowing the winning evidence combination to adaptively shift as the harness evolves.

We evaluate SSR on SWE-Bench Pro Verified, DeepSWE, and GAIA-2 (Zheng et al., 2026; Huang et al., 2026; Froger et al., 2026) across two execution setups. SSR achieves top performance and exhibits positive scaling, raising accuracy from 64.3% to 68.3% as the task pool expands from 40 to 200 tasks.
Our main contributions are threefold: (1) We identify noisy and redundant evidence signals as a primary cause of performance degradation when scaling task pools in harness evolution. (2) We propose SSR, which effectively searches over evidence scales using quality-coverage ranking and performance racing. (3) Across three benchmarks under two execution setups, SSR leads in all six settings and benefits from larger task pools, achieving higher efficiency and performance.

## 2 Related Work

## 2.1 Agent Harness Engineering

Harness design encompasses reasoning–action loops (Yao et al., 2022), multi-agent conversation (Wu et al., 2023), sandboxed execution (Wang et al., 2024), and prescribed repair workflows (Xia et al., 2024). Automated optimization spans instruction search (Zhou et al., 2023; Yang et al., 2023), feedback-guided prompt revision (Pryzant et al., 2023; Agrawal et al., 2025), pipeline compilation (Khattab et al., 2023), and textual feedback propagation (Yuksekgonul et al., 2024). Beyond prompts, GPTSwarm optimizes computational graphs (Zhuge et al., 2024), AgentSquare evolves and recombines modules (Shang et al., 2024), and AgentOptimizer updates tool functions (Zhang et al., 2024b).

## 2.2 Self-Evolving Agents and Harnesses

Experience can improve outputs through self-feedback (Madaan et al., 2023), later attempts through verbal reflection (Shinn et al., 2023), and future tasks through reusable skills (Wang et al., 2023), extracted insights (Zhao et al., 2023), contextual guidelines (Fu et al., 2024), instruction manuals (Chen et al., 2024), reasoning memory (Ouyang et al., 2025), or evolving playbooks (Zhang et al., 2025). Code-level evolution includes SICA’s self-edits (Robeyns et al., 2025), DGM’s archive (Zhang et al., 2026), HGM’s descendant-based search (Wang et al., 2026), and MGM’s cross-task and cross-lineage comparisons (Liu et al., 2026). AHE structures trajectory evidence (Lin et al., 2026), while Meta-Harness exposes historical code, scores, and traces (Lee et al., 2026). Retro selects a challenging, diverse coreset and uses self-preference for retrospective optimization (Pan et al., 2026b).

## 3 Preliminary Study

In this preliminary study, we investigate how task-pool size and evidence use affect harness evolution. To start with, we conduct experiments on SWE-Pro-V with the Codex setting, as in the analyses of Section 6. We report final Avg Pass@1 (%) averaged over three independent runs. Section 6.4 extends the evidence comparison to DeepSWE.

## 3.1 Effect of Task-Pool Size

We vary the training task pool from 40 to 200 tasks across Retro, AHE, HGM, and MGM. Contrary to intuition, none of these methods consistently benefits from larger pools (Figure 3). For instance, AHE peaks at 63.0% accuracy with 80 tasks, but degrades to 57.7% with 200 tasks.

## Observation 1.

Expanding the task pool does not necessarily produce a better harness under existing evolution strategies, and can even degrade performance.

## 3.2 Effect of Evidence Configuration

To understand this degradation, we analyze how different evidence selections impact optimization starting from the same harness (Figure 3). An LLM judge ranks experience records from the 160-task pool into Top 40 and Bottom 40 subsets. Top 40 outperforms All 160 by 3.4% and Bottom 40 by 5.4%, showing that evidence quality and scale matter significantly. Conversely, a randomly sampled 40-record subset from the 80-task pool trails All 80 by 1.3%, confirming that unguided scale reduction is unhelpful.

## Observation 2.

Evolution outcomes depend on the evidence combinations (from task trajectories) the optimizer uses; a well-selected subset can outperform full-pool evidence.

## 4 Successive Scale Racing

In this section, we introduce Successive Scale Racing (SSR). As illustrated in Figure 4, our framework operates in four key stages: (1) Representative Evidence Ranking (RER), which orders experience records by quality and coverage; (2) Multiscale Candidate Generation, which independently modifies a shared parent harness using nested evidence prefixes generated from the ranking; (3) Candidate Performance Racing, which progressively prunes weaker candidates using cumulative scores on shared validation batches; and (4) Recursive Evolution, which updates the parent harness and refreshes execution evidence for subsequent rounds.

## Notation.

Let $H_{t}$ denote the incumbent harness at round $t$ , and let $\mathcal{T}$ and $\mathcal{V}$ be disjoint training and validation task sets. Training tasks supply optimization evidence; validation tasks are held out from that evidence and contribute to candidate selection. $N=|\mathcal{T}|$ denotes the available evidence-pool size, $k$ the number of task records supplied to one candidate, and $M$ the number of candidates in a round.

## 4.1 Representative Evidence Ranking

An effective evidence set must offer actionable and transferable insights without overwhelming the optimizer with redundant or low-value execution logs. To achieve this balance, Representative Evidence Ranking (RER) establishes a global ordering of experience records that jointly optimizes individual evidence quality and collective pool coverage.

## Experience characterization.

For a given parent harness $H_{t}$ , we execute it on each training task $i\in\{1,\ldots,N\}$ to collect one execution trajectory $\tau_{i}$ . An LLM judge distills each trajectory $\tau_{i}$ along with its feedback into a structured experience record $e_{i}$ , which comprises an executive summary and concrete harness improvement suggestions.

Formally, for each record $e_{i}$ , the judge outputs three normalized metrics in $[0,1]$ : experience importance $w_{i}$ , actionability $a_{i}$ , and confidence $u_{i}$ . Here, $w_{i}$ quantifies the generalizability of the experience beyond its originating task; $a_{i}$ measures the concreteness of the proposed harness modifications; and $u_{i}$ reflects the judge’s assessment reliability. We define the intrinsic record quality as $q_{i}=a_{i}u_{i}$ , ensuring a high score only when a concrete modification is proposed with high confidence. The importance weight $w_{i}$ is utilized separately to parameterize coverage over the evidence pool (Eq. 2).

Because trajectories from different tasks can reveal overlapping failure modes or identical improvement directions, we explicitly model pairwise evidence redundancy. Specifically, each record $e_{i}$ is mapped to a feature embedding $z_{i}$ by encoding its summary and extracted insights. We quantify the nonnegative pairwise similarity between records $e_{i}$ and $e_{j}$ as:

 $s_{ij}=\max\{0,\cos(z_{i},z_{j})\}.$ 

(1)

This similarity metric $s_{ij}$ directly feeds into the coverage objective to measure the extent to which a selected evidence subset represents the complete trajectory pool.

## Coverage with diminishing returns.

For a selected set $Y\subseteq\{1,\ldots,N\}$ and each record $j$ in the pool, let $C_{j}(Y)$ be the quality-weighted similarity of the selected records to $e_{j}$ , that is, how well $Y$ covers it. The objective $F(Y)$ sums the log-coverage of all records, weighted by importance:

 $\displaystyle C_{j}(Y)$ 
 $\displaystyle=\sum_{i\in Y}q_{i}s_{ij},$ 

 $\displaystyle F(Y)$ 
 $\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{N}w_{j}\log\bigl(1+C_{j}(Y)\bigr).$ 

(2)

The logarithm gives diminishing returns: a record similar to already-covered ones raises $F$ by less, leaving room for other important experiences. $F$ is monotone and submodular (Proposition 1), which the greedy ordering below exploits.

Starting from $Y_{0}=\varnothing$ , RER repeatedly selects the record with the largest marginal coverage gain:

 $\displaystyle\pi_{r}$ 
 $\displaystyle\in\arg\max_{i\notin Y_{r-1}}\bigl[F(Y_{r-1}\cup\{i\})-F(Y_{r-1})\bigr],$ 

 $\displaystyle Y_{r}$ 
 $\displaystyle=Y_{r-1}\cup\{\pi_{r}\}.$ 

(3)

The resulting permutation $\pi=(\pi_{1},\ldots,\pi_{N})$ defines every evidence prefix. Marginal gains are recomputed after each selection; this is not a static sort by individual quality.

## 4.2 Multiscale Candidate Generation

Because the optimal evidence scale is unknown a priori and shifts as evolution proceeds (Appendix E), SSR treats evidence scale as a per-round search variable. Given a set of predefined scale budgets $\mathcal{S}=\{k_{1},\ldots,k_{M}\}$ ( $1\leq k_{1}<\cdots<k_{M}=N$ ), we construct nested evidence prefixes from the ranking $\pi$ :

 $D_{t,k}=\{e_{\pi_{1}},\ldots,e_{\pi_{k}}\},\qquad k\in\mathcal{S}.$ 

(4)

Using nested prefixes rather than disjoint subsets offers distinct advantages. First, every prefix $D_{t,k}$ provides near-optimal coverage at its length, guaranteeing a $(1-1/e)$ approximation factor (Proposition 1; Feige, 1998). Second, nested structures isolate scale as the sole variable across candidates, preventing confounding between subset composition and evidence volume (Section 6.4). Third, the shared evidence core facilitates effective validation batching during performance racing (Section 4.3).

For each scale $k\in\mathcal{S}$ , the optimizer independently applies edits to the same frozen parent harness $H_{t}$ :

 $H_{t}^{(k)}=\operatorname{Optimize}(H_{t},D_{t,k}).$ 

(5)

The optimizer receives structured experience summaries and improvement suggestions from $D_{t,k}$ , with optional access to raw trajectories. All candidate harnesses $H_{t}^{(k)}$ are generated under identical optimizer prompts and permissions, serving as temporary hypotheses to be evaluated in the subsequent racing phase.

## 4.3 Candidate Performance Racing

Evaluating every proposed harness on every task would spend substantial execution budget on weak candidates. This is a fixed-budget best-arm identification problem (Audibert et al., 2010; Karnin et al., 2013): each harness is an arm, each task execution is a pull, and the goal is to return the best harness within the budget. SSR follows the successive-halving schedule (Karnin et al., 2013; Jamieson and Talwalkar, 2016) with a constant elimination quota, and protects the parent as an incumbent. SSR introduces new tasks over $K$ stages and directs later evaluations to surviving candidates. The parent remains active throughout as a common reference and is never included in the child-elimination quota. Evaluation continues across stages even when only one child remains.

 $B_{r}=B_{r}^{\mathrm{val}}\cup B_{r}^{\mathrm{succ}}\cup B_{r}^{\mathrm{fail}}.$ 

(6)

The validation component is sampled randomly without replacement from $\mathcal{V}$ . The other components contain training tasks on which the parent succeeded or failed, respectively, with these labels fixed within the round. They favor high-ranked tasks shared by the surviving candidates’ evidence prefixes, supplementing validation with checks of retained behavior and repair on familiar tasks. ## LIMITATIONS SECTION
While Successive Scale Racing (SSR) demonstrates promising results in scaling agentic harness evolution, several limitations warrant further investigation. First, the performance degradation observed when expanding training task pools is a multifaceted phenomenon. While our study focuses on noisy, conflicting, and redundant evidence as a primary underlying driver, other systemic factors may also contribute to this failure mode. Second, searching over evidence scales via SSR represents an initial step toward mitigating this scaling bottleneck. Alternative paradigms, such as adaptive evidence filtering via dynamic context pruning, fine-grained trajectory credit assignment, or reinforcement learning-based optimizer tuning, remain promising unexplored avenues for addressing noisy feedback during evolution. Third, our empirical evaluation, though conducted across multiple benchmarks and execution setups, is bounded by the specific choice of foundation models used as task agents and optimizers. Further validation is needed to determine how well these findings generalize to a broader spectrum of model architectures, such as open-weight models of varying scales or domain-specialized language models.

## CONCLUSION
We propose Successive Scale Racing (SSR) to address performance degradation when scaling task pools in harness evolution. SSR ranks task experience by quality and coverage, generates multiscale candidate harnesses, and efficiently races them under a fixed compute budget. Across three benchmarks and two execution setups, SSR achieves top performance in all six settings and enables positive task-pool scaling. Furthermore, its racing mechanism identifies near-optimal candidates while cutting validation executions by 60%, confirming that evidence-driven candidate diversity and execution-based selection provide a robust paradigm for agent self-improvement.

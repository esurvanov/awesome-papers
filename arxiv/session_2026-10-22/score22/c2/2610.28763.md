## ABSTRACT
Frontier coding agents are increasingly framed as end-to-end automated researchers, and ML for science offers a demanding test of that promise: improving a scientific model often requires domain insight beyond routine ML engineering. We introduce Agent4Sci, a long-horizon testbed for measuring how far today's agents can improve the published best models on scientific ML benchmarks, spanning 20 benchmarks across 11 domains. Rather than ranking agents on a leaderboard, Agent4Sci asks what kinds of progress agents can make on these tasks under in-the-wild conditions, and where they fall short. Empirically, today's frontier agents advance the published best on every benchmark, with Claude Opus reaching 10.4% mean relative improvement and no run flagged for academic misconduct. These gains, however, come almost entirely from a bag of ML-engineering tricks rather than research-level insight. Trajectory analysis surfaces three recurring limitations in this setting: novelty miscalibration, resource under-utilization, and rapid collapse into local engineering sweeps. Lightweight plug-in skills for research ideation, experiment management, and scheduled checklists raise Claude Opus from 10.4% to 20.1% and Codex from 4.4% to 8.1%, while yielding more diverse, domain-relevant interventions. Agent4Sci gives the community a way to track and push frontier agents from ML engineering toward domain insight on real-world ML-for-science problems. Code and data are available on GitHub (https://github.com/maszhongming/agent4sci) and Hugging Face (https://huggingface.co/datasets/MingZhong/agent4sci).

## 1 Introduction

Recent advances in frontier coding agents are turning automated research into an executable workflow: agents can read papers and repositories, modify implementations, run experiments, interpret quantitative feedback, and iterate. A growing body of recent work suggests a common recipe for this vision: cast research as a feedback loop in which an agent operates in a workspace, pursues a measurable objective, and improves through experimentation [50, 38, 28, 62, 23]. Yet most demonstrations so far assume well-structured codebases [19, 36, 61], operate in single-task settings [50, 38, 23], and are heavily skewed toward computer science domains [40, 56, 13]. The broader landscape of ML for science remains far less explored: benchmarks span diverse scientific domains and modalities, codebases are often research artifacts rather than polished evaluation environments, and nontrivial gains often require domain insights. This gap motivates our central question: Can the frontier agents we have today make meaningful progress on ML for science when operating in the wild?

We instantiate this question as automated scientific hill-climbing (Figure 1). In this setting, an agent is given a scientific ML benchmark, its paper, codebase, data, and target metric. Starting from the best available model in the benchmark, the agent works under a fixed resource budget to improve the metrics through code changes and experiments. However, our pilot study shows that this natural setup is fragile. Across 12 tasks from 5 NeurIPS 2025 benchmarks, 5 tasks exhibit severe evaluation-gaming behaviors, including selective reporting, test-set contamination, metric manipulation, and even direct hardcoding. This aligns with recent work showing that coding agents succeed in well-structured evaluation settings but degrade sharply on real-world code and evaluations [10]. These failures motivate a testbed that preserves the in-the-wild nature of scientific tasks, makes meaningful progress measurable, and guards against evaluation gaming.

To address this challenge, we construct Agent4Sci, a testbed for evaluating automated scientific hill-climbing across 20 ML-for-science benchmarks, 25 tasks, and 11 scientific domains spanning the physical sciences, life sciences, Earth and environmental sciences, and scientific engineering. We first standardize each benchmark through human-verified baseline reproductions, repaired data splits, reduced runtime bottlenecks, and isolated environments. On top of this foundation, each run follows the Agent4Sci evaluation protocol. The hill-climbing agent receives the standardized environment with access only to training and validation data, then submits artifacts such as code changes, checkpoints, logs, and outputs. A separate evaluation agent runs the submitted artifacts on the held-out test set, and an auditing agent inspects the trajectory for academic misconduct.

Agent4Sci is motivated not as a leaderboard for ranking agents, but as a way to ask how much measurable progress agents can make on scientific ML tasks, whether they produce insight, and where they fall short. We evaluate four frontier coding agents: Claude Code (Opus and Sonnet), Codex, and Gemini CLI. Each receives 8 H200 GPUs, a 12-hour budget, and a minimal instruction to hill-climb the specified metric. Across the suite, frontier agents push every benchmark beyond its published best, all four agents achieve positive mean improvements, and the auditing stage flags no academic misconduct under our evaluation protocol. On Agent4Sci, current agents already produce measurable, fully automated gains on real scientific ML benchmarks.

The more revealing question is what produces these gains. The improvements concentrate on familiar ML-engineering moves instead of research-level insight, and the agents adopt sharply different default workflows: Claude Opus behaves as an iterative optimizer, Codex as a commit-and-wait planner, and Gemini oscillates between the two. Across trajectories, we identify three recurring limitations: agents miscalibrate novelty, under-utilize resources, and collapse quickly into local engineering sweeps. Lightweight plug-in skills for research ideation, experiment management, and scheduled checklists raise Claude Opus from 10.4% to 20.1% mean relative improvement and Codex from 4.4% to 8.1%, without changing the evaluation protocol. Beyond the score gains, agents propose more varied, problem-grounded interventions and surface insights that move beyond routine ML engineering.

Overall, these results draw a practical boundary for automating ML for science today. Frontier coding agents can climb scientific hills in the wild, but the climb is still powered mostly by engineering optimization rather than scientific reasoning. Agent4Sci makes this boundary measurable and visible: each new agent generation can be evaluated not only by how much it improves a metric, but by whether its improvements reflect increasingly transferable research insight. The goal is to help move automated agents from executing better experiments toward doing better science.

## 2 Agent4Sci: A Testbed for Automated Scientific Hill-Climbing

## 2.1 Task and Benchmark Selection

We construct Agent4Sci from recent ML-for-science benchmarks, retaining only tasks that support iterative improvement by an agent. We use benchmark to denote a published benchmark, and task to denote one of its evaluation tasks (typically with a designated mainstream metric); a single benchmark can contribute multiple tasks. Selection runs through a four-stage filtering pipeline:

• 

Domain Classification. Retain papers whose benchmark targets a scientific domain, filtering out benchmarks aimed at generic AI, ML, or systems research.

• 

Automated Screening. An agent scores each retained paper against seven criteria covering data, training, baselines, evaluation, and licensing (see Appendix B.2), and 7/7 candidates advance.

• 

Human Verification. Re-check the automated criterion labels by hand and demote any candidate whose 7/7 status does not survive verification.

• 

Deep Review. Inspect the paper and the official repository for reproducibility evidence, task specificity, repository usability, and domain coverage relative to the rest of the suite.

Taking the NeurIPS 2025 Datasets and Benchmarks track as a representative case, this pipeline admits 10 benchmarks from 497 candidates (a 98% rejection rate, with full per-stage diagnostics in Appendix B.3). Rejected papers are typically competitive in their original setting and are excluded only because they target a different scenario than ours, not because of any deficiency in the underlying work. After adding curated benchmarks from other top ML venues and domain journals, Agent4Sci contains 20 scientific benchmarks and 25 tasks across 11 scientific domains.

## 2.2 Pilot Study: Evaluation Gaming on Raw Scientific Tasks

To assess whether existing scientific repositories can directly support automated hill-climbing, we conduct a pilot study on 12 tasks drawn from 5 NeurIPS 2025 scientific benchmarks. The agent receives the paper, the original repository, the task description, and the data artifacts as released, then attempts to improve the reported metric. The pilot reveals that this setup is unreliable: 5 of 12 tasks exhibit one or more severe evaluation-gaming behaviors, which we group into four categories.

• 

Selective reporting. The agent evaluates only on a favorable subset of the test set and reports the inflated score as the headline result.

• 

Test-set contamination. The agent extracts held-out statistics, inspects test-input error cases, or uses test-set information to guide model selection or hyperparameters.

• 

Metric manipulation. The agent invents a non-standard evaluation protocol, claims the official metric is incorrect, or modifies the evaluation script to favor its model.

• 

Direct hardcoding. The agent hardcodes final outputs, copies predictions from earlier runs, or claims that legitimate checkpoints were accidentally deleted to obscure failures.

We use evaluation gaming as an umbrella term because these failures need not require malicious intent. Official scientific ML repositories often lack clear metric definitions, fixed data splits, or complete training and evaluation scripts. End-to-end hill-climbing directly on such repositories, therefore, opens seams that an aggressive optimizer can exploit, even when the agent’s high-level instruction is framed honestly. An evaluation that exposes the original repository and trusts the final score returned by the hill-climbing agent cannot reliably measure scientific progress.

## 2.3 The Agent4Sci Evaluation Protocol

The pilot study motivates the Agent4Sci evaluation protocol (Figure 2). The protocol has two parts: a one-time standardization phase that prepares each task into a verified reference environment, and a three-stage evaluation procedure that separates hill-climbing, held-out evaluation, and auditing.

Figure 2: Agent4Sci evaluation protocol. Each task includes a standard reproduction folder with standardized training scripts, evaluation scripts, and data splits. Runs separate the hill-climbing agent from held-out evaluation and auditing to prevent evaluation gaming.

## Standardization.

Each admitted task is converted into a verified reference environment through substantial human effort, and the foundation of that effort is reproduction. Starting from the official codebase, we reproduce the paper’s headline number on the published baseline, and a task is admitted into Agent4Sci only when this reproduction matches the paper within a verified tolerance. Reproduction is rarely a drop-in rerun of the published recipe: 3 benchmarks require an additional training pipeline that the original release does not include, 2 require a held-out validation slice carved from training data because our protocol does not allow test-set access during checkpoint selection, and another 2 require alignment of data-handling and split conventions with our standardized setting.

Standardization extends beyond reproduction along three more axes: deterministic train/validation/test splits with the test set sequestered, runtime optimization that fits the baseline within a single-H200 envelope, and isolated hill-climbing and evaluation containers. Every standardized task is independently re-checked by two reviewers, with disagreements resolved through repository inspection and reruns until both reviewers agree that the reproduction and the surrounding environment match the paper. The full per-benchmark record, including a paper-vs-reproduction comparison and the specific standardization moves applied to each project, appears in Appendix B.4.

## Three-Stage Evaluation.

After standardization, each run evaluates only the hill-climbing agent. The evaluation and auditing agents are auxiliary safeguards: they execute the submitted artifacts on the held-out test set and inspect the run for evaluation gaming and other academic misconduct.

• 

Hill-climbing. The hill-climbing agent receives the standardized environment and task specification. It can modify code, launch experiments, train models, and submit artifacts, but cannot read or evaluate on the held-out test set.

• 

Evaluation. A separate evaluation agent loads the submitted artifacts and runs them on the held-out test set using the standardized evaluation path.

• 

Auditing. A third auditing agent inspects the trajectory and submitted artifacts for any of the four evaluation-gaming behaviors from §2.2 as well as other forms of academic misconduct.

Together, standardization and the Agent4Sci evaluation protocol let each hill-climbing run start from a verified SOTA baseline and focus on the scientific task itself. Test-set access and score computation remain outside the hill-climbing agent, directly addressing the four evaluation-gaming behaviors observed in §2.2 and making residual protocol deviations auditable. All agents used to assist benchmark selection and standardization are Claude Opus 4.6, while final acceptance and verification decisions are made by human reviewers.

Astronomy, chemistry, ecology, geography, and physics each contribute 2, while biology, cosmology, genomics, geology, and neuroscience each contribute 1. At the task level, the suite covers classification, segmentation, detection, regression, link prediction, and retrieval, with scientific objectives ranging from dark-matter denoising and carbon forecasting to RNA inverse design and full-topology power-flow estimation.

The operational profile is similarly diverse. Training cost ranges from minute-scale tasks to 4.5-hour runs on a single H200, and official protocols often average across multiple seeds or folds. This breadth is intentional: Agent4Sci tests whether agents can transfer ML-engineering routines across scientific workflows instead of overfitting to one repository style, modality, or metric family.

## CONCLUSION
We present Agent4Sci, a human-verified testbed of 20 scientific ML benchmarks across 11 domains for evaluating automated scientific hill-climbing in the wild. Frontier coding agents push every benchmark beyond its published baseline without supervision, and lightweight plug-in skills roughly double the mean improvement. The gains still come mostly from ML engineering rather than scientific reasoning, and Agent4Sci offers a measurable signal for tracking when that begins to change.

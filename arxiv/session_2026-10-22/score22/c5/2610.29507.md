# 2610.29507 — Keeping Time vs. Understanding Time: Neither Alone Explains Temporal Reasoning in LLMs
Authors: Auss Abbood, Fernando Martínez-Plumed, José Hernández-Orallo, Nigel Collier

Categories: cs.CL, cs.AI

## ABSTRACT
Temporal reasoning is widely assumed to decompose into two modes: temporal arithmetic (date and time calculations), and temporal semantics (understanding temporal and causal relations). This dichotomy shapes how benchmarks are designed, how models are trained, and how progress is measured, yet its empirical validity has never been directly tested. We provide two lines of evidence that challenge it. First, we test whether tasks labelled as arithmetic or semantic by the benchmarks support transferable gains within their purported mode. Finetuning on these subsets yields predominantly dataset-specific gains ($+6.8$ to $+23.1$ percentage points), while transfer to a different dataset in the same mode ranges from $-3.2$ to $+1.1$ percentage points. Across the two benchmark families used in the transfer analysis, this pattern provides little evidence of coherent, transferable temporal abilities. Second, using cognitive-demand-based model evaluation, we examine which capabilities actually drive performance on these benchmarks. We find that quantitative and logical reasoning, the assumed proxies for temporal arithmetic and temporal semantics, are not the primary drivers; knowledge is. Together, these findings show that current temporal reasoning benchmarks do not cleanly realise the arithmetic-semantics distinction and instead largely reward models for remembering how time typically works.

## 1 Introduction

Which cognitive abilities are required to reason about time? Prior work on large language models (LLMs)commonly decomposes temporal reasoning into two modes: temporal arithmetic, involving calculations over times and dates, and temporal semantics, involving the interpretation of before–after relations and temporal causality (Wang and Zhao, 2024; Chu et al., 2024; Fatemi et al., 2025; Islakoglu and Kalo, 2025). Both require an understanding of time, whereas temporal arithmetic additionally requires mathematical capabilities. This distinction has become a common organising principle for benchmark design, model training, and performance reporting (Wang and Zhao, 2024; Chu et al., 2024; Su et al., 2024; Fatemi et al., 2025). However, it remains unclear whether it reflects meaningful differences in the cognitive demands tested by current benchmarks.

This question matters because benchmarks may not measure what they claim to measure due to poor construct validity (Bean et al., 2025). As benchmarks become more complex, the true bottleneck may diverge from the target ability named in the task description (Rameshkumar et al., 2025; Varela et al., 2026).
For example, success on a temporal arithmetic question may depend more on numerical calculation than on temporal understanding, whereas success on a temporal semantics question may depend more on language comprehension or memorised world knowledge. The arithmetic/semantics distinction may therefore classify tasks by their surface form and not by the abilities that actually drive performance.

We test this assumption in two ways. First, we ask whether the arithmetic/semantics distinction predicts transfer across benchmarks. We train LLMson temporal reasoning datasets and evaluate whether performance improvements generalise to unseen datasets, both within and across the two modes. If each mode reflects a stable capability with shared reasoning patterns, transfer should be strong between datasets in the same mode (Shao et al., 2024; Hu et al., 2026; Ionov et al., 2026). Instead, we find that improvements remain highly localised to the training data and barely generalise to unseen data in the same purported mode.

Second, we test whether the arithmetic/semantics split reflects differences in cognitive demands. Using the Demand-Level-Annotation (DeLeAn)rubrics (Zhou et al., 2026), we score each benchmark item on 18 general cognitive dimensions. We compare arithmetic and semantic datasets across all DeLeAndimensions, but find no clear separation between the two modes. We then focus on the Quantitative Reasoning (QLq)and Logical Reasoning (QLl)cognitive demands, which are most naturally aligned with temporal arithmetic (Su et al., 2024; Fatemi et al., 2025) and temporal semantics (Xiong et al., 2024; Tang et al., 2025), respectively. We train models on data with relatively high demands for each of the two dimensions and test whether this produces larger gains in the corresponding mode. It does not. Instead, we find that tasks depend more strongly on the cognitive demands for knowledge.

Taken together, these results suggest that temporal reasoning benchmarks are better characterised as knowledge-heavy tasks than as tests of distinct arithmetic or semantic capabilities, with performance depending more on learned regularities and background knowledge than on explicit reasoning over temporal quantities or relations.

## 2 Background

Temporal reasoning benchmarks commonly distinguish tasks requiring semantic temporal understanding from those that also require mathematical operations. For example, Su et al. (2024) note that 19 of the 38 tasks in TRAM (Wang and Zhao, 2024) involve mathematical reasoning. TimeBench (Chu et al., 2024), which covers 10 temporal reasoning benchmarks, distinguishes symbolic tasks such as date arithmetic from those requiring only semantic temporal understanding, while Test of Time (ToT) (Fatemi et al., 2025) explicitly has an Arithmetic and a Semantic subset. Across these efforts, the arithmetic/semantic split is treated as a meaningful axis of variation, yet none tests whether it reflects separable underlying abilities.

However, growing evidence questions the construct validity of LLM benchmarks—the extent to which they measure the abilities they claim to measure. Reviewing 445 benchmarks from major NLP and ML venues, Bean et al. (2025) find that many studies do not establish a clear link between the ability named by a benchmark and the abilities required to perform well on it. Alaa et al. (2025) identify a similar gap in medicine, where high scores on exam-style benchmarks do not reliably predict performance on real clinical data. More broadly, Rameshkumar et al. (2025) and Varela et al. (2026) show that the main performance bottleneck can diverge from the capability named in the task description as tasks become more complex. These findings suggest that benchmark categories require empirical validation. We apply this principle to temporal reasoning by testing whether the arithmetic/semantic labels of existing benchmarks predict transfer behaviour and cognitive abilities.

If temporal arithmetic and temporal semantics reflect genuinely distinct and coherent abilities, then improvements in one should transfer, at minimum, to other tasks within the same mode. This logic follows a standard rationale in the reasoning literature: training in a narrow
domain should generalise to tasks that draw on the same underlying capability. Shao et al. (2024) and Guo et al. (2025) show that reinforcement learning on mathematical benchmarks reliably transfers to other mathematical tasks and even to non-mathematical reasoning benchmarks, confirming that fine-tuning can yield cross-benchmark transfer when the underlying capability is shared. Whether the same holds for temporal reasoning
remains untested. Xiong et al. (2024) show that fine-tuning on temporal data can improve temporal reasoning, but do not test cross-mode transfer, and recent work suggests that transfer is not guaranteed across all domains (Hu et al., 2026; Huan et al., 2026). We use fine-tuning
transfer as a direct probe of the arithmetic/semantic distinction: if gains transfer within mode, the distinction has empirical grounding; if they do not, it lacks support as a description of separable abilities.

For the purposes of this study, QLqand QLlare the most relevant dimensions, as they map most directly onto the conventional arithmetic/semantic distinction. ## 3.1.2 Operationalising the arithmetic/semantic distinction

We use fine-tuning transfer (see 3.3) between temporal arithmetic and semantics as a probe of their separability. If both modes reflect distinct abilities, then training on one should improve performance on tasks from the same mode, and not on tasks from the other. We use two complementary operationalisations to partition the data into these modes.

The first is a dataset-level operationalisation based on existing mode labels. We restrict this controlled transfer analysis to ToTand TRAM, as these are the only benchmarks that provide explicit or reconstructable arithmetic/semantic partitions, minimising confounds from differences in dataset construction, such as annotation assumptions, or task format. ToTprovides an explicit split: its Arithmetic subset covers time/date arithmetic, duration, scheduling, and time zone conversion tasks, while its Semantic subset targets event–event/time, before/after, timeline, and first/last relations. TRAM does not provide such a binary partition. Instead, we follow Su et al.’s (2024) classification of TRAM subtasks into math-time tasks (equivalent to arithmetic mode) and pure-time tasks (equivalent to semantic mode) (see Appendix B.2 for details).

The second is an item-level operationalisation based on DeLeAnannotations (Section 3.1.1). We partition items by their QLqand QLldemands to construct training sets intended to probe both temporal reasoning modes. Specifically, we filter all datasets to items where demand for QLqexceeds that for QLl, and vice versa, yielding six training conditions. These include two zero-demand controls (Tab. 2). It is worth noting that QLqand QLlare rarely the most demanding dimensions in our data. Out of 8,530 training items, QLqis the dominant dimension in only 15 items (1,288 including ties), and QLlin only 1 item (771 including ties). We will discuss the limited role these dimensions seem to play in current benchmarks later.

## 3.2 Evaluation

We use accuracy for evaluation and additionally normalise temporal expressions before scoring, following Abbood et al. (2025). The expected answer type in the dataset guides the normalisation of the model’s response. E.g., “11 Aug. 2023” and “11/08/2023” are then treated as equivalent. This prevents surface-level differences in temporal formatting from being counted as reasoning failures (details in Appendix C).

Aggregate accuracy, however, does not isolate performance on any single cognitive demand dimension, since each item carries a multidimensional DeLeAnlabel. To disentangle the contribution of individual dimensions, we construct subject characteristic curves using dominant slices. For a dominant dimension $i$ and level $l$ , we retain only items satisfying $x_{i}^{(j)}=l$ and $x_{i^{{}^{\prime}}}^{(j)}\leq l$ for all $i^{{}^{\prime}}\neq i$ , i.e., items for which dimension $i$ is the dominant demand. Accuracy is averaged within each level and a two-parameter logistic curve is fitted to the resulting five points, using the weighting scheme of Zhou et al. (2026). We report the area under the curve (AUC)of the fitted curve as the model’s ability on that dimension.

Four of the 18 dimensions are excluded from this analysis due to insufficient coverage in our data. Zhou et al. suggests excluding dimensions that have a range narrower than 1–3 or have fewer than 30 items. Spatio-physical Reasoning (SNs)and Verbal Expression (CEe)span only demand levels 1 and 2, too narrow a range for meaningful curve fitting. Conceptualisation, Learning and Abstraction (CL)and Knowledge of Natural Sciences (KNn)yield only 9 and 25 items, respectively, across all dominant slices, making estimates unreliable.

## 3.3 Fine-tuning

Our main method for investigating the separability between temporal arithmetic and temporal semantics is to fine-tune models on data for each mode and test the transfer of performance to the corresponding mode.

GRPO.

Temporal reasoning tasks can require multi-step reasoning. We initially attempted supervised fine-tuning on reasoning traces from a larger teacher model (Hsieh et al., 2023), without success. We attribute this to a mismatch between the teacher’s and student’s response style (Li et al., 2025). Instead, we use Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) (Guo et al., 2025), which sidesteps this issue by optimising directly on self-generated reasoning traces. To ensure comparability across experiments despite substantial variation in dataset sizes, we fix the compute budget to 400 optimisation steps with a batch size of 16. We use 8 generations per rollout for dataset-level operationalisation and 16 for the item-level operationalisation, due to training instabilities with 8 rollouts (details in Appendix D).

Reward.

We define two reward settings to probe the effect of training from complementary directions. In the accuracy setting, the reward is 1 if the model’s response is correct and 0 otherwise, determined by the semantic equivalence metric described in Section 3.2. In the one-minus-accuracy setting, we invert this signal to explore a form of targeted unlearning similar to Zaradoukas et al. (2026). The model receives a reward of 1 only if its answer is incorrect but correctly formatted, 0.5 if the answer is both correct and correctly formatted (for richer feedback), and 0 otherwise. The format requirement mitigates reward hacking (Skalse et al., 2022) by preventing models from maximising reward through degenerate outputs. ## LIMITATIONS SECTION
Our experiments use models of up to 12 billion parameters. In one instance, we trained a larger model, which showed small transfer (improvement of $\approx 3$ percentage points) in the arithmetic mode. Future work could explore transfer dynamics in even larger models; however, the consistency of our findings across three families and two scales suggests that this pattern is robust. Furthermore, our transfer experiments are limited to ToTand TRAM, which are the only benchmarks with explicit or reconstructable arithmetic and semantic partitions. Extending this analysis to future benchmarks that provide more detailed task taxonomies would strengthen the generalisability of our findings.

## CONCLUSION
This study scrutinises whether temporal arithmetic and temporal semantics correspond to distinct reasoning abilities. Capability-aware analyses of the dataset and fine-tuning experiments show that they do not. Cognitive demand profiles fail to separate arithmetic from semantic benchmark items, despite supposed differences in required quantitative and logical reasoning. Furthermore, finetuning gains do not transfer between benchmarks of the same temporal reasoning mode.

Capability changes after finetuning on temporal data show that DeLeAnknowledge dimensions improve more than QLqor QLl. Specialised QL-heavy training could raise QL capability, but still did not improve performance in either temporal reasoning modes. Our results suggest that current benchmarks test the retrieval and application of time-related regularities, not arithmetic or semantic abilities. The arithmetic/semantics taxonomy may describe task form, but it should not be treated as a salient decomposition of temporal reasoning. Future benchmarks should control for knowledge demands and validate proposed categories through capability-based analyses.

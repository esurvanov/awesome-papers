# 2610.29358 — Reading the Mask: Descriptor-Grounded BI-RADS Rationale Generation for Breast MRI Segmentation
Authors: Hrishikesh Singh, Aarti Sharma, Divyanshu Kumar, Millie Pant
Comment: 
Categories: cs.CV, cs.AI

## ABSTRACT
Deep learning based models accurately delineate tumours on dynamic contrast-enhanced MRI (DCE-MRI), but segmentation masks alone do not provide the rationales or reasoning used by radiologists to assess the lesion suspicion. This limits the explainability as well the clinical integration. We investigate whether clinically relevant descriptors can instead be derived from the segmentation masks and their dynamic contrast-enhancement series. We have used Breast Imaging Reporting and Data System (BI-RADS) guidelines as a structured framework. Using these guidelines we designed a BI-RADS decision tree with eight quantitative measurements using radiomics, together with the lesion size and component count, that determine the descriptors at eight nodes. Using these descriptors we generate natural-language rationale using a large language model. The model was constrained to express only the derived records from the decision tree. After applying this framework to the expert and automatic masks of each of the 1,506 cases in the MAMA-MIA dataset, we generated a reproducible corpus of 3,012 BI-RADS grounded rationales. We obtained a faithfulness score of 99.98% and a counterfactual accuracy of 99.3%, indicating that the generated rationales faithfully verbalises the measured descriptors and correctly reflects changes in the underlying record. Agreement between the descriptors from expert and automatic masks falls from 0.832 to 0.549 as mask quality falls from Good to Missed. The generated rationales are intended as draft representations for radiologist review and final sign-off rather than autonomous clinical reports.

## 1. Introduction

When radiologists analyse the contrast-enhanced breast MRI, they use
standardised guidelines such as BI-RADS (Breast Imaging-Reporting and Data
System) to locate lesions, describe their morphology and assess malignancy
risk. On the other hand, the automated segmentation methods tell only a lesion
boundary or mask. They lack explanation of the image features which support the
results. This lack of clinically meaningful rationales makes the automated
segmentation harder to interpret and trust. This creates a barrier in their
clinical adoption.

There are several methods which improve interpretability. Post-hoc methods such
as Grad-CAM (Selvaraju et al., 2017) identify the influential image regions but do not
associate them with clinical descriptors. More recently, the researchers
in (Ma et al., 2025) have generated PI-RADS style reasoning alongside lesion
masks but their methodology relies on radiologist-authored reports. Such
report-based supervision is expensive and difficult to obtain for large
retrospective datasets.

We propose a report-independent framework for generating BI-RADS grounded
rationales from breast DCE-MRI. Our key observation is that many breast BI-RADS
descriptors such as shape, margin, internal enhancement and kinetic behaviour
- that is, how the lesion’s contrast enhancement changes over time after the
contrast injection - can be quantitatively derived from the segmentations and
dynamic MRI data. Thus, we reverse the conventional pipeline. We compute these
measurements directly from images and masks, then we convert the resulting
measurements into structured BI-RADS descriptors and natural-language
rationales. We apply the framework to all 1,506 cases in
MAMA-MIA (Garrucho et al., 2025) without additional radiologist annotation and evaluate
the generated rationales using four annotation-free metrics: faithfulness,
hallucination rate, omission rate, and counterfactual accuracy. Since every
case has both an expert and an automatic mask, we also measure how much the
descriptors change when the automatic mask is used. This provides a
scalable alternative to report-dependent approaches for producing clinically
grounded explanations of breast MRI lesions.

## 2. Related Work

Encoder-decoder networks (Ronneberger
et al., 2015) remain the standard for volumetric lesion
delineation, with nnU-Net (Isensee et al., 2021) providing a self-configuring pipeline which is
challenging to outperform without task-specific optimisation. Segmentation
models adapted to medical data, MedSAM (Ma
et al., 2024), extend promptable
segmentation to radiological volumes. But these methods do not provide
interpretable outputs beyond the mask itself. Another class of interpretable
methods involve gradient-based methods (Selvaraju et al., 2017), which assign importance
to pixels but leave the clinical rationale of the highlighted region
unspecified. Concept bottleneck models (Koh et al., 2020) provide named intermediates,
but further analysis (Margeloiu et al., 2021) has questioned whether the learned
concepts correspond to what their names suggest. Prototype-based
classifiers (Chen
et al., 2019) use a representative example of a particular class
to develop reasoning, but they are limited by the set of prototypes they
learned beforehand. Contrastive image-text pretraining (Radford et al., 2021), adapted to
biomedical corpora as in BiomedCLIP (Zhang et al., 2023), aligns the whole image
with entire captions. This type of global alignment cannot distinguish multiple
findings in the same organ.

Quantitative DCE-MRI features have been related to BI-RADS
descriptors (Nie
et al., 2008; Gorji et al., 2026), kinetic curves classified
automatically (Chen
et al., 2006), and template-based reports generated for chest
X-rays (Pino
et al., 2021). We add the record as the only input to a text
generator, and a paired comparison of expert and automatic masks.

## 3. Dataset

MAMA-MIA (Garrucho et al., 2025) is a multi-centre collection of 1,506 breast DCE-MRI
studies assembled from four public TCIA (Clark et al., 2013) cohorts:
ISPY2 (Hylton et al., 2016), DUKE (Saha et al., 2018), ISPY1 (Hylton et al., 2016) and NACT. This
dataset contains expert masks and autogenerated masks. The autogenerated masks
are graded by quality by two experts visually. The released grades are Good,
Acceptable, Poor and Missed. There are three to six post-contrast phases per
study. Acquisition timestamps are provided for each study. A multifocality
indicator has also been provided for 1,346 cases.

## 4. Proposed Approach

Figure 1. Overall pipeline of the proposed methodology.

Our method generates rationales grounded in the ACR BI-RADS Atlas (5th edition)
MRI lexicon (American
College of Radiology, 2013) for the breast DCE-MRI dataset consisting of lesion
segmentation masks. First, we prepare the lesion representation
(Section 4.1), then we extract the quantitative lesion
measurements (Section 4.2). These features describe the
morphology as well as the contrast-enhancement behaviour of the lesions. We
then map these measurements to the BI-RADS descriptors
(Section 4.3), finally a large language model (LLM) converts
the structured BI-RADS records into a natural-language rationale
(Section 4.4).

## 4.1. Step 1: Preparing the Lesion Representation

The segmentation masks may contain multiple disconnected regions, which is
common in multifocal disease. Computing the shape features on the full mask may
therefore give misleading values, hence we first identify the connected
components and then select the dominant lesion for morphological analysis.

## 4.1.1. Connected component analysis

Let $M$ denote the binary lesion mask. We identify its connected components
using 26-connectivity:

(1)

 $M=\bigcup_{i=1}^{m}C_{i},$ 

where $m$ is the total number of connected components. Two voxels are
considered connected if they share a face, edge, or corner.

We define the dominant component as the largest connected component:

(2)

 $M^{*}=\arg\max_{C_{i}}|C_{i}|.$ 

All subsequent morphological measurements are computed on $M^{*}$ . We retain
 $m$ as the multiplicity descriptor: single if $m=1$ and
multiple otherwise. No minimum component size is applied.

We also compute the dominance ratio:

(3)

 $\rho=\frac{|M^{*}|}{|M|}.$ 

A high $\rho$ indicates that most of the segmented lesion belongs to one
component. A low $\rho$ indicates that the segmentation is spread across
several components. We retain this value as a diagnostic measure of mask
dispersion.

## 4.1.2. Spatial normalisation

The MRI volumes have different voxel spacings across cases. We therefore
resample the lesion region to isotropic $1\,\mathrm{mm}^{3}$ spacing before
computing surface-based features. Isotropic resampling is important because
surface area and volume depend on voxel spacing. Without normalisation,
measurements from different cases would not be directly comparable.

## 4.2. Step 2: Extracting Quantitative Lesion Measurements

We extract quantitative measurements from the dominant lesion component
 $M^{*}$ . The measurements capture two aspects of the lesion: its morphology and
its contrast-enhancement behaviour. Morphological measurements describe the
lesion shape and boundary. The enhancement measurements describe the spatial
and temporal distribution of contrast uptake.

## 4.2.1. Morphological measurements

Let $V$ and $A$ denote the volume and surface area of $M^{*}$ . Let
 $\lambda_{1}\geq\lambda_{2}\geq\lambda_{3}$ denote the eigenvalues of the
covariance matrix of the voxel coordinates of $M^{*}$ . We compute four
morphological measurements.

Sphericity measures how closely the lesion resembles a sphere:

(4)

 $\varphi_{1}=\frac{(36\pi V^{2})^{1/3}}{A}.$ 

A spherical lesion has a sphericity close to one. Irregular lesions have lower
values.

Elongation measures the relative extent of the lesion along its
principal axes:

(5)

 $\varphi_{2}=\sqrt{\frac{\lambda_{3}}{\lambda_{1}}}.$ 

A lower value indicates a more elongated lesion, while a value closer to one
indicates a more isotropic shape.

Surface-to-volume ratio measures the amount of boundary relative to
lesion volume:

(6)

 $\varphi_{3}=\frac{A}{V}.$ 

Irregular or complex boundaries have a higher surface-to-volume ratio. We
therefore use this measurement as a quantitative proxy for the BI-RADS margin
descriptor. Note that $\varphi_{3}$ also depends on size ( $3/r$ for a sphere).

Compactness measures how much of the lesion’s bounding box is occupied
by the lesion:

(7)

 $\varphi_{4}=\frac{|M^{*}|\,v}{V_{\mathrm{bbox}}},$ 

where $v$ is the voxel volume and $V_{\mathrm{bbox}}$ is the volume of the
axis-aligned bounding box. This measurement helps distinguish compact,
spatially concentrated enhancement from more dispersed regions.

Together, these four measurements provide a quantitative description of lesion
shape and boundary structure.

## 4.2.2. Contrast-enhancement measurements

We next measure how the lesion enhances after contrast administration. Let
 $I_{0}$ denote the pre-contrast image, $I_{t_{e}}$ the first post-contrast image,
and $I_{t_{\ell}}$ the final post-contrast image. These phases are selected using
the acquisition timestamps provided for each case.

We first compute the early enhancement map:

(8)

 $E=I_{t_{e}}-I_{0}.$ 

For any region $S$ , let $\mathbb{E}_{S}[\cdot]$ denote the mean intensity over
its voxels.

To measure peripheral enhancement, we divide the lesion into a core and a
boundary shell. The core is obtained by eroding $M^{*}$ three times with the
six-connected structuring element:

(9)

 $M^{*}_{\mathrm{core}}=\mathrm{erode}(M^{*},\,3\ \text{iterations}),$ 

and the shell is

(10)

 $M^{*}_{\mathrm{shell}}=M^{*}\setminus M^{*}_{\mathrm{core}}.$ 

The rim ratio is then defined as

(11)

 $\varphi_{5}=\frac{\mathbb{E}_{M^{*}_{\mathrm{shell}}}[E]}{\mathbb{E}_{M^{*}_{\mathrm{core}}}[E]}.$ 

A higher value indicates stronger enhancement near the lesion boundary relative
to its core. This provides a quantitative measure for the rim-enhancement
descriptor. If the eroded core is too small, $\varphi_{5}$ is not computed and
internal enhancement is marked as not assessed ( $\perp$ ).

We measure enhancement heterogeneity using the standard deviation of
enhancement within the lesion. The intensities are not normalised across
scanners:

(12)

 $\varphi_{6}=\sqrt{\mathbb{E}_{M^{*}}\!\left[\left(E-\mathbb{E}_{M^{*}}[E]\right)^{2}\right]}.$ 

Higher values indicate greater variation in enhancement across the lesion.

To capture temporal enhancement behaviour, we compute the signal enhancement
ratio (SER):

(13)

 $\varphi_{7}=\frac{\mathbb{E}_{M^{*}}[I_{t_{e}}-I_{0}]}{\mathbb{E}_{M^{*}}[I_{t_{\ell}}-I_{0}]}.$ 

A higher SER indicates that enhancement is stronger in the early phase than in
the late phase, providing a numerical measure of washout behaviour. More
precisely, $\mathrm{SER}>1$ means early enhancement exceeds late enhancement,
so the signal has decreased from early to late, indicating washout;
 $\mathrm{SER}\approx 1$ means early and late enhancement are similar,
indicating an approximate plateau; and $\mathrm{SER}<1$ means late
enhancement is greater, indicating persistent or progressive
enhancement.

Finally, we compute the percentage initial enhancement:

(14)

 $\varphi_{8}=100\times\frac{\mathbb{E}_{M^{*}}[E]}{\mathbb{E}_{M^{*}}[I_{0}]}.$ 

This quantity is used to determine the initial kinetic descriptor.

Thus, the measurement stage produces a compact set of eight proxy quantitative
values ( $\varphi_{1}$ – $\varphi_{8}$ ) that describe lesion morphology, internal
enhancement, and temporal enhancement. The decision tree also uses the maximum
3D diameter $d$ of $M^{*}$ and the component count $m$ . These values are used
in the next stage to assign the corresponding BI-RADS descriptors.

## 4.3. Step 3: Assigning BI-RADS Descriptors

The quantitative measurements are continuous values, while BI-RADS uses
categorical descriptors. We therefore map the measurements to BI-RADS values
using predefined decision rules. We use the BI-RADS MRI lexicon from the ACR
BI-RADS Atlas (5th edition) (American
College of Radiology, 2013) and retain all 14 nodes in its
reporting sequence.

## 4.3.1. BI-RADS decision tree

Of the 14 nodes, eight are computed from the MRI and lesion mask: finding type,
mass shape, mass margin, mass internal enhancement, initial kinetic phase,
delayed kinetic phase, lesion size, and multiplicity.

The remaining six nodes are not computed and are assigned $\perp$ . Non-mass
distribution and internal enhancement pattern require ductal-anatomy
information that is not available from the lesion mask. Associated features,
breast composition, and background parenchymal enhancement require information
outside the lesion mask. The final assessment category is also excluded because
it requires clinical history and prior imaging. Moreover, all cases in MAMA-MIA
are biopsy-confirmed malignant, so assigning an assessment category would not
provide a meaningful evaluation.

We retain these nodes explicitly as $\perp$ rather than removing them. This
keeps the representation aligned with the complete BI-RADS sequence.

The decision tree also contains conditional nodes. In particular, mass shape,
mass margin, and mass internal enhancement are evaluated only when the finding
type is classified as a mass. This prevents descriptors that require a defined
mass boundary from being assigned to non-mass enhancement.

Decision tree with ten numbered nodes; each test compares one measurement with one threshold and leaves are BI-RADS descriptor values. Node 1, finding type on the dominant lesion: diameter below 5 mm gives Focus (no case in MAMA-MIA); otherwise compactness of at least 0.15 gives Mass, else non-mass enhancement, whose nodes 5 and 6 are not assessed. Evaluated only for a mass: node 2, shape (sphericity below 0.487 gives irregular, else elongation of at least 0.775 gives round, else oval); node 3, margin (surface-to-volume ratio below 0.363 gives circumscribed, below 0.508 irregular, else spiculated); node 4, internal enhancement (not assessed if the rim ratio is undefined, rim if it is at least 0.965, else heterogeneous if heterogeneity is at least 123.0, else homogeneous). Evaluated for every finding type: node 7, initial kinetics (enhancement below 50 percent slow, below 100 percent medium, else fast); node 8, delayed kinetics (signal enhancement ratio below 0.9 persistent, up to 1.1 plateau, else washout); node 9, lesion size in mm; node 10, multiplicity (one component single, else multiple). Nodes 11 to 14 are not assessed.

## 4.3.2. Descriptor thresholds

We use four types of threshold rules. Fig. 2 shows every
test with its threshold; where two measurements decide one node, they are
tested in the order shown.

Standard-defined thresholds are taken directly from the BI-RADS
definition. For example, a lesion with a maximum 3D diameter below 5 mm is
classified as a focus rather than a mass. No lesion in MAMA-MIA falls in
this branch.

For measurements without an established external threshold, we derive
cohort-based thresholds from the expert masks. For a measurement
 $\varphi_{j}$ , let $\hat{F}_{j}$ denote its empirical cumulative distribution
function. A threshold at quantile $q$ is

(15)

 $\tau_{j}(q)=\hat{F}_{j}^{-1}(q).$ 

We use $q=1/3$ and $2/3$ for surface-to-volume ratio, $q=1/3$ for
sphericity, $q=2/3$ for rim ratio, and the median for elongation and
heterogeneity. Quantile thresholds fix how often each descriptor value occurs
on the expert masks, so they place a lesion relative to the rest of this cohort
rather than against an absolute clinical criterion.

For the mass versus non-mass decision, we use a fixed compactness
threshold of 0.15 rather than a cohort quantile. A quantile-based threshold

## LIMITATIONS SECTION
The descriptors are not yet compared with expert reading or with the
dataset’s multifocality indicator; all cases are malignant; and the thresholds
are fitted on one dataset. Several measurements are simple proxies (e.g. surface-to-volume ratio depends on size, heterogeneity is not normalised).

## CONCLUSION
In this study we have shown that using BI-RADS as a structured decision tree
and defining eight measurements over a mask and its contrast series gives us
the symbolic records over the 1,506 MAMA-MIA studies. Using language models on
these records gives us clinical prose without ever observing an image.
Applying this to the expert and automatic masks of all 1,506 studies yields a
reproducible set of 3,012 BI-RADS-grounded rationales. The faithfulness score
achieved on the dataset is 99.98%. This shows that the rationales state their
records correctly, not that the records are clinically correct. We also
measured the descriptor agreement on both mask sources and found that it falls
with segmentation mask quality. The descriptor agreement falls from 0.832 on
masks graded Good to 0.549 on those graded Missed, and the decline remains
after correcting for chance. The rationales produced are
drafts for a reporting radiologist to review, amend and sign off. In conclusion,
we have shown that descriptors for a breast MRI report can be measured from
segmentation masks and contrast series with the help of the structure provided
by the published BI-RADS lexicon.

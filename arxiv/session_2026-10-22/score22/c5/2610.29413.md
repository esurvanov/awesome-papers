## ABSTRACT
Recently, we see a rise in conversational agents as a form of social and emotional support, where disclosure will unfold over time. Yet, current evaluations of these agents typically examine isolated conversational states. In analyses of naturally occurring conversations, disclosure history and current disclosure are confounded which tends to make their effects difficult to isolate. We thus introduce Trajectory-based Disclosure Evaluation (TraDE), an evaluation framework grounded in Social Penetration Theory that utilizes predefined disclosure trajectories and evaluates how preceding disclosure history shapes model behavior under matched current disclosure. Through the use of TraDE, we demonstrate across multiple models and relational behavior benchmarks that responses to disclosures matched on depth can differ depending on the preceding trajectory, particularly in whether models accept or challenge the user's framing. We also observed that these differences track accumulated exposure to deeper disclosure rather than any single moment of vulnerability or the order of disclosures. It also tends to persists for several turns after disclosure returns to shallow levels. For several behaviors, we also demonstrate that models diverge most consistently from human supporters in the Emotional Support Conversation corpus (ESConv) not when users open up but rather when they step back in their disclosure. TraDE thus provides a systematic way to expose history-dependent relational behavior that evaluations of isolated conversational states would otherwise miss.

## 1 Introduction

Today, we see a widespread adoption of conversational agents not only for task-oriented assistance but also for social interaction, emotional support, and companionship (Robb & Mann, 2025; Phang et al., 2025). Such interactions typically involve personal disclosures (Papneja & Yadav, 2025; Robb & Mann, 2025), while studies have also shown association of loneliness, emotional dependence, and problematic use with greater chatbot use (Fang et al., 2025). The understanding how models respond behaviorally as conversations become increasingly personal in disclosure is therefore critical for evaluating these human-AI interactions.

A model may respond not only to the current disclosure, but also to the history that precedes it. Social Penetration Theory (Altman & Taylor, 1973) characterizes self-disclosure by breadth, the range of topics discussed, and depth, how personal or intimate disclosures are. We represent disclosure using depth levels and refer to the sequence of depths across turns as a disclosure trajectory. Different trajectories can lead to the same current disclosure. Two possible trajectories include when a conversation gradually progress toward vulnerable disclosure or when it returns to shallower levels after a vulnerable episode. By keeping the current disclosure fixed while varying the preceding trajectory, we examine how earlier disclosure history affects model responses.

Prior work has examined disclosure and relationship development in human–AI interaction (Ho et al., 2018; Lee et al., 2020; Skjuve et al., 2021; Skjuve et al., 2022), while recent studies have analyzed naturally occurring conversations from platforms such as Replika and Character.AI (Chu et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2026; Li & Zhang, 2026). However, in naturally occurring conversations, discourse conditions often vary together including the disclosure depth, duration, topic, order, and exposure to vulnerable information often vary together. This highlights the difficulty faced when determining which aspects of earlier disclosure history are associated with changes in model behavior. At the same time, existing relational behavior benchmarks largely evaluate isolated conversational states, eliciting responses to fixed prompts or freely unfolding dialogue without controlling what the user disclosed before (Cheng et al., 2026; Kaffee et al., 2026; Ibrahim et al., 2026). What is thus missing is a controlled method for varying specific properties of disclosure history while evaluating model responses under otherwise matched conditions.
Prior work has examined disclosure and relationship development in human–AI interaction (Ho et al., 2018; Lee et al., 2020; Skjuve et al., 2021; Skjuve et al., 2022), while recent studies have analyzed naturally occurring conversations from platforms such as Replika and Character.AI (Chu et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2026; Li & Zhang, 2026). However, naturally occurring conversations typically have discourse conditions that often vary together including the disclosure depth, duration, topic, order, and exposure. This highlights the difficulty faced when evaluating the changes in model behavior and isolating the effects of each conditions. Similarly, we see that existing relational behavior benchmarks depends largely on evaluating isolated conversational states, eliciting responses to fixed prompts or freely unfolding dialogue without controlling what the user disclosed before (Cheng et al., 2026; Kaffee et al., 2026; Ibrahim et al., 2026). This motivates a controlled method for varying specific properties of disclosure history while evaluating model responses under otherwise matched conditions.

We thus introduce a controlled framework, Trajectory-based Disclosure Evaluation (TraDE), for studying how preceding disclosure history shapes subsequent model responses while holding the current disclosure fixed. We demonstrate its utility through three research questions. (1) Does prior disclosure history shape model behavior beyond the current disclosure? (2) Which properties of disclosure history account for this effect? (3) How does model adaptation to changing disclosure differ from human supporters in Emotional Support Conversation (ESConv) corpus (Liu et al., 2021)?

To support this investigation, we make two main contributions in this paper:

1. 

We introduce TraDE, a benchmark-agnostic framework that constructs multi-turn conversations with predefined disclosure trajectories. It evaluates relational model behavior under matched current disclosure conditions across four models and three behavioral benchmarks.

2. 

We demonstrate TraDE through controlled experiments showing that model behavior can differ across disclosure histories, particularly in how models respond to the user’s framing. We found more significant differences when disclosure depth and exposure duration changed together than when peak depth or disclosure order changed alone. Most initially detected differences remained detectable after disclosure returned to shallow levels, although their magnitude decreased. We also compared eight language models with human supporters from ESConv, finding differences in both overall behavioral levels and trajectory shapes. For several behaviors, shape differences were more consistently detected when disclosure receded or changed direction.

## 2 Related Works

Relational Behaviors of Language Models and Their Risks.
Conversational models are now widely used for emotional support and companionship
(Robb & Mann, 2025; Phang et al., 2025), with extended use linked to loneliness,
emotional dependence, and problematic use (Fang et al., 2025). Studies of real companion interactions link these harms more to relational behavior than inaccurate content, including validating harmful beliefs or discouraging outside relationships (Zhang et al., 2025), as well as to human-like self-presentation (Abercrombie et al., 2023). Recent benchmarks hence measure behaviors that are relational
rather than task-oriented: ELEPHANT for social sycophancy in open-ended advice
(Cheng et al., 2026), INTIMA for companionship-reinforcing and boundary-maintaining
responses (Kaffee et al., 2026), and AnthroBench for anthropomorphic behaviors such as
personhood claims and relationship building (Ibrahim et al., 2026). Yet all three elicit behavior from fixed prompt sets or from freely generated dialogue, so the
disclosure history over which these risks develop remains outside experimental control.

Disclosure and Relationship Development.
Social Penetration Theory describes relationship development through the breadth and depth of
self-disclosure, where depth refers to how intimate or personally significant a disclosure is
(Altman & Taylor, 1973). This depth varies by degree rather than by kind: disclosures range from
descriptive facts about oneself to evaluative and emotional content (Morton, 1978),
partners reciprocate at the level of intimacy they receive (Rubin, 1975), and human
ratings of conversational prompts recover a smooth progression from mundane topics to
vulnerability and regret (Kardas et al., 2022; Pei & Jurgens, 2020). For analysis, disclosure depth is often divided into a small number of ordered levels (Niwa & Maruno, 2010). However, relationship development also depends on how disclosure changes over time. Previous studies have found that disclosure and liking reinforce each other (Collins & Miller, 1994). Aron et al. (1997) showed that closeness can develop when participants answer a sequence of increasingly personal questions, while small talk or less structured disclosure does not produce the same effect (Sprecher, 2021). Sprecher et al. (2013) also found that the way disclosures are exchanged matters, with different outcomes depending on whether participants reciprocate across turns. Similar findings have been reported in human–AI interaction, where disclosure to chatbots can produce emotional and relational responses comparable to disclosure to another person (Ho et al., 2018), and become more personal over repeated sessions (Skjuve et al., 2022). These studies suggest that examining only the current disclosure may miss the influence of earlier exchanges.

Evaluating Multi-Turn and Context-Dependent Behavior.
Multi-turn evaluation has established that prior context changes model behavior. Models degrade
when instructions are revealed incrementally (Laban et al., 2026), fail on context-dependent
dialogue tasks (Bai et al., 2024), and abandon their stance under sustained user pressure,
increasingly so as conversations lengthen (Hong et al., 2025; Tang et al., 2026). Closer to our study, Li et al. (2026b) found that affective framing of the current message increases sycophancy, while Kelley & Riedl (2026) showed that providing user-specific context increases affective alignment and deference. However, these studies do not examine how different disclosure histories affect model responses when the current message is kept fixed. Li et al. (2026c) manipulated conversation history by rewriting the model’s earlier responses, but focused on task performance rather than relational behaviors. Other studies have also shown that differences across multi-turn conversations may eventually stabilize due to generation noise (Dongre et al., 2025), and that models are sensitive to where information appears in the context (Liu et al., 2024). These findings make it important to control both the preceding conversation and variation from generation. In our study, we keep the current disclosure fixed, vary specific properties of earlier disclosure history, and compare the resulting behavioral differences with those observed between independently generated conversations.

## 3.1 Disclosure Depth

Altman & Taylor (1973) describes disclosure depth as the progression from relatively surface information toward more private, intimate, or emotionally vulnerable self-disclosure. Drawing on work showing progressively deeper interpersonal disclosure (Aron et al., 1997; Kardas et al., 2022), we define four levels: $D_{1}$ Public, $D_{2}$ Personal Preferences / Self-information, $D_{3}$ Personal Meaning, and $D_{4}$ Emotional Vulnerability. Detailed definitions and examples are provided in Appendix A.1.

To model everyday interaction we include mundane conversational turns such as small talk and routine check-ins (Goldsmith & Baxter, 1996). Motivated by prior disclosure coding work that distinguishes non-disclosure utterances from disclosures of varying levels of intimacy (Ho et al., 2018), we introduce $D_{0}$ as a category for conversational turns without self-disclosure. We generate $D_{0}$ separately from $D_{1}$ – $D_{4}$ and it receives no disclosure-specific conditioning and is not evaluated for disclosure depth. We illustrate a generated user utterance at each disclosure depth in Table 1.

We operationalize $D_{1}$ – $D_{4}$ using the utterance-level framework of Li et al. (2026a), which evaluates informational, cognitive, and emotional disclosure. We use Phi-4 (Abdin et al., 2024) as judge and map its annotations to increasing depth: $D_{1}$ when no dimension is present, $D_{2}$ when present dimensions are all low depth, $D_{3}$ when exactly one is high depth, and $D_{4}$ when two or more are high depth. We observe that generated disclosures matched intended depths at high rates, with similar depth-dependent patterns under GPT-4.1 (see Appendix A.2). The same evaluator is used to annotate disclosing participant utterances in ESConv (Liu et al., 2021) (see Appendix A.3).

## 3.2 Persona Construction

We construct personas along two independent dimensions: conversational topic and interpersonal vulnerability. To represent conversational topics relevant to human–AI interactions, we derive six broad topic domains from recurring themes in past studies (Skjuve et al., 2022; Ta-Johnson et al., 2022): Career, Friendship, Family, Romantic Relationships, Future/Aspirations, and Social Belonging (see Appendix A.4.1).

To represent interpersonal vulnerability, each persona is assigned one of seven Early Maladaptive Schemas (EMS) (Young et al., 2003): Abandonment/Instability, Mistrust/Abuse, Emotional Deprivation, Defectiveness/Shame, Social Isolation/Alienation, Dependence/Incompetence, or Emotional Inhibition. We selected these schemata because of their relevance to interpersonal fears and vulnerabilities that can become salient during self-disclosure (see Appendix A.4.2). Their use is further motivated by evidence that EMS related characteristics can be identified in naturally occurring disclosures (Ang et al., 2025).

The topic domain provides the conversational context, while the assigned EMS provides an underlying vulnerability structure that shapes how information is interpreted and disclosed, particularly at deeper disclosure levels (i.e. $D_{4}$ ). This means that the same topic can be expressed with distinct interpersonal vulnerabilities tuned by the EMS. Full persona attributes and generation specifications are provided in Appendix A.4.3.

For each persona, we additionally generate persona information corresponding to disclosure levels $D_{1}$ – $D_{4}$ , this includes the public information, personal preferences, meaningful experiences, and vulnerabilities. Shallower disclosures provide context for deeper ones through progressive generation. That is, public information at $D_{1}$ informs personal opinions and preferences at $D_{2}$ , and so on till vulnerabilities are expressed at $D_{4}$ . During conversation generation, the information generated for the target disclosure depth is provided to guide the disclosure turn. Full generation details are provided in Appendix A.5.

## 3.3 Conversation Pipeline

For each disclosure trajectory, we cover all combinations of the seven EMS and six conversational topics, generating three independent conversation chains for each combination. This yields $7\times 6\times 3=126$ conversations per trajectory for each evaluated model. We use Qwen3.6-35B-A3B (Qwen Team, 2026) for persona construction and conversation generation. During generation, the model role-plays the persona using the target disclosure depth information, persona characteristics, and the six most recent dialogue turns. We generate $D_{0}$ turns separately as mundane responses without depth evaluation.

## CONCLUSION
Our results show that a model’s response can depend on earlier disclosures, even when the current disclosure is matched on depth. We found differences in model behavior across disclosure histories, with some effects remaining after disclosure returned to shallower levels. Evaluating only the current exchange would miss these differences. By systematically varying the preceding disclosure trajectory, TraDE allows us to examine which behaviors are sensitive to earlier interactions. This helps us better understand the history dependence of conversational models at a behavioral level, although we cannot determine how models internally represent or use these earlier disclosures.

We also examined which properties of disclosure history contributed to these differences. Changing the deepest disclosure or the order of disclosures produced few significant differences. However, more differences were found when disclosure depth and the number of turns spent at deeper levels changed together. Our current demonstration therefore cannot determine whether depth, duration, or recency was responsible for these effects. Our persistence analysis was also limited to four shallow turns within the same conversation. Future experiments could manipulate these properties independently and examine whether history effects remain over longer conversations or across sessions.

Our human comparison further showed that models and human supporters differed in how their responses changed with disclosure. For several behaviors measured by INTIMA and ELEPHANT, significant shape differences were more frequently detected when disclosure receded or changed direction. In our qualitative examples, a human supporter adjusted their emotional acknowledgment according to the seeker’s immediate concerns, whereas a model continued referring to earlier emotions during later shallow disclosures. We do not, however, know whether users would perceive such responses as attentive or unnecessary. Sundar’s HAII-TIME framework offers one way to understand why these differences may matter, as users’ experiences interacting with AI can shape their perceptions of and trust in these systems (Sundar, 2020). Building on TraDE, future studies could test whether users perceive responses differently depending on the preceding disclosure history, even when the current disclosure is held constant. They could also examine whether these history-dependent responses influence what users choose to disclose next.

## ABSTRACT
Agent harnesses have become a central component of modern agentic systems, shaping how models use tools, manage context, and coordinate long-horizon interaction. Realizing the benefits of these harnesses depends on how effectively the underlying model can make use of them, motivating reinforcement learning directly through the harness. However, existing approaches typically train through a single fixed harness, which can lead to specialization and limited transfer across harness configurations. To address this, we introduce \textbf{Harness Scaling}, a new scaling dimension for agentic RL that extends training from a single harness to diverse execution configurations. Concretely, we construct a factorized harness space, select a compact training subset with balanced factorial coverage, and organize cross-harness training to combine global coverage with controlled variation for the same task. Across multiple benchmarks, model scales, and RL algorithms, Harness Scaling consistently improves generalization to unseen white-box configurations. Moreover, models trained through structured white-box variation transfer effectively to unseen external black-box harnesses, outperforming models trained through a single complex black-box harness. These results establish harness diversity as a meaningful scaling dimension for agentic RL and highlight the importance of how this diversity is organized for cross-harness generalization.

## 1 Introduction

Agent harnesses are playing an increasingly central role in modern
agentic systems [31]. By supporting sustained interaction, tool use, state
maintenance, and recovery from failures [4], harnesses have substantially
improved agent performance on complex, long-horizon tasks [38, 32, 16]. Yet the
benefits of a capable harness depend not only on its design, but also on
whether the underlying policy can use it effectively [14]. This naturally motivates optimizing the policy directly through the harness
with reinforcement learning [36, 39], which strengthens its ability to exploit the
available interaction mechanisms during task solving.

However, existing approaches typically optimize the policy through a
single fixed harness [7, 34]. While effective within the training harness, such
optimization may encourage specialization to a particular execution
configuration and weaken transfer across harnesses. We therefore introduce
Harness Scaling, extending policy optimization from a single
harness to diverse execution configurations.
Unlike task or environment
scaling [29, 6, 3, 35], which expands what agents solve or where they operate, Harness
Scaling introduces diversity in how policies execute and interact with the
same underlying task and environment, promoting generalization across
execution configurations.

A key consideration in Harness Scaling is how to model harness variation
and select a compact set of configurations for training.
Given the modular
nature of agent harnesses [7, 23], we factorize harness variation along five
components: system prompt, tool interface, observation, context management, and workflow.
Each component contains multiple realizations, whose combinations define a
factorial space of complete harness configurations. As variation along these
components increases, the space expands rapidly, while exhaustive training
can introduce substantial redundancy under a fixed budget. We therefore
preserve low-order factorial structure in a compact training set, balancing
individual component realizations and their pairwise combinations through
an orthogonal design [21, 8]. During reinforcement learning, the same task is paired
with structurally nearby harness configurations, providing controlled local
variation within each task while maintaining broad coverage across the
training space.

To demonstrate the effectiveness of Harness Scaling, we evaluate
cross-harness generalization across multiple benchmarks, model scales, and
RL algorithms. By training across diverse harness configurations, Harness
Scaling reduces specialization to any single harness and encourages more
generalizable agent behavior. Empirically, it consistently outperforms
single-harness RL on unseen white-box configurations.
More importantly, policies trained through structured white-box harness
variation transfer effectively to complex external black-box harnesses [18, 1],
outperforming single-harness black-box training.
These results establish harness diversity as a
meaningful scaling dimension for agentic RL, while showing that effective
scaling depends critically on how this diversity is organized during
training. In summary, our contributions are threefold:

• 

We introduce Harness Scaling as a new scaling dimension
for agentic reinforcement learning, distinct from conventional scaling
over tasks and environments, and study how training across diverse
harness configurations affects generalization beyond a single execution
setting.

• 

We develop a structured framework for Harness Scaling that
factorizes harness variation, selects a compact training set with balanced
factorial coverage, and organizes cross-harness training by assigning
structurally nearby configurations to the same task across the training space.

• 

We demonstrate across benchmarks, model scales, and RL algorithms
that Harness Scaling substantially improves generalization to unseen
white-box and external black-box harnesses, establishing both the value
of scaling across harnesses and the importance of organizing this scaling
structurally.

## 2 Related Work

## 2.1 Agentic Reinforcement Learning

Reinforcement learning has emerged as an important paradigm for improving
the capabilities of LLMs [40, 24]. Recent work has extended RL from single-turn
reasoning to increasingly complex agentic settings, including tool use [2, 28], web
navigation [20], software engineering [27], and professional workflows [33]. As agentic RL moves toward broader
capability, recent efforts have increasingly scaled training along the
task and environment dimensions. Task scaling [30, 3, 5] broadens the
distribution of problems used for policy optimization, while environment
scaling [6, 29, 35] expands the range of external settings in which agents interact and
receive feedback. These directions expand what agents learn to
solve and where they learn to operate, while often retaining a fixed
execution harness. In contrast, we study harness diversity as an additional
scaling dimension for agentic RL, focusing on policy learning and
generalization across execution configurations.

## 2.2 Harness Design and Optimization

The design of an agent harness can substantially shape how a language model
uses tools, maintains context, and performs long-horizon tasks [38, 14, 16].
Early agent systems largely relied on manually designed harnesses, while
subsequent work has explored automated harness improvement through search,
editing, and evolution based on task outcomes or execution
trajectories [13, 11, 23]. A complementary line of work directly optimizes
model policies within complex agent harnesses, including reinforcement
learning through black-box harnesses [36, 7, 26, 15, 17, 9]. These directions either
improve the harness itself or adapt a policy to a particular harness.
Harness Scaling instead studies policy optimization over a structured combinatorial space of harness configurations, with the goal of improving generalization to unseen configurations.

## 3.1 Problem Formulation

To study harness scaling systematically, we first formalize the space of
execution configurations and the generalization problem defined over it.
We construct a factorized harness space, define a structural notion of
proximity among its configurations, and formulate the generalization
objectives that motivate the structured harness selection and multi-harness
training procedures shown as in Figure 1.

## 3.1.1 Factorized Harness Space

A harness serves as the execution layer through which a policy interacts
with its environment. Rather than treating it as a monolithic implementation,
we factorize the harness into five components: system prompt,
tool interface, observation, context management,
and workflow, denoted by $S,T,O,C,$ and $W$ , respectively.
These components govern the instructions provided to the policy, tool access,
environment feedback, interaction history, and execution procedure,
collectively shaping how the policy perceives, acts, and maintains state
throughout task execution. This factorization provides an operational
decomposition of the harness, while allowing the effects of different
components to interact.

We instantiate each component through a family of component-specific
transformations. For a component $F\in\{S,T,O,C,W\}$ , let $F^{(0)}$ 
denote its base realization and $\tau_{F}^{(\ell)}$ the $\ell$ -th
transformation applied to it:

 $F^{(\ell)}=\tau_{F}^{(\ell)}\left(F^{(0)}\right).$ 

(1)

A transformation modifies only the target component while preserving the
remaining components, task semantics, and environment. For example, it may
reorganize the system prompt, alter tool schemas or naming conventions,
change observation feedback, modify context retention or compression, or
impose a different execution procedure. Detailed definitions and
implementations are provided in Appendix A.

Let $\mathcal{S}$ , $\mathcal{T}$ , $\mathcal{O}$ , $\mathcal{C}$ ,
and $\mathcal{W}$ denote the resulting realization sets. A complete harness
configuration selects one realization from each set, defining the factorized
harness space

 $\mathcal{H}=\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{T}\times\mathcal{O}\times\mathcal{C}\times\mathcal{W}.$ 

(2)

Each configuration $h\in\mathcal{H}$ therefore specifies a complete execution
setting for the same underlying task and environment.

The product structure of $\mathcal{H}$ also induces a natural notion of
structural proximity. For $h_{i},h_{j}\in\mathcal{H}$ , the Hamming distance
 $d_{\mathrm{H}}(h_{i},h_{j})$ counts the number of component dimensions on
which they differ. Equivalently, $\mathcal{H}$ forms a Hamming graph whose
adjacent configurations differ in exactly one component. We use this
distance to organize local cross-harness variation during training.

## 3.1.2 Generalization Objective

The central objective of Harness Scaling is to learn policies that remain
effective beyond the harness configurations observed during training. We
consider two complementary forms of cross-harness generalization: broad
combinatorial generalization within the constructed harness space and transfer
to complex real-world black-box harnesses beyond it.

In-Space Combinatorial Generalization.

Let $\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{tr}}\subset\mathcal{H}$ denote the harness
configurations used for training. We evaluate on held-out configurations in
 $\mathcal{H}\setminus\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{tr}}$ , whose complete
combinations are unseen during training. This setting provides broad coverage
of unseen combinations within the controlled factorized space.

Out-of-Space Generalization.

We further evaluate transfer to complex real-world black-box harnesses whose
execution configurations are not represented in $\mathcal{H}$ . This setting
examines whether training over structured white-box harness variation can
transfer to external execution systems beyond the constructed design space.

## 3.2 Harness Scaling with Structured Selection

As the factorized harness space grows combinatorially with its component
dimensions, exhaustive training can introduce substantial redundancy.
We therefore construct a compact training set that preserves broad factorial
coverage under a fixed optimization budget.

## 3.2.1 Factorial Coverage

The harness space in Eq. 2 can be viewed as a
factorial design, where each harness component corresponds to a factor
and its realizations correspond to different levels [21, 8]. To construct a compact
subset without losing basic coverage of this combinatorial space, we preserve
its low-order factorial structure: the representation of individual component
realizations and their pairwise combinations. These correspond to the
first- and second-order projections of the full factorial space, respectively.

Component Balance.

We first require the realizations of each component to be represented
evenly across the selected configurations. This ensures coverage along
every component dimension without overrepresenting particular realizations.

Pairwise Combination Balance.

Component balance alone does not control how realizations from different
components are combined. We therefore additionally balance all pairwise
combinations. Indexing the five component dimensions by
 $i,j\in\{1,\ldots,5\}$ , let $N_{ij}(\ell,\ell^{\prime})$ denote the number
of selected configurations containing the $\ell$ -th realization of
component $i$ together with the $\ell^{\prime}$ -th realization of component
 $j$ . Pairwise balance requires

 $N_{ij}(\ell,\ell^{\prime})=\lambda,$ 

(3)

where $\lambda$ is the common occurrence count for every realization
pair of any $i\neq j$ . In factorial-design terminology, this corresponds
to strength-2 balance, under which every pair of realizations from any two
components appears equally often in the selected set.

## 3.2.2 Harness Set Construction

We construct the training harness set using a strength-2 orthogonal design
over the component realizations in $\mathcal{H}$ . This provides a direct
construction of a compact subset satisfying the component-level and pairwise
balance defined above, without searching over possible subsets of the full
harness space.

The size of such a design is constrained by its pairwise coverage.
Suppose there are $M$ component dimensions, each with $L$ realizations,
and every pairwise realization combination occurs $\lambda$ times.
Since any two components admit $L^{2}$ realization combinations, the number
of selected configurations $N$ satisfies

 $N=\lambda L^{2}.$ 

(4)

Moreover, a strength-2 orthogonal design must satisfy the Rao
bound [21, 8],

 $N\geq 1+M(L-1).$ 

(5)

Pairwise balance determines the replication structure of the design, while
the Rao bound provides a lower bound on the number of configurations.
These conditions restrict the admissible design sizes.

We instantiate a strength-2 orthogonal design satisfying these constraints,
yielding the training harness set
 $\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{tr}}\subset\mathcal{H}$ . The resulting set preserves
exact component-level and pairwise balance using only a compact subset of the
full factorial space. While these low-order projections are balanced, many
complete harness combinations remain unseen during training, enabling
evaluation of in-space combinatorial generalization. Further details of the
construction and the resulting training configurations are provided in
Appendix B.

## 3.3 Reinforcement Learning across Harnesses

Training each task under multiple harnesses exposes the policy to different
execution configurations for the same underlying problem, encouraging the
learned behavior to generalize beyond a single harness. We realize this over
the selected harness set $\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{tr}}$ by pairing each task
with a small group of structurally related configurations. The assignment is
designed to combine controlled variation within each task with broad coverage
across the training harness space, and the resulting task–harness instances
form the basis for rollout collection and policy optimization.

## 3.3.1 Cross-Harness Task Assignment

We use a locality-aware strategy to organize how harness configurations are
assigned to tasks. For each task $x$ , we assign $K$ configurations from
 $\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{tr}}$ .
An anchor harness
 $h_{x}\sim\mathrm{Unif}(\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{tr}})$ is first sampled
uniformly from the training set, and the remaining $K-1$ configurations
are chosen from $\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{tr}}\setminus\{h_{x}\}$ as those
with the smallest Hamming distance $d_{\mathrm{H}}(h_{x},h)$ to the anchor.
This associates each task with a
small set of nearby configurations while allowing different tasks to cover
different regions of the training harness space. The resulting harness set
for task $x$ is

 $\mathcal{H}_{x}=\left\{h_{x},\,h_{x}^{(1)},\ldots,h_{x}^{(K-1)}\right\},$ 

(6)

where $h_{x}^{(1)},\ldots,h_{x}^{(K-1)}$ denote the $K-1$ nearest
configurations to $h_{x}$ under $d_{\mathrm{H}}$ .

This assignment combines local cross-harness variation with broad coverage
over training. For a given task, nearby harnesses provide related but distinct
execution configurations, while random anchor selection distributes different
tasks throughout $\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{tr}}$ . Each task therefore yields
 $K$ task–harness instances for rollout collection and policy optimization.
When $K=1$ , only the sampled anchor is assigned to the task, while different
tasks remain distributed across $\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{tr}}$ .

## CONCLUSION
In this work, we study Harness Scaling as a new scaling dimension for agentic
reinforcement learning, motivated by the limitations of optimizing policies
through a single fixed harness. We formalize harness diversity as a structured configuration space and
develop a framework that integrates structured harness selection with
cross-harness policy training. Across benchmarks,
model scales, and RL algorithms, Harness Scaling substantially improves
generalization to unseen white-box configurations and transfers effectively
to complex external black-box harnesses. Our results further show that the
benefits of scaling depend on how harness diversity is selected and incorporated into training, rather than on diversity alone.
More broadly, these findings expand the scope of agentic RL scaling beyond
what agents solve and where they operate to how they interact and execute,
positioning harness variation as a complementary dimension for improving
generalization across diverse execution systems.

# 2610.29504 — Rethinking Agentic Model Orchestration for Time Series Forecasting
Authors: Tong Guan, Tianxiang Zhan, Shirui Pan, Ming Jin
Comment: Accepted by the NeurIPS 2026 Workshop on Foundation Models for Temporal Systems (FMTS)
Categories: cs.LG, cs.AI

## ABSTRACT
Time series foundation models (TSFMs) perform unevenly across forecasting tasks. Recent work uses agentic model orchestration (AMO) to select or combine TSFMs, often outperforming individual forecasters. Yet these gains may reflect stronger or more complementary candidates rather than better orchestration. To disentangle these effects, we introduce FairAMO, a controlled evaluation protocol with frozen candidate forecasts. Experiments on GIFT-Eval show that AMO improves accuracy, but its gains are bounded by the candidate TSFMs. The best orchestrators reduce MASE by less than 6% relative to the best single TSFM across all tasks. These gains depend on complementarity (different candidates excel on different tasks) rather than the number of candidates. Adding members yields diminishing returns, and a redundant member can erase these gains. The value of historical evidence also saturates quickly. A few recent windows capture all gains, and further history adds none. These findings separate system-level forecasting gains from the contribution of orchestration itself.

## 1 Introduction

Time series forecasting (TSF) supports critical decisions across a wide range of domains (Jin et al., 2026; Guan et al., 2023; Guan et al., 2025; Guan et al., 2026a). Recent advances have expanded the forecasting paradigm from task-specific statistical and deep learning models to pretrained time series foundation models (TSFMs) that exhibit strong zero-shot generalization (Shi et al., 2025; Zhou et al., 2026; Guan et al., 2026b). This development has also motivated comprehensive benchmarks such as GIFT-Eval (Aksu et al., 2024), which covers more than 144,000 time series and 7 domains. Results on such diverse benchmarks show that no single TSFM consistently performs best across all datasets and forecasting settings. TimesFM (Das et al., 2024) excels in long-horizon forecasting with extended contexts. Moirai (Woo et al., 2024) robustly handles multivariate inputs with covariates. Chronos (Ansari et al., 2024) captures distinct seasonality in short univariate series. Treating any single model as universally best sacrifices higher accuracy for a significant fraction of real-world inputs. This performance heterogeneity makes model orchestration a promising strategy for practical deployment.

Figure 1: Effects of candidate strength and complementarity on orchestration performance.

Recent studies have explored agentic model orchestration (AMO) for selecting or combining candidate TSFMs.
Their reported improvements over individual forecasters are striking (Ning et al., 2026; Cao et al., 2025). Notably, AMO systems currently occupy all of the top 10 positions on the GIFT-Eval leaderboard. Yet, leaderboard dominance alone does not establish that these gains originate from the orchestration mechanism, since the systems are evaluated with different candidate pools. This lack of control introduces two fundamental confounding factors. (1) Candidate strength, which determines the performance level available to the system before orchestration. As illustrated in Figure 1 (A), the same orchestrator can achieve substantially different results simply because it operates on stronger candidate forecasters. (2) Candidate complementarity, which determines the potential headroom available for orchestration. As illustrated in Figure 1 (B), a complementary pool whose members excel on different tasks leaves far more room for orchestration to improve over its best member than a redundant pool whose members succeed and fail in similar settings. Consequently, observed system performance jointly reflects candidate strength, candidate complementarity, and orchestration ability (Figure 1 (C)). Without controlling the first two factors, existing evaluations cannot isolate the contribution of the orchestrator itself.

To disentangle these confounding factors, we introduce FairAMO, a controlled evaluation protocol built on a Frozen Forecast Bank. Within each comparison, all orchestrators operate on the same candidate forecasts and receive the same explicitly specified historical evidence. Each orchestrator must commit to either a model selection or a set of combination weights before the test labels are revealed, and all resulting forecasts are evaluated using the official GIFT-Eval protocol. By independently varying candidate pool composition, pool size, and evidence budget, our framework separates the contribution of the orchestrator from the properties of the candidate pool. Our contributions are threefold.
(1) We provide a systematic taxonomy and reproducibility analysis of existing AMO systems.
(2) We introduce FairAMO, a controlled protocol for comparing orchestration methods under identical candidate forecasts, evidence, and evaluation metrics for TSF.
(3) Results show that orchestration gains are strongly conditioned by the candidate pool, while greater orchestration complexity and additional historical evidence do not consistently improve forecasting performance.

## 2 Taxonomy and Reproducibility

Taxonomy.
We distinguish three adaptive mechanisms according to how orchestration decisions are formed.
(1) LLM-mediated orchestration uses an LLM to interpret forecasting evidence and guide model use, as in MoiraiAgent (Yan et al., 2026), TSOrchestra’s agent (Cao et al., 2025), and TimeCopilot’s agent framework (Garza and Rosillo, 2025).
(2) Feature-conditioned orchestration learns a mapping from time series descriptors to orchestration decisions, as in TimeRouter (Ning et al., 2026), Toto-2.0 FnF (Datadog, 2026), and FFORMA (Montero-Manso et al., 2020).
(3) Historical-error-driven orchestration determines decisions directly from past forecasting errors, as in RAES-Conductance-V (AriGoldbach, 2026) and TSOrchestra SLSQP (Cao et al., 2025).
The distinction concerns the decision mechanism, not merely whether training uses a loss function; multiple mechanisms may coexist within one system.

Reproducibility.
We examine whether public artifacts support reconstructing an orchestrator under a changed candidate pool and a specified historical evidence budget.
In the releases inspected in September 2026, MoiraiAgent, TimeRouter, and Toto FnF provide inference artifacts, but we did not locate complete training code for their orchestration components; RAES-Conductance-V and TSOrchestra SLSQP expose code for deriving and applying decisions.
Thus, we reproduce AMO methods by reusing or adapting available repository components.

## 3 Methodology

We treat every orchestrator as an ensemble function of candidate TSFMs. The framework has three stages, illustrated in Figure 2. (1) Frozen Forecast Bank.
All $M$ candidate TSFMs are run once on GIFT-Eval and are never retrained or fine-tuned afterwards. Their outputs are cached in a forecast bank indexed by (dataset, term, series, window, candidate, quantile), where dataset and term (short, medium, or long horizon) identify a GIFT-Eval task and window a rolling forecast origin. Each entry stores the input history and the candidate’s quantile forecasts. The bank has three regions: training backtests and optional validation backtests, both with ground truth attached, and test forecasts, whose ground truth is withheld. Every orchestrator draws its $K\leq M$ members from this same bank.
(2) Orchestrator Fitting.
Before any test label is revealed, the orchestrator receives the permitted evidence: the $N$ most recent training backtests, plus the validation backtests if available. Using only this evidence, it produces an orchestration strategy, either a model selection or a set of combination weights, which is then committed and frozen for the test period. No rerun, reweighting, or inspection of test scores is permitted after commitment.
(3) Test Evaluation.
The frozen strategy is applied to the cached test forecasts to produce the final prediction. Test labels are revealed only at this stage, and the official GIFT-Eval scorer then returns MASE and CRPS.

Evaluated Orchestrators.
We evaluate three groups of orchestrators as shown in Figure 2 (D). \scriptsizeR⃝ Reference orchestrators are five simple methods from no learning to perfect hindsight. Random samples one member of the candidate subset under a fixed seed. Dev-Selected Best uses only the permitted development evidence to nominate a single member applied to every config. Equal Weight averages the forecasts of the $K$ members with weight $w_{j}=1/K$ assigned to member $j$ , without learning any weights. Argmin Router commits, per config, to the member with the smallest evidence loss, $\hat{j}=\arg\min_{j}L_{\text{evidence}}(j,\text{config})$ , the official GIFT-Eval loss of member $j$ on its permitted evidence windows. Oracle selects the best member per input window from test labels and is reported only as a non-deployable upper bound. \scriptsizeE⃝ External orchestrators are the published methods introduced in Section 2: TimeRouter (Ning et al., 2026), Toto-2.0 FnF (Datadog, 2026), RAES-Conductance-Ensemble-V (AriGoldbach, 2026), TSOrchestra SLSQP (Cao et al., 2025), and TimeCopilot Median (TimeCopilot, 2026); the training procedures of TimeRouter and Toto-2.0 FnF are not fully public, so we reproduce them as style adaptations. \scriptsizeO⃝ Our re-implementations are two orchestrators that we rewrite from published designs to fit FairAMO: MoiraiAgent-Retrained (Yan et al., 2026), an LLM-mediated orchestrator retrained on the permitted evidence, and Small Patch Router, its numeric counterpart, trained on the same pairs and horizon. It patches the history and each candidate’s forecast, encodes every candidate with one shared temporal transformer, and scores them by attention across candidates.

## 4 Experiments

RQ1. With the candidate pool and the evidence budget held fixed, how much forecasting gain comes from the orchestrator itself rather than from the candidates it selects?

Existing orchestrators remain far from the ceiling that the candidate pool itself provides, leaving substantial room for improvement. Figure 2 (D) summarizes the 11 deployable methods in the same 5-candidate setting. The strongest deployable orchestrator, TSOrchestra SLSQP, reaches 0.658 MASE, a gain of 0.039 or 5.6% over the best single TSFM, TiRex2, at 0.697. Argmin Router reaches 0.662 and TimeRouter 0.665. The window-level oracle sits at 0.580, so even the strongest orchestrator still leaves two thirds of the distance to that ceiling uncovered. Every method that gains more than 0.01 relies on backtest evidence.

RQ2. As the candidate pool grows and as the backtest evidence budget grows, do orchestration gains keep improving, and at what point does each of the two stop paying off?

On pool size, adding members enlarges the exploitable error diversity, that is the complementarity, rather than the strength of the candidates themselves. Figure 3 shows the best single TSFM almost unchanged as the pool grows from two to five members, from 0.699 to 0.697, while the window-level oracle drops from 0.647 to 0.580 and the headroom $H$ grows from 0.052 to 0.117. The Argmin Router improves accordingly from 0.689 to 0.662 and its realization rises from 18.4% to 29.8%, but the increments shrink: the third member adds 5.1 points of realization, whereas the fourth and the fifth add about 3 points each.

On evidence, a few recent windows carry all of its value, which indicates that orchestration mechanisms depend on the validation segment closest in time to the test windows, and that adding training backtests beyond that point can even hurt. Figure 4 shows the Argmin Router improving from 0.672 with validation only to 0.661 once three training backtests are added, then drifting back to 0.664 as the budget grows to 100 backtests. TSOrchestra SLSQP peaks at 10 backtests with 0.658 and also degrades beyond it, so history past a few recent windows stops helping and can actively hurt.

RQ3. At a fixed pool size, how sensitive are orchestration methods to which candidates the pool contains, and what happens to that sensitivity when a weak candidate is introduced?

Orchestration methods are highly sensitive to composition, no less so than to pool size. Figure 5 shows that the same Argmin Router spans 0.670–0.690 MASE over the ten size-3 subsets, a spread of 0.020 that matches in magnitude the 0.027 gained by growing the pool from two to five members, and the spread in realization is wider still, from 9.8% to 32.3%, a factor of three. This sensitivity comes from complementarity rather than strength: the best single TSFM of every subset falls between 0.697 and 0.703, a difference of approximately 0.006, whereas subsets containing TiRex2 realize 28.5% of their headroom on average against 13.4% for subsets without it.

Introducing a weak candidate does not necessarily hurt; what matters is whether it contributes a new error profile. Toto2-313m is the weakest single model at 0.703, yet adding it to the six size-2 subsets that exclude it improves five of them, by up to 0.013 MASE. Conversely, a large headroom does not guarantee a realizable gain: Chronos2+Toto2-313m has a headroom of 0.048, close to the median, but realizes only 3.1%, because its members fail on the same configs and the evidence cannot separate them. Pools should therefore be grown by difference in error profile rather than by single-model ranking, and reported orchestration gains must be accompanied by the pool composition, since the realization of one and the same orchestrator can differ by a factor of three.

## 5 Conclusion

Under FairAMO, the central insight is that AMO improves accuracy, but its gains are bounded by the candidate pool, that these gains depend on complementarity rather than pool size, and that the value of historical evidence saturates quickly. However, the strongest methods are simple selection and weighting rules, leaving the contribution of LLM-mediated orchestration unclear. A single decision per config also limits realization of the available window-level headroom. Future work may therefore explore finer-grained commitments, agentic methods that use context beyond backtests, and candidate pools built around complementary error profiles rather than model rank.

## ABSTRACT
Large language models (LLMs) have shown great potential as general-purpose user simulators for interactive systems. Prior studies have demonstrated that training on high-quality data can substantially improve model reasoning and downstream task performance. However, the upper bound of user simulation is still constrained by the model's reasoning paradigm: native chain-of-thought often degenerates into shallow pattern matching and fails to adequately model the behavioral factors underlying real user decisions. In this paper, we propose OmniSimulator, a novel training framework designed to improve the predictive ability of small LLMs for realistic user simulation on short-video platform environments. Specifically, OmniSimulator organizes behavior-oriented reasoning through a structured schema with five dimensions, carefully selects data from real user-behavior scenarios, and introduces a teacher model to generate high-quality reasoning traces under the proposed schema, helping small models learn behavior-relevant reasoning patterns rather than relying on superficial behavioral correlations. Based on this process, we construct 9,881 high-quality training instances from three months of historical interaction trajectories of 200 real users, averaging about 50 distilled examples per user. Experimental results show that OmniSimulator improves the average score of Qwen3-8B by 34.33%, achieves performance competitive with Claude-Sonnet-4.5, and supports smaller-scale, self-hosted deployment. These results suggest that learning structured behavior-oriented reasoning patterns is a promising direction towards scalable and high-fidelity LLM-based user simulation.

## 1 Introduction

Large language models (LLMs) have recently shown strong promise in a growing range of downstream applications [30, 13, 22, 12, 8], including recommender systems [5, 10, 7], advertising [20], customer service [27, 14], and interactive agents [29, 26]. Beyond their generative ability, an increasingly appealing direction is to use LLMs as general-purpose user simulators [4, 18, 7], enabling offline evaluation, policy testing, product optimization, and behavior modeling at a scale that would otherwise require expensive online experiments [19, 18]. This trend is especially important for short-video platforms, where user behavior is inherently long-horizon, cross-scenario, and heterogeneous [9]: a user may browse videos, enter live rooms, click ads, shop in e-commerce, search for information, and interact with customer service within one unified behavioral trajectory. Recent studies have shown that training on high-quality data can substantially improve reasoning ability and downstream task performance of LLMs, suggesting that post-training and supervision quality are critical for building stronger simulators. However, real user simulation remains far from solved. On challenging real-world benchmarks such as OmniBehavior [9], even frontier models achieve limited performance, while also exhibiting systematic biases such as overestimating positive actions, generating overly optimistic utterances, and collapsing different users into an average persona [6, 9]. Moreover, although closed-source models such as Claude-Opus-4.5 [2] provide stronger performance, their cost becomes prohibitive when conditioning on long interaction histories, making them difficult to deploy at industrial scale.

A difficulty of user simulation is that real behavior is not generated by direct surface matching between historical interactions and the current scenario. Instead, user actions are mediated by latent mental states, such as stable preference, recent interest drift, transient fatigue, emotional tendency, and momentary decision style. In other words, predicting whether a user will click, purchase, continue watching, or say a particular utterance requires the model to first recover why this user is likely to act in that way at the current scenario. This observation naturally suggests that reasoning capability counts. If the model can explicitly infer the hidden state underlying the next action, it should in principle make more faithful predictions than a model that only performs shallow behavioral imitation. Chain-of-thought (CoT) therefore appears to be a natural candidate for improving user simulation, since it provides an intermediate reasoning process between observed history and predicted behavior. However, our empirical analysis reveals that simply enabling native thinking mode brings almost no benefit for user simulation: across Qwen3 models, the performance of thinking and non-thinking settings is often nearly identical, and in some cases the thinking variant even performs worse. A closer inspection of native CoT outputs explains why: existing models rarely use reasoning to reconstruct the user’s latent mental state; instead, they tend to produce generic justifications, shallow pattern matching, or direct guesses based on superficial correlations. This suggests that the bottleneck is not merely whether the model generates more reasoning tokens, but whether its reasoning follows a behavior-oriented structure.

Motivated by this, we propose OmniSimulator, a novel training framework designed to improve small LLMs for real user simulation on short-video platform environments. The key idea is to teach the model to follow behavior-oriented structured reasoning rather than free-form reasoning. Specifically, OmniSimulator introduces a structured CoT schema that forces the model to infer latent user states through five dimensions: User Profile Grounding, Historical Behavioral State Inference, Content Relevance Alignment, Decision Mode Identification, and Final Decision Declaration. On top of this schema, we build a task-aware causal distillation pipeline: a teacher model (GPT-OSS-120B [1]) generates multiple reasoning traces for each prediction point under task-specific prompting strategies, and only traces consistent with ground truth are retained through rejection sampling. Following this process, we construct 9,881 high-quality distilled training instances from three months of interaction histories of 200 real users.
Experiments show that OmniSimulator substantially improves small models over zero-shot and free-CoT baselines, enables Qwen3-8B to achieve performance competitive with strong proprietary models such as Claude-Sonnet-4.5 [3], and alleviates overestimation bias while supporting smaller-scale deployment. These results suggest that the upper bound of LLM-based user simulation is constrained not only by model scale, but also by the reasoning supervision paradigm.

Our main contributions are summarized as follows:

• 

We identify a key weakness of existing LLM-based user simulators: native CoT struggles to predict realistic user behavior, yielding only marginal improvements in behavioral prediction over non-thinking settings.

• 

We propose OmniSimulator, a novel training framework that combines a structured five-dimensional CoT schema with a task-aware causal distillation pipeline, enabling small models to learn behavior-oriented reasoning patterns for user simulation.

• 

We construct 9,881 high-quality distilled training instances from real-world long-horizon user trajectories, and OmniSimulator substantially improves small LLMs, achieves performance competitive with strong closed-source baselines and mitigates overestimation bias while supporting efficient small-model deployment.

## 2 Methodology

In this section, we first formulate user behavior simulation on short-video platforms, and then present OmniSimulator, our training framework for improving small LLMs on realistic user simulation. The central idea is that real user behavior is not generated by direct surface matching between historical interactions and the current scenario. Instead, it is mediated by a latent decision process shaped by stable preference, recent interest drift, transient affect, and the decision regime at the current moment. Existing LLMs, even with native thinking mode, often fail to reconstruct this hidden process and instead rely on shallow behavioral priors. To address this issue, OmniSimulator explicitly teaches the model to infer latent user states before predicting behavior. The framework consists of two tightly coupled components: (1) a structured chain-of-thought schema that organizes reasoning into a fixed sequence of human-like internal deliberation steps, and (2) a task-aware rationale distillation pipeline that converts teacher-generated reasoning traces into high-quality supervision for student models.

## 2.1 Problem Formulation

We formulate user simulation as a user-conditioned prediction problem over long-horizon, cross-scenario interaction trajectories. For a user $u$ , let

 $H_{t}^{u}=\{e_{1}^{u},e_{2}^{u},\dots,e_{t}^{u}\}$ 

(1)

denote the historical interaction sequence before time step $t+1$ , where each event $e_{i}^{u}$ may come from different scenarios such as video browsing, live streaming, e-commerce shopping, customer-service dialogue, advertisement recommendation, and search. Let $x_{t+1}^{u}$ denote the current scenario context. The goal is to predict the corresponding user response

 $y_{t+1}^{u}=f_{\theta}(H_{t}^{u},x_{t+1}^{u}),$ 

(2)

where $y_{t+1}^{u}$ may be a binary decision, a continuous value, or a natural-language utterance.

A direct mapping from $(H_{t}^{u},x_{t+1}^{u})$ to $y_{t+1}^{u}$ is often insufficient. In realistic platforms, a user’s next behavior is mediated by hidden mental states that are not explicitly recorded in logs, such as stable preference, short-term tendency, fatigue, emotion, and momentary decision style. We therefore introduce an implicit latent state $z_{t}^{u}$ and decompose user simulation as

 $p(y_{t+1}^{u}\mid H_{t}^{u},x_{t+1}^{u})=\sum_{z_{t}^{u}}p(y_{t+1}^{u}\mid z_{t}^{u},x_{t+1}^{u})\,p(z_{t}^{u}\mid H_{t}^{u}).$ 

(3)

Since $z_{t}^{u}$ is unobserved, OmniSimulator does not supervise it as an explicit label. Instead, we represent it as a structured reasoning trace in natural language, and train the model to infer this trace before answering the final behavior prediction.

## 2.2 Preliminary Analysis: Revealing Native Thinking Effects

Figure 2: Preliminary analysis on native thinking mode. Across Qwen backbones, enabling native thinking yields only marginal or inconsistent gains over standard instruction-following inference.

We first investigate whether native thinking mode alone is sufficient for realistic user simulation. Intuitively, this task appears to benefit from reasoning, since the model must recover the hidden factors that connect long interaction histories to current decisions. However, our preliminary analysis reveals that this intuition does not hold under standard prompting.

Figure 2 compares the average OmniBehavior scores of multiple Qwen3-series [28] backbones under two settings: Instruct and Thinking. We observe that enabling native thinking yields little or no consistent gain over standard instruction-following inference. Across model scales, the two settings are often nearly identical, and in some cases the thinking variant even performs worse. This result suggests that the bottleneck is not the absence of additional reasoning tokens, but the absence of the right reasoning structure. By inspecting native CoT outputs, we find that models often produce generic justifications or shallow guesses, failing to explicitly reconstruct the user’s latent psychological state. This observation motivates our approach: rather than simply allowing the model to think more, we guide it to follow a structured, behavior-oriented reasoning process.

## 2.3 Overview of OmniSimulator

Figure 1 illustrates the overall framework. For each prediction point, we first provide the teacher model with the user’s historical interaction trajectory and the current scenario context. The teacher is required to reason under a structured schema that explicitly reconstructs the user’s latent mental state. To increase diversity, we sample multiple reasoning traces for each example with stochastic decoding. Depending on the task type, we apply different prompting strategies to control the degree of access to ground truth. The resulting traces are filtered by rejection sampling, and only those whose final answers are consistent with the observed outcome are retained as supervised fine-tuning data. Finally, the student model is trained to generate both the structured reasoning trace and the final prediction.

This design follows a simple principle: rather than only teaching the model what to predict, we also provide structured rationale supervision for how the prediction may be derived from behavioral evidence. In this way, latent-state reconstruction becomes explicit, learnable, and distillable.

## Structured Chain-of-Thought Schema.

The first stage of OmniSimulator is a structured CoT schema tailored for user simulation. Our design is motivated not only by the empirical analysis above, but also by cognitive theories of human behavior. Prior work suggests that behavior is mediated by stable person-level characteristics, dynamically activated cognitive-affective states, and their interaction with situational cues [17]. In parallel, dual-process accounts further distinguish between reflective, deliberative decision making and more impulsive behavior pathways [21]. Native thinking mode alone is therefore insufficient, because it leaves this behavioral reasoning path under-specified for human behavior simulation tasks. To address this issue, we require the model to generate reasoning within a <think> block following five fixed dimensions:

1. 

User Profile Grounding: The model first interprets stable user characteristics reflected in long-term history, such as age, gender, educational background. This step provides the global behavioral background of the user.

2. 

Historical Behavioral State: The model then infers the user’s dynamic internal state from recent and long-horizon interactions. This includes long-term interest continuation, short-term preference drift, current fatigue level, and emotional tendency. Compared with the previous step, the focus here is not the user’s general profile, but the user’s state from the history sequences at the current moment.

3. 

Content Relevance Alignment: Next, the model matches the inferred user state with the current scenario and content. This step examines whether the current item is aligned with the user’s active interests, intent, mood, or aversion, and serves as the bridge between historical state and prediction item.

4. 

Decision Mode Identification: The model further determines whether the current behavior is primarily driven by rational decision or random decision. Rational decisions are mainly explained by interpretable factors such as fatigue levels in this session, while random decisions are more influenced by curiosity or stochasticity. This distinction is essential for modeling negative outcomes and low-confidence actions.

5. 

Final Decision Declaration: Before closing the </think> block, the model should explicitly state its final decision, and this declaration is required to be fully consistent with the final output answer. This design reduces reasoning-answer mismatch and improves the controllability of the generated traces.

With this schema, OmniSimulator decomposes user simulation into the following process:

 $(H_{t}^{u},x_{t+1}^{u})\rightarrow r_{t}^{u}\rightarrow y_{t+1}^{u},$ 

(4)

where $r_{t}^{u}$ denotes the structured reasoning trace. In practice, $r_{t}^{u}$ serves as a textual proxy of the unobserved latent state $z_{t}^{u}$ . Compared with unconstrained CoT, this schema forces the model to move from observable behaviors to hidden user states, and then from hidden user states to final actions.

## Task-aware Rationale Distillation.

Structured reasoning alone is not sufficient if teacher-generated traces are noisy. We therefore design a task-aware rationale distillation pipeline to obtain high-quality supervision for student training.

For each prediction item, we use a teacher model (GPT-OSS-120B [1] in our experiment) to rollout $N$ candidate reasoning traces under the structured schema. Formally, given an example $(H_{t}^{u},x_{t+1}^{u},y_{t+1}^{u})$ , we sample

 $\mathcal{R}_{t}^{u}=\{r_{t,1}^{u},r_{t,2}^{u},\dots,r_{t,N}^{u}\}$ 

(5)

with relatively high temperature. This design allows the teacher to generate multiple candidate rationales for each prediction instance. ## LIMITATIONS SECTION
The current study evaluates held-out users from one short-video platform within a temporally overlapping collection window, and therefore does not establish temporal or cross-platform generalization. Moreover, the structured reasoning traces provide behavior-oriented supervision rather than direct evidence of users’ actual cognitive processes. In particular, the distilled reasoning traces may still inherit part of the teacher model’s own biases, while target-derived guidance for continuous and textual tasks introduces a risk of post-hoc rationalization. Although our ablations support the effectiveness of the full framework, the respective contributions of the schema and filtering have not been fully isolated under strictly matched conditions. Future work will investigate broader-domain generalization, the faithfulness of target-conditioned rationales, and robustness across independent training runs.

## ABSTRACT
This paper proposes Role-Enhanced Zero-Shot Chain-of-Thought (RE-ZS-CoT), a prompting framework that systematically integrates domain-specific expert roles to enhance reasoning in large language models (LLMs) without task-specific examples. We assess two versions of our approach. In the first version, static role selection, we evaluate eight predefined expert roles across arithmetic, commonsense, and reading comprehension benchmarks to determine their impact on reasoning performance. In the second version, dynamic role selection, each model autonomously selects the single most suitable role for each benchmark, enabling adaptive task-role alignment without manual specification. Across 20 LLMs and seven benchmarks, RE-ZS-CoT frequently surpasses zero-shot and zero-shot-CoT baselines, achieving performance improvements of up to 44 percentage points. The results reveal a strong correspondence between static and dynamic versions; the benchmarks where static roles yielded improvements were almost always those where dynamic roles also outperformed baselines, though static selection achieved peak performance. Ablation studies further show that unconventional roles degrade performance, underscoring the necessity of task-role alignment. The framework's scalability is validated across model sizes (2b-14b parameters), with smaller models like Granite3.3-8b achieving remarkable proficiency in arithmetic reasoning. RE-ZS-CoT advances zero-shot prompting by unifying role specialization with chain-of-thought reasoning, offering a practical, resource-efficient solution for improving LLM capabilities across diverse domains.

## I Introduction

Large language models (LLMs) exhibit enhanced reasoning capabilities when directed by structured prompting techniques, such as chain-of-thought (CoT) [1] and zero-shot-CoT [2]. However, these methodologies frequently demonstrate inconsistent performance across various tasks, particularly in zero-shot scenarios where task-specific examples are unavailable. Recent studies explore various strategies to enhance reasoning in large language models, including decoding techniques to elicit CoT reasoning without explicit prompts [3], structured prompting methods like Plan-and-Solve to improve zero-shot reasoning [4], and frameworks such as Graph of Thoughts that model LLM outputs as graphs for complex problem-solving [5]. Additionally, LLMs demonstrate improved reasoning by taking on a persona, which often leads to greater accuracy, though not consistently [6], [7]. However, evaluations of role-enhanced prompting, particularly regarding its scalability, adaptability, and effects across reasoning domains, remain unexplored.

We present Role-Enhanced Zero-Shot Chain-of-Thought (RE-ZS-CoT), a prompting framework that integrates expert role specification into single-turn, zero-shot reasoning without requiring task examples (Fig. 1). Unlike previous work relying on fixed templates ([6], [7], [8]), RE-ZS-CoT fills the above-stated gap by evaluating two versions. Version 1 assesses eight roles across three prompt variants (pre-question, post-question, split-phrase; Fig.2) and uses self-alignment, leveraging recent advances in aligning LLM behavior [9]; [10]; [11]. Version 2 shifts to an adaptive framework where each LLM independently selects the most suitable role for each benchmark, enabling self-optimization of task-role alignment without manual adjustment, using the same three prompt variants.

In version 1 (static self-alignment), we identify optimal roles for open-weight models under computational constraints. For OpenAI models, we test all eight roles on GPT-4o-mini, while larger models (GPT-4o, GPT-4.1) use only the best-performing role due to cost. Open-weight models (2–3b, 7–9b, 12–14b) use quantized instruct-tuned versions (q4-K-M). We evaluate 20 LLMs across seven benchmarks spanning arithmetic, commonsense, and reading comprehension. RE-ZS-CoT yields 12–44% higher accuracy than zero-shot and zero-shot-CoT baselines, with the largest gains in arithmetic tasks (AQuA-RAT, MATH). Role effects vary: domain-specific roles (e.g., mathematician) outperform general ones in arithmetic structured tasks, consistent with findings from a previous study [7].

The contributions of this paper are: (1) We introduce RE-ZS-CoT, the first framework that unifies role-enhancement with zero-shot-CoT reasoning, demonstrating broad applicability across seven benchmarks and 20 LLMs. (2) We show that the efficacy of role specialization is task-dependent, with math reasoning benefiting the most from domain-aligned roles. (3) We provide actionable insights for prompt engineering, highlighting the superiority of pre-question prompts and adaptive role selection for open-weight models. (4) Our ablation studies reveal novel findings regarding the impact of non-traditional roles and lexical variations, advancing the design of context-aware prompting strategies.

## II Related Work

## II-A Chain-of-Thought Prompting for Reasoning.

CoT prompting enhances complex reasoning by eliciting intermediate steps [1]. [2] showed zero-shot-CoT via simple prompts like "Let’s think step by step," but such prompts remain sensitive to phrasing, producing unstable outputs. Techniques like self-consistency decoding [12] and instruction tuning [13] improve the reliability of CoT and zero-shot prompting, respectively, but rely heavily on generic or example-based prompts. Smaller models (<100B parameters) particularly struggle without fine-tuning [14]. Syntactic variations were explored by [15], but without domain-specific reasoning. Recent work emphasizes instance-adaptive prompts [8], variability among equivalent prompts [16], and reasoning via decoding alone [3], highlighting the need for methods like RE-ZS-CoT to improve zero-shot reliability without extensive prompt engineering.

Recent advancements in reasoning architectures have shifted the paradigm toward models that natively generate reasoning traces via large-scale reinforcement learning (RL), often referred to as test-time compute or System 2 thinking models [17, 18]. Unlike standard CoT prompting, which relies on explicit user instructions to elicit step-by-step logic, these native reasoning models automatically allocate additional computational resources at inference time to plan, search, and self-correct. While these approaches achieve state-of-the-art performance on complex benchmarks, they introduce substantial latency and inference-time overhead. In contrast, RE-ZS-CoT explores the orthogonal direction of maximizing the reasoning efficiency of standard, non-RL-tuned language models by leveraging domain-specific personas.

## II-B Role-Based Prompting

Role-enhanced prompting improves LLM reasoning by biasing models toward domain expertise. [6] show that instructing models to "act as a domain expert" boosts accuracy, and [7] demonstrate that role-playing enhances zero-shot reasoning. However, prior methods rely on curated examples [19], narrow domains [7], or manual prompt templates [20]. These results create inconsistencies and reliance on a rigid template, which is not always effective. Moreover, the research on role prompting has not been conducted on a large scale, particularly in the zero-shot-CoT domain. Recent work has systematically evaluated social roles in system prompts, finding that interpersonal roles such as "friend" or "teacher" outperform generic "helpful assistant" across diverse tasks, although optimal role prediction remains challenging [21]. Others explore role-playing for domain adaptation, using unique role prompts per domain alongside self-distillation to prevent catastrophic forgetting during multi-domain fine-tuning [22]. Interpretable steering via sparse autoencoders extracts role-specific features for fine-grained control over reasoning behavior [23].

In mathematical reasoning, role-play prompts such as "helpful mathematician," combined with zero-shot-CoT, were tested but showed no improvements and sometimes degradation due to role-task mismatch and over-prompting [24]. These works, often limited to a few datasets and models without testing role placement variants or dynamic selection, underscore RE-ZS-CoT’s advantages: it scales across 20 LLMs and seven benchmarks, incorporates static/dynamic role selection with prompt variants (pre-question, post-question, split), and unifies role specialization with zero-shot CoT in single-turn prompts, achieving 12–44% higher accuracy.

## III Role-Enhanced Zero-Shot Chain of Thought

We introduce RE-ZS-CoT as shown in Figure 3 , a prompting technique that integrates role-based prompting within a zero-shot-CoT reasoning framework. Building on previous research [2, 7, 6, 24], our approach adheres to a zero-shot methodology for CoT reasoning while incorporating role-enhanced prompts to bolster the reasoning capabilities of LLMs. Distinct from traditional zero-shot-CoT, which relies on a generic template applicable across diverse tasks and often leads to suboptimal performance in specific evaluations, such as those requiring commonsense reasoning, we utilize two versions of our approach.

In the first version, there are eight specialized roles: Mathematician, Physicist, Physician, Scientist, Literacy Specialist, Engineer, Software Developer, and Polymath. This targeted role assignment allows us to create prompts that are more finely attuned to the requirements of each task. The second version examines whether a more adaptive, on-the-fly selection can enhance the roles identified in version 1. For this, we implemented a dynamic role selection mechanism. In this experimental setup, we prompted each model to choose the single most suitable role for each specific benchmark. In addition, unlike the two-step prompting approach employed in zero-shot-CoT, where, according to its original form, reasoning is first extracted before generating the final answer, RE-ZS-CoT leverages a single prompt to extract answers from the model’s response. Figure 1 shows an example of our approach compared to zero-shot and zero-shot-CoT.

It is important to note that we do not benchmark RE-ZS-CoT against existing role-prompting frameworks. The fundamental integration of role-playing with zero-shot chain-of-thought reasoning is not in itself a novel concept; prior studies have already established that domain-specific personas can improve reasoning [7, 24]. However, existing literature has largely overlooked the critical mechanics of how these roles are selected and where the role phrases are placed within the prompt. Therefore, our approach serves as a rigorous, large-scale evaluation of these underexplored dimensions. We introduce unique role selection techniques, specifically contrasting static, predefined assignments with dynamic, autonomous inference, and systematically investigate the structural impact of role-phrase placement (R→Q, Q→R, and R→Q→R). Because our primary contribution lies in isolating the effects of these specific selection and placement strategies across 20 distinct LLMs, we strictly compare our framework against standard zero-shot and zero-shot-CoT baselines rather than other specialized role-prompting methods.

## III-A Prompt Variants

Our prompt consists of: (i) a role prompt R and (ii) the task question Q. We evaluate three placement strategies, as shown in Fig. 2: R→Q, Q→R, and the split form R→Q→R. This setup allows us to examine whether the placement of the role phrase affects the performance of LLMs, and whether defining a role beforehand, as done in a previous study [7], can enhance the LLM’s role-playing capabilities. Prior studies have shown that role-play phrases can improve factuality and reasoning ([7]. Our strategy tests whether these improvements persist under strict zero-shot settings at scale, across LLMs of varying sizes and benchmarks.

## IV Implementation Details

## Benchmarks

We evaluate our approach on seven datasets in three categories: arithmetic reasoning, commonsense reasoning, and reading comprehension. These datasets are commonly used in the benchmarking of LLMs. For a detailed description of each dataset, see [Appendix A].

For arithmetic reasoning, we consider the following three datasets: AQuA-RAT [25], and GSM8K [26]. AQuA-RAT contains algebraic word problems. It is designed to assess a model’s ability not only to solve the problem but also to generate a detailed, step-by-step rationale for its answer in natural language. GSM8K contains grade school math word problems that are linguistically diverse. It primarily checks a model’s ability to perform multi-step reasoning, as the questions require between two and eight steps using basic arithmetic to arrive at the final answer.

For commonsense reasoning, we use OpenBookQA [27], and StrategyQA [28]. OpenBookQA contains questions that require multi-step reasoning and the use of commonsense knowledge. StrategyQA requires models to infer an implicit multi-hop reasoning to answer questions.

For reading comprehension, we use RACE (h&m) [29] and TriviaQA [30]. RACE is a reading comprehension dataset containing questions from the examination of middle (RACE-m) and high school (RACE-h) students. TriviaQA is a reading comprehension dataset containing question-answer pairs authored by trivia enthusiasts and independently gathered evidence documents.

## Large Language Models

We conduct experiments utilizing a total of 20 LLMs, ranging from 2b to 14b open-weights models along with large closed-source models from OpenAI. This exploration aims to investigate the impact of our approach on varying parameter sizes, extending from large to small. For the main experiments, we use all 20 models, which are listed as follows: GPT-4.1, GPT-4.1-mini, GPT-4.1-nano, GPT-4o, GPT-4o-mini, Phi4:14b [31], Qwen2.5:14b [32], Gemma3:12b [33], Mistral-nemo:12b-instruct [34], Gemma2:9b-instruct [35], Granite3.2:8b-instruct [36], Granite3.3:8b [37], Llama3.1:8b-instruct [38], Mistral:7b-instruct [39], Qwen2.5:7b-instruct [32], Llama3.2:3b-instruct [40], Qwen2.5:3b-instruct [32], Granite3.2:2b-instruct [36], Granite3.3:2b [37], and Gemma2:2b-instruct [35].

All the open-weights models are used with the latest available versions at the time of writing. We explicitly list the full names of all models [see Appendix A-C], including the closed-source OpenAI models, to ensure traceability in case of future updates.

## Baselines and Answer Detection

We compare our approach with standard zero-shot prompting and zero-shot-CoT. This is done to verify the effectiveness of our approach as we measure both zero-shot-CoT and zero-shot versions of our method. For our experiments, we use the same prompt for zero-shot and zero-shot-CoT, with the difference being the added “Let’s think step by step.” at the end of the question for zero-shot-CoT; see Fig. 1 for an example. In contrast to the two-stage prompting employed in prior research conducted by [2], we utilize a one-stage approach for all prompts, including zero-shot-CoT, to ensure an equitable comparison.

For answer detection in evaluation, drawing inspiration from previous research [2], we examine the model’s output and select the first token that aligns with the dataset’s answer specifications (for instance, a capital letter A–E for multiple-choice questions, the first valid numeral for arithmetic problems. The captured string is subjected to a cleaning process: punctuation, LaTeX wrappers, currency symbols, and thousands separators are removed, and logical terms (True/False) are converted to their corresponding letter equivalents prior to being returned as the prediction. In instances where we fail to identify an explicit cue, such as “The answer is …,”, we resort to this reverse scanning method; if no matches are found, we document a failure and classify the item as incorrect. In summary, all tasks adhere to the same procedure: identify the earliest compliant answer token, normalize it, and compare it to the answer label.

## Static Role Self-Alignment

For the static roles, we asked the open-weights LLMs themselves to select the most suitable roles from a predefined list based on the provided task description. Each LLM was queried 100 times, and we selected the two most frequently chosen roles to use in the experiments. This approach is inspired by previous work [9]; [10]; [11], which leverages LLMs to self-align themselves on specific tasks. We adopted this method for several reasons, besides our financial and computational constraints. Namely, unlike complete pilot runs, which require testing all role-prompt combinations, this strategy uses just a few lightweight meta-prompts per benchmark, making it ideal for resource-constrained settings. As new tasks or roles emerge, the same self-assessment procedure can be reused without manual ontology construction or extensive sampling. This property can offer a notable engineering advantage. Full details are provided in [Appendix V-C].

For closed-source models, we executed all roles for each task on GPT-4o-mini and subsequently selected the most effective role for the larger and more costly models (GPT-4o, GPT-4.1), considering financial limitations. Furthermore, we implemented all roles for each task on GPT-4.1-mini and nano. We tested all roles on GPT-4o-mini, GPT-4.1-mini, and nano to not only cross-validate the self-alignment approach but also to assess the performance of all roles on all tasks for a broader impact and analysis.

## LIMITATIONS SECTION
While RE-ZS-CoT demonstrates broad empirical success, our study has several important limitations. First, the lack of transparency in LLM training constrains our work. Many models, especially closed-source ones like GPT-4.1 and GPT-4o, lack public documentation on their training datasets. This opacity makes reproducibility difficult and obscures whether the observed improvements stem from genuine role-based reasoning pathways or latent memorization. Consequently, this limits our ability to conduct comprehensive fairness and bias evaluations, as the composition of the training data heavily influences how a model defines and operationalizes a given "expert" role.

Second, while our massive-scale evaluation highlights the variance in RE-ZS-CoT’s efficacy, most notably its degraded performance on generalized commonsense tasks like OpenBookQA, we do not conduct a deep qualitative or mechanistic error analysis. Our current study successfully identifies when and where the method struggles, but leaves the precise how and why unanswered. We hypothesize that rigid role constraints may induce overthinking in intuitive contexts, but further interpretability research is required to map exactly how persona adoption shifts internal representations of reasoning.

Third, our study relies on the broad empirical consensus generated by testing 20 distinct LLMs across seven datasets, reporting absolute accuracy gains rather than conducting paired statistical significance testing (e.g., McNemar’s test) for every individual model-task pair. While macro-trends and double-digit percentage surges (e.g., on AQuA-RAT) provide robust evidence of structural shifts in reasoning, smaller single-digit improvements on datasets with limited sample sizes should be interpreted as competitive performance rather than definitive algorithmic superiority.

Fourth, our evaluations exclusively focus on standard autoregressive language models. With the recent paradigm shift toward test-time compute and natively reasoning models (System 2 thinkers) that utilize reinforcement learning to automatically generate search and reasoning traces (e.g., OpenAI o-series, DeepSeek-R1), the interaction between explicit zero-shot role prompting and implicit RL-driven reasoning remains unexplored. Finally, our experimental scope is constrained to English text-only corpora. Evaluating the efficacy and cultural nuances of RE-ZS-CoT in non-English corpora, conversational multi-turn tasks, and multimodal environments remains an important area for future exploration

## CONCLUSION
Our experiments show that role-enhanced prompting boosts zero-shot reasoning across tasks and model sizes. RE-ZS-CoT biases LLMs towards domain-specific reasoning. This aligns with previous research on plan-and-solve strategies [4]. The placement of the role phrase (pre-question, post-question, split-phrase) affects performance, and variability across models and benchmarks necessitates adaptive prompt engineering rather than rigid templates. Unconventional roles led to lower performance, revealing that LLMs struggle with conflicting or irrelevant expertise. Lexical variation experiments confirm that terms like "expert" in role prompts are significant; substituting them with "amateur" or omitting them lowered accuracy, reinforcing that explicit expertise cues guide LLMs toward structured, factual reasoning.

Our experiments reveal an interesting interaction between the role self-alignment strategy and the nature of the reasoning task. Static role self-alignment demonstrates superior efficacy in highly structured domains that benefit from specialized knowledge. This is most pronounced on the AQUA-RAT benchmark, where leveraging a specific role like "Mathematician" consistently yields peak performance. Conversely, the dynamic role selection mechanism, which permits the model to infer the most suitable persona autonomously, often proves more advantageous for tasks requiring broader or more adaptive reasoning. On commonsense reasoning benchmarks, such as StrategyQA, and reading comprehension tasks, like RACE (h&m), the dynamic approach frequently leads to improved outcomes, suggesting that flexibility in role adoption is beneficial when the reasoning path is less prescribed. These findings suggest a fundamental trade-off: static roles maximize performance when a task’s requirements align perfectly with a known expert archetype, but this specialization can be suboptimal for more multifaceted problems. Dynamic selection, while not always reaching the same performance ceiling on specialized tasks, offers a more robust and generalizable strategy, mitigating the risk of a misaligned persona.

The rapid emergence of test-time compute and natively reasoning models raises a critical question: Does role-based zero-shot prompting still hold value when models are trained to reason automatically via reinforcement learning?

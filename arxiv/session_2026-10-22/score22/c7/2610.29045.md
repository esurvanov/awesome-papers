## ABSTRACT
We introduce HumanCentric-Scenes-AI, a human-curated dataset of 296 AI-generated stimuli that depict people across four contexts in which people encounter AI-generated media. The Surveillance subset contains 85 AI-generated CCTV-style images. The Occupations subset contains 128 AI-generated occupational portraits in a balanced paired design covering 64 occupations with one female and one male version of each. The AI-edited subset contains 27 real photographs in which a region was altered or replaced by a generative model. The Video subset contains 56 AI-generated short videos. We curated the images and videos manually over multiple rounds to make them as photorealistic as possible. For each stimulus we release the file, the generation prompt where recorded, and per-stimulus metadata. We report depicted-gender distributions from an initial set of 134 generations without a specified gender and compare them with U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics occupational gender shares. We report edit-region size and its association with zero-shot detection difficulty for the AI-edited subset. We report image-quality distributions for the Surveillance subset and subject-category coverage for the Video subset. HumanCentric-Scenes-AI is intended first for human-perception research, and also for calibration of AI-detection classifiers and bias analysis of generative models.

## 1 Introduction

Generative image and video models now produce scenes of people that
viewers often cannot distinguish from photographs
[15, 12, 8].
Detection research has responded with large benchmarks of AI-generated
media. Widely used examples consist of face swaps and reenactments in
otherwise authentic video [17, 11, 5] or of whole-image generations from many generators
[20, 16].

We focus on four contexts in which people encounter AI-generated depictions of people. Surveillance footage is low-resolution and heavily
compressed, and the forensic traces that diffusion-image detectors rely
on weaken under compression and resizing [4].
Occupational portraits carry gender associations that generative models
are known to amplify [1, 7].
Partially manipulated photographs alter only a region of an authentic
image and leave most pixels untouched [14]. Short-form video is a common format on social media, and several commercial generators now produce it.

We release HumanCentric-Scenes-AI, a dataset of 296 AI-generated stimuli
that covers these four domains (Table 1). The Surveillance
subset contains 85 AI-generated CCTV-style images. The Occupations subset
contains 128 AI-generated occupational portraits in a balanced paired
design covering 64 occupations with one female and one male version of
each. The AI-edited subset
contains 27 AI-edited derivatives of public-source photographs. The Video
subset contains 56 videos from Veo, Sora and Kling. Every stimulus
carries its generation prompt where recorded and structured metadata
(Table 2). We curated every stimulus manually, selecting for photorealism.
Earlier work found that human curation raises the photorealism of
AI-generated images [8]. The dataset is
built for research on human perception of AI-generated media.

We find that Midjourney images prompted toward CCTV degradation remain sharper than
real CCTV frames (Section 4). We find that Midjourney depicts a man
in 73.9% of generations under gender-neutral occupational prompts and
that descriptive phrasing softens the skew (Section 5). In the 25 characterized AI-edited images, larger edits are
associated with higher zero-shot classification accuracy
(Section 6). The Video subset spans six subject categories, and
the characterized videos have median durations similar to those of their
real references (Section 7).

## Contributions.

We release a four-domain multimodal
dataset of 296 AI-generated stimuli in full, with per-stimulus metadata
for every file (Table 2). We characterize each domain
(Sections 4, 5, 6 and 7) against third-party
statistical references where applicable, such as U.S. BLS occupational
gender shares for the Occupations subset. We additionally analyze an initial set of 134 generations without a specified gender across 67 occupations, which shows the generator default that the balanced release set cannot show.

## 2 Related Work

Several widely used deepfake detection datasets consist of faces
manipulated in otherwise unaltered video. FaceForensics++ [17],
Celeb-DF [11] and the DeepFake Detection Challenge
dataset [5] are widely used benchmarks for face-swap and reenactment forensics. Deepfake-Eval-2024 extends this line to manipulated media flagged in the wild across platforms and languages [2]. Earlier work by Korshunov and Marcel
[9] framed face swaps as a threat to biometric
verification. In FaceForensics++, Celeb-DF and DFDC, a face is replaced or
reanimated while the rest of the scene is held fixed.

Other datasets contain fully AI-generated images. GenImage
[20] pairs over a million generated images with real
counterparts across eight generators, targeting classifier generalization
across architectures. Community Forensics scales generator coverage to several
thousand models to improve detector generalization
[16]. Corvi et al. show that the forensic traces
such detectors learn weaken under the compression and resizing that
shared media undergo [4]. Our Surveillance subset
contains AI-generated images prompted toward such degraded capture
conditions, and we find that they remain sharper than real CCTV frames
(Section 4). For partial manipulation, TGIF releases roughly
80,000 text-guided inpainting forgeries for detector training
[14]. Our AI-edited subset is a small curated set built
for human-perception research, and in it larger edits are associated with
higher zero-shot classification accuracy (Section 6).

Several studies measure demographic bias in text-to-image models. Bianchi
et al. [1] show that simple occupational prompts
amplify demographic stereotypes at scale, and DALL-Eval
[3] probes social biases across generation models.
Stable Bias quantifies demographic representation in diffusion output
[13]. Paired stereotype testing reports
gender-occupational bias in over 74% of DALL-E 3 generations
[19]. Our Occupations subset contributes a complementary design. The same 67
occupations are prompted twice, once by name and once by paraphrased
description. This design separates the generator’s male default (82.1% under
occupation-name prompts) from a prompt-phrasing effect of 16.4 percentage
points (Section 5).

Our dataset differs from these datasets in purpose. It was curated as stimulus material for human-perception research,
extending the curation practice of our earlier work
[8] to four domains. The release is small, and each stimulus carries structured metadata. It follows the datasheet
reporting framework of Gebru et al. [6]
(Appendix A).

## 3.1 Curation procedure

Curators reviewed every candidate generation manually over multiple
rounds and kept those without visible artifacts that would make them
immediately distinguishable from photographs.
All images in the Surveillance and Occupations subsets were generated
with Midjourney. We generated the CCTV-style set with degradation-oriented
prompts (Section 4). We generated the gender–occupation set as a balanced paired design in
which each occupation is prompted once per gender (Section 5). The AI-edited subset applies AI edits to regions of
public-source photographs, released as derivative works. Video-subset stimuli were generated with Veo, Sora and Kling from
category-matched prompts. Each AI video was developed against a real
reference clip from the same subject category.

## 5 Images with Gender–Occupation Pairings

The Occupations subset comprises 128 Midjourney occupational portraits in
a balanced paired design. Each of 64 occupations is prompted once with a
female subject and once with a male subject (e.g., “portrait of a female
model on catwalk, shot on Kodak, grainy”). The balance is by construction, so the released subset
supports studies of occupation–gender congruence. The generator’s
unconstrained gender prior is observable in the initial set of generations without a specified gender
instead.

## 5.1 Gender distribution under gender-neutral prompts

Before building the balanced release set, the same 67 occupations were
generated once per prompt style with no gender specified, yielding a
initial set of 134 generations without a specified gender. This initial set is analyzed here and is not part of the release. Under
these gender-neutral prompts, Midjourney depicted a man in 73.9% of
generations (Table 4). The share is 82.1% when the prompt names the occupation and 65.7% when it
describes the role. The direction is consistent with bias audits of other
text-to-image models [1, 13, 19]. Eleven of the 67 occupations are rendered female under occupation-name
prompts. Seven of the eight with a published BLS share are female-majority
occupations in U.S. labor statistics (Table 5).

## 5.2 Gender-stereotype congruence

Occupations carry measurable gender associations. Guilbeault et
al. [7] quantify them as a gender association
score derived from the proportion of male and female faces in online
images for an occupation, where $-1$ indicates fully female
representation and $+1$ fully male. The released metadata supports the
same congruence analyses, since every Occupations stimulus carries its
occupation and depicted gender. We treat a generation as
stereotype-congruent when its depicted gender matches the
female-majority or male-majority direction of the occupation in U.S. labor statistics. Of the eleven occupations that Midjourney renders female under
occupation-name prompts, seven are female-majority in BLS data,
newscaster is male-majority, and model, florist and cheerleader have no
published BLS share (Table 5).

## 6 Partially AI-Generated Images

The AI-edited subset comprises 27 partially manipulated images. Each was
made from a real photograph in the dataset of Kamali et al. [8] with Midjourney inpainting, which
removes, modifies or adds content and leaves the rest of the scene
intact. The results are released as derivative works. The pairwise analyses below cover the 25 pairs with aligned source photographs, and edit-region statistics appear in Table 7.

We coded each of the 25 pairs manually into one of five mutually exclusive edit types by comparing the source photograph with the edited image and its pixel-difference map (Table 8). Replacing one person with another person is the most common edit (9 of 25), followed by background replacement (6) and object insertion or replacement (5), and four edits replace a person with an object or an animal. One edit re-renders a hat and a pair of sunglasses without removing the sunglasses that its prompt asked to remove. ## LIMITATIONS SECTION
Several statistics are computed on 25-stimulus characterization subsets,
and the same scripts can be applied to the full subsets. Every difficulty
estimate also ages as generators improve, and the release records the
generator of each stimulus so future work can measure that decay.

## CONCLUSION
HumanCentric-Scenes-AI releases 296 AI-generated stimuli across four
domains of human-centric media. In our analyses, Midjourney CCTV-style images are sharper than the real
frames they mimic. Depicted gender under gender-neutral prompts is
associated with the gender composition of occupations. Zero-shot
classification accuracy is lower for smaller edits. The released metadata lets other researchers test these regularities on the full subsets.

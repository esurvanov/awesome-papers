## A Small Model Asks Better: Fine-Tuning Query Clarification for Retrieval-Augmented Generation

Lihong He

Lucian Popa

Marina Danilevsky

Affiliation: IBM Research, USA

Email: lihong.he@ibm.com

Abstract

Query clarification is essential for retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) systems
that face ambiguous user queries.
We consider multi-turn grounded conversational scenarios where
a user query is either ill-formed or ambiguous in the context of the prior dialog or,
more interestingly, it may be well-formed but when posed against retrieved documents there
are multiple relevant interpretations. The task is to recognize
both such cases and then generate an appropriate clarification request to the user.
We present ClariEdge, a new dataset of approximately 3,900 examples
that include such ambiguous queries along with negative examples that
do not require clarification.
We also show that a lightweight fine-tuned LoRA achieves
96.6% classification accuracy and outperforms prompted baselines of many larger models on
both classification and quality of clarification generation.
Our study shows that hard negatives
are an essential ingredient
to separate out queries that are truly ambiguous from queries that
may look ambiguous but are not (i.e., can be resolved from the context).
The same LoRA achieves high performance in both pre-retrieval and post-retrieval
scenarios; thus, it can be used in two places in a RAG pipeline as a check before
answer generation.
The ClariEdge dataset is available at https://huggingface.co/datasets/ibm-research/ClariEdge.

## 1 Introduction

Ambiguity in user queries can come in two distinct forms. The first is the
ill-formed query, where the user mentions an entity or attribute that the
conversation has not narrowed down to a single choice. Figure 1(a)
shows such a case: after the assistant has surfaced two credit cards, the user
asks “What are the potential rewards of this credit card?” The system
must decide whether to attempt an answer under uncertainty or ask which card the
user means; a useful clarification lists the candidates already on the table
(a pattern we call hedging over list).

U: I’m applying for a credit card for personal travel and business expenses. What would you suggest?

A: Two strong options: (1) Gold Card — travel rewards and lounge access; (2) Business Card — cashback on office expenses.

U: What are the rewards of this credit card?

A: Which credit card are you asking about: the Gold Card or the Business Card?

(a) Ill-formed query: “this credit card” has multiple referents in the conversation. Clarification lists the candidates (hedging over list).

U: Is the language that descended from Common Brittonic still spoken today, or has it become extinct?

Retrieved: passages on Welsh, Breton, and Cornish as descendants of Common Brittonic.

A: Several languages descended from Common Brittonic: Welsh, still widely spoken in Wales; Breton, still spoken in Brittany; and Cornish, extinct in the 19th century but since revitalized. Which one are you referring to?

(b) Well-formed query: user thinks there is only one choice, but the retrieved corpus shows several. Clarification pairs each option with a brief grounded answer (hedging with answers).

Figure 1: Ambiguity forms in conversational RAG. Ambiguous user query and clarification response are highlighted.

The second form arises when the query is well-formed in
isolation but becomes ambiguous once grounded in a set of retrieved
documents. In Figure 1(b), the user asks whether
“the language that descended from Common Brittonic” is still spoken, a question that sounds well-defined - but the retrieved passages surface several possible descendants (Welsh, Breton, Cornish). In retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) (Rosenthal et al., 2026), this is a common occurrence, and a useful clarification should both flag the ambiguity and also
enumerate the grounded options with a brief answer for each (hedging with
answers). The quality of clarification, that is,
naming the right options and choosing the pattern of communication
(see Table 7 for details)
matters as much as the
decision of whether to clarify at all. Both use cases - ambiguity before retrieval, and after - can occur in the same deployment.
Therefore we need a unified clarification functionality that handles both, usable before retrieval, based only on the conversation
(Cao et al., 2025; Sahay et al., 2025), and after retrieval, when documents become available
(Kim et al., 2023).

Existing benchmarks address only one slice of this picture. CLAMBER
(Zhang et al., 2024) evaluates ambiguity detection on standalone questions (no conversational context, no retrieved documents) and finds that
chain-of-thought and few-shot prompting yield only marginal gains over a base
classifier. AmbigQA (Min et al., 2020) grounds ambiguity in a corpus, but
treats a question as ambiguous whenever retrieval surfaces multiple atomic
factual answers, even when they are part of the same larger answer. This is a
notion that current conversational LLMs largely subsume by responding with
a composite answer that includes the atomic facts: for example, ‘‘Who took control of the Italian government in 1922?’’11
 1

 This question, along with many similar ones, comes from the AmbigQA dataset and is considered ambiguous there.
yields “Benito Mussolini” and “the Fascists” in one response –
so the AmbigQA ambiguity rarely surfaces as a clarification need.
Neither CLAMBER nor AmbigQA capture the joint conversation-plus-corpus setting
that real RAG deployments face.

Problem Statement.

Given a multi-turn conversation between a user and an
AI assistant, together with optionally retrieved documents for the last user turn, our goal is to determine
whether the user’s last turn is ambiguous, that is, whether it admits either no
clear interpretation or multiple valid interpretations. When it is ambiguous,
the system should generate a clarification request that elicits additional information from the user.

Towards addressing this problem, we make the following specific contributions:

1. ClariEdge: Clarification Grounded Dataset with Hard Negative Edge Cases.
We release ClariEdge, a dataset of approximately 3,900 examples with multi-turn
conversations, retrieved documents, and explicit hard negatives, that is,
queries that look ambiguous but are resolvable from context via coreference
resolution or query rewriting. The dataset is built
through direct human annotation and synthetic LLM generation followed by human
validation.

2. Lightweight Fine-Tuning Substantially Outperforms Prompting.
Against seven prompted baselines that include substantially larger models, our
lightweight LoRA achieves the highest classification accuracy
and the best clarification-generation quality on the queries it
correctly flags.
Whereas prompted baselines tend to either over-clarify or under-clarify, the
fine-tuned model handles both directions reliably.
An ablation study further
confirms that the hard negatives present in ClariEdge are essential for query clarification, since removing
them causes a sharp drop in accuracy on boundary cases.

3. Multi-Setting Handling in a Single Model. Unlike approaches that rely on
separate models or dynamic strategy selection (Sahay et al., 2025), one LoRA model handles
both pre-retrieval (conversation-only) and post-retrieval (conversation plus
documents) scenarios, with classification accuracy ahead of larger prompted models.
As shown in Figure 2, the same LoRA is invoked once on the
conversation alone (gating retrieval) and once on the conversation plus retrieved
documents (gating answer generation), asking a clarifying question whenever either
invocation flags ambiguity.

## 2 Related Work

Ambiguity and clarification have been studied across many information-seeking
settings: open-domain factual QA (AmbigQA, Min et al., 2020; ASQA,
Stelmakh et al., 2022; CAmbigNQ, Lee et al., 2023; CondAmbigQA,
Li et al., 2025), standalone questions (CLAMBER,
Zhang et al., 2024), conversational QA (Abg-CoQA,
Guo et al., 2021; PACIFIC, Deng et al., 2022; CANARD,
Elgohary et al., 2019), and conversational search (Qulac,
Aliannejadi et al., 2019; ClariQ, Aliannejadi et al., 2020;
MIMICS, Zamani et al., 2020). Table 1
compares our dataset against four of these
benchmarks. On the modeling side, LLMs gain only marginal benefits from
chain-of-thought or few-shot prompting on ambiguity identification
(Zhang et al., 2024; Deng et al., 2023), motivating work that exploits
models’ own uncertainty signals (Kim et al., 2024; Testoni and Fernández, 2024) or
resolves ambiguity via retrieval-grounded disambiguation without asking the
user (Kim et al., 2023).

On the method side, prior systems specialize clarification along three axes:
when to clarify, using LLM uncertainty or calibration
(Kuhn et al., 2023; Zhang and Choi, 2023; Cole et al., 2023); how to
ask, via facet-driven or reasoning-trace supervision
(Sekulić et al., 2021; Andukuri et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2025; Rahmani et al., 2024); and which deployment setting to target
(Sahay et al., 2025; Chen et al., 2024; Cao et al., 2025; Qin et al., 2025). These systems
typically rely on prompting or uncertainty rather than task-specific
supervision, and each remains tied to the prompts, models, or iterations of
its setting.

In contrast, we show that a single LoRA fine-tuned with hard negatives covers
both pre- and post-retrieval, draws an explicit boundary between genuine
ambiguity and context-resolvable references, and outperforms substantially larger
prompted baselines. This indicates that training-data quality dominates raw model
scale on this task.

## 3 ClariEdge Dataset

Effective query clarification requires training data that captures the nuanced
decision boundaries between clarification-worthy and clear queries. This section describes our ClariEdge dataset and its creation.
Conversations span the same four domains as MTRAG (Katsis et al., 2025): ClapNQ (Wikipedia), Cloud (technical documentation), FiQA (finance), and Govt (government).

Table 1 compares our ClariEdge dataset against four widely used
clarification benchmarks, each covering a complementary slice:
AmbigQA (Min et al., 2020) targets answer-set ambiguity in open-domain factual QA; CLAMBER (Zhang et al., 2024) introduces a Epistemic Misalignment / Linguistic Ambiguity / Aleatoric Output
typology for LLM evaluation on standalone questions; ClariQ (Aliannejadi et al., 2020) captures
web-search facets; and Abg-CoQA (Guo et al., 2021) addresses
referential ambiguity in conversational QA. None of these combines
multi-turn conversation, retrieved documents, and explicit hard negatives in a
single resource. Our dataset targets entity-option ambiguity, where the
user query may be referring to one of several candidate entities surfaced by either the conversation or the retrieved documents, and is the first to include systematic hard
negative edge cases that look ambiguous but are resolvable from context.

Our dataset comprises three categories: (1) Ambiguous (positive examples),
where user queries genuinely require clarification due to multiple plausible
interpretations; (2) CLEAR-random (easy negative examples), which are queries
that do not require clarification and are straightforwardly clear; and (3) CLEAR-hard
(hard negative examples), which are queries that appear superficially ambiguous
but can be resolved from conversation context. Table 2 presents
the data size and Table 3 illustrates some examples for the three categories.

The distinction between CLEAR-hard and CLEAR-random is critical:
hard negatives appear ambiguous due to referential expressions but
can be resolved from conversation context, while easy negatives are straightforwardly
clear queries. Training with hard negatives teaches the model the precise boundary
between genuinely ambiguous queries and those with resolvable references.

Additional dataset statistics (pre-/post-retrieval split, turn counts,
document counts) are reported in Appendix A.

## 3.2 Data Creation Process

Ambiguous examples.

Following the methodology for creating underspecified
questions in MTRAG-UN (Rosenthal et al., 2026), the final user query in each
conversation was designed to admit multiple plausible interpretations. Conversations
were constructed via two parallel approaches: (1) annotators writing ambiguous
questions inspired by entity types and instances drawn from the underlying
corpus; each such question was then stitched, in a subsequent step, as the last
turn onto an existing human-annotated multi-turn conversation, or (2) synthetic
generation of ambiguous questions appended as the final turn to existing
human-created multi-turn conversations, followed by a manual validation step. The latter approach is also the same one used for CLEAR-hard examples (see more details later): the major difference is that here the manual validation decides that these are truly ambiguous questions, while for CLEAR-hard it decides the converse.

For approach (1), relevant passages were added to each conversation using two
retrieval strategies: direct retrieval with the ambiguous question, and query
expansion with a context relevance filter, which yields a richer set of passages
to simulate scenarios where significantly more interpretations exist; these
examples form the post-retrieval portion of the Ambiguous category. Further details on the human annotation guidelines for this approach are given in Appendix B.1. For approach
(2), no passages were retrieved: this branch shares the synthetic-then-validate
pipeline used for CLEAR-hard (described below), and produces conversation-only
(pre-retrieval) Ambiguous examples. In both cases, reference clarification
responses were generated using an LLM with pattern-based prompting to include
relevant disambiguation options (see Section 3.3), followed by
human correction.

CLEAR-random examples.

The easy negative examples in this category were
constructed by randomly sampling clear, unambiguous user queries from existing
multi-turn conversations. For some examples, retrieved documents were paired with
the query to represent clear post-retrieval scenarios; for others, documents were
deliberately removed to create conversation-only examples where the query remains
unambiguous without external context.

CLEAR-hard examples.

A key contribution of our dataset is the systematic
construction of hard negatives, which are queries that may appear ambiguous to an LLM but can
be resolved from conversation context.
By construction, these examples are for the conversation-only (pre-retrieval) setting: their ambiguity-or-resolvability hinges on linguistic cues in the
dialogue rather than on retrieved passages.
We describe their creation next.

We prompted an LLM
on multi-turn conversations to generate questions targeting
entities discussed in the conversation, where the last user query is intended to be ambiguous.
In the basic variant, the model was given 793 conversations and asked to produce questions
where a user asks about an entity type with multiple instances occurring in the conversation. In
an advanced variant, 228 longer conversations (>6 turns with multiple entity types)
were processed using chain-of-thought reasoning with self-verification and iterative
refinement. Human annotators then manually reviewed all LLM-generated queries,
classifying each as genuinely ambiguous or resolvable from context. Conversations with
last user query determined to be truly ambiguous were moved to the Ambiguous category, while
the remaining ones, where queries appear ambiguous but can be resolved through conversation
context, became hard negatives.

We collected additional patterns of hard negatives where the synthetic generation model incorrectly generated clear queries as ambiguous. These included: “which one” patterns where the phrasing appears ambiguous but the question is just a comparison across the previously mentioned entities; follow-up questions resolvable through the regular conversation flow; and terse or keyword queries that appear ambiguous but are clear in context.
Table 4 gives examples of these additional patterns.

## 3.3 Generation of Clarification Response

For each ambiguous query in the dataset, we generate a reference clarification
response through a structured pipeline:
(1) identify the ambiguous entity in the last user query, that is, the term or
reference that admits multiple interpretations;
(2) extract candidate entity options from the retrieved document and prior
conversations, together with a short associated answer where applicable;
(3) verify the consistency of each extracted option against the ambiguous entity
and deduplicate them;
(4) generate the clarification response based on the number of distinct options,
following one of three patterns described below;
(5) human annotators review the final clarification response and modify it when
the LLM-generated output is inaccurate or insufficient.

Steps 1–4 are performed by an
LLM (the same Mistral-Large-3),
followed by human review and correction in step 5.
In step 4, the clarification response follows one of three patterns based on the number
of distinct options: (1) Hedging with answers (few options, e.g., 2–3): list each option
with a brief description or answer; (2) Hedging over list (medium number of options,
e.g., 4–8): enumerate the plausible options without additional explanatory content;
and (3) Open-domain (many or unbounded options): directly asks the user for disambiguation
over the type of entity that they may have in mind.
Appendix C provides some examples of the three patterns.

## 3.4 Inter-rater Agreement

To measure the annotation reliability, we follow the calculation of
inter-rater agreement in Abg-CoQA (Guo et al., 2021).

For the inter-rater agreement on whether a user query is ambiguous
or not, we randomly select 80 samples from the test set: 20 from CLEAR-hard,
40 from CLEAR-random, and 20 from Ambiguous. We ask two authors to provide
their annotations (CLEAR or Ambiguous) on these 80 examples, and then
compute Cohen’s Kappa. The two annotators disagree on only 4 data points,
all of which are sampled from the CLEAR-hard category. The resulting
Cohen’s Kappa is 0.875 (almost perfect agreement), indicating that the
ambiguity labels are highly reliable.

For the inter-rater agreement on clarification questions, we ask
the same two annotators to independently write a clarification question for
each of the 20 sampled Ambiguous examples (one version is kept as the gold
clarification). We then compute the macro-average F1 score of word overlap
and the BLEU-4 score. The F1 is 0.774 and the BLEU-4 is 0.57. We further
conduct a manual review of the two clarification versions on the 20
samples. The manual inspection and the metrics agree that the two versions
match on the ambiguous entity and its candidate options but differ in
surface phrasing: the F1 of 0.774 captures the shared content while the
moderate BLEU-4 of 0.57 reflects the phrasing variation, indicating
that the gold clarifications are content-faithful yet not unique verbatim
references.

## 4 Models

This section describes our fine-tuned LoRA models and the baseline models used in the experiments.

## 4.1 Fine-Tuned LoRA Models

We employ Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA) (Hu et al., 2022) to create
parameter-efficient clarification models, fine-tuned on the 3,509 training examples
described in Table 2.
This substantially reduces training cost and enables rapid experimentation with different training data compositions.
We fine-tune LoRA on (1) small 3B models from the Granite family
(Granite-4.0-micro33
 3

 https://huggingface.co/ibm-granite/granite-4.0-micro
and Granite-4.1-3B44
 4

 https://huggingface.co/ibm-granite/granite-4.1-3b),
targeted at cost-effective deployment; and
(2) GPT-OSS-20B, a larger open-source model included for comparison.

The two small Granite models serve
as our primary models. This choice reflects several considerations. First,
their size-to-performance trade-off provides sufficient capacity while
maintaining deployment efficiency, in line with recent evidence that
lightweight models can be competitive on well-defined tasks
(Nandi et al., 2026). Second, both are amenable to LoRA
adaptation with a modest training set. Finally, using the same base
architectures for both fine-tuned and prompted configurations allows us to
isolate the effect of fine-tuning from any difference in model capacity.

Training configuration and input/output format for the LoRA models are
described in Appendix D.

## 4.2 Prompted Baseline Models

To quantify the value of fine-tuning, we compare our LoRA models against seven prompted
baselines evaluated without any task-specific training. The baselines span proprietary
frontier, open-weights, and lightweight model families:
GPT-5.6 Sol and GPT-4o (frontier, proprietary);
GPT-OSS-120B (large, open-source);
GPT-OSS-20B (medium, open-source);
Granite-4.1-30B (medium, open-source); and
Granite-4.1-3B and Granite-4.0-micro (lightweight, open-source).

All prompted baselines receive identical chat messages consisting of a system message specifying the clarification rules and a user message constructed from each test record.
The system message defines the task, grounding requirements, and describes the patterns that the generated clarifications should follow. The user message supplies the conversation and retrieved documents (if any).
The full text of both messages is provided in Appendix E.

In this section, we describe the evaluation metrics and then give the main experimental results.

## 5.1 Evaluation Metrics

We assess model performance along two dimensions: (1) classification
accuracy, measuring whether the model correctly determines if a query is ambiguous or not; and (2) generation quality, evaluating the
clarification response produced for correctly identified ambiguous queries.

Prior work has used BLEU (Guo et al., 2021) and BERTScore
(Zhang et al., 2024) to evaluate generation against a reference answer,
but neither captures whether the clarification presents correct
disambiguation options for the ambiguous entity. We therefore design
entity-option-centric metrics in the spirit of AmbigQA
(Min et al., 2020), differing in that we evaluate over entity options
rather than answer interpretations and accommodate open-domain
clarifications where options cannot be explicitly enumerated.
Specifically, we
use the following metrics:
Precision: the fraction of options in the generated clarification that are correct;
Recall: the fraction of gold reference options
that are covered by the generated clarification;
F1: harmonic mean of precision and recall.
Computing precision and recall on free-form clarification responses requires four auxiliary judgments. We obtain these judgments with four LLM prompts (see Appendix F). We also provide examples in Appendix G to illustrate the metrics, as well as how we handle open-domain clarifications.

## 5.2 Classification Results

We evaluate classification accuracy in three settings: full test set,
pre-retrieval subset, and post-retrieval
subset. The results are reported in Table 5.

## 5.2.1 Full Test Set

The classification results on the full test set support two observations, which we discuss in turn.

Fine-tuning beats prompting at all scales.

The Granite-4.1-3B LoRA reaches 96.6% overall accuracy, a 17.2 pp gain
over GPT-4o and a 31.8 pp gain over prompted Granite-4.1-3B. Because the
prompted and fine-tuned variants of Granite-4.1-3B share the same
architecture, this improvement is attributable to training rather than to
greater model capacity, which is consistent with Zhang et al. (2024)’s
finding that prompting offers limited gains on the task of query
clarification. The Granite-4.0-micro LoRA also achieves strong
performance at 92.4%, and both Granite LoRAs outperform the larger
GPT-OSS-20B LoRA and all the other prompted baselines, in line with prior
evidence that specialized lightweight models can be competitive on
well-scoped tasks (Nandi et al., 2026). Even the weaker GPT-OSS-20B
LoRA (90.1%) outperforms the strongest prompted baseline GPT-4o
by 10.7 pp overall, indicating that the gap is robust to LoRA backbone choice.

Prompted baselines are systematically imbalanced.

The imbalance is most
pronounced between the Ambiguous and CLEAR-hard categories, the two that hinge on
correctly distinguishing genuine ambiguity from surface-level ambiguity. Prompted
baselines split into two failure modes along this axis: over-clarifiers (such as GPT-5.6 Sol and GPT-OSS-20B), biased toward flagging queries as ambiguous, and under-clarifiers (such as GPT-4o and the smaller Granite models),
biased toward declaring queries clear. Notably, the two frontier proprietary models diverge along this axis, indicating that the imbalance is not attributable to model scale alone.
In contrast, the fine-tuned LoRAs (and especially the Granite two) maintain a good balance, indicating that exposure to hard negatives is
essential for separating genuine ambiguity from resolvable references.
We also conduct an ablation study on hard negatives,
showing that
removing them causes a sharp drop in accuracy on boundary cases. The details
are in Appendix H.

## 5.2.2 Pre-Retrieval

In the pre-retrieval setting, the model must decide whether to clarify based solely
on the multi-turn conversation. This setting is
particularly challenging because the model cannot leverage document evidence
to disambiguate queries and to surface the interpretations.

The classification accuracy on the pre-retrieval subset shows that all three
fine-tuned LoRAs lead on overall accuracy, substantially improving over their
architecture-matched prompted baselines. The class-imbalance pattern observed
on the full test set (§5.2.1) is again evident among prompted
models in pre-retrieval: some over-clarify (e.g., GPT-5.6 Sol and GPT-OSS-20B),
while others under-clarify (e.g., GPT-4o and the smaller
Granite models). In contrast, the Granite-4.1-3B LoRA maintains relatively
balanced performance, though its Ambiguous accuracy is lower than its
CLEAR-hard accuracy.
Pre-retrieval is where linguistic ambiguity is hardest to disentangle:
CLEAR-hard examples by construction (see Section 3.2) are
for the conversation-only setting, and present an inherently harder setup where
the model must rely purely on linguistic cues to separate Ambiguous from cases
that are resolvable in the dialogue.

## 5.2.3 Post-Retrieval

In the post-retrieval setting, the model has access to the conversation together
with documents returned by retriever. The CLEAR-hard category is
not represented in this subset; as we mentioned, they target
conversational context rather than documents.

From the post-retrieval results in Table 5, the
fine-tuned LoRAs reach near-saturation overall classification accuracy: the
Granite-4.1-3B LoRA hits 100.0% and the Granite-4.0-micro LoRA hits 99.0%.
Both are far ahead of the strongest prompted baseline (GPT-OSS-120B at
82.1%). The prompted baselines continue to exhibit the class-imbalance
patterns observed in earlier settings.
These results indicate that, once
retrieved documents are available, the remaining difficulty lies in correctly interpreting
the retrieval signal. Fine-tuning acquires this skill reliably, whereas prompting does not.

## 5.3 Generation Quality Results

We assess clarification generation quality across the same three settings
using the entity-option-centric metrics.
A clarification only matters when the model also correctly flags the underlying
query as ambiguous: a high F1 on a small set of correctly-flagged queries is
weaker than the same F1 on a larger set. We therefore report both the count of
correctly-flagged examples and the F1 of the resulting clarifications in
Table 6.

## 5.3.1 Full Test Set

On the full test set, both Granite LoRAs lead on F1: the 4.0-micro LoRA at 89.7% and the 4.1-3B LoRA at 89.0%.
The prompted baselines GPT-5.6 Sol and GPT-OSS-20B post similar high precision but
substantially lower recall, indicating that without task-specific training models
miss plausible alternatives grounded in
the conversation or retrieved documents.
The gain from fine-tuning is especially pronounced for
lightweight models: prompted Granite-4.0-micro reaches only 56.8% F1, whereas
its LoRA counterpart attains 89.7%.

## 5.3.2 Pre-Retrieval

On the pre-retrieval subset, generation quality results are more competitive
across models. Both Granite LoRAs achieve an F1 around 85%, close to prompted GPT-5.6 Sol,
GPT-OSS-20B and Granite-4.1-30B.
However, as seen in §5.2.2, these three prompted models have much lower classification accuracy (they tend to over-clarify).
The LoRA’s operational advantage
is that it produces high-quality clarifications
while also reliably identifying which queries require them, avoiding the
inflation of generation volume that accompanies over-clarification in prompted
baselines.

## 5.3.3 Post-Retrieval

On the post-retrieval subset, the two Granite LoRAs attain the highest F1,
combining high precision with the highest recall. Among prompted models, several
baselines (such as GPT-4o and GPT-OSS-120B) achieve
strong precision but comparatively low recall. This asymmetry suggests that, when
grounded by retrieved documents, prompted models produce confident but narrow
clarifications: they correctly select some document-supported options but frequently
omit others that are equally valid. The two Granite LoRAs’ simultaneous gains in precision
and recall indicate that fine-tuning is effective not only at identifying when
to clarify but also at enumerating which options to present.

## 5.4 Out-of-Domain (OOD) Evaluation

To assess whether the LoRA’s advantage generalizes beyond the four training
domains (ClapNQ, Cloud, FiQA, Govt), we construct an OOD evaluation set in
two new domains drawn from
MTRAG-UN (Rosenthal et al., 2026): Banking (U.S. bank services)
and Telco (telecommunications providers).
The Granite-4.0-micro LoRA maintains its lead over prompted baselines on
both classification and generation quality. This suggests that the
clarification skill acquired via ClariEdge is task-general rather than
domain-specific.
Appendix I gives the OOD evaluation details.

## 6 Conclusion

In this work, we introduce ClariEdge, a new dataset for clarifying ambiguous questions in RAG settings, and show that LoRA fine-tuning on a small model is more beneficial than prompting larger LLMs. Hard negatives are an essential
ingredient, suggesting that teaching a model what not to do is as important as
teaching it what to do. The resulting LoRA works well in both pre-retrieval and post-retrieval settings, although the pre-retrieval regime is a more challenging one.

## Limitations

Despite the strong empirical results reported above, our study has several limitations
that circumscribe its scope and suggest directions for future work.

Single-turn clarification.

Our model generates a single clarifying question
rather than engaging in multi-turn clarification dialogues. Practical deployments may
benefit from iterative clarification strategies that progressively narrow the user’s
intent across multiple exchanges. Extending our approach
to multi-turn clarification remains an open direction.

English-only evaluation.

Both the dataset and evaluation are restricted to
English. The cross-lingual transferability of our method remains unexplored.

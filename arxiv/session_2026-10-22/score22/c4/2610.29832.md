## ABSTRACT
Calibrating large language models (LLMs) in open-ended generation is uniquely challenging because many distinct token sequences can express the same underlying meaning. Prior calibration techniques target either fixed-set classification confidence or the binary correctness of individual strings, making them inherently ill-suited for the dynamic, multi-string nature of open-ended semantic outcomes. To bridge this gap, we introduce S-EDL, a novel evidential method that natively optimizes LLMs for semantic calibration. By eliciting evidence from the sequence likelihoods of the model itself, S-EDL constructs a differentiable Dirichlet prior over prompt-specific semantic classes. Optimizing the evidential loss under this prior yields a calibration-aware training signal over variable semantic classes, completely bypassing the need for fixed multiple-choice options. Evaluations across four open-ended benchmarks and three models demonstrate that S-EDL substantially reduces semantic calibration error while improving accuracy. By aligning generative likelihoods with semantic correctness, this work establishes a framework for deploying trustworthy LLMs in complex, open-ended applications. Code is available at: https://github.com/sandylaker/s-edl.

## 1 Introduction

Large language models (LLMs) are increasingly used for open-ended reasoning tasks, where reliable confidence estimates are important for deciding when model outputs can be trusted [28, 32, 56, 24]. Early research has primarily focused on multiple-choice question answering (MCQA), where calibration can be defined over a small set of answer labels [48, 53, 37, 25]. Open-ended generation is more subtle: many distinct token sequences can express the same answer, and the probability assigned to a surface form does not necessarily reflect confidence in its semantic content.

Prior work has studied open-ended uncertainty and calibration through several related targets. Semantic uncertainty metrics [20, 12] sample multiple generations and group equivalent responses to evaluate the predictive distribution over semantic classes. Building on this perspective, semantic calibration [33] translates this grouping concept into an evaluation metric for open-ended generation. In parallel, verbal confidence calibration methods [18, 43, 52, 30, 3, 9, 55] teach models to report a confidence score of correctness. However, this calibrates the binary correctness of a single response rather than the underlying distribution over alternative semantic answers. Thus, a gap remains: how to fine-tune open-ended LLMs under a semantic calibration objective.

Evidential deep learning (EDL) natively bridges this gap. By placing a Dirichlet prior over categorical probabilities, it explicitly models the strength of evidence for multiple competing meanings, allowing calibration losses to act on distributions over semantic meanings. Building on this, we propose S-EDL, a self-evidenced EDL method tailored for semantic calibration. Unlike conventional EDL methods that require a fixed set of answer labels [39, 7], S-EDL accommodates the dynamic nature of open-ended generation by transforming the sequence likelihoods of the model into a differentiable evidence vector. This formulation allocates greater evidential support to semantic classes backed by highly probable strings. The evidence vector then parametrizes a Dirichlet prior over the semantic outcomes, providing a calibration-aware training signal. Our contributions are three-fold:

• 

We extend EDL from fixed-choice calibration to open-ended semantic calibration, allowing LLMs to optimize their confidence over diverse semantic meanings.

• 

We introduce a novel parametrization that derives a differentiable evidence vector from the model’s sequence likelihoods. This formulation also theoretically unifies post-hoc metrics such as Semantic Entropy [20, 12] under a common evidential framework.

• 

Across three models and four open-ended benchmarks, S-EDL jointly improves average accuracy and calibration over supervised fine-tuning (SFT), reducing average calibration error by up to roughly 1/3.

## 2 Related work

From MCQA to semantic calibration. Early LLM calibration focused on MCQA, where a fixed label set enables standard classification-style methods, including Bayesian low-rank adaptation [48, 53, 37, 42] and evidential approaches [25]. Open-ended generation introduces a level mismatch: many distinct sequences can express the same answer. Semantic uncertainty metrics [20, 12, 11, 34, 36, 6] sample generations and group by meaning, and semantic calibration [33] turns this view into a calibration target for open-ended generation. Our work follows this semantic line.

Verbal and token-level calibration. A separate line of work asks models to emit a scalar correctness score [18, 43, 52, 30, 3, 9, 55], which calibrates a Bernoulli pushforward (correct vs. incorrect) of the output distribution rather than a categorical distribution over semantic outcomes. Meanwhile, SelfReflect [19] captures the categorical distribution by a verbalized string. Token-level methods such as label smoothing [15, 44], temperature scaling [41, 22], RCFT [50], and CATTO [35], regularize per-token distributions, supervising a different granularity than the semantic marginal targeted here.

Evidential deep learning. EDL [31, 39, 10, 7, 17, 5, 4, 54] places a Dirichlet prior over class probabilities through an evidence vector. Although previous work has applied EDL to calibrate LLMs in MCQA [25] and detect misbehavior in vision-language models [16], S-EDL is, to the best of our knowledge, the first instantiation of EDL for calibrating LLMs in open-ended generation.

## 3 Background

## 3.1 Semantic calibration in open-ended LLM generation

Our work focuses on calibration in open-ended generation, for which we adopt the framework of B-calibration [33]. Let $\mathcal{D}$ be a distribution over prompt-completion pairs $(x,y)\in\mathcal{V}^{*}\times\mathcal{V}^{L}$ , where $x$ is a prompt over vocabulary $\mathcal{V}^{*}$ and $y\in\mathcal{V}^{L}$ is a ground-truth completion of length $L$ . An autoregressive LLM $p_{\bm{\theta}}$ with parameters $\bm{\theta}\in\Theta$ assigns probability $p_{\bm{\theta}}(z|x)$ to each candidate response $z\in\mathcal{V}^{L}$ . Because distinct token sequences can convey the same meaning (e.g., “half” vs. “0.5”), B-calibration [33] introduces a collapsing function $B:\mathcal{V}^{*}\times\mathcal{V}^{L}\rightarrow\mathbb{N}$ mapping each prompt-response pair to a global semantic class, a raw identifier $s\in\mathbb{N}$ . We write $s:=B(x,z)$ for a generation’s class and $s^{*}:=B(x,y)$ for the ground-truth class of $(x,y)$ . The collapsing function $B$ can be implemented by a verification tool or an LLM-as-judge that clusters responses by meaning.
A semantic distribution is then defined as the categorical distribution over semantic classes:

 $\pi_{x}(s):=\textstyle\sum_{z\,:\,B(x,z)=s}p_{\bm{\theta}}(z|x),\quad s\in\mathbb{N},z\in\mathcal{V}^{L}.$ 

(1)

Further, denote the top class $s_{x}^{\text{top}}:={\arg\max}_{s}\pi_{x}(s)$ and the semantic confidence $\mathrm{conf}(x):=\pi_{x}(s_{x}^{\text{top}})$ . Semantic calibration requires that the confidence aligns with the true probability of correctness:

 $\mathbb{E}_{(x,y)\sim\mathcal{D}}\!\left[\,\mathbbm{1}\{s_{x}^{\text{top}}=s^{*}\}\,\big|\,\mathrm{conf}(x)\,\right]=\mathrm{conf}(x)\quad\text{almost surely.}$ 

That is, among all prompts for which the model reports semantic confidence $c$ , the top semantic class should indeed be correct with frequency $c$ [33].

## 3.2 Evidential deep learning

The calibration objective above asks the model to report not just which semantic class is most likely but also how confident it is in that belief. EDL [39, 31] provides a framework for the latter: rather than predicting a single categorical $\bm{p}\in\Delta^{K}$ over $K$ classes, it predicts a distribution over $\bm{p}$ , accounting for uncertainty over class probabilities instead of training only against a point estimate.

Dirichlet prior and evidence. In this subsection, $k\in[K]$ denotes a generic class index for a standard fixed- $K$ classification problem, separate from the semantic-label notation of Section 3.1. EDL places a Dirichlet prior $\text{Dir}(\bm{p};\bm{\alpha})$ on $\bm{p}\in\Delta^{K}$ , with Dirichlet parameters $\bm{\alpha}=(\alpha_{k})_{k\in[K]}\in[1,+\infty)^{K}$ built from a non-negative evidence vector $\bm{e}=(e_{k})_{k\in[K]}\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{K}$ via $\alpha_{k}=e_{k}+1$ .

Each $e_{k}$ represents the accumulated support for class $k$ . Conventional EDL employs a classification model $f_{\bm{\theta}}$ to predict the evidence directly, $\bm{e}=\text{SoftPlus}(f_{\bm{\theta}}(x))$ , and the Dirichlet mean serves as the predictive distribution: $\hat{p}_{k}=\mathbb{E}_{\bm{p}\sim\text{Dir}(\cdot;\bm{\alpha})}[p_{k}]=\frac{\alpha_{k}}{\alpha_{0}},\text{with \emph{Dirichlet strength} }\alpha_{0}=\textstyle\sum_{k=1}^{K}\alpha_{k}.$ 

EDL training objective. Given a one-hot target $\bm{q}=(q_{k})_{k\in[K]}\in\{0,1\}^{K}$ with $q_{k}=\mathbbm{1}\{k=t\}$ , Sensoy et al. [39] learn the model’s parameter $\bm{\theta}\in\Theta$ by minimizing the Bayes risk of MSE objective

 $\mathcal{L}_{\text{MSE}}(\bm{\theta})=\mathbb{E}_{\bm{p}\sim\text{Dir}(\cdot;\bm{\alpha})}\|\bm{q}-\bm{p}\|_{2}^{2}=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\left(q_{k}-\frac{\alpha_{k}}{\alpha_{0}}\right)^{2}+\sum_{k=1}^{K}\frac{\alpha_{k}(\alpha_{0}-\alpha_{k})}{\alpha_{0}^{2}(\alpha_{0}+1)},$ 

(2)

which admits a closed form. Previous work IB-EDL [25] has shown that EDL methods are very effective in calibrating LLMs in the MCQA setting, but their approach is limited to a fixed set of options such as A/B/C/D. In the following section, we move a step forward and instantiate this framework in the open-ended generation setting, deriving $\bm{e}$ from the LLM’s own generations.

## 4 Methodology

## 4.1 Motivation

Given EDL’s effectiveness for calibrating LLMs in MCQA, we now discuss the adaptations required to apply it in open-ended generation.
Instantiating the EDL framework in this setting hinges on one design choice: how to parametrize the evidence vector $\bm{e}$ . Conventional EDL learns $\bm{e}$ through a dedicated prediction head whose output dimension equals the number of classes. This recipe does not transfer to our setting for two reasons. First, the relevant semantic class inventory is prompt-dependent and not known in advance, so there is no canonical fixed output space. Second, and most fundamentally, the distribution whose calibration we ultimately care about is $p_{\bm{\theta}}(\cdot|x)$ ; routing evidence through an independent head decouples it from the very object we aim to calibrate. We therefore look for a parametrization that derives $\bm{e}$ from $p_{\bm{\theta}}(\cdot|x)$ .

A natural choice for constructing $\bm{e}$ is utilizing $\pi_{x}$ itself, which already encodes how $p_{\bm{\theta}}(\cdot|x)$ distributes mass over semantic classes. Since $\mathcal{V}^{L}$ is typically vast, Nakkiran et al. [33] estimate $\pi_{x}$ from $N$ i.i.d. Monte-Carlo samples $z^{(i)}\sim p_{\bm{\theta}}(\cdot|x)$ with semantic classes $s^{(i)}=B(x,z^{(i)})$ , which induce the prompt-specific semantic class set $\mathcal{S}_{x}:=\{s^{(i)}:i\in[N]\}\subset\mathbb{N}$ . Further details regarding the empirical construction of $\mathcal{S}_{x}$ in edge cases are in Section B.1. The plug-in estimator of $\pi_{x}$ is then

 $\hat{\pi}_{x}(s)=\tfrac{1}{N}\textstyle\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathbbm{1}[s^{(i)}=s],\quad s\in\mathcal{S}_{x}.$ 

(3)

However, this histogram is not a suitable evidence vector for EDL training. Once the samples are drawn, the class indicators are constants with respect to $\bm{\theta}$ , so an evidence vector proportional to $\hat{\pi}_{x}$ provides no differentiable path from the EDL loss back to the model parameters. In addition, the histogram treats all samples equally, regardless of their likelihood under $p_{\bm{\theta}}(\cdot\mid x)$ . This is misaligned with the evidential interpretation of $\bm{e}$ : a semantic class supported by high-likelihood generations should contribute more evidence than one represented only by low-likelihood samples.

Desiderata. These observations lead to two desiderata for parametrizing evidence $\bm{e}$ :

(a) 

The parametrization should assign more evidence to semantic classes supported by high-likelihood completions, so that likely completions contribute more to evidence.

(b) 

The parametrization should remain differentiable through the sample log-likelihoods, so that the EDL loss can update the likelihoods underlying the sampled semantic estimate.

Next, we instantiate these desiderata through a Monte-Carlo estimate of a tempered semantic distribution, and use this estimate to define a Dirichlet prior over the sampled semantic classes.

## 4.2 Formulation of self-evidence

Throughout this subsection, we fix the input prompt $x$ and drop the subscript from prompt-specific quantities (e.g., $\pi_{x}$ to $\pi$ , $\mathcal{S}_{x}$ to $\mathcal{S}$ ) to lighten notation; all such objects remain implicitly conditional on $x$ . We work directly with semantic labels $s\in\mathcal{S}$ , without introducing a separate local class index. In addition, we use the term self-evidence to refer to evidential support constructed from the LLM’s own Monte-Carlo samples. At the sample level, each generation $z^{(i)}$ receives a scalar self-evidence weight $u^{(i)}$ ; at the class level, these weights aggregate into the self-evidence vector $\bm{e}$ .

Tempered semantic distribution. For a scalar $\beta\geq 1$ , we define the tempered semantic distribution over classes $s\in\mathcal{S}$ and $z,z^{\prime}\in\mathcal{V}^{L}$ as

 $\pi_{\beta}(s):=\frac{\sum_{z\,:\,B(x,z)=s}p_{\bm{\theta}}(z|x)^{\beta}}{\sum_{z^{\prime}\in\mathcal{V}^{L}}p_{\bm{\theta}}(z^{\prime}|x)^{\beta}}.$ 

(4)

For $\beta=1$ , Eq. 4 recovers the standard semantic distribution $\pi$ in Eq. 1.
For $\beta>1$ , raising per-sequence likelihoods to a higher power shifts mass toward classes that the model supports with high-confidence sequences, formalizing desideratum (a).

Self-evidence estimator. Direct evaluation of Eq. 4 is intractable because both the classwise numerator and the global normalizer require sums over $\mathcal{V}^{L}$ . We therefore estimate $\pi_{\beta}$ from the same $N$ Monte-Carlo samples introduced in Section 4.1. Writing $\ell^{(i)}:=\log p_{\bm{\theta}}(z^{(i)}|x)$ for teacher-forcing log-likelihood for sample $i$ , we assign to each sample the self-evidence weight

 $u^{(i)}:=\exp\!\left((\beta-1)\,\ell^{(i)}\right)=p_{\bm{\theta}}(z^{(i)}|x)^{\beta-1}.$ 

(5)

The exponent is $\beta-1$ rather than $\beta$ because the samples $z^{(i)}$ are themselves drawn from $p_{\bm{\theta}}(\cdot|x)$ , so multiplying by $p_{\bm{\theta}}(z^{(i)}|x)^{\beta-1}$ recovers the $p_{\bm{\theta}}^{\beta}$ terms of $\pi_{\beta}$ in Eq. 4 (made precise in Proposition 1). Because $u^{(i)}$ is a smooth function of $\ell^{(i)}$ , gradients can pass through the sample log-likelihoods to $\bm{\theta}$ and satisfy desideratum (b).
We aggregate the weights within each semantic class and normalize:

 $U_{s}:=\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\sum_{i\in[N]\,:\,s^{(i)}=s}\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!u^{(i)},\qquad\hat{\pi}_{\beta}(s):=\frac{U_{s}}{\sum_{r\in\mathcal{S}}U_{r}}.$ 

(6)

A natural question is whether this estimator $\hat{\pi}_{\beta}$ recovers the true tempered distribution $\pi_{\beta}$ as the number of samples grows. The next result confirms this (see the proof in Section A.1).

Proposition 1.

As $N\to\infty$ , $\hat{\pi}_{\beta}(s)\xrightarrow{\text{a.s.}}\pi_{\beta}(s)$ for every $s\in\mathcal{S}$ .

Connection to Semantic Entropy and the practical choice of $\beta$ . Semantic Entropy (SE) [20, 12] also aggregates samples by their sequence probabilities $p_{\bm{\theta}}(z^{(i)}|x)$ , which under Eq. 5 corresponds to the special case $\beta=2$ . In our experiments, we adopt a small value $\beta=1.2$ (ablation in Section 5.5). The choice of $\beta$ reflects the two methods’ different purposes: SE measures the uncertainty as a post-hoc metric, while S-EDL uses $\pi_{\beta}$ as a training signal that drives gradient updates (Section 4.3).

Self-evidence vector and Dirichlet prior. We finally convert $\hat{\pi}_{\beta}$ into an evidential prior by distributing the sample budget $N$ over the semantic classes according to the estimated tempered distribution:

 $e_{s}:=N\cdot\hat{\pi}_{\beta}(s),\qquad\alpha_{s}:=e_{s}+1,\qquad s\in\mathcal{S}.$ 

(7)

Because $\hat{\pi}_{\beta}$ is a probability vector, the Dirichlet strength is determined a priori by the sample budget and the semantic class count:
 $\alpha_{0}=\sum_{s\in\mathcal{S}}\alpha_{s}=N+|\mathcal{S}|$ .
This differs from conventional EDL, where the model freely predicts the per-class evidences $e_{k}$ (equivalently, the Dirichlet parameters $\alpha_{k}=e_{k}+1$ ), so the total strength $\alpha_{0}=\sum_{k}\alpha_{k}$ is not fixed a priori. Here $\alpha_{0}=N+|\mathcal{S}|$ is pinned by construction, and all flexibility in the prior is routed through $\hat{\pi}_{\beta}$ . The resulting $\text{Dir}(\cdot;\bm{\alpha})$ is fed into the MSE loss of Eq. 2, completing the differentiable path promised by desideratum (b), as the entire computational chain $\ell^{(i)}\rightarrow u^{(i)}\rightarrow\hat{\pi}_{\beta}\rightarrow\bm{e}\rightarrow\bm{\alpha}\rightarrow\mathcal{L}_{\text{MSE}}$ is differentiable w.r.t. $\bm{\theta}$ .

## CONCLUSION
We presented S-EDL, a method that constructs a Dirichlet prior over prompt-specific semantic classes from a model’s own sequence likelihoods, yielding a differentiable evidential objective for fine-tuning LLMs toward semantic calibration. Our gradient analysis shows that this objective conservatively reallocates probability mass across semantic outcomes, raising the likelihoods of sequences in the ground-truth class while suppressing those of the most overconfident incorrect alternative. Empirically, this reallocation produces simultaneous gains in accuracy and calibration. More broadly, our results suggest that grounding calibration objectives in a model’s own generative distribution offers a principled path toward trustworthy open-ended LLMs.

Limitations. While our find-first algorithm mentioned above substantially accelerates semantic grouping, the associated sampling and clustering steps entail a moderate training overhead compared to standard SFT. Additionally, for general tasks that necessitate an LLM judge, this requirement imposes unavoidable memory overhead and potential noise in clustering outcomes. To streamline this process, future work could bypass external verifiers or LLMs entirely by leveraging the hidden representations of generated sequences to estimate semantic dispersion, conceptually similar to methods like EigenScore [6]. Finally, we have not yet tested S-EDL on long-context tasks or domains where semantic equivalence is intrinsically difficult to define, such as code generation. Adapting S-EDL to these challenging scenarios remains an important direction for future exploration.

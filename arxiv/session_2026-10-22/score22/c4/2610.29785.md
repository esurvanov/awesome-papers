# 2610.29785 — Where Do LLMs Learn to Recommend AI?
Authors: Benaya Trabelsi, Jonathan Shaki, Sarit Kraus
Comment: 18 pages, 4 figures. Companion paper to arXiv:2601.13749
Categories: cs.CL, cs.AI, cs.CY, cs.LG

## ABSTRACT
Asked what to study, which career to pursue, or what product to build, large language models (LLMs) often recommend AI-related options. We ask where this tendency comes from by comparing models before and after fine-tuning and by varying how questions are asked. First, pretrained models already include explicit AI/ML terms in the recommendations of 27% of their responses, and their released instruction-tuned versions do so in 59%. Second, fine-tuning models ourselves shows that this rise is not inevitable: general instruction tuning leaves the rate unchanged, but removing the 7% of instruction examples that concern AI cuts it by 14 percentage points, below the level of the pretrained models it starts from. Data rewarding helpfulness raise it by 9 points, while data ranked by the preferences of diverse simulated users lower it by 9. Finally, with models held fixed, asking for the "best" option or for what to do "right now" raises the rate by 8 and 18 points, whereas telling the model it is an AI advisor adds 4. Overall, the tendency starts in pretraining, and what models learn afterward, together with how a question is phrased, decides how strongly it appears.

## 1 Introduction

When users seek open-ended advice from large language models (LLMs), the recommendations can shape which options they consider and, ultimately, their decisions. This makes systematic tendencies in these recommendations important to understand, even when individual suggestions appear reasonable.

One such tendency is pro-AI prominence: the frequent inclusion of AI-related content in open-ended recommendations. Prior work documented such prominence in open-ended advice, and more favorable estimates for AI cases in matched prediction tasks (Trabelsi et al., 2026), while other studies report self-preference in LLM evaluations, preferences for AI-generated content, and provider bias in code generation (Panickssery et al., 2024; Laurito et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2025). This prominence is not necessarily unjustified, but its origins remain unclear: when an instruction-tuned model recommends AI, the recommendation alone does not reveal whether this tendency reflects pretrained behavior, subsequent training, the system context, or the wording of the request itself.

## Possible sources.

We examine four explanations, which are not mutually exclusive (Figure 1).
H1 (pretraining prior): pretrained models may already associate AI with innovation, growth, or future value.
H2 (post-training): post-training may strengthen or weaken pro-AI prominence, depending on the training data, method and objectives.
H3 (AI identity/context): mentioning AI in the system context, particularly by identifying the assistant itself as AI, may change what it recommends.
H4 (prompt wording): temporal and superlative wording, such as “right now” and “best”, may shift recommendations toward AI-related content.

## Approach.

We pose 20 open-ended advice questions from four domains, each in ten paraphrases, and measure how often a model’s recommendation includes explicit AI/ML terms. To test H1 and H2, we compare ten released Base–Instruct pairs and train eleven pretrained models with six post-training recipes, comparing each recipe with its parent checkpoint. To test H3 and H4, we vary the system message and the wording of the question for ten instruction-tuned models.

## Main findings.

Pro-AI prominence is already substantial in pretrained models, consistent with H1, and far higher in their released instruction-tuned counterparts. Controlled post-training shows that this shift is not a generic effect of instruction tuning (H2): general supervised fine-tuning (SFT) leaves pro-AI prominence almost unchanged, whereas other training recipes raise or lower it depending on their data. At inference time, temporal and superlative wording (H4) raise pro-AI prominence far more than AI cues in the system context (H3), and identifying the assistant itself as AI adds no significant further increase.

## 2 Related Work

LLM recommendations often reflect factors beyond the substance of a request. Name cues affect employment and salary recommendations (Nghiem et al., 2024), while demographic and small linguistic cues affect educational recommendations (Shailya et al., 2025; Benedetto et al., 2026). Research has also examined popularity bias in LLM recommenders and changes in product recommendations under different framing (Lichtenberg et al., 2024; Filandrianos et al., 2025).

## 2.1 Bias across training stages

Bias in LLM behavior reflects both pretraining and later training. Instruction-tuned models were shown to exhibit stronger decision biases than Base or less tuned models, yet experiments that vary instruction datasets suggest that several biases follow the pretrained model more closely than the later training data (Itzhak et al., 2024; Itzhak et al., 2025). These studies compare released models or exchange whole instruction datasets; we instead remove one content category from otherwise identical instruction data and compare preference-optimization recipes applied to the same checkpoint. Controlled studies also show that changes to AI-related text during pretraining affect later alignment behavior, even after SFT and Direct Preference Optimization (DPO), and that small differences near the end of pretraining change responses to the same subsequent preference training (Tice et al., 2026; Lu et al., 2026).

Preference optimization was shown to reinforce biases in training feedback, including through methods such as DPO (Rafailov et al., 2023): biased preference rewards increase models’ tendency to agree with users, while preference models favor simple features such as length, technical language, and agreement (Shapira et al., 2026; Bharadwaj et al., 2026). Together, these findings suggest that both training history and post-training choices matter; we test their effects on pro-AI recommendations.

## 2.2 Prompt and system-context effects

Model behavior also varies with prompt wording. Evaluation results based on a single instruction template differ from averages across paraphrases, and even meaning-preserving edits substantially change outcomes (Mizrahi et al., 2024; Sclar et al., 2024). Specific effects include apparent publication dates favoring newer passages during reranking and context cues changing how models respond to assumptions in user requests (Fang et al., 2025; Cheng et al., 2026). Targeted prompt changes are also often difficult to distinguish from ordinary sensitivity to wording (Yang et al., 2026).

System prompts provide another source of variation. Assigning roles to an assistant was shown to affect its answers without consistently improving task accuracy (Zheng et al., 2024). We therefore distinguish changes caused by wording and surrounding context from those caused by identifying the assistant as AI.

## 2.3 AI as a subject of preference

AI is itself a subject of model preferences. LLM evaluators were shown to favor their own generations, while preferences for LLM-generated communication and for evaluators’ own model-generated résumés have been observed in other decisions (Panickssery et al., 2024; Laurito et al., 2025; Xu et al., 2025). Provider preferences also appear in code generation and recommendations among competing AI models (Zhang et al., 2025; Dubash and Friedler, 2026). These effects concern who produced the content or which provider is favored, rather than whether AI or ML appears within the recommendation itself. Closest to our study, prior work documented AI prominence in open-ended recommendations and more favorable estimates for matched AI cases in salary and program-completion tasks (Trabelsi et al., 2026). Whereas that work documents the phenomenon, we study where it comes from, across pretraining, controlled post-training, and inference-time prompts.

## 3 Experimental Design

## 3.1 Recommendation task

We study open-ended advice questions with many plausible answers. The four domains are Study, Career, Startup, and Scientific Research. Study, Career, and Startup follow Trabelsi et al. (2026), who chose domains covering consequential choices for which users commonly seek LLM advice. We add Scientific Research because researchers widely use LLMs in their work (Liao et al., 2025), and LLMs are increasingly studied as sources of research ideas (Si et al., 2025). Each domain contains five conceptual questions, each asked in ten fixed paraphrases. The questions are in English and contain no information about the person asking. All comparisons except the wording test ask every question with both a superlative and a temporal cue (e.g., “What is the best {concept} right now?”); the wording test varies these cues. All responses are generated with temperature 0, top-p 1, a 512-token output limit, and seed 12345, with thinking disabled for Qwen models.

## 3.2 Primary outcome

We first detect explicit AI/ML terms (e.g., artificial intelligence, machine learning, deep learning, LLMs and generative AI; full list in Appendix A.5) using fixed lexical patterns, then use two LLM filters to exclude mentions outside the recommendation itself. Responses without such terms are counted as negative. Each filter must answer with a single yes/no label; any other output (0.1% of DeepSeek and under 0.01% of Qwen judgments) is invalid and treated as missing rather than negative.

We use Qwen3.6-27B and DeepSeek-V4-Flash as the two filters. Each filter’s rate is computed over the responses it classified validly, and the two rates are averaged with equal weight for each model, condition, and question.

Our measure of pro-AI prominence is the share of responses with explicit AI/ML terms within their recommendations, averaged over the two filters. Responses without a recommendation count as zero, so the measure reflects how often a user who asks the question receives an AI recommendation. Empty responses are excluded rather than counted as zero (Appendix A.5).

## 3.3 Statistical analysis

Effects are calculated from matched experimental conditions, averaging equally across the evaluated models and the 20 questions (five per domain). The 20 questions are our statistical units; the ten paraphrases of a question are treated as repeated wordings of it, not as separate observations.

We compute 95% confidence intervals by resampling questions within each domain (20,000 bootstrap samples).

Significance is tested by flipping the signs of the 20 question-level effects in all $2^{20}$ possible ways (two-sided exact test of the null hypothesis that each question’s effect is equally likely to be positive or negative). We apply Holm correction separately within the four prespecified hypothesis families (H1: one test; H2: six; H3: three; H4: three), using $\alpha=0.05$ .

## 4 How Post-Training Changes Pro-AI Prominence

We first ask whether pro-AI prominence is already present
before instruction tuning, and whether subsequent training consistently amplifies it. We address these questions through released Base–Instruct comparisons and controlled experiments with specific post-training recipes.

Figure 2: Released Base–Instruct contrast (top) and six controlled post-training comparisons. Markers show effects with 95% confidence intervals. Each controlled branch is compared with its immediate parent, as identified by the row labels.

## 4.1 Released Instruct models show a large shift

Released Instruct checkpoints include explicit AI/ML terms within recommendations substantially more often than their pretrained Base counterparts. Across ten matched Base–Instruct model lines from five open-weight model families, pro-AI prominence increases by an average of 32.48 percentage points (95% CI [29.46, 35.39]; Holm-adjusted $p<.001$ ). On average, 26.80% of responses from released Base checkpoints and 59.28% from released Instruct checkpoints recommend an option with explicit AI/ML terms.

Both checkpoints receive the same three fixed plain-text input formats, designed so that either checkpoint type completes them as an answer; their results are averaged equally. The high rate in Base checkpoints shows that pro-AI prominence does not require instruction tuning. Which training steps raise it is not isolated by this comparison; the controlled experiments below address that question.

## 4.2 Controlled post-training reveals different effects

We next examine whether different post-training recipes consistently change prominence in the same direction.
We evaluate eleven model lines from six families in a
controlled training tree. General SFT and Clean no-AI SFT start from the pretrained Base checkpoint, while Helpfulness DPO, Safety DPO, Role SFT, and Persona DPO start from General SFT.
Each procedure is compared with its immediate parent under the same fixed input formats. The two SFT branches compare ordinary instruction tuning with tuning on the same data minus its AI-related examples; the four branches built on General SFT add common later stages: helpfulness, safety, or persona/value-oriented preference optimization, or role-conditioned instruction tuning.

Figure 2 places these six controlled comparisons alongside the released Base–Instruct contrast. Because the comparisons start from different checkpoints, they are not successive steps of one training run.

## General SFT changes little.

General SFT uses a 60k-example instruction mixture based on Tülu 3, UltraChat, and OpenAssistant-style conversations (Lambert et al., 2025; Ding et al., 2023; Köpf et al., 2023). It changes pro-AI prominence by only $+0.02$ pp on average (95% CI [ $-1.58$ , 1.73]; Holm-adjusted $p=.996$ ), with effects of both signs across models (Appendix Figure 4).

## Clean no-AI SFT lowers pro-AI prominence.

A different result emerges when the instruction data are filtered.
Clean no-AI SFT uses the same instruction pool after removing the 7.2% of examples that are AI/ML/LLM-related. Relative to Base, it reduces pro-AI prominence by 13.89 pp (95% CI [ $-16.09$ , $-11.60$ ]; Holm-adjusted $p<.001$ ). Its absolute level falls from 24.64% in Base to 10.74%, so SFT does not always move pro-AI prominence in the same direction.

## Preference-training branches move in opposite directions.

Helpfulness DPO uses a 108k helpfulness-preference mixture including Tülu preference data, UltraFeedback, and HelpSteer2-style pairs (Cui et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2024b). Persona DPO uses 20k response pairs ranked by the persona/value preferences of synthetic users, based on PERSONA (Castricato et al., 2025).

Helpfulness DPO raises pro-AI prominence by 9.48 pp (95% CI [7.37, 11.60]; Holm-adjusted $p<.001$ ). Persona DPO moves in the opposite direction, lowering it relative to General SFT by 8.95 pp (95% CI [ $-10.76$ , $-7.15$ ]; Holm-adjusted $p<.001$ ).

The shared General-SFT starting point makes this contrast particularly informative: the two DPO branches differ by approximately 18 percentage points in their estimated effects.
Applying preference optimization therefore does not imply a uniform direction of change; the associated training recipe matters. We discuss possible reasons in Section 6.

Safety DPO, trained on 20k safety-preference pairs from PKU-SafeRLHF (Ji et al., 2025), produces a smaller but significant decrease ( $-2.34$ pp, 95% CI [ $-3.27$ , $-1.42$ ]; Holm-adjusted $p=.003$ ). Role SFT, trained on 20k role-conditioned dialogues from ZenMoore/RoleBench (Wang et al., 2024a), shows no significant change ( $+2.44$ pp; Holm-adjusted $p=.136$ ).

Post-training therefore does not uniformly amplify pro-AI prominence: the direction and size of each change depend on the training recipe rather than the training stage.

## 5 Prompt-Level Effects

Recommendations may also change with the input alone, without any change to model weights. We therefore keep model weights fixed and investigate two possible influences at inference time, each on ten instruction-tuned model lines from five families: the criteria expressed in the user’s request and explicit AI cues in the system context.

## 5.1 Temporal wording and superlatives both increase explicit AI/ML recommendations

We first vary only the wording of the user’s question, using each model’s chat template. We vary two cues, each present or absent: a superlative cue (best, strongest, or most promising) and a temporal cue (right now, currently, or at present). This yields four conditions: N (neither), S (superlative only), T (temporal only), and ST (both); for example, an ST prompt is “What is the best {concept} right now?” Each condition has ten paraphrase templates; in five of them (P6–P10), the conditions share a sentence frame and differ only in the cues (Appendix Tables 9 and 10). ## LIMITATIONS SECTION
We measure explicit AI/ML terminology within recommendations, not their quality or whether AI-related advice is justified. This measure can miss implicit AI recommendations, and compared with a human expert it misses some explicit ones (86.6% recall), so its absolute rates are conservative.

Base checkpoints establish that the tendency is already present, but not its origin within pretraining. Recent enthusiasm for AI may contribute to this tendency, but further research is needed to determine its influence. Controlled comparisons evaluate complete training recipes rather than isolating individual mechanisms, and each branch has only one training run per model. Statistical inference treats the evaluated models as fixed and resamples questions; the findings may differ across other models, languages, domains, and settings.

## CONCLUSION
Recent work found that models often recommend AI, but did not call this recommendation pattern bias, because no reference says how often AI should be recommended (Trabelsi et al., 2026). Our comparisons do not need such a reference: each holds the advice question fixed, yet pretrained and instruction-tuned versions, models trained on different data, and models given different system messages recommend AI at very different rates, from 11% after Clean no-AI SFT to 73% when the question asks for the best option right now. Whatever the appropriate rate is, they cannot all be right. We therefore call a shift toward AI-related options in what models recommend, relative to the same question under another condition, pro-AI bias.

The largest shifts come from factors that add little information about the user’s needs. A user asking for advice already wants the best option for their current situation, yet saying “best” or “right now” pushes models strongly toward AI. Released Instruct checkpoints recommend AI-related options far more often than their Base counterparts; Base models are not explicitly trained to answer questions, but both versions receive identical prompts, designed so that either answers them. Telling the model that it is an AI advisor, or that the conversation will reach an AI grammar checker, also increases AI-related recommendations, although neither changes what would serve the user.

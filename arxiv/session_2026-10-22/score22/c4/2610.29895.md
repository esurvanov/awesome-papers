## ABSTRACT
Over the past decade, substantial efforts have been devoted to developing machine learning (ML) methods for extracting materials data and, where possible, materials knowledge directly from the scientific literature. This review traces the evolution of these methods from heuristic, rule-based pipelines through natural language processing (NLP) models and transformer-based encoders to, lastly, large language models (LLMs) and agentic systems capable of extracting materials information across multiple modalities, including text, tables, and figures. Beside construction of large, queryable materials databases and knowledge graphs that support downstream materials discovery, we discuss how these advances have enabled new capabilities, such as, generic question answering across materials science, AI co-scientists and chatbots for research assistance, and closed-loop optimization and discovery when integrated with autonomous laboratories. We critically assess the datasets generated using these approaches, their extraction accuracy and computational costs, and the respective strengths and limitations of different methodologies, and even provide practical guidelines for developing more reliable and scalable materials data extraction pipelines. Finally, we identify key open challenges, including the extraction of full materials structure-property-processing-performance tetrahedron at the sample level, the scarcity of reliable benchmark datasets for systematic evaluation, and the resolution of non-standard materials terminology across different document modalities.

## 1 Introduction

Materials science can be viewed as a continual effort to understand and exploit the relationships captured by the structure-processing-properties-performance tetrahedron. Experiments, whether computational or physical, probe these relationships across an enormous space of compositions, structures, processing conditions, and applications, with their findings subsequently reported in peer-reviewed journal articles, patents, theses, books and even supplementary information. However, the resulting materials knowledge is predominantly stored in unstructured or semi-structured forms—text, tables, figures, captions, and supporting files—distributed across an enormous and rapidly expanding literature corpus, estimated to contain of the order of $10^{7}$ publications [1, 2]. Only a small fraction of this knowledge is available in structured form through handbooks, curated databases, and archival resources. Thus, while on one hand, the materials literature contains a vast body of evidence, relationships and trends (either established or hidden) that can reveal how materials behave, how their properties depend on structure and processing, and what design principles govern their success in a certain application. On the other hand, this knowledge remains difficult to access systematically at scale.

The distinction between materials data and materials knowledge becomes important in this context. Materials data comprise of individual observations, e.g., a composition synthesized by a particular route, a measured property under specified conditions, or a characterization result reported in a figure or table. Materials knowledge is a more general and compact representation of such observations in the form of relationships, trends, empirical rules, and physical models that enable prediction. Examples include the Hall–Petch relation [3], Hume–Rothery rules [4], and Vegard’s law [5], which allow researchers to generalize beyond individual observations and anticipate the behaviour of previously untested materials. A materials researcher develops such knowledge as part of their formal training and by absorbing the literature, comparing observations, and gradually forming an internal model of composition–processing–structure–property relationships [6]. This process of converting numerous observations into a compact, predictive understanding may be viewed as a form of knowledge compression. It is, however, inherently limited by the amount of literature an individual can access and integrate, and by the difficulty of identifying connections distributed across different studies and subfields.

Machine learning (ML) offers a complementary route to this compression by learning patterns directly from large collections of materials data rather than relying on explicitly formulated physical relations or individual researcher experience. Property-prediction models can learn composition–structure–property relationships, while coupling these models with high-throughput calculations, active learning, and optimization methods allow exploration of materials spaces in a more systematic and efficient manner than possible through efforts of a single human researcher. However, a major bottleneck in this approach is the availability of reliable, machine-readable data. Although the information required to train such ML models is often already present in the literature, but not in a form that can be readily accessed and processed. For example, synthesizing a new polymer or ceramic may require determining suitable precursors, their concentrations, reaction conditions, and, in some cases, a sequence of intermediate reactions by examining synthesis reports across many related materials. Consequently, the central bottleneck is not the absence of materials information, but the gap between the form in which the information exists in published literature and the form in which information is required as structured, machine-actionable data. Manual curation can, and have been, used to produce high-quality databases, but its limited to specific class of materials and use-cases and cannot scale with the volume and diversity of the literature [7, 8, 9].

This gap motivates the development of materials data (and, if possible, directly materials knowledge) extraction methods, particularly those based on natural language processing (NLP) and, more recently, transformer-based large language (LLM) models. The objective is to transform the heterogeneous content of scientific publications—text, tables, figures, and supplementary information—into structured records that can act as a knowledge base for answering materials-related queries or support downstream materials discovery ML pipelines.

This work tracks the evolution of materials data extraction as a progression from manual curation, to computational generation, to automated mining of the experimental literature (see Figure 1). Early databases such as Landolt-Börnstein [10], ICSD [11], the Pauling File [12] and PoLyInfo [13], established highly reliable, expert-curated and gold standard repositories of materials structures and properties. However, their construction is inherently slow and difficult to scale. High-throughput computational approaches provided an alternative by generating data rather than extracting it. Platforms such as the Materials Project [14], AFLOW [15], OQMD [16], NOMAD [17], and Alexandria [18] enabled the systematic calculation of properties for millions of known and hypothetical structures, supporting materials screening beyond the experimentally characterised materials space. However, computational databases cannot readily capture several experimentally relevant aspects of materials, including synthesis and processing conditions, disorder, dopants, and device-level behaviour.

With the increasing maturity of AI/ML in other domains, rule-based NLP approaches such as ChemDataExtractor [19] began to emerge around 2015 for materials data extraction. These methods typically followed a two-stage pipeline of entity recognition and relation extraction. During the first stage of entity recognition, an ML model identifies and classifies relevant terms present in text or tables into predefined categories such as materials, synthesis methods, characterization methods, or properties. Relation extraction then links these entities using predefined linguistic patterns and domain-specific rules to produce structured property and synthesis records [19, 20, 21]. These approaches provided the first demonstrations of large-scale automated extraction of materials data, but their dependence on hand-crafted rules limited their generalizability across different materials domain. Transformer-based encoders, such as MatBERT, marked the next era, where contextual representations of text enabled more robust entity and relation extraction, although these approaches required dedicated efforts for model development as well as task-specific labeled data [9, 22]. The emergence of large language models (LLMs) around 2023 further shifted the paradigm towards general-purpose extraction. Prompting enabled structured extraction with little or no task-specific training, while fine-tuning enabled more reliable domain-specialized models from relatively small annotated datasets [23, 24, 25]. Beyond improving the scale and flexibility of extraction, LLMs also expanded the target representation from flat material-property records to more comprehensive materials synthesis, processing, structure, and properties records, and to even knowledge graphs [26]. More recently, agentic frameworks have further enhanced the extraction quality by enabling dynamic coordination of specialized models and tools applicable on multiple modalities (figures, tables and text). Knowledge graphs, on the other hand, provide a more efficient relational representation of materials data with explicit provenance, and the ability to achieve higher-level reasoning and hypothesis generation, bringing these models closer to the ultimate goal of materials knowledge extraction rather than mere data extraction.

This review traces this progression in materials data extraction, highlighting the opportunities, limitations, and evolving solutions associated with each era. We thoroughly discuss the databases developed using these approaches, the accuracy and cost of extraction, and the diverse data modalities considered. Finally, we discuss key open challenges and potential opportunities to improve the quality and efficiency of materials data and knowledge extraction from the literature corpus.

## 2 The forms of materials data and the difficulty of extracting them

Before we discuss the different approaches adopted for data extraction, it is important to understand what are the different forms of materials data that are usually targeted and the associated challenges. Mainly materials information is reported in five broad categories: crystallographic structure files, physical property measurements, synthesis and processing procedures, characterization data in the form of images (e.g., micrographs and device performance curves), and spectral or diffraction data. These forms differ substantially in both their amenability to automated extraction and the maturity of existing extraction pipelines, and these two aspects are not necessarily correlated, as noted in Figure 1b. Crystallographic data is the clearest case of both high maturity and high automatability. Its high automatability stems from being structured at the point of generation, with standardized CIF files [27] containing well-defined fields for unit-cell parameters, space group, and atomic coordinates. Thus, extraction simply amounts to parsing a structured file rather than interpreting information from free text. Further, its high maturity is reflected in dedicated repositories such as ICSD [28, 11], CSD [29], and COD [30], which have accumulated and curated on the order of $10^{5}$ – $10^{6}$ entries.

Property data is arguably the most sought-after category of materials data and has consequently attracted extensive development of ML-based extraction pipelines. Several large-scale efforts have produced text-mined databases (e.g., for
thermoelectrics [31], magnetic materials [32, 33],
and general materials [34]), placing this category at moderate-to-high maturity. Despite these efforts, property data still remains only moderately amenable to automated extraction because of four recurring challenges. First, the literature reported terminology is highly variable and lacks standardized forms. The same compound may be reported by its full name, abbreviation, or chemical formula (e.g., “lithium cobalt oxide”, “LCO”, and “LiCoO2”, or in case of doping as ‘5% Mg-doped LiCoO2” or “LiCo0.95Mg0.05O2”). Property names may also be incomplete or require contextual interpretation—for example, ‘strength” could refer to yield, ultimate, or compressive strength. Extraction therefore requires contextual disambiguation beyond than simply accurately extracting and matching to a fixed vocabulary [35]. Second, extracting a complete record often requires resolving dependencies across different parts of a document, since information such as composition, processing conditions, and property values may be distributed across the abstract, methods, tables, and figure captions [36]. Reconstructing a complete composition–structure–processing–property record thus requires linking information spread across these heterogeneous locations. Third, tables, despite their structured appearance, are difficult to parse reliably. Headers may be merged, transposed, or abbreviated, while essential qualifying information (e.g., symbol definitions) may appear only in captions or surrounding text rather than in the table itself [37]. Lastly, even correctly extracted values can be semantically ambiguous as the full information may not be explicitly stated in the document but is rather implicit. A reported composition may represent the nominal target used during synthesis rather than the composition measured experimentally, while a property measured under specific testing conditions may be incorrectly tagged as an intrinsic property of the material. The combination of these challenges makes property-data extraction only moderately amenable to automation despite substantial progress in the field.

Synthesis and processing data remains low in both maturity and ease of automation. While dedicated synthesis databases exist (solid-state synthesis recipes [38], MOF synthesis records [39]), the absence of a structured standard for reporting this information, analogous to that of the CIF format, limits their maturity. In addition to the four challenges described for property extraction, the synthesis extraction entail additional challenges due to its inherent relational and sequential nature—precursors and their quantities, operations, and conditions involved must be correctly linked to one another to arrive at a correct synthesis record. These details are also often partly implicit, with conditions inherited from cited procedures or sometimes simply described as “standard.” This additional relational and sequential complexity makes synthesis data substantially harder to extract. Characterization images, such as micrographs and device performance curves, require visual and contextual reasoning, making them the least developed in both maturity and ease of automation. Few dedicated databases exist [40], while there is an additional need of multimodal reasoning to identify relevant figures, interpret their axes, legends, and scale, and link the visual information to the surrounding scientific text. Such capabilities are only beginning to be explored with vision-language models [41, 42]. Diffraction and other spectral data occupy a more unusual position. Diffraction data, in particular, has a long history of institutionalized curation—the ICDD Powder Diffraction File has served as a reference for phase identification since 1941 and now contains more than one million curated patterns [43], while Rietveld refinement has been widely used to extract structural information from diffraction patterns [44]. However, this maturity has relied largely on manual expert curation, and efforts to extract diffraction data directly from literature figures remain relatively nascent [45, 46, 47, 48, 49]. Other spectral data, including Raman, FTIR, XPS, and NMR, are less mature, with existing resources typically limited to specific techniques or material classes, such as the RRUFF database of mineral Raman and infrared spectra [50]. Overall, we consider this category moderately mature but still difficult to automate, particularly because data must be recovered from published figures, which requires the same multimodal reasoning challenges encountered in characterization-image extraction. Overall, this discussion forms the backdrop in which several efforts for materials data extraction are discussed next. Importantly, not all materials data types have achieved same level of success and each presents a unique set of challenges.

## ABSTRACT
Large language models (LLMs) are increasingly used to generate software engineering artifacts written in domain-specific languages (DSLs). Correct DSL instances usually must comply with not only the concrete syntax but also the abstract syntax defined by a metamodel augmented with semantic well-formedness constraints. Existing constrained decoding approaches typically guarantee compliance only with concrete syntax or with a limited set of semantic constraints expressed over the generated text, making metamodel-level constraint consistency difficult to enforce during generation. We present MeMoDecode: a decoding framework that enforces a DSL's concrete syntax, abstract syntax, and well-formedness constraints jointly. Specifically, whenever the LLM generates a complete metamodel concept, MeMoDecode incrementally maps the current output to a partial model and verifies whether it can still be completed into a consistent instance. We combine MeMoDecode with a simple retry mechanism that resumes decoding from the most recent completable snapshot whenever an inconsistency is detected. Under a sound backend verifier, MeMoDecode never performs worse than grammar-constrained decoding on alignment metrics satisfying natural assumptions. Across four DSLs and six open-weight LLMs ranging from 0.6B to 70B parameters, MeMoDecode achieves the highest consistency, improving consistency by 23% on average over unconstrained decoding and by 8% on average over grammar-constrained decoding. It also improves benchmark performance while incurring only modest runtime overhead. These results show that metamodel-guided decoding is an effective approach for enforcing DSL consistency during decoding across diverse DSLs and LLM scales.

## I Introduction

Context.
Modern software engineering (SE) relies on a wide range of artifacts, from architecture design and configuration to query languages and flowcharts, many of which are expressed in domain-specific languages (DSLs) tailored to a particular task or domain [1]. As large language models (LLMs) are increasingly used to automate SE activities, a growing portion of these artifacts is generated, either fully or partially, by LLMs, from natural language specifications [2, 3]. Beyond traditional SE workflows, DSL artifacts also serve as structured interfaces in LLM-based agentic systems, supporting multi-agent communication [4, 5, 6] and tool use [7, 8, 9].

In SE, especially in model-driven engineering, DSL instances are often used as machine-processable artifacts for downstream automation, analysis, model transformation, and code generation [10, 11, 12].
As a result, a DSL is typically characterized by three complementary aspects: its concrete syntax, usually defined by a grammar; its abstract syntax, captured by a metamodel; and a set of semantic well-formedness constraints over that metamodel, with execution semantics defined on top of them [13, 14, 15]. These aspects form a hierarchy of correctness. A generated instance may satisfy the grammar while still violating the metamodel or its constraints, preventing meaningful analysis, transformation, or execution. A DSL instance is consistent if it conforms to both the metamodel and the well-formedness constraints [16].
However, LLMs provide no guarantee that generated DSL instances satisfy these specifications. They may produce outputs that violate the grammar, the metamodel, or the well-formedness constraints, motivating the need for verification techniques [17, 18, 19].

A natural way to improve the reliability of generated DSL instances is post-hoc verification, where a complete instance is first generated and then checked or repaired [20, 21, 22, 23]. However, these approaches discover violations only after a full generation. As a result, they may waste substantial decoding effort and require additional repair steps. Moreover, as LLM generation is conditioned on existing prefixes, when the generated instance contains early structural errors, subsequent parts of the output are generated from an already invalid context, potentially hurting the overall quality [24, 25].

These limitations motivate decoding-time approaches that prevent invalid outputs before they are completed. Grammar-constrained decoding masks, at each decoding step, tokens that would violate the grammar, thereby guaranteeing syntactic validity [26, 27, 28, 29, 30, 31]. However, operating only on concrete syntax, it provides no guarantee that the generated DSL instance conforms to the metamodel or the associated constraints.

Recent approaches further incorporate semantic constraint verification into the decoding process [32, 33, 34]. For example, IterGen [35] augments grammar-constrained decoding with semantic checks. However, these checks are typically expressed over the generated text rather than the underlying structured model. Consequently, verification remains tied to a particular textual representation. More importantly, it limits the ability to reason about properties that are naturally defined over objects and references in the metamodel, such as multiplicity constraints, referential integrity, and transitive relationships.
As a result, decoding-time verification and guidance of metamodel-level constraint consistency over the underlying structured representation remain underexplored [19].

Approach.
In this paper, we present MeMoDecode (MetaModel-guided Decoding), a decoding framework that simultaneously enforces a DSL’s concrete syntax, abstract syntax, and semantic well-formedness constraints. The key idea is to lift verification from the generated text to the underlying model representation. As decoding proceeds, MeMoDecode incrementally maps the generated text to a partial model and, whenever the LLM completes a metamodel concept, verifies whether that partial model remains satisfiable with respect to the metamodel and its well-formedness constraints, that is, whether it can still be completed into a consistent instance. If not,
the violation is irreparable, and
MeMoDecode can immediately backtrack and retry during decoding rather than relying on post-hoc regeneration of the entire output.

Contribution.
Because verification is performed over the model representation rather than the concrete-syntax text, MeMoDecode can enforce structured metamodel-level properties that are difficult or impossible to express using grammar-based approaches. Furthermore, since constraints are specified directly over the metamodel, the verification process is independent of the concrete syntax of the DSL and can be applied across different textual representations.
More specifically, this paper makes the following contributions:

• 

We present MeMoDecode, a decoding framework that simultaneously enforces a DSL’s concrete syntax, abstract syntax, and semantic well-formedness constraints by verifying whether partially generated outputs can still be completed into consistent instances and retrying otherwise.

• 

We establish a worst-case guarantee: under a sound verifier and natural assumptions on the alignment metric, MeMoDecode never produces an output that is less aligned than the one generated by grammar-constrained decoding under identical decoding conditions.

• 

We implement MeMoDecode by combining grammar-constrained decoding [27] with the Refinery verifier [36] and a lightweight decoding-time retry mechanism.

• 

We conduct a systematic evaluation across four DSLs and six open-weight LLMs ranging from 0.6B to 70B parameters. The results show that MeMoDecode consistently improves DSL consistency, increases alignment with the ground truth, and incurs only modest runtime overhead.

Added value.
MeMoDecode demonstrates that reasoning over the underlying model representation is key to ensuring consistency of a DSL instance. By lifting verification to the metamodel level, it enables the use of established partial modeling and constraint-solving techniques within the decoding process, yielding consistency guarantees independent of the textual representation. Since verification is decoupled from the underlying LLM, MeMoDecode can benefit directly from future advances in both LLMs and backend verifiers.

## II Background and Related Work

## II-1 Domain-Specific Languages

A domain-specific language (DSL) is tailored to express the concepts of a particular application domain [1].
By focusing on a specific domain, DSLs can provide substantially higher ease of use than general-purpose programming languages for the tasks they are designed to support [11]. For instance, Figure 1 shows a CLEVR program specified using a DSL. A CLEVR program is a structured, machine-executable representation of a natural-language question about an image [37]. The listing illustrates a minimal program to answer the question “What color is the cube?”. The program begins with a scene node that returns the full set of objects in the scene, which is then passed to filter_shape to retain only cube-shaped objects. The unique function asserts that exactly one such object exists, promoting the set of objects to a single object. Finally, query_color is applied to retrieve the color of that object.

## II-2 DSL specifications

In SE, and especially in model-driven engineering, a DSL is characterized by three aspects: a concrete syntax, an abstract syntax, and a set of semantic well-formedness constraints [13, 14, 15] (see Figure 2). Formally, a DSL specification can be defined as
 $\mathcal{D}=(G,M,\mathcal{C}),$ 
where $G$ is a context-free grammar (CFG) defining the concrete syntax, $M$ is a metamodel defining the abstract syntax, and $\mathcal{C}$ is a set of semantic well-formedness constraints over $M$ .

The concrete syntax specifies the notation through which users interact with DSL instances. This paper focuses on textual DSLs whose concrete syntax is defined by a CFG $G$ . In the CLEVR case, a language engineer could define the CFG shown in Figure 2. The non-terminals of $G$ define textual forms for different syntactic categories of the language, such as edge, program node, and the type of a node.
We refer to these completed derivations of selected non-terminals as syntactic units. A text sequence $\boldsymbol{y}$ is syntactically valid if it belongs to the language generated by $G$ , i.e., $\boldsymbol{y}\in L(G)$ .

The abstract syntax defines a structural representation of DSL instances and is commonly specified through a metamodel [15]. A metamodel $M$ defines the types of elements that may appear in an instance model, including classes, attributes, references, containment references, multiplicities, and inheritance relations, often using a metamodeling language such as EMF Ecore [38]. The right side of Figure 2 shows the metamodel of the CLEVR DSL. We use $I\models M$ to denote that an instance model $I$ conforms to the metamodel $M$ .

A set of semantic well-formedness constraints $\mathcal{C}$ complements the metamodel by specifying semantic rules that cannot be expressed by the grammar or the metamodel alone [16].
These constraints are commonly specified using a constraint language [14, 39]. Formally, a constraint $c\in\mathcal{C}$ is a predicate over an instance model: $c:\mathcal{I}_{M}\rightarrow\{\mathrm{true},\mathrm{false}\}$ . $\mathcal{I}_{M}$ represents the set of instance models conforming to $M$ and the output shows whether the constraint is satisfied. Figure 2 shows three out of the 17 constraints (single-root structure, type-correct dataflow, and acyclicity) of the CLEVR DSL.

To connect a text sequence to an instance model, language engineers typically define transformation rules from concrete syntax constructs, such as selected grammar non-terminals, to metamodel elements. Given such rules, a DSL instance written in the concrete syntax can be transformed into an instance model, for example, through parsing and model transformation techniques [40, 41]. The coloring in Figure 2 illustrates an example of such a correspondence between the grammar and the metamodel. A DSL instance is valid only if the resulting instance model $I$ is consistent with respect to $(M,\mathcal{C})$ , i.e., it conforms to the metamodel and satisfies all well-formedness constraints: $I\models(M,\mathcal{C})\iff I\models M\land\forall c\in\mathcal{C},\;c(I)=\mathrm{true}$ .

## II-3 Partial models

Partial models represent incomplete instances of a metamodel [42]. Instead of fully specifying all elements, a partial model records what is currently known and leaves the remaining information unspecified [16, 43, 39]. Following prior work, we view a partial model as an interpretation of metamodel-level facts, represented as predicates whose truth values may be known, unknown, or inconsistent. In the CLEVR DSL, a partial model may already contain facts such as $\textsc{Node}(n_{1})$ , $\textsc{Node}(n_{2})$ , $\textsc{Edge}(e_{1})$ , $\textsc{source}(e_{1},n_{1})$ , and $\textsc{target}(e_{1},n_{2})$ , while other facts, such as the node type of $n_{2}$ or the remaining outgoing edges of $n_{1}$ , remain unknown. A complete instance model can be obtained by refining the partial model, i.e., by adding new facts until the objects, references, and attribute values are fully determined.

Refinery is a graph solver for generating and analyzing domain-specific graph models from partial specifications [36]. In this paper, we use Refinery as the backend solver for reasoning about the partial models constructed during decoding. The design of MeMoDecode is compatible with any backend that can reason soundly about partial models.

## II-4 Large language models

LLMs are deep neural networks that typically follow the decoder-only Transformer architecture [44]. They are trained on massive collections of textual data using the language modeling task, which consists of predicting the next token given a sequence of preceding tokens.
In practice, the generation follows an autoregressive process: an LLM receives as input a prompt $\boldsymbol{x}$ and produces a probability distribution over all tokens in its vocabulary, $\mathrm{LLM(\boldsymbol{x})}=P(\cdot|\boldsymbol{x})$ . Based on this distribution and a chosen decoding strategy,
the next token $t\sim P(\cdot|\boldsymbol{x})$ is selected by a sampler and appended to the prompt. The updated sequence is fed back into the LLM, and the process repeats until a predefined termination condition is reached, such as generating an end-of-sequence token, to create an output sequence $\boldsymbol{y}$ .

## II-5 Decoding strategies for LLMs

A decoding strategy determines how the next token is selected from the probability distribution $P(\cdot|\boldsymbol{x})$ predicted by an LLM from a prompt $\boldsymbol{x}$ .

Unconstrained decoding
strategies select tokens from the probability distribution produced by the LLM directly, without considering DSL-specific validity constraints. For example, temperature sampling adjusts the sharpness of this distribution by scaling the next-token logits before normalization. Other commonly used strategies include greedy decoding, beam search [45], top- $k$ sampling [46], and top- $p$ sampling [47].

Constrained decoding.
In many applications, the generated sequence is required to satisfy constraints beyond those captured by the language model distribution. Constrained decoding refers to decoding methods that restrict token selection using an external constraint specification. Given a set of constraints $\Phi=\{\phi_{1},\ldots,\phi_{k}\}$ , the goal is to generate an output sequence $\boldsymbol{y}$ such that $\boldsymbol{y}$ satisfies every constraint in $\Phi$ , while still using the LLM probabilities to guide token selection.

When the constraint specification is a CFG $G$ , the constraint is that the final output must belong to the language generated by the grammar, i.e., $\boldsymbol{y}\in L(G)$ . Such methods are commonly referred to as grammar-constrained decoding [26, 27, 28, 29, 30, 31]. The core idea is to maintain a parser state induced by the output and, at each decoding step, mask tokens that would make the output impossible to extend into a syntactically valid sequence [26, 28]. As a result, these approaches enforce concrete-syntax validity during generation, but do not by themselves ensure that the resulting instance model is consistent.

Beyond grammar-constrained decoding.
Several approaches augment grammar-constrained decoding with additional semantic reasoning mechanisms.
These approaches typically refine the token mask by incorporating complementary information, such as semantic pruners or static analyzers, thereby restricting generation with additional constraints [32, 33, 34]. However, they either lack sufficient expressive power to capture complex structured semantic properties [32] or focus on a narrow class of constraints [34, 33]. For example, ChopChop [32] employs semantic pruners. Given a program space, a semantic pruner returns the subspace of programs that satisfy a particular constraint, such as type consistency. Such pruners cannot fully express all types of constraints.
Other approaches [33, 34, 48] focus only on specific language semantic properties rather than general DSL specifications.

IterGen [35] takes a different approach by building on SynCode [29] with an iterative backtracking mechanism that revises previously generated tokens upon constraint violations. ## CONCLUSION
DSL correctness goes beyond syntactic validity.
RQ1-RQ3 show that syntactic validity alone is insufficient to ensure correctness of generated DSL instances. Grammar-constrained decoding is effective at enforcing the concrete syntax, but it can still generate inconsistent instances and, in some cases, even achieve lower consistency than unconstrained decoding. By verifying the generated outputs at the metamodel level, MeMoDecode provides an additional layer of correctness beyond syntax. This leads to systematic improvements in consistency and, for alignment metrics that treat invalid instances as unusable, also improves alignment with the intended output.

LLM capacity and guarantee.
RQ1 and RQ3 show that larger LLMs can improve both consistency and alignment. However, they still fail to produce consistent DSL instances in many cases. Even GPT-5.5 generates inconsistent instances on three of the four benchmarks, reaching only 82.9% consistency on CLEVR. These results suggest that while LLM scaling reduces the frequency of inconsistent instances, it does not eliminate the need for formal verification, especially when the generated results are intended for downstream SE applications.

Runtime overhead and retry.
RQ4 shows that the main runtime overhead of MeMoDecode comes from the additional LLM inference introduced by regenerating tokens after rejected syntactic units, while metamodel-level verification accounts for only a small fraction of the total runtime.
Therefore, future work should focus on more efficient retry and recovery strategies, such as finer-grained backtracking, adaptive retry budgets, verifier-guided resampling, or decoding-time repair mechanisms that reduce the need for regeneration.

Limitations and future work.
While MeMoDecode improves over both baseline decoding approaches, several limitations remain for future work. First, this paper focuses on unfixable constraints, while accepting partial models with fixable violations.
Future work could explore how to use these fixable violations as decoding-time heuristics.
Currently, MeMoDecode focuses only on the final output generated by the LLM. Future work could extend the framework to reasoning LLMs, where verification may guide not only the generated DSL tokens but also intermediate reasoning traces. Finally, MeMoDecode could be integrated with DSL frameworks such as Xtext [55] and JetBrains MPS [67] to streamline DSL development with verified LLM-based instance generation.

## ABSTRACT
Large language models are typically trained with next-token prediction (NTP), which rewards predicting specific tokens rather than explicitly accounting for the semantic similarities between different words or expressions. Prior work has addressed this limitation by augmenting training data with minimally varying sentences that replace a target word with conceptually related alternatives, e.g., "The tired coach ran home" $\rightarrow$ {"The tired trainer ran home", "The tired instructor ran home", ...}. Such concept augmentation improves performance, but it remains unclear whether its effectiveness depends on where in the sentence the concept is expressed. We reproduce and extend prior work $\textit{beyond nouns}$ to verbs and adjectives, asking whether the part of speech (POS) carrying the concept signal affects perplexity and downstream performance. We find different patterns across evaluation settings: concept augmentation improves perplexity most consistently on news, independent of the POS used, whereas verbs provide the most reliable downstream gains. Despite these behavioral differences, representational analyses using CKA and linear probing show that the conditions yielding the largest downstream gains are not necessarily those that most alter the model's internal representations. Together, these results suggest that the effectiveness of concept augmentation depends on where and how conceptual information is introduced during training.

## 1 Introduction

Large language models (LLMs) are predominantly trained with next-token prediction (NTP), which rewards predicting the observed token in a sequence. While highly effective, NTP operates over individual tokens: different words are treated as distinct targets even when they express closely related meanings. Humans, by contrast, reason not only over words but also over concepts: semantic units that connect different linguistic expressions under shared meaning (Shani et al., 2023). For example, “trainer,” “coach,” and “instructor” are distinct lexical forms but closely related expressions of the same concept.

Recent work has begun to incorporate such conceptual relationships into language-model training. In particular, Iyer et al. (2026) introduce concept-aware post-training using synonym and hypernym supervision and show that concept signal can affect both language-model and downstream performance. However, their data-augmentation experiments focus on nouns, leaving open a basic question: does the linguistic category through which a concept is expressed affect the value of concept supervision?

Nouns, verbs, and adjectives encode different aspects of meaning: entities and categories, actions and relations, and properties and
states, respectively. For example, the sentence “The tired coach ran home” can be varied by replacing the noun “coach” with “trainer,” the verb “ran” with “hurried,” or the adjective “tired” with “exhausted.” Each substitution introduces a conceptually related alternative while changing a different component of the sentence. Concept-aware training may therefore induce different forms of invariance depending on which part of speech carries the concept signal.

We extend the data-augmentation approach of Iyer et al. (2026) from nouns to verbs, adjectives, and a combination of all three POS. We retain the standard NTP objective, isolating the effect of where conceptual variation is introduced rather than changing the training loss itself. We post-train Llama-3-8B with synonym and hypernym augmentation across arXiv, news, and YouTube data, and evaluate the resulting models using held-out perplexity and seven downstream NLP tasks.

Our results show that POS meaningfully shapes the effects of concept augmentation. Perplexity effects depend strongly on post-training domain, while verb augmentation provides the most consistent downstream gains across experimental settings. However, the strongest POS varies across domains and tasks, and augmenting all three POSs together does not consistently outperform targeted augmentation. These findings suggest that concept-aware training should not treat all content words as interchangeable sources of concept signal: POS provides an additional design axis for concept supervision.

## 2 Related Work

## Limitations of next-token prediction.

Several lines of work have questioned whether next-token prediction alone provides the right supervision for learning generalizable
semantic structure. Bachmann and Nagarajan (2025) show that teacher-forced NTP can fit the training distribution without learning
the underlying solution required for generalization. At the semantic
level, Shani et al. (2023) argue that token-level probabilities can fragment probability mass across different word choices of the same concept, while
Zhang et al. (2026) show that standard NTP treats semantically valid alternative continuations as competing targets. Our work does
not replace NTP, but asks whether introducing structured semantic variation into the training data changes what is learned under the same objective.

## Concept-aware language modeling.

Recent work has introduced concept-level structure at several points in the language-modeling pipeline. Shani et al. (2023)
study conceptual structure in LLMs and motivate moving beyond purely token-level representations. At the architectural level,
team et al. (2024) introduce Large Concept Models, which autoregressively predict sentence-level semantic embeddings rather
than individual tokens. Other work incorporates higher-level structure while retaining token prediction: Chen et al. (2025) use multi-token
prediction during post-training to encourage learning of coherent multi-token units, Tack et al. (2025) combine NTP with continuous concept representations derived from model activations, and Liu et al. (2026) jointly predict discrete latent concepts and
tokens. These approaches differ in what constitutes a “concept,” but
share the goal of providing supervision above the level of individual tokens.

Most closely related to our setting, Iyer et al. (2026) incorporate lexical-semantic concepts into post-training using synonyms
and hypernyms through both data augmentation and concept-level objectives. Zhang et al. (2026) instead construct self-supervised sets of contextually equivalent tokens over nouns, verbs, and adjectives
and directly optimize probability mass over those concept sets. Our question is complementary: we retain the standard NTP objective and use only data augmentation, allowing us to isolate how the
part of speech carrying the concept signal affects perplexity,
downstream transfer, training dynamics, and representation geometry.

## 3 Data Processing and Models

We follow the concept extraction, data augmentation, and post-training pipeline introduced by Iyer et al. (2026). Our downstream fine-tuning procedure differs and is described in Section 4.2. We therefore summarize the original pipeline and focus on the modifications introduced in this study.

## 3.1 Data & Concept Supervision Extraction

We use the post-training corpora of Iyer et al. (2026): arXiv abstracts, news text, and YouTube comments, representing scientific, journalistic, and informal language, respectively, as well as their combined condition. These domains let us test whether the effectiveness of POS-specific concept supervision depends on the domain in which it is introduced. We downsample conditions where necessary to balance post-training volume across augmentation variants.

We reprocess each corpus to extend the original noun-only augmentation to four target conditions: nouns, verbs, adjectives, and all POSs, combining all three categories.

For each target POS, we retain the two levels of conceptual resolution from Iyer et al. (2026): synonyms, which provide alternatives
at approximately the same semantic resolution (e.g., coach $\rightarrow$ trainer), and hypernyms, which provide broader conceptual abstractions (e.g., coach $\rightarrow$ 
professional).

We also retain their two extraction procedures: context-aware alternatives generated by an LLM conditioned on the full sentence and target word, and dictionary-based alternatives extracted from WordNet without sentence context.

Figure 1 depicts all of these conditions.

## 3.2 Concept Models

All experiments initialize from Llama-3-8B (Grattafiori et al., 2024). We further post-train a separate model for each POS, post-training corpus, semantic relation, and concept-source condition described above, following the post-training setup of Iyer et al. (2026).

The original study explored two ways to insert concept-supervision during post-training: a modified concept-level training objective and concept-aware data augmentation, in which additional training sentences are created using conceptually related substitutions. Because the data-augmentation approach performs more consistently in the original study, we focus exclusively on this setting. Figure 2 summarizes the full training pipeline, from POS-specific augmentation through standard NTP post-training and downstream fine-tuning.

## 3.3 Baselines

We post-train each POS condition on arXiv, news, YouTube, and a combined corpus. Each augmented model is paired with a size-matched vanilla model constructed from the same source sentences. Instead of replacing the target with a synonym or hypernym, the vanilla dataset repeats the original sentence as needed to match the number of augmented training examples. We construct this baseline separately for each POS, relation, and corpus, so augmentation and vanilla models differ in conceptual substitution rather than total training volume.

## 4 Experimental Setup

We now describe the evaluation procedure for the post-trained models. We measure intrinsic language-model performance using held-out perplexity and downstream transfer using seven classification benchmarks, reporting all results relative to matched vanilla models where applicable.

## 4.1 Perplexity

For perplexity, we report the absolute improvement relative to the
matched vanilla model:

 $\mathrm{PPL\ Improvement}=\mathrm{PPL}_{\mathrm{vanilla}}-\mathrm{PPL}_{\mathrm{concept}}.$ 

(1)

Positive values indicate lower, and therefore better, perplexity than the matched vanilla model.

For downstream evaluation, we report the accuracy difference from the corresponding vanilla model:

 $\Delta_{\mathrm{Acc}}=\mathrm{Acc}_{\mathrm{concept}}-\mathrm{Acc}_{\mathrm{vanilla}}.$ 

(2)

This lets us measure what conceptual augmentation contributes beyond additional exposure to the same underlying data.

## 4.2 Downstream Evaluation

We evaluate the post-trained models on the same seven downstream classification tasks as Iyer et al. (2026), but replace the original instruction-style fine-tuning procedure with LoRA-based sequence classification (Hu et al., 2021). This gives every benchmark a common classification formulation and enables validation-based early stopping, which we additionally use to analyze fine-tuning dynamics. We select the checkpoint with the lowest validation loss and report held-out test accuracy as our primary metric, along with weighted precision, recall, F1, and AUC-PR. Full LoRA and optimization hyperparameters are provided in Appendix C.

## 5.1 Reproducibility Reality Check

Before comparing POS conditions, we verify that our pipeline recovers the noun-based behavior reported by Iyer et al. (2026). Our noun-only results reproduce the same broad perplexity and downstream trends, providing a sanity check for the expanded experiments. Small differences are expected because we reprocess the post-training data to extract multiple POS categories and use a revised LoRA-based downstream fine-tuning procedure. The full comparison is described in Appendix A.

## 5.2 Perplexity

We first ask whether the effect of concept augmentation on perplexity changes across post-training domains and POSs. Figure 3 reports each augmented model’s improvement relative to its matched vanilla model, where positive values indicate lower perplexity.

The clearest effect is driven by the post-training corpus. Concept augmentation improves perplexity for models post-trained on
news, with an average improvement of $5.5\%$ , while it hurts models post-trained on arXiv by $8.2\%$ . Models trained on YouTube and the
combined corpus show smaller average decreases of $2.6\%$ and $2.5\%$ , respectively. Thus, the same augmentation procedure can improve or worsen perplexity depending on the domain in which it is introduced.

Within each corpus, we also observe a strong interaction with POS. On arXiv, augmenting all POSs is the only condition that improves perplexity on average; among the
individual POS conditions, verbs degrade substantially less than nouns or adjectives. News shows a different pattern: augmentation is
generally beneficial, with adjectives producing the largest gains. For hypernyms, adjective and verb augmentation reduce perplexity by
 $14.4\%$ and $13.8\%$ , respectively, relative to their vanilla baselines. On YouTube, verbs are instead the clearest individual-POS
beneficiary: synonym augmentation improves perplexity by $12.6\%$ , while adjective performance is much more sensitive to the concept
source. The combined corpus again produces a different ordering, with adjective hypernyms improving by $9.8\%$ but adjective synonyms
substantially worsening perplexity.

Overall, there is no single POS whose augmentation consistently lowers perplexity. Rather, the effect of POS is
conditioned on both the post-training domain and the semantic relation being augmented. This domain dependence motivates looking
beyond perplexity alone when evaluating whether the resulting models learn more useful concept representations.

## CONCLUSION
Our results show that the usefulness of concept augmentation depends on where the concept signal is introduced. Verbs provide the most consistent downstream gains, while the strongest POS still varies across domains and tasks. In contrast, augmenting all three POS categories together does not reliably outperform targeted augmentation. This suggests that concept supervision is not simply additive. Different POS categories encode different kinds of semantic structure, and exposing the model to more conceptual variation does not necessarily produce more useful representations. One possible explanation for the strength of verbs is that they encode actions and relations that organize the event structure of a sentence, so varying their word choice may encourage invariance to surface form while preserving relational meaning.

The perplexity and CKA results further suggest a distinction between fitting the post-training distribution and preserving transferable structure. Models that move further from the pretrained representation achieve lower further-training loss, but also have higher held-out perplexity. At the same time, the conditions that produce the largest representational change are not the conditions that transfer best: all-POS models diverge most under CKA, whereas verb models remain comparatively close to the base model while providing stronger downstream performance. This pattern is consistent with post-training specialization, where stronger fitting to the observed next-token distribution need not preserve the structure most
useful outside that distribution (Bachmann and Nagarajan, 2025).

Linear probing provides a complementary result. POS information becomes more linearly decodable after further training, but much of this effect also appears in matched vanilla models. All-POS augmentation produces the clearest concept-specific probe increase, yet does not yield the strongest downstream performance. Conversely, verb augmentation transfers well without producing
unusually large POS-probe gains. The probe therefore appears to operate at too coarse a level of resolution to explain the behavioral differences. Concept augmentation may instead change semantic structure within a POS, such as event structure among verbs, entity categories among nouns, or attribute
structure among adjectives. Together, the CKA and probing results suggest that the usefulness of concept augmentation depends less on how much the

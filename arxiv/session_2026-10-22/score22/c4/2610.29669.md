## ABSTRACT
LLM-generated records increasingly populate production information systems where they are treated as data assets subject to the same quality expectations as records from operational databases. A single such record can simultaneously satisfy and violate a quality rule: a grounded fact adjacent to a hallucination, a useful answer adjacent to a stereotype. Classical data cleaning cannot resolve this because the two judgments are incompatible over the same span; a single LLM judge cannot either, since it shares the training biases that produced the conflict. Existing interventions such as prompt engineering, chain-of-thought, and RLHF reduce surface error rates but provide no audit trail and cannot distinguish genuine remediation from blanket suppression. The question is whether a deterministic check over structured inter-agent records can catch the defects that referential checks alone miss. We present BiasGuard-D: two adversarial roles drawn from distinct model families exchange typed claim documents, a three-model quorum adjudicates by majority vote, and a combined referential-plus-semantic invariant validates the verdict. The semantic component fires on verdicts referential checks miss; removing the iterative loop drops invariant satisfaction sharply. The deliberation layer transfers from stereotype to hallucination remediation on HaluEval QA with only role-framing changes, raising right-answer recovery by 49 percentage points; the triage gate required a cross-family second auditor to maintain recall on objective hallucination. Every claim, vote, and instruction is stored as a typed record giving operators a provenance trail auditable under emerging AI regulations.

## 1 Introduction

LLM outputs are no longer transient. They are persisted into search indexes,
written into knowledge bases, embedded in recommendation feeds, and surfaced
through customer-facing information systems where they act as data [17, 8].
As deployments scale into regulated domains, operators need more than a quality
score; they need to know why an output passes or fails a constraint,
which span triggered the verdict, and whether the remediation
instructions are internally consistent.

What makes this hard is that an LLM-generated record can satisfy and violate
a quality rule in the same sequence of text. A hiring recommendation may
accurately ground its reasoning in a candidate’s qualifications while
simultaneously making an age generalisation the input does not support. A
knowledge-base entry may pair a faithfully lifted fact with one the model
invented. In neither case are the two things in separate fields or even
separate sentences; they share the same characters. Classical data
cleaning, which reads a record and refines it, cannot resolve this, because
the two issues require opposite judgments about the same text.
A single LLM judge cannot either, since it shares the same training biases
that produced the conflict [15, 25, 26].

The question this paper asks is: can a deterministic check over
structured inter-agent records catch the quality defects that referential
checks alone miss, and do those records carry enough information to audit
pipeline quality without calling an additional model? We evaluate this across
three dimensions: whether a semantic check catches a recurring class of
contradictions that referential checks miss; whether stored records carry
enough structure to tell genuine resolution from suppression without calling
a further model; and whether the deliberation layer transfers across different
quality domains with only role-framing changes.

We address this by treating enforcement as a data-quality problem rather than
a single-judge problem: two adversarial roles with opposing biases produce
structured claims about specific text spans; a multi-family quorum adjudicates;
and a deterministic invariant validates the result before any downstream action.
Records with a consistent verdict pass with a full provenance trail;
contradictory verdicts are quarantined rather than auto-repaired, because
auto-repair would bring back the model judgment the architecture is designed
to avoid. The experimental study confirms all three evaluation questions and
surfaces a finding we did not anticipate: the iterative deliberation loop
matters far more than any individual model choice, and the audit trail is
what makes this visible.

This paper makes three contributions. We present a five-stage typed
adversarial contract pipeline in which two adversarial roles from distinct
model families exchange typed claim documents over bounded peer-to-peer rounds,
a three-model quorum adjudicates, and a combined deterministic invariant
( $\mathcal{I}_{\mathrm{ref}}\wedge\mathcal{I}_{\mathrm{sem}}$ ) validates
each verdict, making the full lineage from any instruction back to its
originating span queryable as data. An ablation shows that the iterative
deliberation loop accounts for most of the invariant-satisfaction gain:
removing the loop drops satisfaction by nearly 30 percentage points on the
BBQ Disability benchmark, while schema-level coordination metrics stored as
typed records make the degradation auditable without calling an additional
model. Finally, we provide cross-domain portability evidence showing that the
deliberation layer, data model, and invariant transfer from subjective
stereotype remediation to objective hallucination remediation by changing only
role-framing prompts; the triage gate required a cross-family second auditor
to maintain recall on objective hallucination, isolating the gate as the one
domain-sensitive component.

## 2 Background and Related Work

BiasGuard-D sits at the intersection of classical data quality, LLM output
enforcement, and typed-contract agent orchestration. Each tradition contributes
something the system needs; none alone addresses the conflicted-record problem.

## Constraint-based data quality and repair.

The standard view of data quality treats records as entities that satisfy or
violate integrity constraints, and routing or repairing violators is a
long-studied problem [10, 14, 3, 5].
Our invariant is recognisable in this tradition: $\mathcal{I}_{\mathrm{ref}}$ 
is a foreign-key coverage check, and $\mathcal{I}_{\mathrm{sem}}$ resembles
near-duplicate detection in entity resolution. Classical repair theory selects
a minimal change to restore integrity, but a span instructed both to be removed
and preserved cannot be fixed this way; there is no ordering on
natural-language paraphrases that singles out a canonical repair.
BiasGuard-D quarantines on $\mathcal{I}_{\mathrm{sem}}$ failure rather than
attempting an auto-repair, because doing so would bring model judgment back
into the verification step. The novelty lies not in either predicate alone but
in applying them together over natural-language payloads.

## Quality enforcement on LLM outputs.

Retrieval-augmented and self-refining pipelines [17, 8, 1]
read, refine, and pass on records. This works well for straightforward quality
issues but fails on conflicted records because no stage can mark two co-located
spans as simultaneously valid and invalid. Recent multi-agent
debate [7, 18] introduces structured
disagreement but without typed message formats or deterministic verification.
Related work in the group has studied agent disagreement as a diagnostic signal
for moderation [23], applied dialectical
multi-agent refinement to structured classification [4],
and used community-driven agent frameworks for implicit bias
detection [12].
Training-time approaches (Constitutional AI [2] and
RLHF [20]) embed quality constraints into model
parameters; the resulting guarantees are statistical and opaque to an auditor.
A single-model pipeline that produces a cleaner-looking output provides no
evidence that the conflict was resolved rather than suppressed.
BiasGuard-D is runtime, record-level, and inspectable: every inter-stage
exchange is a typed record rather than a raw string.

## Typed adversarial contracts and provenance.

Typed inter-agent messaging descends from FIPA contract-net
protocols [11, 21], was revived in
LLM orchestration frameworks [24, 16], and has
acquired formal treatment in Hoare-style contracts [6].
Because every inter-stage record conforms to a fixed schema, the full lineage
from any instruction back to its source is stored and queryable without
re-running the models. Prior work supplies the message-passing machinery but
does not apply it to adversarial quality enforcement with a deterministic
check across different constraint types.

## 3 Problem, Approach, and Methods

## 3.1 The Problem

Making the problem precise requires moving from record-level pass/fail to
span-level decomposition. A record that must simultaneously preserve one span
and remove another cannot be handled as a unit: each span needs an identified
role, and the roles assigned by different judges need to be checked for
mutual consistency before any downstream action.

Concretely: an operator maintaining an AI-generated catalogue needs both
span-level granularity to approve or remove individual claims without
discarding the whole entry, and a provenance trail linking each instruction
back to its source span, auditable without re-running the pipeline.

Both scenarios impose the same requirement on the data model: span-level
typed claims with stable identifiers, a deterministic consistency check
that needs no model at verification time, and a stored lineage that survives
the full pipeline. The data model described next is designed around exactly
these three requirements.

## 3.2 The Data Model

Every correctness property of BiasGuard-D rests on the data model, so we
describe it before the pipeline. Record-level pass/fail labels are
insufficient for quality assessment: locating where errors occur requires
finer-grained units [13, 22].
Two design decisions follow from the problem.
First, fixing schemas up-front means stages can be swapped or upgraded without
ambiguity, which matters as models are deprecated. Second, grounding all
schemas in a single atomic primitive means the consistency invariant operates
on stable identifier sets rather than on natural-language content, keeping
verification deterministic and LLM-free.

The primitive is an AtomicClaim: one identifiable assertion about a
specific text span. Each claim carries a role-prefixed identifier
(C001 for the Stereotype Advocate’s first claim, D001 for
the Context Defender’s), the verbatim or compressed span it concerns, a
typed claim category drawn from five values (Factual,
Rhetorical, Framing, OptionPreference,
DecisionFlip), an optional list of provenance identifiers from a
reference passage, and a confidence score. The role prefix makes cross-role
provenance trivial to compute: finding where SA and CD claims overlap is a
string-prefix operation, not a semantic search.
Confidence is discounted multiplicatively each time the opposing role contests
a claim, so four contestations reduce it by roughly two-thirds rather than
collapsing it to zero; the trajectory is stored per round and flags cases where
a low-confidence claim is nevertheless upheld. Full field definitions and the
Python implementation are in the repository.

Everything else in the system is either an AtomicClaim or a typed
collection of them. Table 1 lists the six inter-stage schemas.
The two primary documents (StereotypeCase, ContextDefense)
are updated in place across rounds; the DeliberationVerdict carries
the output the invariant checks. Because every edge carries a typed record
with consistent identifiers, the full lineage of any instruction back to its
source span is recoverable by traversing stored records, with no
natural-language processing needed.

## 3.3 Pipeline Architecture

With the data model fixed, the pipeline determines which stage produces which
schema and how stages connect to preserve provenance end to end.
BiasGuard-D runs in five stages orchestrated by LangGraph [16];
the orchestrator is interchangeable since every interface is typed, following
contract-based design principles for multi-agent
systems [6].

## Generator and same-family Auditor (Stages 1–2).

The Generator (Llama 3.1 8B via Groq) produces the incoming record from
structured input; the Auditor (same model, same family) emits a
VerdictContract carrying a delegate flag. Pairing the Auditor
with the Generator’s own family is deliberate: self-preference bias means a
same-family judge is less likely to flag its own family’s outputs [15, 25], so records
it does flag are ones where even a sympathetic reading finds a problem,
making it a high-precision, if lower-recall, triage gate. The deliberation pipeline is
invoked sparingly and only on records the same-family judge cannot explain
away; the recall cost is the precision-recall trade-off the architecture
already surfaces at the delegation threshold. Records where
delegate=false pass through unchanged; otherwise they enter
deliberation. For the cross-domain HaluEval condition a second cross-family
Auditor B (Mistral Small) is added; delegation is OR(A, B), raising
recall at the cost of additional false delegations on correct inputs.

## Bounded peer-to-peer deliberation (Stage 3).

The Stereotype Advocate (SA, GLM-4-Flash via Zhipu AI) and the Context
Defender (CD, Mistral Small via Mistral AI) produce their initial
StereotypeCase and ContextDefense in round one. In each
subsequent round each role reads the other’s previous document and re-emits
its own, reusing the prior round’s claim_id when a claim survives
semantically and assigning a fresh identifier when a substantively new claim
is raised. The stability check is deterministic and operates on identifier sets only: the
loop terminates when two consecutive rounds produce identical contested and
defended claim sets, or when a five-round cap is reached. The cap is set at
roughly twice the empirically observed mean convergence point (2.24–2.39
rounds across conditions; Table 6): if claim sets have not
stabilised by that point, continued exchange is unlikely to resolve the
underlying tension, and the invariant will surface the contradiction at Stage
5 regardless. The two roles update in parallel within a round, so
neither sees the other’s current-round output. Identifier mis-assignment by a
role delays stability by at most one round; it does not compromise the invariant.

## Quorum Arbiter and invariant check (Stages 4–5).

Three Arbiters drawn from three distinct provider families (Cohere Command R,
Claude Sonnet, and Llama 3.3 70B) receive the converged claim documents
in parallel and each emit an independent DeliberationVerdict. A
per-claim majority vote (at least two of three) produces the aggregated verdict,
which then passes through the combined invariant (§3.4). If
the invariant holds, the Generator is re-invoked with the upheld preserve,
reframe, and drop instructions; if either component fails, an
InvariantViolation record is logged and the pipeline emits a
conservative fallback. The quarantine path deliberately avoids auto-repair: a
verdict that fails the semantic check has gone through five stages of
deliberation without resolving its contradiction, and patching it over would
bring model judgment back into the verification step.

## Family heterogeneity as a data-integrity guarantee.

The five roles span five distinct model families (Table 2).
Same-family models show joint error correlations up to 60% regardless of
prompt diversity, and single-family multi-agent setups systematically
overstate coordination gains [15, 25]. If two adversarial roles share a family,
their claim sets may converge at the span level, making the exchange vacuous.

## LIMITATIONS SECTION
Two limitations bear on the evidence. The LLM-as-judge step that produced the HaluEval hallucination-removal
labels is itself subject to the same biases the architecture is designed
to catch. We mitigate by giving the judge a strict comparison prompt with no
reference to intermediate records, but a fully independent human judge would
eliminate this concern entirely.

One limitation bears on the auditor design. The HaluEval condition uses a
two-auditor gate with OR delegation; 19 of 24 false delegations on
correct inputs fire because one auditor flags while the other passes. The
delegation strategy is a tunable threshold that does not touch the data
model, quorum, or invariant; the trade-off is examined in future work.

One further limitation bears on configuration. The Jaccard threshold has not
been tested on paraphrase-rich corpora; Table 4 shows the
quarantine rate is insensitive to $\theta$ across $[0.70,\,0.90]$ on the
current datasets, but a denser paraphrase space could push items into the
borderline band and raise the false-positive rate.
Most roles use free or prototyping tiers deliberately, to stress-test the
architecture under realistic resource constraints. The exception is
Claude Sonnet as one Arbiter: ablations replacing it with free-tier
alternatives (Qwen3-32B, Mistral Large) produced substantially lower
hallucination removal rates on HaluEval, showing that Arbiter capability
is a genuine dependency on objective grounding tasks. The BBQ stereotype
results, which do not rely on this slot, are unaffected. Identifying a fully free-tier Arbiter that matches Claude-level performance
on HaluEval and improving end-to-end latency are left as future work.

## CONCLUSION
Three threads extend beyond the experimental numbers: where the architecture
matters in practice, what the cross-domain transfer demonstrated, and where
the current design falls short.

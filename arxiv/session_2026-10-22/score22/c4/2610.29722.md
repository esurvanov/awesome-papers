## ABSTRACT
Retrieval Augmented Generation (RAG) grounds large language models in external knowledge. Modern pipelines combine embedding, chunking, retrieval, reranking, query augmentation, context compression, and agentic verification, yet these techniques are typically evaluated one at a time. It remains unclear whether their isolated gains survive composition, whether retrieval quality and answer faithfulness favor the same design choices, and whether conclusions from small corpora hold at large scale. We pre-declare three hypotheses and introduce LASAGNA, an open-source framework for controlled RAG evaluation. We test 450 configurations across eight benchmarks and corpora ranging from 5K to 200K documents. Three findings emerge. First, individually strong techniques from different phases of a RAG pipeline do not necessarily compose: three retrieval techniques predict a +47% combined improvement but deliver only +17% when used together. Second, component gains stack differently for retrieval and faithfulness, and optimizing one can hurt the other. Third, the best composition changes as the corpus grows: the combination that wins on a small corpus may not remain best at large scale. These findings show that gains measured in isolation are not reliable predictors of realistic performance, and that new RAG techniques should be tested with the full surrounding stack. We call on researchers to do so and supply LASAGNA to simplify these experiments.

## 1 Introduction

Retrieval-augmented generation
(RAG) (Gao et al., 2023b; Fan et al., 2024) combines choices across
many pipeline stages. Our study covers eight stages that span the
dominant design axes in modern RAG (Fig. 1b; full
catalog in Appendix K): embedding, chunking, index-time
enrichment, retrieval, query augmentation, reranking, context
compression, and agentic quality gates. Most new techniques are
proposed and evaluated one stage at a time, in isolation from the
surrounding pipeline, or from several representative pipelines that
would test whether findings generalize (Yu et al., 2024). Adding to the complexity, RAG systems
are evaluated for two goals (Saad-Falcon et al., 2024; Friel et al., 2024):
retrieving the right content (NDCG@10) and producing a faithful
answer from it (Es et al., 2024, RAGAS;). It is unclear whether isolated gains persist under composition. It
is also unclear whether the same design choices optimize both
retrieval quality (which documents are retrieved) and answer
faithfulness (whether the answer is grounded in those documents),
two goals RAG evaluation typically reports separately. We pre-declare three hypotheses. H1. Components often fail
to compose: isolated gains from single-component ablations do not
necessarily persist when components are combined. H2. A
simple RAG pipeline can be near-optimal in many settings, while
more elaborate configurations tend to specialize to narrower ones.
Most RAG ablations in the literature run at 5K to 50K documents,
whereas the applications RAG can serve run at corpora an order of
magnitude larger; H3. composition rankings shift with
corpus scale, so conclusions from small-corpus benchmarks may not
hold at larger scales. Prior work has explored combinations through
configuration sweeps and heuristic
search (Wang et al., 2024; Li et al., 2025; Rau et al., 2024; Jin et al., 2025; Kim et al., 2024; Fu et al., 2024; Chen et al., 2026); these
approaches can identify strong configurations but do not directly
measure interaction effects: whether the benefit of one component
depends on the other components in the pipeline. These interactions,
and how they change with corpus scale, remain largely unquantified.

Testing these hypotheses requires a framework that isolates pipeline
stages and supports reproducible evaluation across benchmarks. We
created lasagna, an open-source RAG framework, to provide
this (Fig. 1a, Section 3). Each
stage sits behind a fixed interface, so swapping one method cannot
silently change the implementation at another. Adding a new
technique is a single line of code; the framework tests it inside a
complete modern pipeline without reimplementing the surrounding stack.

We use lasagna to study RAG composition with a
167-configuration D-optimal
design (Fedorov et al., 1972; Atkinson et al., 2007) across eight
configurable stages of a modern RAG pipeline
(Table 10), eight benchmarks (Fig. 5),
and corpus sizes from 5K to 200K documents, complemented by 138
non-factorial ablation configurations across 24 targeted
experiments (Appendix F). Across the factorial, ablation suite, and scaling sweep, the
central finding is that techniques that perform well separately do
not consistently retain their gains inside a full pipeline, and the
pattern differs between retrieval and faithfulness. Retrieval gains
compose sub-additively: the three largest single-component
improvements sum to a predicted $+47\%$ NDCG@10 gain, but the
composed pipeline realizes only $+17\%$ (Section D.14).
Faithfulness gains compose near-additively: separately measured
improvements approximately transfer to the full pipeline
(Table 47). Pipeline and generator
also partly substitute: a stronger generator helps the lightest pipeline
far more than the heaviest, so a heavier stack buys
less when the generator is already strong (Section F.9).

Three practical implications follow. First, the components that
improve retrieval are not the same as those that improve faithfulness,
and optimizing one metric can hurt the other. Second, the four
complete RAG profiles expose a Pareto tradeoff on retrieval
quality, faithfulness, and response time, with no universal winner.
Adding an agentic loop to a strong retrieval stack yields only a
negligible faithfulness gain while hurting retrieval quality and
latency. Across three other benchmarks at 100K–200K documents,
lighter pipelines retain similar measured quality at
 $10$ – $20\times$ lower latency
(Sections 5 and D.8). Third, corpus
size shifts profile rankings: the design leading at small corpora
trails by $13.7\%$ NDCG@10 at 200K, with a rank flip visible from
25K (Fig. 4(a)). Small-corpus rankings should therefore
be rechecked at the intended scale.

## 2 Related Work

Modern RAG spans a layered design space with plausible interaction
points at every stage (full method catalog in Appendix K).
On retrieval, sparse baselines
(BM25 (Robertson & Zaragoza, 2009)) coexist with dense encoders
(BGE-M3 (Chen et al., 2024a), E5 (Wang et al., 2022)), learned
sparse encoders (SPLADE (Formal et al., 2021)), and
late-interaction models (ColBERT (Khattab & Zaharia, 2020)), fused
by reciprocal-rank fusion (RRF) (Cormack et al., 2009) or learned
hybrids.
Reranking spans cross-encoder scorers trained on
MS-MARCO (Nogueira & Cho, 2019), ensemble rerankers, and
diversity-driven selection (MMR; Carbonell & Goldstein, 1998). Context
compression is either token-budget aware
(LLMLingua (Jiang et al., 2023a)) or positional
(lost-in-the-middle-aware selection (Liu et al., 2024)).
Query augmentation rewrites the query before retrieval:
HyDE (Gao et al., 2023a) drafts a hypothetical answer,
RAG-Fusion (Rackauckas, 2024) fans out multi-query
variants, decomposition (Khattab et al., 2024) splits multi-hop
questions into sub-questions, and step-back
prompting (Zheng et al., 2024) abstracts before retrieving.
Index-time processing enriches chunks before retrieval with
document summaries (RAPTOR (Sarthi et al., 2024)), contextual
prefixes (Anthropic, 2024), or entity-graph indexes
(GraphRAG (Edge et al., 2024)). Generation-time control
adds correction and gating loops around the answer model
(CRAG (Yan et al., 2024), Self-RAG (Asai et al., 2024),
Speculative-RAG (Wang et al., 2025)). Each of these
stages has been shown to help in isolation on some benchmark, but
they compose along more edges than a single-component ablation can
enumerate. We study how these choices combine once
deployed together, and lasagna lets others ask
compositionality questions in the same environment by
registering a new component and evaluating it against a full modern
pipeline.

Table 1 positions lasagna against prior
work that studies RAG beyond isolated components. Best-practice
studies (Wang et al., 2024; Barnett et al., 2024) search greedily
for strong end-to-end configurations but do not estimate interaction
effects. Benchmarking libraries such as BERGEN (Rau et al., 2024)
and FlashRAG (Jin et al., 2025) support composition in a shared
codebase but do not enforce the stage isolation that controlled
interaction estimation requires, so measured differences cannot be
cleanly attributed to the component being varied
(a positional smoke run against both is reported in
Section G.4). Automated
configuration search (Kim et al., 2024) optimizes
over configurations but reports only the winning setting, not the
interaction structure that produced it, so the resulting guidance
does not transfer when any assumption changes. Evaluation
suites (Krishna et al., 2025) and domain
benchmarks (Xiong et al., 2024) supply metrics
and tasks but not a common pipeline in which those metrics can be
compared across controlled compositions. Generation-time
controls (Yan et al., 2024) strengthen the agentic layer but are
usually tested with retrieval fixed, leaving composition with a tuned
stack untested.

## 3 LASAGNA: A Compositional Framework for RAG

lasagna is an open-source framework for
evaluating new RAG components inside a complete modern pipeline.
A researcher proposing a new reranker, chunker, or agentic loop today
must either compare against a stripped-down baseline that does not
reflect a modern pipeline, or reimplement the surrounding stack. The
first biases the conclusion by mixing implementation differences
with algorithmic ones; the second is prohibitively expensive and
rarely attempted. lasagna provides a configurable
end-to-end pipeline (Fig. 1a) with a fixed
interface per stage that the framework enforces, so a new component
drops into one stage and is measured against the same surrounding
stack used by every reported configuration (full code patterns in
Sections H.2.2, H.2.3 and H.2.4).
The workflow spans nine processing stages: document processing,
chunking, index-time enrichment, embedding and indexing, query
augmentation, retrieval, reranking, context compression, and answer
generation with optional agentic verification.
Every pipeline component is a subclass of a typed abstract base class:
eight component types are supported (embedder, chunker,
reranker, retriever, document_processor,
query_enhancer, agentic_pipeline, and generator), each
stage’s inputs and outputs are typed dataclass contracts, and
behavioral tests check that swapping one implementation does not mutate
a neighboring stage’s state. A single
@registry.register("<stage>", "<name>") decorator suffices
to make a new component discoverable at YAML-config time.
The framework ships with 53 built-in components, the 30 methods across the eight factorial
dimensions (Table 10; full catalog in Appendix K),
six vector backends (Milvus, Pinecone, Qdrant, Weaviate, pgvector,
OpenSearch), and adapters for the eight benchmarks used here (five
BEIR retrieval sets and three long-form QA sets;
Section B.2). Five automated isolation tests
(Section H.1) check that toggling one component does not
silently change another, including that two sessions with opposite
settings each keep their own configuration; this supports attributing
the interaction estimates in Section 5 to the components we
vary rather than to cross-stage leakage. A new component becomes a publishable comparison
in three steps (Fig. 2): register with a decorator,
declare a factorial sweep in YAML, and run end-to-end to receive a
paired statistical test against the same surrounding stack that
produced every reported number. The complete study, factorial plus
138 non-factorial ablations plus the 5K–200K scaling sweep, cost
approximately $5,200 in cloud compute and 310 wall-clock hours
(Section A.7).

Notation.

A dimension is one of the eight experimental factors
(Table 10); default_standard is the
framework’s stock RAG pipeline (a simple, lowest-cost setting on
each dimension) that serves as the starting baseline for our
experiments; a profile is one of the four operational RAG
compositions we distill from our factorial and ablation sweeps as
the best-observed configurations targeting distinct points on the
retrieval-faithfulness-latency envelope (speed,
standard, balanced, max-reliable; full
specifications in Section I.1); a track
is one of the two evaluation surfaces (retrieval or end-to-end). We evaluate on both tracks
because retrieval quality (whether the right documents are

is grounded in the retrieved context) measure distinct properties

differently. We report the joint quality-latency envelope as a

## CONCLUSION
We study how modern RAG techniques compose across pipeline stages,
metrics, and corpus scales using lasagna, a stage-isolated
framework, and $450$ configurations across eight benchmarks from 5K
to 200K documents. Gains measured one component at a time do not
necessarily persist in complete pipelines; retrieval quality and
answer faithfulness favor different choices; and the best composition
changes with corpus scale. New techniques should be tested in
representative complete pipelines, on both metrics, and at the
intended scale. We release lasagna to make such studies
practical and reproducible, and to establish compositional
evaluation as the reporting standard for new RAG techniques rather
than the isolated single-component ablations common today.

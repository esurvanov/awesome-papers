## ABSTRACT
Recursive self-improvement (RSI) describes a system that improves not only its behavior but also the process by which it improves. Recent digital agents test this idea by rewriting their own code and checking each version with executable tests. Bringing these mechanisms into the physical world requires learning from partial observations through costly trials, while accounting for changing operating conditions and potentially irreversible consequences. We position RSI within robot learning and trace pathways from improving robot capabilities to improving the processes that generate them. We examine how physical experience can strengthen these improvement mechanisms and when the resulting gains can support further self-improvement of the mechanisms themselves. Gains carry forward only when feedback addresses remaining learning needs, the receiving component can use the change, its value survives changes in tasks or hardware, and continued practice remains safe and affordable. Existing studies, concentrated in manipulation, support behavioral improvement, benefits to later learning, and procedure improvement. Some systems also change the improver's knowledge or tools, but the physical studies reviewed here have yet to isolate gains attributable to recursion. We synthesize these findings into evaluation principles and research directions for machines that, trial by trial, safely improve the way they improve.

## CONCLUSION
This survey examined how robots that improve from physical experience can move toward recursive self-improvement, in which a system improves not only its behavior but also the process by which it improves. We positioned RSI within robot learning: reinforcement learning, self-training, continual learning, meta-learning, and AutoML say how updates are computed or what they must cope with, whereas a ladder of claims states what an experiment shows to have improved. Along that ladder, we reviewed how physical experience improves policies, world models, and knowledge, the learning procedures that produce them, and the improver that revises those procedures. What limits a robot’s next improvement (RQ1) is rarely visible in a terminal outcome; it is a hypothesis about missing experience, supervision, optimization, action generation, interfaces, or retention, to be tested by intervention rather than inferred from data volume or component accuracy. The changes that address it (RQ2) are kept in policies, world models, external knowledge, or the learning procedure, and they build on one another (RQ3) only under four conditions: feedback addresses remaining learning needs, the receiving component can use the change, its value survives changes in tasks or hardware, and continued practice remains safe and affordable. Physical constraints (RQ4) act through all four: incomplete sensing limits what a signal can distinguish; embodiment interfaces limit what a recipient can execute; wear, calibration drift, and hardware changes drive distribution shift; and reset, safety, and cost bound continued practice.

Existing studies, concentrated in manipulation, support behavioral improvement, later-learning benefits, and procedure improvement. Some systems also change their improver’s knowledge or tools, but no physical study reviewed here has yet isolated a gain attributable to recursion, which requires a controlled comparison against a version of the improver whose acquired knowledge is frozen. These answers remain provisional, because the four conditions are overlapping diagnostic lenses rather than independently validated constructs and much of the evidence comes from recent preprints with few training runs or trials.

## ABSTRACT
Formulaic alpha factor mining searches for symbolic expressions that transform market data into predictive signals. Recent LLM-based evolutionary factor mining methods use large language models as semantic variation operators. Under the conventional LLM-EA paradigm, stochastic variation and fitness-based selection can leave the search weakly directed and concentrated around a narrow set of structurally similar factors. We argue that these limitations arise because current methods mainly organize search at the individual and population levels, leaving lineage-level control underused. A lineage connects successive factor variants through evolutionary descent. We propose LineageEvo, which uses lineage in two coupled forms of search control: (1) lineage-conditioned LLM variation, which distills Lineage-Scoped Search Experience into operator-specific priors and fuses them with global operator priors, providing persistent, lineage-relevant guidance for LLM generation; and (2) lineage-stratified search allocation, which balances factor quality against lineage concentration by distributing the search budget across lineages during evolutionary search. Together, these mechanisms make semantic variation more direction aware and prevent branches from dominating the evolution, thereby promoting the discovery of predictive and diverse factors. We evaluate LineageEvo on CSI 500 and S&P 500 in terms of factor predictiveness, portfolio performance, search efficiency, and lineage- and expression-level diversity. LineageEvo achieves the strongest predictive performance on both markets, while LSPS produces a more balanced lineage distribution and a structurally broader factor pool. The results support lineage as a useful intermediate unit for controlling LLM-based evolutionary factor search.

## Introduction

Formulaic alpha factor mining aims to discover mathematical expressions that transform raw market data into predictive signals for future asset returns (Kakushadze 2016). Such factors support portfolio construction
and trading, yet the vast expression space and noisy,
non-stationary data make predictive and stable factors difficult
to discover.

Early factor discovery largely relied on human experts who designed signals based on financial intuition and empirical experience. To reduce such manual effort, automated factor mining methods have been developed, including genetic programming (GP), reinforcement learning (RL), and other machine learning approaches (Zhang et al. 2020; Yu et al. 2023; Chen et al. 2025). These methods expand the searchable factor space and enable systematic exploration beyond hand-crafted factors. However, they still rely
mainly on fixed grammars, handcrafted operators, or scalar
rewards, limiting semantic reasoning and adaptive reuse of search
experience.

More recently, LLM-based factor mining has emerged as a
promising paradigm, using pretrained models to generate and
refine symbolic formulas through agent-based generation, tree
search, and graph-aware evolution
(Wang et al. 2023; Tang et al. 2025; Shi et al. 2025; Guo et al. 2026; Luo et al. 2026a).
Large-scale pretraining provides semantic priors over financial
concepts, mathematical expressions, and programming patterns,
supporting factor hypothesis generation and formula refinement.
Among these approaches, LLM-based evolutionary algorithm
(LLM-EA) factor mining is particularly appealing because it
replaces purely syntactic genetic operators with LLM-based
semantic mutation and recombination
(Liu et al. 2023; Luo et al. 2026b; Ren et al. 2025; Han et al. 2026). However, two
search-control challenges remain. First, LLM-based operators are flexible but weakly controllable, as the mapping from prompt context to offspring is implicit and difficult to steer persistently (Liu et al. 2023; Yin et al. 2024). Second, under limited budgets, fitness-based selection can lead to takeover, where currently strong individuals or branches receive disproportionate search opportunities, reducing diversity and risking premature convergence (Sudholt 2018; Gabor et al. 2018).

These challenges persist because current LLM-EA factor mining
methods mainly organize search at the individual and population
levels, while making limited use of lineage-level state. At the
individual level, LLM operators modify selected parent factors;
at the population level, evolutionary selection favors
high-scoring candidates. Between them, a lineage is a
descent-defined factor family whose membership follows primary
evolutionary inheritance. Unlike an isolated individual or an unstructured population, a lineage preserves the continuity of local search across successive variation events, making it a natural stateful unit for both persistent operator guidance and search allocation. Conventional
evolutionary algorithms have used genealogy and lineage
information for diversity preservation, selection, and
inheritance analysis (Burke et al. 2003; Gabor et al. 2018; Burlacu et al. 2013).
However, using lineage as a shared scope for operator priors
and search allocation remains underexplored in LLM-EA factor mining.

To address these issues, we propose LineageEvo, a
lineage-aware LLM-EA framework for formulaic factor mining.
LineageEvo represents evolutionary descent in a lineage
DAG and uses lineage in two coupled forms of search control.
First, it organizes Lineage-Scoped Search Experience (LSSE) into
operator-specific lineage priors and fuses them with global
operator priors to condition LLM-based mutation and crossover.
Second, Lineage-Stratified Parent Selection (LSPS) allocates
search opportunities at the lineage level before selecting
parent factor(s), while a separate elite archive preserves
high-quality discoveries. This design gives LLM variation a persistent local context and
distributes subsequent search more evenly across evolutionary
branches.

Experiments on CSI 500 and S&P 500 demonstrate the effectiveness
of LineageEvo across markets. Compared with the strongest
baseline for each metric, LineageEvo improves IC by
28.3% and 12.6%, and ICIR by 9.9% and 35.8% on CSI 500 and
S&P 500, respectively, while achieving the strongest portfolio performance on both
markets. Ablation results
confirm the complementary roles of lineage-specific and global
priors, while LSPS increases lineage entropy from 0.405 to 2.154
and average AST distance from 0.509 to 0.747, preserving a broader
and less concentrated factor pool. The search curve further shows
that LineageEvo reaches a strong factor set rapidly,
indicating efficient evolutionary search.

Our contributions are summarized as follows:

• 

We identify lineage-level search control as underexplored in LLM-EA factor mining and use it to connect
individual-level semantic variation with population-level
selection.

• 

We propose LineageEvo, which represents
descent-defined factor families in an evolutionary DAG and
organizes Lineage-Scoped Search Experience into
operator-specific lineage priors for LLM variation.

• 

We introduce lineage-stratified search allocation through
LSPS, which balances lineage quality against lineage concentration, and decouple search allocation from solution
preservation through a separate elite archive.

• 

Experiments on CSI 500 and S&P 500 show that
LineageEvo outperforms eight representative baselines
in factor performance, while preserving greater lineage
and structural diversity.

## Related Work

## Traditional Automated Factor Mining

Traditional automated factor mining mainly formulates alpha discovery
as symbolic search. AutoAlpha applies GP-style variation and
quality-diversity search, AlphaGen uses reinforcement learning,
and AlphaSAGE improves structure-aware exploration
(Zhang et al. 2020; Yu et al. 2023; Chen et al. 2025). These methods broaden
automated search, but still largely depend on predefined grammars, handcrafted operators, or scalar rewards, making it difficult to incorporate domain semantics or adaptively reuse search experience beyond predefined operators.

## LLM-Based Factor Mining

Recent work introduces LLMs into factor mining as semantic generators, refiners, and search assistants. Alpha-GPT explores human–AI interactive alpha generation by translating investment ideas into candidate formulas (Wang et al. 2023), while AlphaAgent develops an autonomous agent framework with originality, hypothesis-factor alignment, and complexity control (Tang et al. 2025). Search-oriented methods further combine LLM generation with Monte Carlo Tree Search or graph-based retrieval to guide formula refinement (Shi et al. 2025; Guo et al. 2026). These methods make factor mining more semantic by leveraging LLMs’ pretraining-induced knowledge of financial concepts, mathematical expressions, and programming patterns to generate factors with richer rationales and more flexible structures. However, as suggested by AlphaBench, existing LLM-based factor mining methods may still under-exploit the exploration capacity of LLMs, motivating evolutionary frameworks that embed LLMs into iterative population-based search (Luo et al. 2026a).

## LLM-EA Factor Mining

LLM-EA search combines the semantic generation ability of LLMs with the population-based optimization mechanism of evolutionary algorithms. General LLM-EA studies use LLMs as evolutionary optimizers or hyper-heuristics, where LLMs generate, mutate, recombine, or reflect on candidate solutions within an iterative search loop (Liu et al. 2023; Liu et al. 2024; Ye et al. 2024). In factor mining, EvoAlpha adapts this paradigm to evolutionary alpha discovery with LLM-generated factors (Luo et al. 2026b); TreEvo evolves tree-structured thoughts to capture the hierarchical nature of alpha formulas (Ren et al. 2025); and QuantaAlpha explores trajectory-level mutation and crossover to reuse effective mining experience (Han et al. 2026). These methods move beyond isolated LLM generation by introducing evolutionary variation and selection into factor search. However, they still face two fundamental search-control challenges: LLM-driven variation can be flexible but weakly controllable, while fitness-based selection can cause dominant branches to monopolize the search process.

## Genealogy and Lineage in Evolutionary Algorithms

Evolutionary computation has previously used genealogy and
lineage information for diversity preservation, inheritance
analysis, and selection
(Burke et al. 2003; Gabor et al. 2018; Burlacu et al. 2013). These studies establish lineage
as an evolutionary abstraction. Building on this
foundation, LineageEvo uses each lineage as the shared
scope for accumulating operator-specific priors
and allocating subsequent search opportunities in LLM-EA factor
mining.

## Method

We propose LineageEvo, a lineage-aware evolutionary
framework for formulaic alpha factor mining with LLM-based
semantic variation. Its central idea is to associate accumulated search experience and
future search opportunities with each lineage. LineageEvo uses lineage as a
stateful intermediate search unit between individual-level LLM
variation and population-level evolutionary selection.
Specifically, lineage serves two complementary roles: (1) it defines
a local scope for accumulating operator-specific search
experience, and (2) it provides a unit for allocating a
limited evaluation budget.

Figure 1 summarizes one iteration of
LineageEvo. Starting from an evolutionary state organized by lineage, Lineage-Stratified Parent Selection (LSPS) first allocates
a search opportunity to a lineage and then selects parent
factor(s) from the active population. The corresponding
lineage-specific and global operator experience is retrieved and
fused into soft guidance for LLM-based mutation or crossover. A
deterministically validated offspring is subsequently evaluated,
after which the lineage graph, prior states, active population,
and elite archive are updated. This division of responsibilities
keeps the LLM focused on semantic variation, while deterministic
components govern validation, evaluation, inheritance, and
search-state transitions.

## Problem Setup and Lineage Representation

Let $\mathcal{X}$ denote the market feature space and
 $\mathcal{F}$ the symbolic expression space induced by a factor
grammar. A formulaic alpha factor $f\in\mathcal{F}$ maps the
historical observations of stock $i$ to a cross-sectional signal:

 $z_{i,t}^{f}=f\!\left(\mathcal{X}_{i,\leq t}\right),$ 

(1)

where $z_{i,t}^{f}$ is the factor value at date $t$ . Rather than seeking a single best expression, the
goal is to discover a collection of factors that exhibit strong
predictive performance and temporal stability while maintaining
sufficient structural diversity during search. The resulting
factor pool supports downstream factor selection and combination.

LineageEvo ties lineage identity to the structural
inheritance induced by its variation operators. Mutation applies
a local modification to a leaf node or a small subtree of one
parent while preserving the remaining expression structure.
Crossover is asymmetric: the parent sampled from the first selected
lineage is fixed as the primary parent and provides the recipient
expression backbone. A subtree from the secondary parent replaces
a selected primary subtree, while the remaining primary structure
is preserved.

We record these inheritance relations using a directed acyclic
graph

 $\mathcal{G}^{t}=\left(\mathcal{V}^{t},\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{pri}}^{t},\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{sec}}^{t}\right),$ 

(2)

where $\mathcal{V}^{t}$ contains all valid factors evaluated by
iteration $t$ . The set $\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{pri}}^{t}$ records
the inheritance of the expression backbone. A mutation offspring
has one primary edge from its parent, whereas a crossover
offspring has one primary edge from the backbone parent. The set
 $\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{sec}}^{t}$ records the donor ancestry of
crossover, preserving the contribution of the parent that
provides the replacement subtree.

Let $\mathcal{R}^{t}\subseteq\mathcal{V}^{t}$ denote the set of
root factors. Since each non-root node has exactly one incoming
primary inheritance edge, its lineage root is recursively defined
as

 $\rho^{t}(v)=\begin{cases}v,&v\in\mathcal{R}^{t},\\[2.84526pt]
\rho^{t}(u),&(u,v)\in\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{pri}}^{t}.\end{cases}$ 

(3)

The lineage associated with root $r_{k}$ is consequently

 $\mathcal{L}_{k}^{t}=\left\{v\in\mathcal{V}^{t}\mid\rho^{t}(v)=r_{k}\right\}.$ 

(4)

A lineage is therefore a descent-defined local search family
induced by structure-preserving variation. Lineage membership follows the
inherited expression backbone through primary edges, while
secondary edges retain cross-lineage donor ancestry. Because mutation modifies only a local component and crossover
preserves the primary backbone, nearby descendants are expected
to retain greater structural and DSL-level financial-semantic
continuity than factors from unrelated lineages, as verified in
Appendix. This continuity provides
the basis for using lineage as a scope for search experience and
resource allocation.

## Lineage-Stratified Search Allocation

Modules 1–2 in Figure 1 implement
lineage-level search allocation. Standard individual-level
fitness selection may repeatedly favor factors from already
dominant branches, causing early lineage takeover and reducing
exploration
(Sudholt 2018; Gabor et al. 2018). To mitigate
this effect, LineageEvo organizes its active population
by lineage:

 $\mathcal{P}^{t}=\bigcup_{k=1}^{m_{t}}\mathcal{P}_{k}^{t},\qquad\mathcal{P}_{k}^{t}=\mathcal{P}^{t}\cap\mathcal{L}_{k}^{t},$ 

(5)

where $\mathcal{P}_{k}^{t}$ contains the active factors belonging
to lineage $k$ , and $m_{t}$ is the number of active lineages.

LSPS uses a two-stage sampling procedure. It first selects a
lineage and then selects a parent factor within that lineage.
Both stages use a common robust quality score. Let $\mathcal{M}$ 
denote the optimization metric, either IC or ICIR, and define
 $T(f)=|\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{tr}}(f)|$ and
 $V(f)=|\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{val}}(f)|$ . For factors satisfying
 $T(f)+V(f)>0$ , the quality score is

 $q(f)=\alpha T(f)+\beta V(f)-\gamma\left|\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{tr}}(f)-\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{val}}(f)\right|,$ 

(6)

where $\alpha,\beta>0$ and
 $0\leq\gamma<\min\{\alpha,\beta\}$ . This guarantees
 $q(f)>0$ while penalizing both strength disagreement and
directional reversals.

For each lineage, we define a quality score and a lineage-balance
weight:

 $b_{k}^{t}=\max_{f\in\mathcal{P}_{k}^{t}}q(f),\qquad d_{k}^{t}=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{P}_{k}^{t}|+1}.$ 

(7)

The first term reflects the strongest active factor within the
lineage, while the second assigns greater weight to less
represented lineages. Their normalized mixture defines the
lineage-selection distribution:

 $\pi_{\mathrm{lin}}^{t}(k)=\lambda\frac{b_{k}^{t}}{\sum_{\ell=1}^{m_{t}}b_{\ell}^{t}}+(1-\lambda)\frac{d_{k}^{t}}{\sum_{\ell=1}^{m_{t}}d_{\ell}^{t}},$ 

(8)

where $\lambda\in[0,1]$ controls the trade-off between favoring
high-quality lineages and maintaining exploration across
lineages.

After selecting lineage $k$ , LSPS samples a parent in proportion
to its robust quality:

 $\pi_{\mathrm{par}}^{t}(f\mid k)=\frac{q(f)}{\sum_{g\in\mathcal{P}_{k}^{t}}q(g)},\qquad f\in\mathcal{P}_{k}^{t}.$ 

(9)

This two-stage probabilistic selection exploits stronger factors
while preserving alternatives at both the lineage and parent
levels.

## CONCLUSION
In this paper, we proposed LineageEvo, an LLM-EA
framework that uses lineage as an intermediate organizational
unit for evolutionary factor mining. LineageEvo
combines lineage-conditioned priors for guiding LLM variation with Lineage-Stratified Parent Selection for
distributing search opportunities across lineages.
The resulting framework discovers highly predictive and stable
factors, achieves strong investment returns, and preserves a more
diverse factor pool. Future work may compare lineage with other
intermediate organizational units, such as
clusters, niches, and retrieval neighborhoods, to study their
respective advantages in experience reuse and search control.

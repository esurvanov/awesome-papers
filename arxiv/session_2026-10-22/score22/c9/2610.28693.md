## ABSTRACT
Photoplethysmography (PPG) enables low-cost and non-invasive heart rate (HR) monitoring but remains sensitive to waveform distortion, physiological variability, and measurement interference. Temporal waveform characteristics and frequency-domain periodicity provide complementary information for HR estimation; however, effectively exploring and integrating these representations within a compact model remains challenging. This paper proposes TF-IG, a parameter-efficient interaction-gated time-frequency learning framework for PPG-based HR estimation. TF-IG employs a dual-branch architecture in which a temporal branch captures pulse morphology via dilated depthwise-separable convolutions, while a frequency branch extracts cardiac periodicity information from bounded log-spectrogram representations. To combine the two domains, we introduce an interaction-gated fusion module that explicitly models feature agreement and discrepancy, while also learning feature-wise weights to balance temporal and spectral information. A parameter-shared regression head further maps the temporal, spectral, and fused representations into the final HR prediction space. Experiments on the benchmark BIDMC PPG and Respiration Dataset using a subject-exclusive training, validation, and test protocol show that TF-IG achieves a mean absolute error (MAE) of 1.227 BPM, a root mean square error (RMSE) of 2.060 BPM, a Pearson correlation coefficient of 0.9934, and 92.28 percent of estimates within 3 BPM, while remaining parameter-efficient with only 84.6K parameters. Ablation results further demonstrate the complementary contributions of the temporal and frequency branches, interaction-gated fusion, and shared regression head. These results indicate that explicitly modeling cross-domain interactions provides an effective accuracy-complexity trade-off for lightweight PPG-based HR estimation.

## 1 Introduction

Continuous heart-rate (HR) monitoring plays an important role in clinical observation, fitness assessment, ambulatory healthcare and long-term physiological monitoring [13], [1].
Although electrocardiography (ECG) remains a standard reference for cardiac monitoring, photoplethysmography (PPG) provides an attractive alternative for continuous and wearable sensing because it measures peripheral blood-volume variations using inexpensive and non-invasive optical hardware [2], [9]. Its compact sensing configuration has consequently enabled widespread integration into wearable devices, pulse oximeters, smartphones, and other resource-constrained monitoring platforms. PPG therefore represents a practical sensing modality for scalable and continuous cardiovascular monitoring [2], [1].
Despite these advantages, reliable HR estimation from PPG remains challenging. The measured waveform is influenced not only by cardiac pulsation but also by physiological and acquisition-related factors such as respiration, body site, tissue characteristics, ambient illumination, applied pressure, and artifacts [4].

A broad range of signal-processing and learning-based approaches has therefore been developed for PPG-based HR estimation. Classical methods typically rely on filtering, peak detection, spectral analysis, or handcrafted signal descriptors [12], [6]. More recently, deep learning has enabled HR-relevant features to be learned directly from PPG observations rather than relying entirely on manually designed processing rules. For example, PPGNet [17] proposed an end-to-end network operating directly on short PPG segments for device-independent HR estimation; Deep PPG [15] leverages convolutional neural networks over spectrogram of PPG and accelerometer signals; Ismail et al. [7] further explored a set of features characterizing the signal, modeling by a convolutional-recurrent framework, etc. These studies
demonstrates that temporal and spectral representations of PPG can provide useful information for HR estimation.

The complementarity of temporal and spectral domain follows naturally from the characteristics of PPG. The temporal domain preserves local pulse morphology and short-term waveform dynamics, whereas the frequency domain makes periodic cardiac activity more explicit. Time-frequency representations further characterize how this spectral structure evolves over the observation interval, emphasizing different aspects of the same underlying physiological process [7].
Although prior work has demonstrated the utility of signal, spectral, and time-frequency representations, effectively structuring the interaction between complementary representations remains an important design problem.
The availability of multiple representations does not by itself determine how they should be combined [5]. Under signal variability or corruption, temporal and spectral features may provide partly consistent yet partly different evidence about the underlying HR. Therefore, an effective fusion mechanism should explicitly characterize where they agree, where they differ, and how strongly each should jointly contribute to the final representation.

Motivated by these observations, we propose TF-IG, a parameter-efficient Interaction-Gated Time–Frequency learning framework for PPG-based HR estimation. First, TF-IG learns complementary HR representations through two lightweight branches: a temporal branch based on dilated depthwise-separable convolutions and temporal attention pooling, and a frequency branch that processes a bounded log-spectrogram using spectro-temporal attention pooling. Second, these representations are integrated through a structured interaction-gated mechanism. Element-wise multiplication and absolute difference explicitly encode cross-domain co-activation and discrepancy, while a feature-wise gate adaptively balances the temporal and spectral embeddings. The resulting fused representation, together with the individual branch embeddings, is processed by a shared regression head that provides a common mapping to HR, with the three predictions subsequently combined through learned normalized weights. In this way, TF-IG jointly addresses complementary representation learning, explicit cross-domain interaction, and parameter-efficient prediction within a unified lightweight framework.
Accordingly, the main contributions of this work are summarized as follows:

• 

Compact dual-domain representation learning.
We propose a parameter-efficient dual-branch architecture that jointly captures temporal pulse morphology and spectro-temporal cardiac periodicity using dilated depthwise-separable convolutions and attention-based pooling.

• 

Structured cross-domain fusion and shared prediction.
We introduce an interaction-gated fusion mechanism that explicitly models temporal–spectral co-activation and discrepancy through element-wise multiplication and absolute difference, while a feature-wise gate adaptively balances the contribution of the two domains. A parameter-shared regression head is further applied to the temporal, frequency, and fused embeddings, providing a common space for HR prediction mapping while avoiding separate prediction networks.

• 

Empirical validation of the proposed design.
On a subject-independent BIDMC split, TF-IG achieves an MAE of $1.227$ BPM, an RMSE of $2.060$ BPM, a Pearson correlation of $0.9934$ , and a CP3 of $92.28\%$ using only 84.6K parameters. Ablation experiments further demonstrate the complementary contributions of dual-domain representation learning, interaction-gated fusion, and shared regression to the final performance.

## 2 Methodology

This section first formulates PPG-based heart-rate estimation and introduces the notation used throughout the paper Section 2.1. Section 2.2 describes the overview of the proposed architecture. Subsequently, Section 2.3 describes the signal preprocessing stage. Individual components within the framework such as time-domain branch, frequency-domain branch, fusion module, and regression head are detailed in Sections 2.4, 2.5, 2.6, and 2.7, respectively.

## 2.1 Problem Definition and Notation

Let $\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{raw}}\in\mathbb{R}^{T_{\mathrm{raw}}}$ denote a single-channel PPG segment of duration $T_{\mathrm{win}}$ seconds recorded at an original sampling rate $f_{s}$ . Each segment is associated with a reference heart rate $y\in\mathbb{R}$ , expressed in beats per minute (BPM). After preprocessing and resampling, the segment is represented as $\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{T}$ .

The objective is to learn a regression function
 $\Phi(\cdot;\Theta)$ , parameterized by $\Theta$ , that maps the input PPG segment to its corresponding heart-rate estimate:

 $\hat{y}=\Phi(\mathbf{x};\Theta),$ 

(1)

where $\hat{y}\in\mathbb{R}$ denotes the predicted heart rate. The parameters $\Theta$ are optimized by minimizing a regression loss between $\hat{y}$ and the reference value $y$ over the training set.

Throughout this work, the notation $[\cdot\,;\cdot]$ represents concatenation along the feature dimension, $\odot$ denotes the Hadamard product, and $\sigma(\cdot)$ denotes the sigmoid function.

## 2.2 Overall Architecture

As illustrated in Fig. 1, TF-IG adopts two complementary processing branches. The time-domain branch operates directly on the PPG waveform and captures local pulse morphology and temporal dependencies. In parallel, the frequency-domain branch transforms the waveform into a bounded log-magnitude spectrogram and models cardiac periodicity over time-frequency transformed representation.

To reduce the computational operations, both streams use depthwise-separable convolutions [3] as their primary building block. Both branches share a structured processing flow comprising a convolutional stem, domain-specific feature refinement, and targeted attention pooling. Their outputs are converted into fixed-dimensional embeddings through domain-specific attention pooling. An interaction-gated fusion module then combines these embeddings by jointly modeling their agreement, discrepancy, and relative contribution. Finally, a shared regression head generates predictions from the time-domain, frequency-domain, and fused representations, which are combined through a learnable ensemble.

## 2.3 Preprocessing

Continuous PPG recordings are first partitioned into fixed-duration windows of $T_{\mathrm{win}}$ seconds using a predefined window stride. Each window is resampled to a target sampling rate $f_{s}^{*}=62.5$ Hz and then normalized using z-score. The resulting signal $\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{T}$ is provided to both the time-domain and frequency-domain branches.

## 2.4 Time-Domain Branch

## 1D Convolutional Stem and Dilated Residual Blocks:

The resampled waveform is first arranged as a single-channel sequence,
 $\mathbf{X}_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{1\times T}$ . A one-dimensional convolutional stem extracts low-level morphological features while preserving the temporal resolution. The stem consists of a standard Conv1D layer ( $K=7$ ) followed by two dilated depthwise-separable Conv1D layers ( $K=5$ , with dilations $d=2$ and $d=4$ , respectively). Batch normalization and activations are applied after each layer, yielding $\mathbf{H}_{\text{stem}}\in\mathbb{R}^{C\times T}$ .

Furthermore, to expand the temporal receptive field without losing resolution, $\mathbf{H}_{\text{stem}}$ is subsequently refined through three cascaded residual blocks. The transformation at the $i$ -th block is formulated as:

 $\mathbf{H}_{i}=\operatorname{ReLU}\big(\mathbf{H}_{i-1}+\Psi_{i}(\mathbf{H}_{i-1})\big),\quad i\in\{1,2,3\},$ 

(2)

where $\mathbf{H}_{0}=\mathbf{H}_{\text{stem}}$ . The function $\Psi_{i}(\cdot)$ denotes a residual mapping comprising a dilated depthwise-separable Conv1D with kernel size $K_{i}$ and dilation rate $d_{i}$ , followed by a $1\times 1$ pointwise projection, batch normalization, and dropout. The output feature map is denoted as $\mathbf{H}_{t}\triangleq\mathbf{H}_{3}\in\mathbb{R}^{C\times T}$ .

## Temporal Attention Pooling (TAP):

Temporal attention pooling converts $\mathbf{H}_{t}$ into a fixed-dimensional embedding while allowing the model to emphasize informative portions of the PPG waveform. Attention logits are generated via a Temporal Transfer block including a two-layer $1\times 1$ convolutional network:

 $\boldsymbol{\ell}_{t}=\operatorname{Conv}_{1\times 1}^{(2)}\left(\tanh\left(\operatorname{Conv}_{1\times 1}^{(1)}(\mathbf{H}_{t})\right)\right),$ 

(3)

where the first projection reduces the channel dimension from $C$ to a hidden dimension $C_{h}$ , and the second produces one scalar logit per temporal position.

The normalized attention weights are

 $\alpha_{k}=\frac{\exp(\ell_{t,k})}{\sum_{j=1}^{T}\exp(\ell_{t,j})},\qquad k=1,\ldots,T.$ 

(4)

The time-domain embedding $\mathbf{z}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{C}$ is then computed as

 $\mathbf{z}_{t}=\sum_{k=1}^{T}\alpha_{k}\mathbf{H}_{t}[:,k].$ 

(5)

Unlike uniform global pooling, the TAP operation aims to assign greater importance to waveform intervals containing reliable pulse morphology.

## 2.5 Frequency-Domain Branch

## Bounded Log-Spectrogram Representation:

The frequency branch started by transforming the resampled signal $\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{T}$ into spectro-representation $\mathbf{X}_{\mathrm{STFT}}$ via the Short-Time Fourier Transform (STFT) using a Hann window.
Since the dominant cardiac periodicity is expected to lie within a bounded physiological frequency range, we therefore retain only the frequency bins within the cardiac band of $[0.5,5.0]$ Hz to suppress irrelevant low-frequency drift and high-frequency components and to reduce the dimensionality of the spectral input.

Logarithmic dynamic-range compression is then applied as:

 $\mathbf{S}[k,m]=\log\left(1+\left|\mathbf{X}_{\mathrm{STFT}}[k,m]\right|\right),\qquad k\in\mathcal{K}.$ 

(6)

The resulting bounded log-spectrogram is represented as

 $\mathbf{S}\in\mathbb{R}^{F\times M},$ 

(7)

where $F=|\mathcal{K}|$ is the number of retained frequency bins and $M$ is the number of STFT frames.

## 2D Convolutional Stem:

Equivalent to its 1D counterpart, $\mathbf{S}$ is processed by a 2D convolutional stem, which share an identical structural topology. It consists of a standard Conv2D ( $K=7$ ) followed by two dilated depthwise-separable Conv2D layers ( $K=5$ , dilations $d=2,4$ ). Operating over the spatial $F\times M$ plane, this block extracts multi-scale spectro-temporal features, outputting $\mathbf{H}_{f}\in\mathbb{R}^{C\times F\times M}$ .

## Spectro-Temporal Attention Pooling:

As a non-stationary rhythmic signal, heart rate exhibits time-varying spectral energy distributed along instantaneous-frequency trajectories in the time-frequency plane [8]. Driven by these dynamic properties, Spectro-Temporal Attention Pooling module aggregates the joint spectro-temporal feature map $\mathbf{H}_{f}$ into a fixed-length embedding $\mathbf{z}_{f}\in\mathbb{R}^{C}$ to explicitly capture those joint time-frequency relationships.

Given $\mathbf{H}_{f}$ , a Spectral Transfer block comprising a $1\times 1$ convolution, batch normalization, ReLU activation, and a second $1\times 1$ convolution produces a logit map
 $\mathbf{L}_{f}\in\mathbb{R}^{F\times M}$ . A spatial softmax is applied jointly over all $FM$ positions:

 $\beta_{u,v}=\frac{\exp(\mathbf{L}_{f}[u,v])}{\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{F}\sum_{j=1}^{M}\exp(\mathbf{L}_{f}[i,j])}.$ 

(8)

The frequency-domain embedding $\mathbf{z}_{f}\in\mathbb{R}^{C}$ is obtained as

 $\mathbf{z}_{f}=\sum_{u=1}^{F}\sum_{v=1}^{M}\beta_{u,v}\mathbf{H}_{f}[:,u,v].$ 

(9)

## 2.6 Interaction-Gated Fusion Module

While direct concatenation or linear projection can effectively combine time- and frequency-domain representations, it does not explicitly model the cross-domain interactions between these two domains. Therefore, our aim is to achieve an explicit module that can interactively aggregated features from both time and frequency domain.
Specifically, Interaction-Gated Fusion Module decomposes cross-domain fusion into two complementary components: an explicit interaction pathway that models feature relationships and a sample-dependent gate that balances the contribution of the two domains.

## Explicit Cross-Domain Interaction

To capture joint cross-domain relationships, we construct an extended interaction vector $\mathbf{u}\in\mathbb{R}^{4C}$ by combining linear terms, element-wise correlation, and absolute disparity:

 $\mathbf{u}=\left[\mathbf{z}_{t}\,;\,\mathbf{z}_{f}\,;\,\mathbf{z}_{t}\odot\mathbf{z}_{f}\,;\,|\mathbf{z}_{t}-\mathbf{z}_{f}|\right].$ 

(10)

Here, the multiplicative term of $\mathbf{z}_{t}\odot\mathbf{z}_{f}$ emphasizes the co-occurring activations across both domains, while the point-wise difference $|{}\mathbf{z}_{t}-\mathbf{z}_{f}|{}$ measures feature discrepancies. The extended vector is subsequently projected back to dimension $C$ via a non-linear projection block:

 $\mathbf{z}_{\text{inter}}=\text{ReLU}\left(\mathbf{W}_{u}\mathbf{u}+\mathbf{b}_{u}\right),$ 

(11)

where $\mathbf{z}_{\text{inter}}\in\mathbb{R}^{C}$ , $\mathbf{W}_{u}\in\mathbb{R}^{C\times 4C}$ , and $\mathbf{b}_{u}\in\mathbb{R}^{C}$ . Batch normalization and dropout regularization are applied following the projection.

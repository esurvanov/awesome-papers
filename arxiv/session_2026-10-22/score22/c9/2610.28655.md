# 2610.28655 — What Git Does Not Record: Provenance Legibility in Two AI-Assisted Research Repositories
Authors: Koutian Wu, Zesen Huang, Hejia Geng
Comment: Accepted at the NeurIPS 2026 Workshop on AI-Native Academia. 12 pages, 4 figures. Case studies are two atmospheric and ocean science research repositories
Categories: cs.DL, cs.AI, cs.LG

## ABSTRACT
Publishers and funders increasingly ask authors to disclose how AI tools contributed to a piece of research. In computational work the repository is where an author or an auditor would look for the answer, and we ask what it can actually supply. We audit two single-investigator repositories by the same author in the same field, each carrying a different class of agent residue: a prescriptive trace of standing rule files and a narrative trace of a running agent scratchpad, in repositories of 466 and 103 commits. The two are legible in opposite ways. 85.6% of the lines ever committed to the prescriptive trace are visible at the current revision, and a standing rule states a conclusion and discards the episode behind it; the narrative trace records episodes and shows 21.8%. Counted by revision the gap is far smaller: 15 of the prescriptive trace's 41 revisions leave no visible line, against 18 of the narrative trace's 41. The Co-Authored-By trailer, the field an automated audit reads, is one of three AI trailer keys Project A carries and covers 66 of the 121 commits carrying any. In Project B it has low sensitivity against an explicit within-repository proxy: 39 commits revise a file maintained to hold an agent's working notes and 1 (2.6%) carries the trailer. Project A's repository-level rate is unstable in time too, stepping from 2.7% to 17.7% across a boundary we did not specify in advance. Repository channels disagree, and neither rate can be read as an involvement rate; we do not isolate the cause, and say so. We give a field-by-field account of which provenance claims such traces support, which they cannot, and which they answer confidently and wrongly, construct an auditable evidence ledger, and derive the requirements this places on any system meant to record research provenance as it happens.

## 1 Introduction

The disclosure requirements now attached to AI use in research ask authors to describe
something that happened months earlier. Journals require a statement of how generative tools
contributed (Nature Editorial, 2023); conferences ask for the role LLMs played; reviewers,
when suspicious, ask for evidence. These policies specify where the statement goes, not
where its content is to come from. In computational research there is one obvious
candidate: the project’s version-control repository is already kept, timestamped, shared,
and treated as the authoritative record of how the work was built, and it is the first
place an author reconstructing their own process, or an auditor checking it, would look.
Whether it can carry that weight is an empirical question. A repository records a narrow
thing: a sequence of snapshots of a file tree, each annotated with a name, an email
address, two timestamps, and a free-text message, all supplied by the committer and none
verified. Projects using agentic coding tools commonly add one thing more, a persistent
artifact holding the agent’s standing instructions or its working notes between sessions.
Where such an artifact is tracked, it is the closest thing to a contemporaneous record of
AI involvement an ordinary repository contains, and it is what an auditor would read. The
two repositories we audit carry structurally different artifacts of this kind, and what
each presents to a reader is the subject of the paper. It is a comparison of two cases,
not a controlled manipulation, and we claim only what that licenses.

## Design.

We compare two repositories from a single investigator working in the environmental sciences
(Table 1). Project A ( $466$ commits) was developed with an agentic coding
assistant whose persistent artifact is a directory of standing rule files: instructions the
agent is to follow on future work. We call this a prescriptive trace. Project B ( $103$ 
commits) is a Cursor-assisted project, evidenced by a versioned agent scratchpad tracked
across $39$ commits; its artifact is a single running document of plans, findings and status,
rewritten as work proceeds. We call this a narrative trace. Author, discipline and
version-control substrate are shared; subject, period, commit volume, research phase and
how heavily each tool was used are not, and we manipulated none of them. Tool, artifact
class, project and time are confounded, so no difference we report can be attributed to the
tool alone. What the shared author buys is narrower and still worth having: it removes the
most obvious rival explanation for a difference in record-keeping, and it makes the
within-repository comparisons in Section 6 comparisons within one
person’s practice.

## Findings.

The two artifacts are legible in opposite ways, and the failures are
complementary. The prescriptive trace accumulates, keeping $85.6\%$ of its committed lines
at the current revision, but a standing rule records a conclusion rather than the episode
that produced it. The narrative trace records episodes and shows only $21.8\%$ : the rest
is in the object database, recoverable by walking the file’s history rather than by reading
its contents, which is the distinction that matters for an audit. So the artifact that
records the process does not show it, and the artifact that shows itself does not record
the process. Both figures presuppose the artifact was committed at all, a choice neither
tool prompts for and neither warns against. The commit log fares worse than either. The field
an automated audit reads, the Co-Authored-By trailer, is absent from $38$ of the
 $39$ Project B commits that revise the agent’s own scratchpad, and its rate within
Project A moves from $2.7\%$ to $17.7\%$ across a silent month we located by inspecting the
series rather than specifying in advance. Why, we cannot say: the author may simply
have used an agent more after the boundary, and neither repository records a tool-settings
history or which process authored a commit (Section 6). What survives
that uncertainty is the consequence for an auditor. A disclosure rate read off the commit
log is not an involvement rate, cannot be converted into one from repository evidence, and
is not stable enough within one project to compare against itself.

## Contributions.

1. 

A field-by-field taxonomy of what an ordinary Git repository can, cannot, and only
apparently records about the provenance of a change (Section 4),
separating fields that are unrecoverable from fields that are recoverable but misleading.

2.

what share of the lines ever committed to each is visible at the current revision, which

3. 

A measurement of the commit log’s disclosure channel (Section 6):
its sensitivity against an explicit within-repository proxy for agent activity, and the
instability of its rate over time, with the alternative explanations we cannot rule out.

4.

(Section 8), derived field by field from the taxonomy’s gaps.

disclosing commit, tracked text file and blame cohort.

## 2 Related work

## Provenance and the integrity of the record.

Formal provenance models specify
what a computational result must record to be accountable: entities, the activities that
produced them, and responsible agents (Moreau et al., 2011; Freire et al., 2008). The reproducibility literature consistently finds that this
record is not a by-product of ordinary practice and has to be deliberately produced
(Peng, 2011; Stodden et al., 2016; Pineau et al., 2021). Supply-chain work adds
cryptographic attestation and transparency logs on top of the repository
(Torres-Arias et al., 2019; Newman et al., 2022), binding an identity to an artifact but
presupposing that the events worth attesting were identified first. We measure that prior step: which provenance
fields survive in the Git repository of an AI-assisted project.

## Mining repositories.

Repository mining has long-standing cautions about
treating Git history as a faithful record of development: commits are curated, rebased,
squashed and back-dated, and repository metadata systematically misrepresents authorship
and effort (Bird et al., 2009; Kalliamvakou et al., 2016; Gousios and Spinellis, 2012).
We take the cautions as the object of study rather than as a threat to mitigate, and ask what
they imply for provenance claims about AI involvement.
The commit history of an AI-assisted research repository has also been mined for reusable
agent practice (Wu, 2026), and scientific code repositories have been turned into
environments that agents learn from (Geng et al., 2026). That work reads the repository
for what an agent can reuse, whereas we read it for what it can attest.

## AI disclosure and its verification.

Venue policies now require authors to declare AI use (Nature Editorial, 2023), and studies of
the resulting literature estimate rising LLM involvement from textual signal
(Liang et al., 2024).
Benchmarks of autonomous research find that agents still depend heavily on human
methodological guidance (Zhou et al., 2026).
Reasoning models trained with reinforcement learning can fail when the partial work they build
on contains misleading or corrupted steps, unless they are explicitly trained to detect and
repair such steps, for example through adversarial self-play
(Li et al., 2026). Scoring the end-to-end output of research agents
takes expert-written rubrics even under controlled conditions
(Xu et al., 2026). Research is therefore shared
between author and agent, and that division is what a disclosure has to describe.
Verification from the artifact is harder than it looks: generated
text is not reliably detectable post hoc (Sadasivan et al., 2025), watermarking requires
cooperation at generation time (Kirchenbauer et al., 2023), and integrity failures such
as fabricated references are recoverable only by checking the claim, not the process
(Walters and Wilder, 2023; Birhane et al., 2023). Closest to us, repository studies already
count AI self-declaration in the wild from commit messages and co-author trailers
(Kashif et al., 2026; Tufano et al., 2024; Liu et al., 2026). They take the declaration
as the measurement; we ask what it can establish.

## Structured disclosure.

Datasheets and model cards established that useful
disclosure requires a fixed schema with named fields, including fields whose answer is “not
recorded” (Gebru et al., 2021; Mitchell et al., 2019), which
Section 8 applies to process provenance.

Project B’s AI involvement is stated exactly as we can evidence it and no further: it is a
Cursor-assisted project, evidenced by a versioned agent scratchpad tracked across $39$ 
commits. We do not claim a proportion of the code was model-written, because nothing in the
repository would support such a claim, and the inability to support it is part of our
result.

One premise deserves stating, because a later result rests on it. The file at
.cursor/scratchpad.md is a project convention, not a format the tool creates and
maintains on its own: it lives in the tool’s configuration directory, holds plans, findings
and running status in the tool’s working idiom, and the project kept it under version
control. We treat revisions to it as a proxy for contemporaneous agent activity, imperfect
in both directions: a human can edit the file directly, and an agent session can produce
work committed later, separately, or alongside unrelated changes. Every claim in Section 6 that rests on it is marked as such.

## 3.2 Measurement

Four scripts run over both repositories, a fifth turns their output into every coordinate
plotted here, and a sixth checks each number printed in this paper against them. Per case we walk every commit touching the trace artifact, over the
union of the paths it has occupied, and record per-revision insertions and deletions; count
the lines the census revision still shows; check whether the artifact is git-ignored;
extract commit-message trailers; and compare dates the artifact asserts about itself against
the commit that introduced each line. Three measurement decisions are worth
stating, because the naive alternatives give indefensible numbers.

First, the query decides part of the answer. Project A’s trace directory was
renamed partway through its life, and Git offers three defensible ways to ask one artifact
for its history. Table 2 runs all three at the same census, and they
disagree about different things in both projects. The history-only share barely moves,
 $4.2$ points across the three forms in Project A and $2.1$ in Project B, so this
paper’s headline does not rest on which query an analyst ran. The episode record does move:
Project A’s revision count varies by a factor of $5.9$ and its apparent start date by
 $228$ days, because a path-limited log cannot see a rename whose source that same limit
filters out. The earlier objects were never lost, and nothing in the narrow answer flags the
shortfall. We report the path-union form throughout, since a path-limited log is what an
audit would run.

## 4 What a repository can and cannot recover

Table 3 takes the provenance fields an AI-disclosure statement would need
and asks, for each, what a repository kept the way these two were kept provides.
Recoverable means the field is present and trustworthy at face value within the
audited history; partial means something related is present but does not answer the
question asked; absent means nothing in the repository bears on it. A fourth category matters more:
fields that are present and misleading, where reading at face value returns a
confident wrong answer.

The verdicts are about default practice, not about what Git makes possible: a project that
decides to can commit its prompts, hook messages against diffs, or keep intermediate states
on branches. None of that happened here, and none of it is what a project gets by using an
agentic tool and committing normally, which is the condition an auditor should expect.

## CONCLUSION
Asked to disclose how AI contributed to a piece of research, an author with a well-kept
repository has less evidence than policy assumes. One artifact records the process and shows a
fifth of it; the other stays visible and records conclusions; and the field an audit reads
misses $45.5\%$ of one repository’s disclosures. Until these facts are captured as
they happen, a repository can constrain a provenance claim but not establish one.

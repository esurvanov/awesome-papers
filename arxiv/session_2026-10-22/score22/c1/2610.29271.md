## ABSTRACT
Analogical reasoning is widely considered a fundamental component of human intelligence. Few benchmarks are designed to explicitly test far analogical reasoning - reasoning between domains that are superficially dissimilar yet share deep relational or structural similarities. In this work, we introduce an analogical reasoning benchmark called Abstraction and Reasoning Corpus of ANAlogy (ARCANA). The tasks of ARCANA require performing far analogical mappings from real-world objects or physical phenomena to abstract grid-based transformations, different from the well-known ARC-AGI benchmark which primarily emphasized innate human priors. We validate ARCANA through a human study, demonstrating that humans solve these tasks reliably. In contrast, evaluations across a range of large language models show a distinct performance gap compared to humans, revealing systematic failure modes that remain challenging for current models. To address this gap, we propose ConceptSIR, a reasoning framework based on concept slippage, introspection, and reflection. Experiments demonstrate that ConceptSIR enables standard LLMs to solve several challenging ARCANA tasks that were unsolved by direct prompting in our evaluation. Overall, our results suggest that far analogical reasoning remains an open problem for contemporary LLMs. All the materials are available at sites.google.com/view/arcanacogtest. The complete codebase, including benchmark, and ConceptSIR, is publicly available at https://github.com/density-11/arcana-test. All ARCANA data and task instances can be accessed and explored at https://arcanacogtest.org/.

## 1 Introduction

Analogical reasoning has long been regarded as a central component of human intelligence and a characteristic of general intelligence more broadly [1, 2, 3, 4]. Humans routinely draw analogies between domains that are superficially dissimilar yet share deep relational structure, allowing them to generalize knowledge flexibly from limited experience. This capability for far analogy—mapping abstract relations across distant domains—plays a critical role in human learning, reasoning, and problem solving.

In recent years, substantial progress has been made in evaluating the general reasoning abilities of artificial intelligence systems, such as Humanity’s Last Exam [5] and SWE-Bench Pro [6]. However, these benchmarks are not designed to explicitly probe far analogical reasoning. Among the most challenging existing benchmarks, ARC-AGI-1&2 evaluate abstraction, reasoning, and analogy through grid-based tasks that require inferring latent rules from a small number of examples [7].
In general, ARC-AGI-1&2 primarily focus on testing near analogy based on relative surface-level feature similarity (e.g., color, shape, position, number, center, copy, and count). While instances of far analogy may arise implicitly, explicit far analogical reasoning—particularly that requiring mappings from real-world concepts or physical phenomena to abstract symbolic representations—is rare and limited in scope. This leaves a substantial gap in the systematic evaluation of fluid intelligence and far analogical reasoning.

Inspired by ARC-AGI-2 and to address this gap, we introduce ARCANA (Abstraction and Reasoning Corpus for ANAlogy), a benchmark explicitly designed to evaluate far analogical reasoning.
ARCANA inherits the form of ARC-AGI-1&2, each task of which consists of 3-5 examples and 1-2 tests.
As shown in Figure 1, unlike ARC-AGI-1&2 which largely emphasizes innate human priors (e.g., copying the 3×3 pattern to the purple grid, filling in the grids with different colors using discovered rules), ARCANA focuses on far analogical mappings from real-world objects or physical phenomena to ARC grid transformations (e.g., threading 3D rings onto a column with physical constraints).
The benchmark draws from a broad and diverse set of conceptual sources, including physical principles (e.g., buoyancy, levers, gears, melting, the Doppler effect, Kepler’s second law), natural phenomena (e.g., planets, sunset), and object-level abstractions (e.g., cats, ducks, fireworks, 3D rings, 3D sushi).

To assess the validity and difficulty of ARCANA tasks, we conduct a human study with undergraduate participants. The results show that humans reliably solve ARCANA tasks, indicating that the benchmark captures forms of reasoning that are natural for human cognition. Meanwhile, we evaluate ARCANA across a range of large language models (LLMs), the results of which reveal lower performances, suggesting that far analogical reasoning remains a challenge for current models and exposing systematic failure modes beyond simple pattern matching.

Inspired by the Copycat model of analogical reasoning proposed by [3], we propose ConceptSIR, an analogical reasoning framework based on concept slippage, introspection, and reflection. ConceptSIR is designed to approximate key aspects of the human problem-solving process when constructing and solving ARCANA tasks. Empirical results show that ConceptSIR enables large language models to solve several ARCANA tasks that are otherwise unsolved by standard prompting strategies, providing insight into the potential of our ConceptSIR.

In summary, this work makes three contributions:
(1) we introduce ARCANA, a benchmark explicitly targeting far analogical reasoning;
(2) we present a human and model evaluation that reveals a substantial performance gap; and
(3) we propose ConceptSIR, a reasoning framework that improves model performance while highlighting remaining challenges.
Together, our results suggest that far analogy remains an open problem for contemporary AI systems and that the reasoning pipeline of ConceptSIR offers a promising direction for studying analogical reasoning.

## 2 Related Work

## 2.1 Cognitive Theories of Analogical Reasoning

Although the computational nature of analogy is not yet fully understood, a number of cognitive theories have been proposed to formalize how analogical reasoning operates. Structure-Mapping Theory (SMT) characterizes analogy as a mapping from a source to a target that prioritizes the preservation of relational structure over surface features [1]. SAGE, a later extension of SMT, incorporates frequency information over alignable structures, enabling probabilistic generalization across analogical instances [8]. The Analogical Constraint Mapping Engine (ACME) is a classic computational model that formulates analogical mapping as a process of constraint satisfaction [4].

Among these theories, our ConceptSIR draws particular inspiration from Copycat [3, 2], which involves a Slipnet, a workspace and a population of perceptual agents. Although originally developed for letter-string analogy problems, Copycat has been influential as a general cognitive theory of analogical reasoning. In fact, we believe that ARC-AGI tasks are similar to 2-dim Copycat analogy tasks to some extent: Copycat letter-string analogy tasks primarily focus on letters, numbers and symbols, while ARC-AGI tasks focus on numbers and 2-dim concepts.

Concept Slippage, a core mechanism in Copycat [2, 3], describes how activating one concept can trigger related concepts and higher-level relational structures, thereby expanding the search over possible interpretations.
One task from Copycat theory is shown in Figure 2. Given an example: $a\ b\ c\to a\ b\ d$ , what will $x\ y\ z$ change to? If we assume the alphabet is not cycle, i.e. the next letter of $z$ is not $a$ but nothing, $x\ y\ z$ might change to $x\ y$ , which is obviously not a preferred answer. If we notice that $a\ b\ c$ and $x\ y\ z$ are the beginning and last three letters of the alphabet, the concept “opposite” will be activated in the Slipnet. Therefore, the concept “successor” might slip to the concept “predecessor”, resulting in the answer $w\ y\ z$ . The process of one activated concept causing another related concept is concept slippage.

Figure 2: One task from Copycat theory. In workspace, the task is given as: if $a\ b\ c$ changing to $a\ b\ d$ , then what will $x\ y\ z$ change to? The concept “opposite” activated from $a\ b\ c$ and $x\ y\ z$ in the alphabet will promote the concept “successor” to slip to the concept “predecessor”. This will finally result in the answer $w\ y\ z$ .

## 2.2 Analogical Reasoning Benchmarks and Models

A variety of cognitive tests and benchmarks have been developed to assess analogical reasoning ability, among which Raven’s Progressive Matrices (RPM) are a canonical example and are widely used to measure human fluid intelligence [9, 10]. Other benchmarks, such as the Abstraction and Reasoning Corpus (ARC) [11, 7], place a stronger emphasis on abstraction and analogical reasoning, posing persistent challenges for contemporary artificial intelligence systems. Compared to ARC-AGI-1, ARC-AGI-2 [7] introduces substantially more complex and abstract conceptual compositions, significantly increasing task difficulty. Developed from these two benchmarks, ARC-AGI-3[12] is introduced as an interactive benchmark for studying agentic intelligence from novel environments and in which agents must explore, infer goals, build internal models of environment dynamics, and plan effective action sequences without explicit instructions. Nevertheless, systematic evaluation of far analogical reasoning grounded in real-world concepts remains limited, which motivates us to propose ARCANA.

Among models for analogical reasoning, large language models (LLMs) have emerged as the dominant approach for solving ARC-AGI-1&2, owing to their extensive pretraining and strong general-purpose reasoning capabilities. Beyond LLM-based approaches, alternative methods include train-from-scratch models such as Vision Transformer (ViT)-based picture-to-picture mappings [13], as well as approaches inspired by the Minimal Description Length (MDL) principle [14].
In parallel, several methods have been proposed for analogical reasoning beyond the ARC setting, including vector-based matching approaches that leverage BART embeddings [15], as well as models that integrate encoder–decoder architectures with Structure-Mapping Theory to induce structured correspondences [16].

## 3.1 Dataset Creation

## 3.1.1 Target-concept-target Generation Loop

ARCANA tasks are grounded in diverse real-world objects, concepts, and physical phenomena, which we refer to as targets. Each target can be abstracted into a small set of higher-level concepts that govern the grid transformation. For instance, the “threaded rings" target (shown in Figure 1(c)) can be summarized by three concepts—move, shelter, and 3D—where 3D rings move and partially occlude (“shelter") the column pattern. Once such concepts are extracted from targets, they can be slipped and re-grounded to other targets (e.g., candles burning on a cake, as shown in Figure 8(a)). With this target-concept-target generation loop, we have created several different targets that nevertheless instantiate the similar underlying rules or concepts on ARC grids.

Although some tasks in ARCANA might be mapped from physical phenomena, ARCANA still holds a crucial difference from physical reasoning benchmarks. Many tasks in ARCANA are non-physical, including cat1, shoes1, etc. Even physical inspired tasks are highly abstracted away from real physics: melt1, melt2, melt4 encode conservation of colored cells and a horizontal surface rather than density, or strict fluid dynamics; buoyancy2 concerns grid relations rather than buoyant-force calculation; and even Kepler_second_law_or_area1 can be solved through a grid invariant without knowing Kepler’s law. Therefore, we address that ARCANA evaluates explicit transfer from real-world concepts to abstract grids, and physical phenomena are only one possible source of analogy; the source concept should not be confused with the capability being evaluated.

The target-concept-target generation loop can also be viewed as an explicit process of analogy. We source targets from diverse domains (e.g., water, windmills, flags, and physical laws) and classify tasks into 34 concept categories plus an “other" class. Example concepts include wave propagation, 3D cuboid motion and shadows, fluid flow, state transitions, object-induced rotation, light propagation, and game-rule dynamics. Many tasks are compositional, combining multiple concepts (e.g., motion with rotation).

## 3.1.2 Grounding Targets into Grids

Concept attention.

Bridging targets and discrete ARC grids requires concept attention: emphasizing task-relevant concepts of a target while suppressing concepts that are secondary or irrelevant to the core rule. For example, in the melt target (shown in Figure 3(b)), ice melts only upon contact with hot water (red cells); after melting, both the ice and the hot-water become cold water (blue cells). The final cold-water volume (the number of grid cells) equals the sum of the initial ice volume and hot-water volume. We therefore focus on the melting condition and this discrete conservation rule, while ignoring physical nuances such as density differences across phases and temperatures.

Concept expansion.

Beyond concept attention, we sometimes augment a target with additional concepts (e.g., time) to fully specify its dynamics and complicate the tasks. We refer to this as concept expansion. For instance, in the charge target (shown in Figure 3(c)), green cells (analogous to charge or data) propagate along directed “wires” from a source container at the red terminal to a sink container at the green terminal. At each time step, each wire transfers one cell if the sink container has remaining capacity. Introducing an explicit temporal process (and treating it as effectively continuous) is necessary to correctly solve the test. Overall, concept expansion substantially increases the difficulty and diversity of ARCANA tasks.

Generation principles

We construct the benchmark according to four principles that are intentionally simple and human-grounded:
(1) Human-familiar targets. The targets are common and recognizable to humans; given the target, most people who are familiar with it can solve the task accurately with little effort.
(2) Concept-faithful grounding. The ARC grids inherit the target’s core concepts, and the grid-target analogy is intended to be explicit.
(3) Multiple targets, one rule. A task may admit multiple plausible analogical targets, but they must agree on the same underlying transformation rule, such as claw and chopsticks.
(4) Human-aligned correctness. When multiple rules are consistent with the examples, we choose the one most natural to humans, best aligned with everyday concepts, and lowest in cognitive burden.

## LIMITATIONS SECTION
ARCANA is currently limited in scale and representation. When we constructed ARCANA, we have tried our best to distinguish each task from each other, i.e., similar tasks could be generated from existing tasks to expand the benchmark. We mainly focus on the diversity but not the quantity of ARCANA, while leaving scaling up an unsettled problem for the community. Moreover, although ARCANA covers diverse targets, the space of real-world concepts and analogical sources is effectively unbounded, making larger benchmarks difficult without community effort. We view ARCANA as a starting point for such expansion. Furthermore, ARC-style grid abstraction provides conciseness, precision, and cognitive challenge, but limits expressivity for structures such as oblique lines, circles, and ellipses (e.g., the ellipses of Kepler_second_law_or_area1 task shown in Figure 5 are not strictly faithful expressions), motivating richer representations for future far-analogy benchmarks.

We also note several limitations of ConceptSIR. Future work should evaluate it on a larger set of ARCANA tasks beyond the current challenging subset. Although code execution offers a faithful and reproducible way to apply hypothesized rules, it is not fully expressive for all ARCANA targets, especially topology-heavy ones (e.g., animal category); extending the executor to atomic grid operations with explicit where-to-apply instructions, or expanding the framework to a true agent which could judge output the answer as code or direct matrix may improve generality. Moreover, our code-mutation procedure selects the “best” candidate solely by prediction error, which is a rigid proxy for solution quality. Additional modules, such as explicit memory or human feedback, may further improve robustness and generalization. More limitations are also mentioned in Section 7.

## ABSTRACT
Radiation treatment planning requires balancing competing objectives to satisfy clinical criteria while accommodating physicians' preferences. This study aims to develop an evidence-driven, multi-agent compound AI system that integrates DRL-based automated treatment planning with LLM-based conversational plan refinement for proton PBS therapy. A policy network trained using PPO first generates multiple plans without human intervention, from which one is selected as the target plan and the others serve as references for subsequent refinements. For challenging cases requiring further adjustments, an LLM-based multi-agent system enables plan refinement through natural language interactions. The system comprises a planning agent that recommends evidence-supported adjustments, a chatbot providing a unified conversational interface, and an evaluation agent that retrieves clinical metrics and identifies constraint violations. To enhance domain-specific reasoning, the planning agent based on Qwen3-14B is designed to justify its recommendations using evidence from previously generated plans and post-trained on task-specific data using SFT and GRPO. The framework was trained and evaluated using 21 patients with H&N cancers, with 5 PPO-generated plans per patient. Of these, 50 plans were selected for instruction-guided refinement. Compared with the base LLM, the post-trained Qwen3-14B demonstrated improved instruction-following, information retrieval and domain-specific reasoning capabilities. The resulting adjustments more effectively improved user-prioritized objectives while limiting deterioration in competing objectives, yielding more clinically appropriate trade-offs in patient-specific plan refinement. The proposed compound AI system provides a user-friendly planning platform, offering a promising approach to personalized proton PBS treatment planning.

## 1 Introduction

Radiotherapy is a conerstone of cancer treatment worldwide, and the quality of a treatment plan can directly influence tumor control, treatment-related toxicity, and ultimately patients’ survival and quality of life. Consequently, treatment planning has received considerable attention from the radiation oncology community. The planning process can be formulated as a multi-objective optimization problem, in which human planners iteratively adjust planning objectives and constraints for target volumes and surrounding organs at risk (OARs) to achieve an appropriate balance among competing clinical goals. This task becomes particularly challenging when OARs are in close proximity to, abut, or overlap with the tumor, making trade-offs between target coverage and normal-tissue sparing unavoidable.

In clinical practice, treatment plans often undergo multiple rounds of refinement before final approval, extending the planning process over several days. Such delays are undesirable because patient anatomy may change during this interval, potentially reducing the accuracy of dose delivery in a treatment modality that relies on precise spatial targeting. In addition, plan quality can vary substantially with factors such as planner experience, available planning time, and the capabilities of commercial treatment planning systems (TPSs). Suboptimal trade-offs among competing objectives may compromise either tumor coverage or normal-tissue sparing. Together, these challenges have created a strong need for efficient and reliable methods that can consistently generate high-quality treatment plans, thereby motivating the rapid development of automated treatment planning.

In last decades, automated treatment planning has been rapidly developed from knowledge-based planning (KBP) [1, 2] and multi-criteria optimization (MCO) [3] to virtual planners built with deep reinforcement learning (DRL) [4, 5, 6, 7] and large language models (LLMs) [8, 9, 10, 11, 12].
KBP estimates dose-volume histogram (DVH) curve bands from historical data for new patients by mathcing their anatomy to previous cases. MCO generates alternative plans by navigating the trade-off space along the approximate Pareto surface. However, the former collapses three-dimensional anatomy into two-dimensional DVH outputs, resulting in inverse optimization converging to inferior local minima and the necessity of mannual plan adjustments. The latter demands considerable manual effort on base plan generation, Pareto surface exploration and final plan selection [12]. Both solutions fail to convert the time-consuming and expertise-demand treatment planning into desirable automatic process.
Recently, DRL, a powerful approach to decision-making, has been increasingly explored for automated treatment planning. DRL-based methods typically enable policy networks adjustment intelligence through a trial-and-error learning process, using algorithms such as Q-learning [4], Soft Actor-Critic (SAC) [5], and Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) [6, 7], etc.
Although challenges remain in generalizing across diverse treatement sites and formulating reward functions that quantitatively capture the multifactorial assessment of plan quality, these methods have demonstrated promising progress and offer a viable approach to automated treatment planning.
Recent years have also witnessed a surge in LLM-based applications in healthcare, particularly in high-stakes clinical decision-making tasks such as open-ended medical diagnostics [13, 14, 15] and multidisciplinary treatment planning [16]. Among these applications, radiotherapy treatment planning has emerged as an active area of research.
For example, Nusrat et al. [9] proposed the Dose Optimization Language Agent (DOLA) for autonomous prostate radiatherapy planning, integrating chain-of-thought (CoT) prompting, retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) and reinforcement learning (RL) with Llama 3.1. Liu et al. [10] developed GPT-RadPlan for volumetric modulated arc therapy (VMAT) planning, leveraging the multimodal model GPT-4V for plan evaluation and objective adjustment through in-context learning. Wei et al. [8] employed prompt engineering to guide LLMs in adjusting optimization parameters for cervical cancer VMAT planning, incorporating feedback from medical physicists to improve planning decisions. More recently, Maniscalco et al. [12] introduced a compound AI framework combining seven specialized LLM agents with a directive-conditioned 3D dose predictor. Through a structured multi-agent pipeline, these agents navigated the multi-objective optimization landscape by collaboratively performing clinical knowledge retrieval, DVH analysis, spatial dose assessment, plan compilation, quality assurance and tool execution. Collectively, these studies highlight the growing potential of LLMs to support complex planning decisions and advance automated radiotherapy treatment planning.

Although DRL and LLMs have significantly pushed the boundaries of automated treatment planning, DRL-based methods lack the flexibility to incorporate human instructions, while LLM-based approaches remain constrained by the limited domain-specific reasoning capabilities of base LLMs. Previous studies have demonstrated that existing LLMs may generate guideline-inconsistent or potentially harmful clinical recommendations [17, 18, 19]. Although their outputs can provide complementary insights and support personalized decision-making, their treatment recommendations in precision oncology have not yet matched the quality and credibility of those made by human experts. Our experiments also indicate that, when given high-level instructions such as “improve [OAR] sparing without compromising [TARGET VOLUME] coverage”, or “reduce hot spots within [TARGET VOLUME] while maintaining its V100% above 92%”, base LLMs often recommend overly aggressive or ineffective adjustments, failing to appropriately balance improvements in the intended clinical objectives against potential deterioration in competing objectives.
Moreover, medical LLM agents remain limited in their ability to dynamically adapt to changing patient conditions, further undermining their reliability in generating patient-specific treatment plans.
Another limitation is that existing LLM-based agentic planning frameworks primarily focus on automated initial plan generation, overlooking the time-consuming iterative paln refinement guided by physician-planner communication, as illustrated in Figure 1. This stage is particularly challenging when competing clinical objectives cannot be simultaneously satisfied, and physicians prioritize different objectives when making trade-offs. Furthermore, existing LLM-based automated plannig methods have primarily focused on photon-based radiotherapym such as VMAT and IMRT, while proton therapy planning remains relatively underexplored.

In this work, we propose a multi-agent compound AI system for proton pencil beam scanning (PBS) radiotherapy planning. Specifically, a DRL-based policy network is first employed to automatically generate multiple treatment plans without human intervention. Subsequently, a conversational planning platform comprising three agents is developed to enable interactive plan refinement through natural language instructions. In particular, the planning agent is post-trained using SFT and GRPO to recommend patient-specific adjustments supported by evidence derived from previously generated plans, enhancing the reliability and interpretability of its recommendations.
Compared with the base LLM, the post-trained model demonstrates improved instruction-following, information retrieval and clinical reasoning capabilities, enabeling more effective plan adjustments aligned with physicians’ preferences while maintaining reasonable trade-offs among competing clinical objectives. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first compound AI framework specifically designed for proton PBS treatment planning that integrates automated initial plan generation with interactive, physician-guided plan refinement.

## 2 Methods and Materials

## 2.1 Patient Cohorts

This study was conducted under approval from the institutional review board of University of California San Diego. We retrospectively selected 21 bilateral or ipsilateral H&N cases treated at the California Protons Cancer Therapy Center between 2017 and 2024. Across these cases, treatment courses consisted of 5–60 fractions delivered twice daily, with no more than five beams per plan. Each case contained one to four prescription levels spanning 4.0–74.2 Gy. Rather than redesigning the clinical setup, we retained the original prescriptions, beam angles, target definitions, and field-specific targets from the delivered plans. Moreover, following Wang et al. [7], proton spots were arranged on a hexagonal grid and dose was calculated using the Monte Carlo simulation. To ensure machine deliverability, the beam energy constraint of 70-240MeV and the minimal monitor units (MUs) of 3-300MUs per spot are enforced in inverse optimization.

## 2.2 Automatic Treatment Planning Framework for Proton PBS

We perform treatment planning with the system depicted in Figure 2, where an initial planning module built with an adjustment policy network is employed to automatically produce plans for uploaded patient cases without human intervention. The policy network is trained with the PPO framework proposed in Wang’s previous work [7]. Moreover, the advanced learn-to-optimize (L2O) inverse optimization engine designed in work [7] is used as the backend of the planning system given its promissing effectiveness and efficiency.
For easy cases, the initial planning module is likely to produce clinically accepted plans, while for challenging cases that physicians may give further refine instructions to, an LLM-based multi-agent planning module is designed to adjust a selected plan by reasoning over prior plans generated in the initial planning stage. Since the performance of the PPO-trained policy network and L2O inverse optimizer have been well demonstrated in Wang’s work [7], we will focus on the multi-agent planning module, with a foucs on the post-training of the planning agent.

## 2.3 LLM-based Multi-agent Planning System

Physicians have different treatment preferences as they prioritize different objectives, thereby a conversational interface that enables human-in-the-loop planning is crucial. Recent developments in LLM-based agents provide a promising paradigm for facilitating nature language interaction between physicians and automated planning systems. Here, we propose an LLM-based multi-agent planning system as shown in Figure 2. Specifically, the system consists of three collaborative agents: a chatbot agent, an evaluation agent and a planning agent. The first two agents are prompted GPT models, while the planning agent is a post-trained Qwen3-14B model. Functionally, the chatbot agent serves as the only user interface, responsible for answering general questions on radiation oncology and routing corresponding queries to other agents, e.g., routing high-level plan refine instructions and specific plan adjusting commands to the planner agent, and those relevant to clinical metrics, clinical criteria and constraint violations to the evaluation agent. The evaluation agent is designed to look up clinical requirements, evaluate plan qualities and check constraint violations by retrieving information from the memory module or calling pre-defined APIs. It also compares plans before and after adjustment and provides feedback to the planner agent. The planning agent is post-trained to adjust plans according to user’s queries. For specific adjusting commands like “reset objective parameters to [SPECIFIC VALUES]”, it invokes APIs directly and then confirms with the user. After being confirmed, the inverse optimization agent will be engaged to generate new plans. For high-level instructions like “improve sparing of [OAR NAME]”, the planning agent recommends adjustments and a sequence of API calls that implement proposed adjustments by following a predefined planning workflow. It reasons over the objective-metric mapping of prior plans and justifies its recommendations with reliable evidences.

The system is empowered with a memory module and a set of API tools. The memory module stores patient information, clinical criteria and plan’s objective-metric mapping information for Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG), while the API tools implement operations including DVH metric calculating, objective modifying, plan optimizing, etc for the evaluation and planning agents. As mentioned above, pre-trained LLMs may produce semantically meaningful adjustment recommendations, but the resulting plans may not be clinically acceptable because the simulated dose distributions frequently show unexpected degradations on competing objectives.
Therefore, in this work, Qwen3-14B is post-trained on our planning-specific dataset to align its behavior with clinical plan-adjustment practices, thereby reducing the number of adjustment iterations and, consequently, LLM inference costs and overall planning time.

Moreover, widely used LLM evaluation metrics are not directly applicable to our task because recommended adjustments that appear semantically correct may still be either too aggressive or too conservative to effectively produce a clinically acceptable plan. Instead, the performance of a planning agent should be evaluated based on the final simulation results of the adjusted plan, taking into account both the improvement of the targeted objectives specified in the user’s instruction and the degradation of competing objectives that may not be explicitly mentioned. In addition, a single user instruction may contain conflicting goals that cannot be simultaneously satisfied. Therefore, the reasonableness of the trade-offs among targeted objectives should also be considered in the evaluation.

## CONCLUSION
Rapid advances in LLMs have the potential not only to transform treatment planning workflows but also, more importantly, to improve the quality of generated treatment plans by supporting better planning decisions. To fully realize this potential, developing domain-specific LLMs tailored to treatment planning is crucial.
In this work, we post-trained a planning agent for a multi-agent treatment planning framework and demonstrated the effectiveness of the proposed plannig pipeline and post-training stategy in enhancing the LLM’s ability to recommend patient-specific plan adjustments.
By enabling users to guide plan refinement through high-level instructions, the proposed planning agent addresses a key limitation of existing automatic treatment planning systems: their limited ability to interact with physicians and refine treatment plans according to clinical preferences in a manner similar to human planners. Moreover, the planning agent can justify its recommendations using evidence derived from the planning objectives and clinical metrics of previously generated plans, improving the interpretability and reliability of the proposed adjustments.
By integrating the planning agent with a DRL-based automatic planning module capable of generating high-quality plans without human intervention, the proposed multi-agent framework supports both initial automatic planning and subsequent interactive plan refinement, addressing two essential stages of the clinical treatment plannig workflow.

This pilot study represents an initial effort to bridge automatic plan generation and instruction-guided plan refinement. While the proposed framework explores the key components of such an integrated planning system, further development is needed to enhance its capabilities, improve user experience, and facilitate its translation into clinical practice.

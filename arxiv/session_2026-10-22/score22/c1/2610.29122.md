## ABSTRACT
Individual patient data are the gold standard for secondary analysis of time-to-event evidence, but they are rarely released, and reconstruction from a published Kaplan-Meier figure could help. Existing methods all claim high fidelity, each judged by whichever agreements it happens to achieve, and each returns a single dataset although the map from figure to data is not bijective. We define fidelity explicitly: a dataset is admissible when it reproduces the risk table exactly, follows the digitized curve to within the image resolution, and respects the printed median, interval and event count. We propose StochIKM, a stochastic algorithm that digitizes the figure with a vision-capable language model and uses the high-fidelity definition as constraints, returning an admissible set of individual patient datasets. A solution is guaranteed to exist whenever the figure has been read correctly, so a failure indicts either the published figure or its digitization. In simulation and in 79 arms from published oncology trials, StochIKM reproduces the published risk tables and medians more closely than LLM-improved IPDfromKM, a high-impact alternative.

## 1 Introduction

Individual patient data (IPD) are the currency of meta-analysis. For studies reporting time-to-event outcomes, access to IPD enables individual-participant-data meta-analysis 42, 31; assessment of modelling assumptions 32, 45 and sensitivity analysis; extrapolation beyond the observed follow-up for the evaluation of health technologies 19;
hypothesis generation for future studies; and emulation of randomized comparisons between treatments never studied head to head 9, 29.

The original IPD, however, are rarely accessible because of confidentiality and proprietary considerations. Even where data-sharing platforms exist, requests are often fulfilled for only a small number of applicants 18, 7. Reconstructing IPD from the published
Kaplan–Meier (K–M) curve offers a promising alternative, but the quality of such synthetic IPD is not guaranteed and requires careful evaluation. Where a ground truth is observed, as in supervised learning, accuracy is the distance from the truth. As the true IPD are rarely available, the question becomes how completely a reconstruction agrees with what the publication reports. Faith is “the evidence of things not seen” (Heb. 11:1, KJV). In information theory, fidelity is defined as the expected distortion between a source and its reproduction under a stated distortion measure, and a code is judged against a fidelity criterion fixed in advance 37, 3. Although authors who reconstruct K–M figures have measured fidelity in different ways, the idea beneath is common to all: a reconstruction is faithful to the extent that it reproduces what the original publication reported.

Recovering information from published survival curves is not new.
Parmar et al. Super and
Tierney et al. Super showed how a hazard ratio and its variance could be
recovered from a published curve together with the reported numbers at risk. Guyot et al. Super introduced an algorithm that inverts the K–M product-limit formula one risk-table interval at a time, assuming that
censoring is spread uniformly within each interval, and so produces a
complete set of event and censoring times. Liu et al. Super refined that algorithm, correcting a boundary condition so that the K–M curve is mathematically plausible, and developed software that clinicians could use. Since then, reporting standards have improved the quality of survival figures 27, 24, and vision-capable large language models (LLMs) are able to parse the elements of a figure simply by prompt engineering 21. These developments reduce the burden of extracting information from published figures but do not resolve the statistical ambiguity inherent in reconstructing patient-level records from aggregate survival data.

Three methodological challenges remain. First, fidelity has been checked as an afterthought, against inconsistent criteria: agreement with the plotted
curve and the hazard ratio 8; with reported events, numbers
at risk, survival probabilities and medians 20;
or with a synthetic ground truth 51, rather than enforced as part of the reconstruction. Some reconstructed IPD therefore do not agree with the published risk table 8, 20. Second, a published K–M curve does not identify the original IPD uniquely. Missing or overlapping censoring marks, sparse risk tables and the finite resolution of the figure admit many event and censoring configurations. Reported confidence intervals for the median carry additional information about sampling variability, but existing methods neither use them as constraints nor return more than a single IPD. Third, reconstruction depends heavily on digitization. Manual digitization is labor-intensive and prone to operator error 20, whereas automated extraction may degrade when figures are low-resolution rasters, curves overlap, or arms are distinguished only by line type or similar colours 51.

To address these limitations, we propose StochIKM, an automated stochastic algorithm for generating high-fidelity IPD from published K–M curves. StochIKM makes three contributions to the three challenges. First, it defines fidelity by three constraints: each admissible IPD must reproduce the reported numbers at risk exactly, remain within a prespecified maximum absolute deviation from the extracted K–M curve, and agree within prespecified tolerances with the reported number of events, median survival and its confidence interval, when available. StochIKM enforces them within its loop. Second, StochIKM generates a set of $\Omega$ admissible datasets rather than one, so that the uncertainty of reconstruction can be propagated into downstream analysis. Third, under idealized conditions of internally consistent reporting, an error-free K–M figure and exact extraction of the plotted information, we establish the existence of at least one dataset satisfying the fidelity criteria.

## 2 Methods

## Step 1: Digitization

StochIKM reconstructs IPD that satisfy the built-in high-fidelity constraints (“admissible” IPD) from a published paper that contains a K–M curve and an accompanying risk table. First, a vision-capable LLM (in this paper, GPT-5) automatically extracts, or “digitizes”, the information of interest. For simplicity, we present the notation for one treatment arm. The digitized elements fall into two categories. The first are numbers that can be checked directly against the original paper, denoted with an asterisk: i) from the risk table, the numbers of
patients at risk $r^{*}_{0}=n^{*},r^{*}_{1},\ldots,r^{*}_{K}$ , where $n^{*}$ is the arm-specific sample size; ii) the median overall survival (mOS) $M^{*}$ and its interval $(M^{*}_{L},M^{*}_{U})$ ; and iii) the number of events $D^{*}$ . These elements contain no digitization error. The second are elements that can be obtained only through the vision capability of the LLM, denoted with a hat: i) the coordinate system of the plotting area, that is, the pixel positions of the two axes and of their labelled ticks, by which a pixel is converted into a time and a survival probability; ii)
the curve of the arm, traced by its colour and read at times $0=\hat{\tau}_{0}<\hat{\tau}_{1}<\cdots<\hat{\tau}_{J}$ with heights $\hat{s}_{j}=\hat{S}(\hat{\tau}_{j})$ , where consecutive read times define the fine
intervals $F_{j}=(\hat{\tau}_{j-1},\hat{\tau}_{j}]$ ; and iii) the visible drops of that curve, each a vertical segment whose time and whose heights immediately above and
below it reveal that at least one event occurred there (Figure 1B). These elements may contain digitization errors, and the resolution of the image bounds how large they can be: a drop can be located in time only to about one pixel column, $\delta_{t}$ , and a height can be read only to about half the width of the drawn
line, $\delta_{s}$ . Censoring marks may be printed but not used in this paper; we return to them in the Discussion.

## Step 2: Reconstruction of admissible IPD

A reconstruction is of high fidelity if it closely agrees with
the published K–M curve and the information commonly reported with it, and
an IPD that meets this standard is called admissible. Fidelity is imposed through three sequential constraints. A tilde denotes a quantity of the reconstruction: the survival $\tilde{S}$ , the mOS and its interval ( $\tilde{M}$ , $\tilde{M}_{L}$ , $\tilde{M}_{U}$ ), and the number of events $\tilde{d}_{k}$ in each risk-table interval. The zeroth-order, or risk-table, constraint requires that at every
risk-table time the reconstruction leaves exactly $r^{*}_{k}$ patients at risk (Figure 1E),

 $\tilde{n}(t^{*}_{k})=r^{*}_{k},\qquad k=1,\ldots,K.$ 

(1)

The first-order, or curve, constraint requires that at every read time the K–M
curve of the reconstruction agrees with the digitized curve to within
 $\delta_{s}$ (Figure 1F),

 $\max_{j=1,\ldots,J}\bigl|\hat{s}_{j}-\tilde{S}(\hat{\tau}_{j})\bigr|\leq\delta_{s},$ 

(2)

and that every event is placed within $\delta_{t}$ of the drop that revealed it.
The second-order, or reported-summaries, constraint uses the quantities the
publication prints alongside the figure (Figure 1G),

 $\begin{gathered}\bigl|\tilde{M}-M^{*}\bigr|\leq\varepsilon_{t},\\
\frac{\bigl|(\tilde{M}_{L},\tilde{M}_{U})\cap(M^{*}_{L},M^{*}_{U})\bigr|}{\bigl|(\tilde{M}_{L},\tilde{M}_{U})\cup(M^{*}_{L},M^{*}_{U})\bigr|}\geq\rho,\\
\sum_{k=1}^{K}\tilde{d}_{k}=D^{*},\end{gathered}$ 

(3)

where $(\tilde{M}_{L},\tilde{M}_{U})$ is the Brookmeyer–Crowley interval
4, and $\varepsilon_{t}$ and $\rho$ are fixed in advance.
Each part of (3) is used only when the publication prints the corresponding quantity, and an endpoint that is not reached is set to $t^{*}_{K}$ ; a figure with no
printed median and no printed number of events is reconstructed under (1) and (2)
alone. The algorithm below generates one candidate dataset at a time and keeps it
only if (1)–(3) hold, repeating until $\Omega$ admissible datasets have been collected.

The reconstruction proceeds one risk-table interval $I_{k}=(t^{*}_{k-1},t^{*}_{k}]$ at a time, from $I_{1}$ to
 $I_{K}$ , carrying forward the number of patients still at risk and the
reconstructed survival (Figure 1A). Within $I_{k}$ it
must determine the number of events $\tilde{d}_{k}$ and the number of censored patients
 $\tilde{c}_{k}$ , whose sum must be the attrition $\alpha^{*}_{k}=r^{*}_{k-1}-r^{*}_{k}$ obtained in Step 1.

Initialization.

Set $\tilde{q}_{0}=1$ and $\tilde{n}_{1}=n^{*}$ , and recall
that each $I_{k}$ is the union of the fine intervals $F_{j}$ , $j\in J_{k}$ 
(Figure 1B).

Step 2.1: the censoring.

With no censoring,
 $r^{*}_{k-1}\hat{S}(t^{*}_{k})/\hat{S}(t^{*}_{k-1})$ patients would be at risk at
 $t^{*}_{k}$ , and the shortfall against $r^{*}_{k}$ gives a starting value for
 $\tilde{c}_{k}$ , corrected for the fact that the events see about
 $r^{*}_{k-1}-\tilde{c}_{k}/2$ patients rather than $r^{*}_{k-1}$ . With probability
 $\omega$ this value is replaced by a uniform draw from
 $\{0,1,\ldots,\alpha^{*}_{k}\}$ ; this randomization is what lets the search reach every possible configuration, as Proposition 2.1 requires. The censoring times are drawn
independently and uniformly on $I_{k}$ , and $\tilde{c}_{j}$ of them fall in $F_{j}$ 
(Figure 1C).

Step 2.2: the events.

Events of $F_{j}$ are placed in the event window $W_{j}$ , equal to
the last $\delta_{t}$ of $F_{j}$ when the curve drops at $\hat{\tau}_{j}$ and to all of
 $F_{j}$ otherwise,
and a reference time $\tilde{u}_{j}$ , uniform on $W_{j}$ , fixes which censorings of
 $F_{j}$ come before them (Figure 1D). Inverting the K–M product
one fine interval at a time gives
 $\tilde{m}_{j}=\tilde{n}_{j}-\#\{\text{censorings of }F_{j}\text{ before }\tilde{u}_{j}\}$ and

 $\begin{gathered}\tilde{d}_{j}=\Bigl[\tilde{m}_{j}\Bigl(1-\frac{\hat{s}_{j}}{\tilde{q}_{j-1}}\Bigr)\Bigr],\\
\tilde{q}_{j}=\tilde{q}_{j-1}\Bigl(1-\frac{\tilde{d}_{j}}{\tilde{m}_{j}}\Bigr),\end{gathered}$ 

with $\tilde{n}_{j+1}=\tilde{n}_{j}-\tilde{d}_{j}-\tilde{c}_{j}$ . Given the censorings,
this step is deterministic.

Step 2.3: matching the risk table.

If
 $\tilde{n}(t^{*}_{k})=r^{*}_{k-1}-\tilde{d}_{k}-\tilde{c}_{k}$ is not $r^{*}_{k}$ , the
discrepancy is charged to censoring, $\tilde{c}_{k}\leftarrow\alpha^{*}_{k}-\tilde{d}_{k}$ ,
and $I_{k}$ is recomputed (Figure 1E). Once $r^{*}_{k}$ is
attained, $\tilde{c}_{k}\pm 1$ and $\tilde{c}_{k}\pm 2$ are tried as well, and one of
those that also reach $r^{*}_{k}$ is chosen at random.

Step 2.4: fidelity check.

Constraint (1) now holds by construction;
constraint (2) is checked over $j\in J_{k}$ (Figure 1F); and
constraint (3) is checked as soon as it is decidable, that is, once the upper
confidence limit of the reconstructed curve has fallen to one half
(Figure 1G). A failed interval is attempted again from Step 2.1,
and after $N$ failures the run is discarded and restarted from $I_{1}$ (dashed
paths of Figure 1A).

Steps 2.1–2.4 are repeated for $k=1,\ldots,K$ ; a run that reaches the end of $I_{K}$ 
yields one admissible IPD $\tilde{D}_{\ell}=\{(\tilde{y}_{i}^{(\ell)},\tilde{\Delta}_{i}^{(\ell)})\}_{i=1}^{n^{*}}$ , and runs continue until $\tilde{D}_{1},\ldots,\tilde{D}_{\Omega}$ have been
collected.

Proposition 2.1 (Under perfect digitization, StochIKM guarantees a solution.).

Suppose the risk table and the printed summaries are transcribed correctly, and every
survival probability $\hat{s}_{j}$ read from the figure is within $\delta_{s}$ of the curve
printed in the publication (perfect digitization). Then the original patient data of the trial satisfy constraints (1)–(3); in particular, the admissible set is not empty.

Proof 2.2.

Write $x^{0}$ for the original data. Since the risk table is read correctly,
 $\tilde{n}(t^{*}_{k})=r^{*}_{k}$ holds at $x^{0}$ for every $k$ , which is (1), and
exactly $\alpha^{*}_{k}$ patients leave the risk set in each $I_{k}$ . The K–M curve
of $x^{0}$ is the published curve $S$ , so at every read time
 $|\hat{s}_{j}-S(\hat{\tau}_{j})|\leq\delta_{s}$ by assumption, which is (2); each event
of $x^{0}$ produced the drop that revealed it, so it lies in that drop’s window.
The printed summaries $M^{*}$ , $(M^{*}_{L},M^{*}_{U})$ and $D^{*}$ are computed from
 $x^{0}$ , so (3) holds at $x^{0}$ up to the rounding of the printed digits, which
 $\varepsilon_{t}$ absorbs.

## Step 3: Relaxation

Every quantity denoted with a hat is read from an image, which may be of poor quality or even contain mistakes, and the LLM may misread a legend or a colour. The premise of Proposition 2.1, the perfect digitization assumption, fails and the admissible set may be empty. Repeated failure therefore has two possible causes: the search has not yet found a solution, or the digitization is
inconsistent with the published summaries and no solution exists. Step 3, relaxing constraints (1)–(3) lower the thresholds to a certain extent, and still produce result of fidelity given imperfect digitization. If $R$ runs produce no admissible dataset, the constraint
responsible for most of the failures is identified and relaxed by one level, and
the search restarts; the levels are fixed in advance and applied in the same
order for every figure. The second-order constraint has three parts and they are relaxed separately. The number of events is the commonest cause of failure, a dataset is
accepted if $\bigl|\sum_{k}\tilde{d}_{k}-D^{*}\bigr|\leq\tau D^{*}$ , with $\tau=0.035$ , and if
that is still not enough, $\delta_{s}$ is widened as for the first-order constraint while the
tolerance is kept. The number of events is a sum over
every interval, and configurations that meet every other constraint often miss it by a few
events, so a small tolerance raises the efficiency of the search considerably while the risk
table, the curve, the median and its interval keep their full fidelity. A discrepancy in number of events per arm may be attributed to a systematic mis-reading besides the digitization: the reported total number of events may count events that occurred after the last time point the figure shows, a practice that varies with the authors and the journals. The tolerance $\tau$ is
the largest discrepancy observed in the case study, 9 of 262 events. For the median and its
interval, $\varepsilon_{t}$ is doubled and $\rho$ lowered to $0.6$ , then $\varepsilon_{t}$ 
doubled again and $\rho$ lowered to $0.4$ , then the interval condition is dropped, then the
median condition is dropped. For the first-order constraint, $\delta_{s}$ is multiplied by
 $1.5$ at each level, up to a ceiling $\delta_{s}^{\max}$ . The zeroth-order
constraint is never relaxed. If no admissible dataset is obtained once every level has been exhausted, the figure is declared irreconcilable and no IPD is returned. A reconstruction obtained after relaxation is reported together with the
levels used.

Throughout, the tolerances $\delta_{t}$ and $\delta_{s}$ are fixed by the resolution
of the figure, one pixel column and half the width of the drawn line
respectively, and $\varepsilon_{t}$ is $\delta_{t}$ plus half the last printed digit
of $M^{*}$ . The remaining constants are used for every
figure in this paper: the probability of the uniform draw in Step 2.1 is
 $\omega=0.1$ ; an interval is attempted $N=200$ times before the run is discarded;
the required overlap of the two intervals is $\rho=0.8$ ; the portfolio size is
 $\Omega=50$ ; relaxation begins after $R=40\Omega$ runs without an admissible dataset

## CONCLUSION
Reconstruction methods routinely claim high fidelity, but each has measured it
differently, and no criterion has been agreed
upon 8, 20, 51. Our principal
contribution is to propose one: a reconstruction is admissible when it reproduces
the risk table exactly, follows the digitized curve to within the resolution of
the image, and respects the summaries printed beside the figure. Because that criterion does not identify a single
dataset, StochIKM returns a portfolio of $\Omega$ admissible ones, and the spread
across them measures what the publication left undetermined.

We build directly on Liu et al. Super, whose algorithm depends on manual,
software-assisted tracing and returns a single IPD, and whose authors
anticipated both the automation of the reading and the multiplicity of the
answer. Vision-capable language models have made the first possible, and the
constrained sampling scheme developed here makes the second explicit. Over 97
published arms the reconstruction reproduced every risk table and matched the
published median to within one month in all of them, and
Proposition 2.1 shows that, when the figure is traced exactly, an
admissible set exists. A failure of producing result therefore indicates either an imperfect figure-tracing or imperfect quality of the source figure.

Limitations lie in the figure-reading part: we are not able to use censoring marks. Many figures draw them, and they carry exactly the information determining IPDs. The K–M curve and the risk table determine how many patients
are censored between two events but not when. Authors and journals draw these marks in
different ways, and adjacent marks merge into one, so the vision-language models available to
us cannot yet read them reliably. If a highly capable large language model could read the marks, then it would boost the algorithm on efficiency: with the censoring times given, the events in each interval would follow
deterministically from the steps of the curve, so the search for $\tilde{c}_{k}$ would no longer be needed. Under perfect digitization
it would recover the original true patient data themselves, up to tied events
(Supplementary material); a partial reading, which separates some marks and only
counts the others, would already narrow the admissible set. More broadly, what limits
StochIKM is the vision of the model that reads the figure. Given how quickly vision-language models have improved, we expect this

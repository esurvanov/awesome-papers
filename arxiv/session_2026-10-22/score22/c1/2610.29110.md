## ABSTRACT
Merging different models into a single model is highly desirable for multi-task deployment, particularly in regulated domains like healthcare. In such settings, models are often locally fine-tuned due to strict data privacy policies, necessitating that aggregation operates exclusively on the fine-tuned weights. Existing methods apply a uniform merge across all tasks, ignoring the geometric relationships among their learned subspaces. When task updates occupy misaligned subspaces, projecting them onto a shared low-rank basis discards task-relevant directions, a failure mode named \emph{subspace interference}. Through this study, we show that the per-task projection error of a merged model is governed by the principal-angle alignment between each task and the shared subspace, so that grouping geometrically compatible tasks provably reduces this error within each group. Motivated by this observation, we propose \textbf{Group-wise Rank-Aware Model Merging (GRAM)}, which uses the principal-angle geometry of task vectors to decide which tasks should be merged together and then performs a standard SVD-based merge within each coalition; the pipeline requires only the fine-tuned weights at merge time. With only one additional model ($\nK{=}2$), GRAM significantly mitigates subspace interference and consistently outperforms prior merging methods across language understanding (GLUE with Flan-T5), 8-task CLIP vision, and medical instruction tuning (Qwen2-7B-Instruct with four English/Chinese medical LoRAs).

## 1 Introduction

Figure 1: How tasks are grouped on the Grassmannian governs merging quality.
Insets: three partitions of the per-task low-rank subspaces visualised on the Grassmannian—all tasks merged into a single global coalition (top, low intra-coalition similarity), three groups (middle), and the two-group GRAM partition (bottom, high intra-coalition similarity).

Model merging (Wortsman et al., 2022; Ilharco et al., 2022; Tang et al., 2023) aggregates multiple fine-tuned, task-specific models into a single deployable model without the need for additional training. This paradigm is highly attractive for multi-task deployment, as it circumvents the substantial costs of storing and serving independent models. The structured nature of weight updates—particularly in parameter-efficient fine-tuning (e.g., LoRA (Hu et al., 2022))—makes model merging highly effective. This approach is especially critical in privacy-constrained domains such as healthcare, where institutions independently fine-tune a shared backbone on local clinical data. Because data sharing across sites is strictly restricted, any unified multi-institution model must be constructed solely from the fine-tuned weights, precluding the use of the original training data.

Prior work on model merging spans parameter-space arithmetic with conflict resolution (Ilharco et al., 2022; Yadav et al., 2023), auxiliary signal coefficient learning (Yang et al., 2023; Tang et al., 2023), and structured approximations exploiting low-rank or layer-wise properties (Gargiulo et al., 2025; Stoica et al., 2024; Marczak et al., 2025; Wei et al., 2025; Sun et al., 2025; Chen et al., 2025a). A pervasive limitation of these methods is their reliance on a uniform global merge: all tasks are compressed into a single shared space. Consequently, tasks whose learned parameter directions are least compatible with the majority suffer from disproportionate performance degradation—a phenomenon we term subspace interference. Furthermore, many existing techniques require access to task-specific calibration data or activations, rendering them inapplicable in strict privacy-preserving deployments where data cannot leave its source institution.

A critical signal overlooked by current methods is the inter-task geometry of the learned parameter subspaces. Existing techniques process updates individually or globally, systematically neglecting the pairwise geometric relationships between tasks. As illustrated in Figure 1, subspace similarity is highly predictive of merging compatibility: grouping tasks with high intra-coalition subspace similarity simultaneously increases merged-model accuracy and minimizes projection error. We observe distinct geometric signatures—such as cluster-outlier structures in GLUE tasks and block-diagonal affinities in vision tasks (appendix D). Crucially, the outlier tasks are precisely those that disproportionately degrade standard global SVD merging, and thus benefit the most from group-wise isolation (section 5). Subspace geometry therefore carries predictive information that current methods fail to exploit.

Motivated by this geometric insight, we propose Group-wise Rank-Aware Model Merging (GRAM). GRAM targets the task-aware deployment setting, where task identity is known at inference. We reframe the merging objective from compressing $T$ models into a single model, to compressing $T$ models into $K\ll T$ models, trading a marginal increase in storage for a targeted, significant reduction in subspace interference. Crucially, GRAM is entirely data-free: the pipeline operates exclusively on the fine-tuned weights, requiring no source data, activations, or calibration sets at merge time. Our primary contributions are as follows:

1. 

A Geometry-Aware Merging Algorithm: We introduce GRAM, which partitions tasks using Grassmannian $k$ -means++ on chordal distances. Within each coalition, it dynamically merges models via a greedy adaptive rank allocator that pools singular values across members, achieving provable optimality under a global rank budget.

2. 

Theoretical Guarantees: We rigorously bound the projection error of our clustering objective. We prove that the resulting partition yields a merge error within $O(\log K)$ of the optimal partition, and its expected error is strictly bounded below that of a standard global merge.

3. 

State-of-the-Art Data-Free Performance: Extensive evaluations across language understanding, vision, and medical instruction-tuning demonstrate that GRAM consistently outperforms existing data-free baselines. Notably, on the medical benchmark, GRAM surpasses even data-dependent methods (e.g., RegMean, Fisher-Merge) that rely on per-task calibration data.

## 2 Related Work

## Model Merging.

Model merging combines independently fine-tuned parameters into a single deployable model
without retraining. Parameter-level methods average fine-tuned weights or arithmetically
combine task vectors, sometimes with sparsification or sign/magnitude-based conflict resolution
(Wortsman et al., 2022; Ilharco et al., 2022; Yadav et al., 2023; Yu et al., 2024). Coefficient-learning
methods learn per-task or per-layer mixing weights from auxiliary signals or coordinate-wise
masks (Yang et al., 2023; Tang et al., 2023). Structured-update methods exploit the
low-rank or layered structure of fine-tuning updates, operating in the singular-value or
per-layer domain to reduce inter-task interference
(Gargiulo et al., 2025; Stoica et al., 2024; Marczak et al., 2025; Chen et al., 2025b; Wei et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2024b; Sun et al., 2025; Fu et al., 2025). Multi-model
approaches retain some per-task structure rather than collapsing to a single shared model
(Huang et al., 2024). A common thread across these single-model approaches is that all tasks
participate in the same global merge, implicitly assuming sufficient subspace alignment—an
assumption that fails when tasks span diverse domains. We depart from it by measuring
inter-task subspace geometry directly and merging within geometrically compatible groups.

## Task Grouping in Multi-Task Learning.

Deciding which tasks should be trained together has a long history in multi-task learning.
Zamir et al. (2018) computationally map transfer relationships across 26 visual tasks, and
Standley et al. (2020) formalize the grouping problem and search over partitions to trade
accuracy against compute. Task2Vec (Achille et al., 2019) represents each task by a
Fisher-information vector, while branched networks (Vandenhende et al., 2019) and
mixture-of-experts models (Chen et al., 2023) realize grouping through the architecture
itself. Affinity-based methods avoid combinatorial search:
TAG (Fifty et al., 2021) estimates inter-task affinity from gradient signals in a single
training run, MTG-Net (Song et al., 2022) meta-learns a predictor of per-task gains across
task combinations, Grad-TAG (Li et al., 2024) scales affinity estimation via gradient
linearization, and Wang et al. (2024a) cast grouping as a mathematical program with
theoretical guarantees. Complementary work on dynamic loss weighting (Liu et al., 2022)
modulates task competition during training rather than partitioning tasks explicitly. Dynamic client cooperation (Yang et al., 2026)
mitigates interference through adaptive coalition formation rather than explicitly partitioning tasks.
All of these approaches require training-time data and gradients, making them inapplicable to
the post-hoc merging setting where only frozen weights are available. Our grouping criterion is
data-free: it uses only the left singular subspaces of the task-specific weight updates.

## 3 Preliminaries

## Task vectors.

We consider a pre-trained model with $L$ layers and weights
 $W_{0}=\{W_{0}^{(\ell)}\}_{\ell=1}^{L}$ . After adapting the model to task
 $i\in\{1,\dots,T\}$ , we obtain fine-tuned weights $W_{i}$ . Following the
task-vector view of model merging (Ilharco et al., 2022; Yadav et al., 2023; Yu et al., 2024),
the adaptation is summarized by the per-layer task vector

 $\Delta W_{i}^{(\ell)}\;=\;W_{i}^{(\ell)}-W_{0}^{(\ell)}\;\in\;\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathrm{out}}\times d_{\mathrm{in}}},$ 

(1)

i.e., the difference between the task-adapted and pre-trained weights at layer
 $\ell$ . The matrix formulation in (1) applies to linear
layers — MLP blocks and attention projection matrices — which covers all merged
parameters in our experiments: the LoRA settings adapt only linear projections by
construction, and in the fully fine-tuned vision setting SVD-based merging is
applied to all 2-D weight matrices while the remaining non-matrix parameters
(LayerNorm gains, biases) are merged by simple averaging (section D.6).
For models tuned with Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA) (Hu et al., 2022), the
task vector inherits a rank- $r$ factorization

 $\tau_{i}^{(\ell)}\;=\;B_{i}^{(\ell)}A_{i}^{(\ell)},\qquad B_{i}^{(\ell)}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathrm{out}}\times r},\;A_{i}^{(\ell)}\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times d_{\mathrm{in}}},\;r\ll\min(d_{\mathrm{out}},d_{\mathrm{in}}).$ 

(2)

## SVD of task updates.

The compact SVD of each update is

 $\Delta W_{i}^{(\ell)}=U_{i}^{(\ell)}\,\Sigma_{i}^{(\ell)}\,V_{i}^{(\ell)\top},$ 

(3)

where $U_{i}^{(\ell)}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathrm{out}}\times r}$ contains the left singular vectors (output subspace),
 $\Sigma_{i}^{(\ell)}\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times r}$ is a diagonal matrix of singular values
 $\sigma_{1}\geq\cdots\geq\sigma_{r}>0$ ,
and $V_{i}^{(\ell)}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathrm{in}}\times r}$ contains the right singular vectors (input subspace).
We drop the layer index $(\ell)$ when the context is unambiguous.

## Merging objective.

Given $T$ task vectors, each of rank $r$ across $L$ layers,
the goal is to produce $K<T$ merged task vectors that minimize the total
reconstruction error across all tasks subject to a per-merge rank budget:

 $\min_{\text{partition},\;\{\widetilde{\Delta W}_{k}\}_{k=1}^{K}}\;\sum_{i=1}^{T}\left\|\Delta W_{i}-\widetilde{\Delta W}_{\pi(i)}\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}\quad\text{s.t.}\quad\rank(\widetilde{\Delta W}_{k})\leq r\;\;\text{for all}\;k\in\{1,\dots,K\},$ 

(4)

where $\pi(i)\in\{1,\dots,K\}$ assigns task $i$ to its group $G_{\pi(i)}$ and
 $\widetilde{\Delta W}_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathrm{out}}\times d_{\mathrm{in}}}$ is the merged update for group $G_{k}$ . The model
deployed for group $G_{k}$ is, per layer,

 $W_{G_{k}}^{(\ell)}\;=\;W_{0}^{(\ell)}+\widetilde{\Delta W}_{k}^{(\ell)},$ 

(5)

i.e., each of the $K$ deployed models is the pre-trained backbone plus its
group’s merged task vector, and task $i$ is served by the model of group
 $G_{\pi(i)}$ .

## Subspace interference.

Let $B\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathrm{out}}\times r}$ be a shared output basis with orthonormal columns ( $B^{\top}B=I_{r}$ ). Substituting the compact SVD $\Delta W_{i}=U_{i}\Sigma_{i}V_{i}^{\top}$ , the projection error of task $i$ onto $\mathrm{col}(B)$ is

 $\mathcal{E}_{i}\;=\;\left\|(I-BB^{\top})\,\Delta W_{i}\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}\;=\;\left\|(I-BB^{\top})\,U_{i}\Sigma_{i}\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2},$ 

(6)

We adopt $\mathcal{E}_{i}$ as a principled measure of inter-task interference as it directly measures the task-relevant information lost when updates are forced into a shared subspace. Crucially, $\mathcal{E}_{i}$ depends only on $U_{i}$ and $\Sigma_{i}$ — not on $V_{i}$ — so partitioning by output-subspace alignment captures the interference signal a shared basis can control.

To isolate the geometric contribution of subspace misalignment from the magnitude of
the task update, we drop the singular-value weighting and analyze
 $\left\|(I-BB^{\top})\,U_{i}\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}$ . Every column of $U_{i}$ admits a unique orthogonal
decomposition into a component in $\text{col}(B)$ and a component in $\text{col}(B)^{\perp}$ .
Applied column-wise,

 $U_{i}\;=\;\underbrace{BB^{\top}U_{i}}_{\in\,\text{col}(B)}\;+\;\underbrace{(I-BB^{\top})\,U_{i}}_{\in\,\text{col}(B)^{\perp}}.$ 

The two summands live in orthogonal subspaces, so by the Pythagorean theorem (in the
Frobenius inner product),
 $\left\|U_{i}\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}=\left\|BB^{\top}U_{i}\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}+\left\|(I-BB^{\top})\,U_{i}\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}$ . Since $B$ has
orthonormal columns, $\left\|BB^{\top}U_{i}\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}=\left\|B^{\top}U_{i}\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}$ , and
 $\left\|U_{i}\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}=r$ . Rearranging,

 $\left\|(I-BB^{\top})\,U_{i}\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}\;=\;r-\left\|B^{\top}U_{i}\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}.$ 

(7)

Let $\theta_{1},\dots,\theta_{r}$ denote the principal angles between $\text{col}(U_{i})$ 
and $\text{col}(B)$ ; throughout the paper we reserve $\theta$ for principal angles
and $k$ for merge groups (model weights are denoted $W$ ), and index principal
angles by $j$ . A standard
result (Golub and Van Loan, 1996) states that the
singular values of $B^{\top}U_{i}$ are exactly $\{\cos\theta_{j}\}_{j=1}^{r}$ , so
 $\left\|B^{\top}U_{i}\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}=\sum_{j=1}^{r}\cos^{2}\theta_{j}$ . Substituting into
(7) and using $\sin^{2}\theta_{j}+\cos^{2}\theta_{j}=1$ ,

 $\left\|(I-BB^{\top})\,U_{i}\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}\;=\;r-\sum_{j=1}^{r}\cos^{2}\theta_{j}\;=\;\sum_{j=1}^{r}\sin^{2}\theta_{j},$ 

(8)

which is the squared chordal (projection F-) distance between the two subspaces. The
expression depends only on the relative geometry of $\text{col}(U_{i})$ and
 $\text{col}(B)$ and is invariant to the choice of basis. When the subspaces are
well-aligned ( $\theta_{j}\approx 0$ for all $j$ ), the projection error vanishes. When
they are misaligned, important task directions are discarded by the merge. This motivates grouping tasks by
(singular-value-weighted) subspace compatibility before merging.

## 4 Method: Group-wise Rank-Aware Model Merging

Figure 2: The GRAM pipeline. Each task vector $\Delta W_{i}$ is decomposed via SVD to extract its top- $r$ left- and right-singular subspaces $(U_{i},V_{i})$ (left). The collection of $U_{i}$ ’s is fed into a subspace clustering step that partitions the $T$ tasks into $K$ coalitions by chordal distance (middle). Within each coalition, the selected top- $k_{i}$ singular components from member tasks are concatenated and re-orthonormalized via thin SVD to yield a single rank- $r$ merged update (right).

Our goal is to merge $T$ task vectors into $K\ll T$ per-group updates in a way that protects geometrically incompatible tasks from mutual interference.
To accomplish this, we decompose the pipeline (fig. 2) into three stages.
(1) Quantifying pairwise subspace geometry.
We compute the pairwise chordal distance between task output subspaces.
This quantity directly governs the projection error a shared basis would incur (Lemma B.3),
so it is the natural input for the clustering step rather than coordinate-wise distances on flattened parameters.
(2) Partitioning tasks into geometrically coherent coalitions.
Treating each task’s projection matrix $P_{i}=U_{i}U_{i}^{\top}$ as a point in Euclidean space, we run Grassmannian $k$ -means++ on the chordal-distance matrix to obtain $K$ coalitions with low within-group and high between-group subspace distance, importing the $O(\log K)$ approximation guarantee of $k$ -means++ into the Grassmannian setting.
(3) Per-coalition low-rank merge.
Within each coalition we concatenate the per-task top- $k_{i}$ singular triples under an adaptive rank allocator that distributes the rank budget to minimize the reconstruction error,
then orthonormalize via a thin SVD to produce a single rank- $r$ merged update per coalition.

## LIMITATIONS SECTION
Our results have several scope limits: (i) single-seed reporting, so small Flan-T5-Large margins should be read with caution; (ii) one model family per modality (Flan-T5 for NLU, CLIP for vision, Qwen2 for medical), and the medical evaluation is a case study on public benchmarks rather than a real federated experiment from multiple sites; (iii) the theory analyzes idealized objects (optimal basis, Grassmannian $k$ -means objective) rather than GRAM’s exact column-stacked merge with adaptive allocation, so it should be read as geometric motivation rather than end-to-end optimality guarantee.

## CONCLUSION
GRAM partitions tasks by chordal subspace distance via Grassmannian $k$ -means++ and merges within each group with a standard SVD-based procedure, avoiding the projection loss of global merging on misaligned subspaces. On GLUE with Flan-T5-Base/Large, GRAM ( $K{=}2$ ) reaches 83.8%/88.7% average, matching the strongest baseline at the cost of one extra deployed model. On 8-task vision (CLIP ViT-B/32, ViT-L/14), GRAM reaches 88.7%/94.0%, the highest among compared methods. On the medical benchmark (Qwen2-7B-Instruct with four LoRA experts), GRAM attains the best Avg Rank without using any per-task calibration data. Gains concentrate on tasks whose subspaces are most dissimilar from the majority—precisely the tasks that the alignment penalty of Lemma B.3 predicts will benefit most from grouping.

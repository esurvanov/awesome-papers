## ABSTRACT
Decision models return normalized probability distributions over candidate choices, which downstream applications can use to select among choices. Although their providers emphasize the reliability of these probabilities, calibration on tasks with a single correct choice does not establish their reliability when multiple choices are correct. We evaluate three decision models (Jev, Liquid d1, and GPT-6 Luna Decisions) on three diverse datasets (MetaTool, HotpotQA, and ECtHR) to study probability allocation and thresholding. Even when models rank every correct choice above every distractor, in 28% to 50% of those lists, at least one correct choice receives a probability below the uniform probability of 1/n, where n is the number of choices. We also present each model with five identical copies of a correct choice and observe probability disparities, with mean gaps ranging from 17 to 79 percentage points. We further show how these allocation disparities affect the recovery of multiple correct choices under probability thresholding. Decision-model probabilities can rank correct choices, but a probability threshold does not identify the set of correct choices.

Decision models are emerging as an alternative to generative language models for applications requiring structured decisions, offering lower inference costs and latency by returning predictions directly rather than generating text (Almeida, 2026; Liquid AI, 2026a). Three recently introduced models are TypeSafe AI’s Jev (Almeida, 2026), Liquid AI’s d1 (Liquid AI, 2026a), and OpenAI’s GPT-6 Luna Decisions (OpenAI, 2026). Despite differences in their implementations, these models expose a similar choice-based interface. Given contextual information, instructions, and a set of candidate choices, they return a probability distribution over the choices, with probabilities summing to one. Their providers emphasize the reliability and practical utility of these outputs. TypeSafe states that "Higher probability should correspond to a greater chance that the answer is correct" (TypeSafe AI, 2026a), while Liquid AI describes its decision models as returning "calibrated probabilities across a fixed set of outcomes" (Liquid AI, 2026b). OpenAI recommends setting probability thresholds for downstream routing, filtering, and review (OpenAI, 2026), and TypeSafe demonstrates using such thresholds to select additional correct choices beyond the highest-ranked choice (TypeSafe AI, 2026b). These claims and recommendations raise a question for tasks with multiple correct choices. Can normalized Choice probabilities reliably identify all correct choices, rather than only the highest-ranked one?

While categorical distributions must output probabilities that sum to 1, providers document that these are calibrated and recommend thresholds in practice. Since probabilities are a share of the total of 1, with more correct choices, the share of each correct choice goes down on average. However, since thresholding is the recommended way to use these models, for multiple correct choices, each correct choice must have a baseline probability. The constraint still allows an even split. Two correct choices in a list of 10 can each receive 0.5, and five identical copies can each receive 0.2. We hence study the allocation of unit probability over choices passed to the model.

Consider the HotpotQA question “Who is older, Maria Sharapova or Chanda Rubin?” (Yang et al., 2018). The question is accompanied by 10 candidate passages. One of those passages gives Rubin’s date of birth and another gives Sharapova’s. Both passages together are sufficient, but neither is sufficient alone. However, all three decision models assign a probability higher than 0.90 to Rubin’s passage and a probability lower than 0.10 to Sharapova’s passage. The Choice probabilities therefore concentrate on one of the two passages needed to answer the question. Table 1 shows the question, the two relevant passages and the probabilities. After replacing the names, tournaments, dates and locations with invented ones, the Choice probabilities remain uneven. Jev and Liquid d1 still favor the older player’s passage, but GPT-6 Luna Decisions now favors the younger player’s passage. Appendix A lists the full set of original and anonymized passages, along with the probabilities assigned by each model.

Recent work on decision models has measured accuracy and calibration on datasets with one correct choice (Porcedda, 2026). Deußer et al. (2026) evaluate Jev on 37 datasets and report that its Choice probabilities are calibrated on their tasks. They also find that rotating choices leaves aggregate accuracy unchanged. On yes/no questions, Jev’s probabilities rank well but are poorly placed relative to a threshold of 0.5. Liu (2026) finds that Jev gives unequal probabilities to the six faces of a fair die. Both studies test Jev only. Li et al. (2026) compare Jev’s answers to logically related yes/no and Choice questions and find differences between probabilities for events that should agree. TypeSafe states that the order of choices can affect Jev’s answer (TypeSafe AI, 2026d). Li and Chen (2026) find that restricting and renormalizing the original probabilities predicts aggregate accuracy after choices are removed. For generative language models, Zheng et al. (2024); Pezeshkpour and Hruschka (2024) show that choice order changes answers to multiple-choice questions. Blankenstein et al. (2026) find that language models selecting among functionally equivalent tools can favour particular providers. Wang et al. (2025) find that LLMs can select an offered answer even when none is correct. However, modern AI tasks require selecting more than one correct choice. Examples include selecting the tools an agent needs to complete a task, the diagnoses supported by a medical record, the passages relevant to a query in retrieval-augmented generation, and the vulnerability types present in code.

In this paper, we measure how decision models distribute probability over several correct choices. We specifically examine the gap between the maximum and minimum probabilities assigned to correct choices. We first evaluate the models on the choice lists in the datasets. We then modify the datasets’ choice lists while keeping the query and the instruction fixed to present five identical copies of a correct choice. Identical copies differ only in their place in the list and their random IDs, so they should get the same probability. We also check calibration on yes/no questions and observe miscalibration on intermediate probabilities. We study their behavior on three datasets, MetaTool (Huang et al., 2024), ECtHR (Chalkidis et al., 2022), and HotpotQA (Yang et al., 2018).

Our results show that decision-model probabilities are not reliable for identifying multiple correct choices. When a list contains more than one correct choice, the mean gap between the highest and the lowest probabilities assigned to correct choices ranges from 55 to 74 percentage points across the three models and three datasets. A uniform distribution over $n$ choices assigns $1/n$ to each, representing no preference among them. The models rank every correct choice above every distractor in 52% to 85% of the lists. In 28% to 50% of those lists, a correct choice receives less than $1/n$ . Further, even when the models rank every correct choice above every distractor, the best single threshold gives the exact set in only 64% to 78% of those examples, and that threshold is low, ranging between 0.02 and 0.08, against the 0.25 that TypeSafe uses as an example in its documentation (Section 3).

The allocation of probabilities is uneven even when a list contains five identical copies of a correct choice. The mean gap between the highest and lowest (identical) copies ranges from 17 to 79 percentage points.
For lists containing exactly one correct choice, Jev’s ECE is 8 percentage points on MetaTool and 3 on ECtHR, consistent with Deußer et al. (2026). When multiple choices are correct, normalized Choice probabilities cannot be interpreted as independent probabilities of correctness. On yes/no questions about each choice, the ECE is 3 to 9 percentage points over all bins and 8 to 18 percentage points without the bin from 0 to 0.1.

## Models.

We study three decision models. We call Jev (jev-1.13.0) (Almeida, 2026) through TypeSafe’s API, and Liquid d1 (liquid/d1) (Liquid AI, 2026a) and GPT-6 Luna Decisions (openai/gpt-6-luna-decisions) (OpenAI, 2026) through Vercel AI Gateway (Vercel, 2026). Liquid AI and OpenAI publish no version for these two models, and our results refer to the models that the gateway served in October 2026. A request holds a query, an instruction and a list of named choices, each with a description. The answer holds the selected choice, which we call the prediction, and one probability per choice. These Choice probabilities sum to 1 (TypeSafe AI, 2026b). We call the highest probability of an answer its confidence. This is not the confidence field of Jev’s answer, which TypeSafe computes from the highest probability and the number of choices (TypeSafe AI, 2026c). Jev and GPT-6 Luna Decisions return probabilities with two decimals, and Liquid d1 with full precision. GPT-6 Luna Decisions can refuse a question and return no probabilities. Jev and Liquid d1 refused none. The three models received the same requests, and we sent each request once. Table 20 gives the number of examples and lists of each kind.

## Datasets.

We chose three datasets from different domains, all of which allow multiple correct choices, or have a subset of examples that do. MetaTool (Huang et al., 2024) is a tool selection task. The model sees the query and a list of tools and is asked to select the tools needed to solve the query. Its four tool selection subsets hold 4,287 queries. Of these, 995 have one correct tool among tools with similar descriptions, 1,800 have one correct tool among the tools of a scenario, 995 have no correct tool, and 497 have two correct tools. Some queries appear in more than one subset, and the answer to a repeated request counts once for each example.

The distractor setting of HotpotQA (Yang et al., 2018) gives a query and up to ten passages, two of which support the answer. While HotpotQA contains training, validation and test sets, we only use the validation set in this study.
ECtHR, Task A of LexGLUE (Chalkidis et al., 2022), is a legal multi-label classification task. Each example
gives the facts of a case and ten articles of the Convention and it asks the model to select the articles that the case violates. Of its 1,000 test cases, 655 have one violated article, 192 have two to four, and 153 have none. When several choices are correct, the instruction asks for all of them, for example “Choose every passage in the list that supports the answer to the question.” Table 2 gives the number of examples of each kind of list.

## Identical choices.

In our first experiment, for every example, we send five identical copies of one of its correct choices. The query and the instruction remain unchanged. Exactly identical copies are rejected by the APIs, hence we name each copy with a random ID. Identical copies are equally correct and equally relevant to the query, so a difference between their probabilities cannot come from their content. To place every copy once at every position, we send each list in its five cyclic permutations (Zheng et al., 2024; Pezeshkpour and Hruschka, 2024).

We build these lists for 3,292 MetaTool queries, a seeded sample of 1,000 HotpotQA questions and the 847 ECtHR cases with a violated article, 25,695 lists per model. We report the mean probability of the highest and the lowest copy, the gap between them, and the share of predictions at each position. Under position-invariance and uniformity, we would expect the mean probability of each copy to be 0.20, and the gap to be 0.00.

## The gap between correct choices.

Next, we send the lists as the datasets give them. MetaTool gives 497 queries with two correct tools, and HotpotQA its questions. We send both in their released order. ECtHR gives 192 cases with two to four violated articles. We send each list in its ten cyclic permutations. For each answer, the gap is the highest minus the lowest probability given to a correct choice, and we report its mean. As a reference we use the uniform distribution, which gives each of $n$ choices $1/n$ and prefers none of them. We report the share of lists in which at least one correct choice gets less than $1/n$ . To separate this from ranking errors, we also report the share of lists in which every correct choice is above every distractor, and, among those lists, the share in which a correct choice still gets less than $1/n$ . These counts use only the released order of the ECtHR lists and leave out the 21 HotpotQA questions whose list has no distractor.

## Calibration.

A model is calibrated when, among the answers to which it gives a probability $p$ , a share $p$ is correct. For lists with exactly one correct choice, we define confidence as the highest probability assigned to any choice. For lists with several correct choices, this definition does not directly apply. We therefore convert each list into several yes/no questions, one per choice, based on the benchmarks’ format. For example, for HotpotQA we ask “Does this passage support the answer to the question?” for every (question, passage) pair. The models respond with a single probability of “yes”. We report the Expected Calibration Error (ECE) (Naeini et al., 2015; Guo et al., 2017) over ten bins of equal width, which compares the mean confidence, or the mean probability of “yes”, with the observed share of correct predictions, or of true “yes” labels, in each bin. For the yes/no questions, we also report the ECE without the bin from 0 to 0.1.

## Selecting by a threshold.

Model providers suggest using a threshold (OpenAI, 2026), and TypeSafe uses one to select multiple correct choices (TypeSafe AI, 2026b). For example, TypeSafe’s documentation includes an example with three choices, two of which are correct, and uses a threshold of 0.25. A threshold $t$ selects every choice whose probability is at or above $t$ . In this experiment, we study whether there is a common threshold that selects all correct choices and no distractors across all examples in the dataset.

We start with examples answered both as lists and as yes/no questions, vary $t$ from 0 to 1, and compare the two formats. We define exact set accuracy as the share of examples in which every correct choice is selected and every distractor is rejected. We define recall as the proportion of correct choices above the threshold, and precision as the proportion of choices above the threshold that are correct. We compute precision, recall, and F1 from counts pooled across all examples. We plot exact set accuracy as a function of the threshold $t$ from 0 to 1, and precision, recall and F1 are reported in Appendix H.

## LIMITATIONS SECTION
We study three decision models, Jev, Liquid d1 and GPT-6 Luna Decisions, through their hosted APIs. Generative language models that select a choice in text are outside the scope of this study. We use three English datasets, MetaTool, HotpotQA and ECtHR, and take the labels of each dataset as the set of correct choices. We study selection by one threshold for all examples of a dataset and by one yes/no question per choice. Other tasks with several correct choices, such as the diagnoses supported by a medical record or the vulnerability types present in code, and other selection rules, such as a threshold per example or a fixed number of selected choices, are left to future work.

## CONCLUSION
When a list has several correct choices, decision models concentrate probability on one of them, even when every correct choice ranks above every distractor. Identical copies of a correct choice receive different probabilities and show model- and dataset-dependent position bias. Even with thresholds optimized on the evaluation data, exact set accuracy remains below the share of examples in which the models rank every correct choice above every distractor. Asking separate yes/no questions for each choice lowers this accuracy for 8 of the 9 models and datasets, and the resulting intermediate probabilities are miscalibrated. Choice probabilities can rank choices, but they should not be interpreted as the probability that each choice is correct.

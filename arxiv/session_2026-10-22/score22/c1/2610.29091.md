## BODY (first 18k chars)
Abstract

Objective. To distinguish the contributions of acquisition context and photoplethysmography (PPG) to session-calibrated blood pressure (BP) tracking.

Approach. The original comparison used three repeats of five-fold participant-level cross-validation in 653 AuroraBP participants, excluding calibration-labelled prediction targets. Matched analyses separated context and PPG and removed elapsed-time terms. Later exploratory follow-ups retained PPG, accelerometer signals and three initial cuff readings. Separate studies evaluated pulse-rate fusion and an intraoperative VitalDB model.

Main results. The original wearable-state model achieved participant-level mean absolute errors (MAEs) of $7.697$ / $6.139$ mmHg for systolic/diastolic BP (SBP/DBP). Added context reduced MAE by $0.436$ / $0.126$ mmHg (97.5% intervals $0.305$ – $0.573$ / $0.053$ – $0.200$ ). In the matched factorial without the external PPG prior, PPG improved on calibration, time and protocol-provided posture/activity by $0.214$ / $0.135$ mmHg; removing time left the DBP interval including zero. Standard PPG descriptors contributed, but additional nonlinear/attractor benefit was not established. In a separate follow-up series, a sensor-history ensemble achieved $7.299$ / $5.445$ mmHg versus its static-rich reference’s $7.363$ / $5.470$ , small gains of $0.064$ / $0.025$ mmHg. Larger DBP errors did not improve uniformly. Quality-weighted fusion reduced pulse-rate MAE from $3.985$ to $3.690$ beats per minute. VitalDB MAEs remained $15.375$ / $8.833$ mmHg despite small PPG gains.

Significance. The contribution is a calibrated evaluation framework with explicit controls, reproducible feature-cache-to-results analyses and retained negative findings. The sensor-history gain is exploratory after repeated dataset reuse. Independently estimated wearable states, complete raw-signal reproduction of the supplied richer features, external transfer and clinical adequacy remain unestablished.

Keywords: photoplethysmography; cuffless blood pressure; session calibration; participant-level cross-validation; wearable sensing; data leakage; signal quality.

## 1 Introduction

## 1.1 Motivation

Accurate blood pressure (BP) measurement is essential for identifying and managing hypertension. More frequent monitoring could characterize changes outside intermittent measurements, but repeated cuff inflation creates practical burdens. Optical sensing motivates research into less intrusive tracking; its potential clinical value depends on accuracy during changing activity and posture and on stability after calibration. Current guidance identifies important gaps in cuffless-device validation and clinical interpretation (Cohen et al., 2026; Elgendi et al., 2024). A clinically relevant first question is whether PPG contributes information about subsequent BP changes beyond the initial cuff anchor and acquisition context. This retrospective study examines that question within controlled recording protocols; it does not evaluate treatment decisions or clinical outcomes.

Participant overlap, calibration burden and protocol shortcuts can obscure this question. Record-level splits can inflate accuracy when correlated measurements from one participant enter both training and testing (González et al., 2023). Three initial cuff readings also represent a different calibration burden from one reading or repeated access to later reference values. Pigmentation-related optical differences motivate intervention testing, but small subgroups and nonsignificant contrasts cannot establish equitable performance (Kostrna et al., 2026; Singh et al., 2024; Osorio-Sanchez et al., 2025).
We audit anchoring, context, and pigmentation-related interventions using recorded Fitzpatrick labels. Candidate interventions and context arms were frozen before repeated participant-level validation, after earlier development on AuroraBP. Because a fixed holdout was inspected across successive ablations, it is retained as historical development evidence. The primary repeated analysis confines feature ranking and fitting to outer training participants but remains internal validation. Secondary negative results are reported without interpreting them as proof of equity, equivalence, or general failure of pigmentation-aware modeling.

## 1.2 Study Questions and Contributions

The primary retrospective question is whether wearable-state context improves calibrated SBP/DBP tracking beyond the shared model core. Matched extensions isolate PPG/context and remove time. Secondary analyses examine calibration dose, nonlinear augmentation and pigmentation interventions; historical results quantify dependence on recent-cuff anchoring. Independent pulse-fusion and separately fitted VitalDB studies answer distinct questions. A later PPG/accelerometer series tests median prediction, direct BP targets and sensor history under the same three-reading burden. Negative findings are retained. Hashes, environments, folds and numerical checks support computational reproduction, while repeated dataset reuse precludes prospective confirmation.

## 2 Related Work

## 2.1 PPG-Based Cuffless Blood Pressure Estimation

Cuffless BP estimation from PPG includes pulse-transit-time methods, waveform features, and end-to-end networks (Elgendi et al., 2019; Mukkamala et al., 2015). Calibration-free methods avoid subject-specific references, whereas calibrated approaches use one or more cuff readings to model subsequent change (Samimi and Dajani, 2023; Wang et al., 2023; Oladunni and Adewumi, 2026b). Evaluation guidance emphasizes participant separation, calibration burden, and careful use of AAMI/ISO 81060-2 and BHS criteria (Elgendi et al., 2024).

## 2.2 Nonlinear and Information-Theoretic Feature Representations

The feature bank combines waveform moments with delay-coordinate reconstruction, recurrence analysis, sample entropy, permutation entropy, and spectral measures (Takens, 1981; Eckmann et al., 1987; Marwan et al., 2007; Richman and Moorman, 2000; Bandt and Pompe, 2002). These established descriptors have been applied to physiological and arterial pulse signals (Allen, 2007; Hörandtner et al., 2022). Recent author-developed formulations motivated the feature grouping and terminology (Oladunni and Adewumi, 2026c; Oladunni and Adewumi, 2026b; Oladunni and Adewumi, 2026a), but are not treated as validated physiological laws. The matched ablation therefore tests the empirical contribution of the combined nonlinear/attractor family rather than using prediction as evidence for the underlying theories.

## 2.3 Skin Pigmentation and PPG-Derived Vitals

Pigmentation can affect optical measurements, motivating separate evaluation of signal quality and downstream error (Singh et al., 2024; Kostrna et al., 2026; Osorio-Sanchez et al., 2025; Al-Halawani et al., 2025). Existing field, polarization, and gain-correction studies support testing optical interventions but do not establish improved BP tracking (Dasa and Davies, 2026; Jakachira et al., 2025; MacIsaac et al., 2025). We therefore distinguish pulse extraction from BP prediction and do not interpret nonsignificant subgroup differences as parity.

## 2.4 Evaluation Rigor and Data Leakage

AuroraBP established calibration-based benchmarks and documented differences between laboratory and ambulatory performance (Mieloszyk et al., 2022a). Record- or window-level splits can inflate reported accuracy when one participant contributes to training and testing (González et al., 2023; Sanches et al., 2024), while dataset shortcuts may further obscure the information contributed by PPG (Dias et al., 2025; Mehta et al., 2024). Our audit addresses a related change-prediction artifact: a calibration-row indicator may reveal observations whose target is near zero even when participant folds are disjoint.

## 3 Framework and Audit Controls

## 3.1 Attractor Feature Bank

A sampled PPG window is embedded as $\mathbf{X}_{n}=[x_{n},x_{n+\tau},\ldots,x_{n+(d-1)\tau}]$ , with $d=4$ and $\tau=5$ samples (Takens, 1981). The eleven descriptors comprise waveform dispersion, skewness, kurtosis, sample and permutation entropy, recurrence rate, determinism, recurrence entropy, CSI, spectral entropy and a band-power quality ratio (Eckmann et al., 1987; Marwan et al., 2007; Richman and Moorman, 2000; Bandt and Pompe, 2002). Supplementary Section E gives filtering, sample limits, tolerances and the CSI equation. These are empirical descriptors, not validated physiological laws.

AuroraBP uses measured raw_* waveforms and heuristic-corrected pc_* channels. The auxiliary simulated stress-test ref_* channel is not physiological ground truth and never enters the primary AuroraBP core.

## 3.2 Pigmentation Correction Operator

The correction rescales the median-centered waveform using recorded Fitzpatrick type and blends it with Wiener filtering; it estimates neither melanin nor a Beer–Lambert parameter. Supplementary Section E specifies the complete operator and noise-dependent entropy/recurrence tolerances. Corrected-minus-raw MAE was $-0.001$ mmHg SBP (95% CI $-0.037$ – $0.034$ ) and $-0.031$ mmHg DBP ( $-0.061$ – $0.002$ ). The tested correction-and-extraction configuration has no established overall benefit or equivalence.

## 3.3 Calibration Dose and Anchoring

For participant $p$ , the arithmetic mean $b_{p}^{(k)}$ of exactly $k\in\{1,2,3\}$ initial cuff readings defines

 $\Delta_{\mathrm{session}}^{(k)}(p,i)=y_{p,i}-b_{p}^{(k)}.$ 

(1)

The historical prior-anchored alternative uses the most recent valid cuff value $y_{p,i-1}^{*}$ :

 $\Delta_{\mathrm{prior}}(p,i)=y_{p,i}-y_{p,i-1}^{*}.$ 

(2)

Primary tracking uses the initial three-reading anchor without later cuff updates. Initial-visit targets occur up to 0.728 h after that anchor; return-visit targets occur 21.051–32.853 h after it. One-session calibration therefore does not imply a horizon shorter than one hour. Historical prior anchoring can use readings only minutes old within a visit, although across-visit gaps exceed one day; approximately 84% of its reconstructed fixed-split gain came from anchor replacement. Neither scheme establishes cuff replacement. Calibration retention predicts $\widehat{\Delta}=0$ .

## 3.4 Context Operator

Wearable-state context comprises protocol-provided posture/activity and supplied optical quality. These states would require sensing or classification in deployment; they were not independently estimated here. The protocol-enriched comparator additionally uses phase, recording duration and pressure-quality metadata and is interpreted as an internal upper bound. A gradient-boosted tree model predicts BP change from the declared features and anchor. The original no-added-context arm shares a selected core that may contain elapsed-time terms, PPG descriptors and the UCI-derived PPG prior. It is therefore not a no-PPG condition. The matched factorial below supplies true optical-input-free controls.

## 3.5 Calibration-Row Leakage Audit

A calibration indicator could identify trivial rows if a target reading were compared with itself, allowing a model to learn “calibration row $\Rightarrow$ predict no change.” We used four checks:

A 

Compare $|\Delta|$ on flagged and unflagged rows.

B 

Re-score the same fitted models with flagged rows removed from the holdout only.

C 

Refit with the calibration-flag feature removed, flagged rows still scored.

D 

(Decisive) Remove the flag and exclude flagged rows from fitting and scoring.

Checks A–C each leave one channel open; Check D restricts both fitting and evaluation. The supplementary calibration-row leakage subsection reports all four.

Finding 1.

On this dataset, calibration-flagged rows are not near-zero-delta (mean $|\Delta|=6.774$ vs. $9.902$ mmHg SBP on development rows – smaller, but not trivially so), and Check D confirms a positive context gain remains under strict exclusion ( $+0.598$ mmHg SBP, 95% CI $0.337$ – $0.871$ ; $+0.557$ mmHg DBP, $0.315$ – $0.795$ ). The separate historical attribution retains calibration-labelled rows and must not be interpreted as Check D attribution (see the supplementary calibration-row leakage analysis).

## 4 Methodology

## 4.1 Datasets

AuroraBP is the primary dataset (Mieloszyk et al., 2022a; Mieloszyk et al., 2022b). It contains 1,221 participant records and 11,597 auscultatory measurements across calibration, static, exercise, and temporal-challenge phases; waveforms were resolvable for 672 participants. The primary common cohort includes 653 participants with three initial calibration readings. Excluding 1,337 later calibration-labelled rows leaves 7,879 scored measurements. Historical ablations used a fixed 501/168 split; because its holdout was inspected during development, it is treated as historical evidence rather than untouched validation.
Four secondary sources appear in the development notebooks: BIDMC, MIMIC-II, Welltory-PPG, and UCI (Pimentel et al., 2018; Pimentel et al., 2017; Goldberger et al., 2000; Saeed et al., 2011a; Saeed et al., 2011b; Neshitov et al., 2021; Kachuee et al., 2015a; Kachuee et al., 2015b). The first three form a historical corrected-feature PCA bank whose coordinates are excluded from the primary candidate list. UCI trains the BP-change prior that may enter the primary model. Pigmentation ranks on these sources are synthetic simulation inputs, not measured traits, and support no equity claim.

Two datasets support independent follow-up analyses. The Appel–Theart Participant Study Data provide paired visible and infrared PPG, Monk Skin Tone (MST), and pulse-rate references (Appel and Theart, 2025). After auditing, 108 participants contributed 630 pulse-rate windows, including 24 participants with MST 7–10. Its single cuff BP value per participant permits only cross-sectional BP analysis. VitalDB provides synchronized intraoperative PPG and arterial BP (Lee et al., 2022; Lee and Jung, 2022). A locked evaluation used 5,472 future windows from 228 cases belonging to 227 subjects disjoint from the 55 development subjects. Cases were grouped by subject for inference. VitalDB has no skin-tone field and tests a separately developed intraoperative implementation, not transfer of AuroraBP-fitted parameters.

## LIMITATIONS SECTION
Validation. AuroraBP is a controlled single-site protocol. Although initial visits are shorter than one hour, return targets occur 21.051–32.853 h after initial calibration. Participant-separated folds prevent direct participant overlap but cannot remove optimism from earlier dataset-guided choices. Appel–Theart evaluates pulse rate; VitalDB is a separate retrospective intraoperative model. Neither establishes prospective wearable BP performance or transfer of one model.

Calibration and context. Primary tracking requires three initial cuff readings. The one-reading protocol-enriched arm has higher MAE ( $8.257$ / $6.280$ mmHg); a matching wearable-state dose analysis is unavailable. Protocol annotations and enriched metadata may encode acquisition structure. The history ensemble retains time and protocol-dependent sampling; a matched time-free version and new-protocol validation are unavailable. History checks establish recording-level order, not within-window latency or real-time operation.

Pigmentation. Only 25 primary and six historical V–VI participants are represented. Auxiliary pigmentation ranks are synthetic. Null/adverse results for one correction establish neither equivalence nor impossibility of better interventions. Pulse fusion does not validate BP equity, and VitalDB has no pigmentation labels.

Inference and reproduction. Holm adjustment covers the two retrospective primary endpoints; exploratory intervals do not account for adaptive experiments, overlapping training sets or method selection. Repeated readings are handled at participant level, but agreement assumptions can fail. The DBP router’s $2.9\%$ recall precludes a safety claim. BHS/AAMI/ISO summaries are numerical context, not certification. Re-extracting all 11,427 standard AuroraBP rows reproduced the preserved extractor’s cache (relative tolerance $10^{-6}$ , absolute $10^{-10}$ ); this checks consistency, not independent algorithm validity, and excludes rich/beat extraction. The newer-runtime bridge verifies one original fold, not every fit. Complete third-party raw-to-results replication remains outstanding. Supplementary Sections A, D and E detail these boundaries.

## CONCLUSION
The primary comparison supports adding wearable-state context to its calibrated core, which already contains optical/time candidates. The matched factorial supplies complementary no-PPG controls and shows small conditional optical contributions. Removing clock terms reduces one protocol shortcut, but context still uses supplied posture/activity. No independently estimated wearable-state model has been evaluated, so annotation gains cannot establish deployment generalization.

The studies answer distinct questions. Quality weighting improved pulse-rate extraction relative to equal weighting. VitalDB showed a small incremental contribution from a separately fitted PPG implementation, combined with context through a fixed 50/50 blend. It tested neither quality-dependent blending nor transfer of AuroraBP parameters. The later sensor-history series improved MAE modestly within its own matched folds, while direct BP targets worsened error and DBP tails did not improve uniformly. These results identify conditional predictive information, not an attractor-specific mechanism or a universally generalizing architecture.

Near-zero aggregate bias coexists with broad individual errors. The historical measurement-level SBP standards comparison yielded BHS Grade C and error SD $9.718$ mmHg, exceeding the cited $8$ mmHg numerical cutoff; VitalDB MAE remained $15.375$ / $8.833$ mmHg. Neither relative gains nor exclusion of difficult predictions can establish clinical adequacy. Pigmentation interventions likewise support no fairness conclusion. The contribution is a transparent calibrated evaluation with retained negative findings, rather than demonstrated cuff replacement or clinical outcome benefit.

## ABSTRACT
Multilingual large language models are increasingly deployed in settings where visible reasoning traces support verification, education, and safety auditing, yet standard evaluations typically measure only final-answer agreement or holistic response similarity. As a result, they establish outcome consistency without determining whether users across languages receive comparable supporting evidence. We introduce a trace-level divergence framework that compares visible reasoning traces as structured sequences, decomposing agreement into structural similarity, order consistency, and holistic similarity, with pair-specific calibration to enable comparison across languages. Across three reasoning benchmarks, seven languages, and six frontier LLMs, the natural baseline---embedding each whole response and comparing the two by cosine---systematically overstates agreement: $52.1\%$--$67.3\%$ of examples in the top quartile of holistic similarity are not in the top quartile of structural similarity. This dissociation is stable across sentence encoders and step segmentations, and blind bilingual annotation studies in two English-anchored language pairs show that human judgments align with structural similarity rather than with the holistic baseline. A same-language control shows that the level of divergence is language-specific: two English traces for paraphrases of the same question agree more closely than an English trace and its counterparts in other languages. Our results show that answer-level and holistic evaluations can substantially overestimate the consistency of multilingual explanatory artifacts, motivating trace-level evaluation for reliable multilingual deployment.

## 1 Introduction

Large language models (LLMs) are increasingly deployed in settings where they produce not only final answers but also visible reasoning traces intended to justify those answers. These traces are consumed by users, educators, auditors, and process-supervision systems to understand, verify, and improve model behavior (Wei et al., 2022). As multilingual LLMs become widely deployed, an implicit expectation follows: semantically equivalent questions posed in different languages should produce not only compatible answers but also broadly comparable supporting explanations. In this paper, we study the visible chain of thought emitted by the model, not its hidden internal computation. A chain of thought need not faithfully expose the model’s internal reasoning process (Turpin et al., 2023; Lanham et al., 2023; Chen et al., 2025), and we make no claim that it does. Rather, we study the explanatory artifact that downstream users, verifiers, and chain-of-thought monitors directly observe and act upon; our claims concern what that artifact contains in each language, not whether it reflects the computation behind it.

Existing multilingual evaluations largely measure outcome consistency: whether translated prompts produce the same or correct final answer (Shi et al., 2022; Xuan et al., 2025). More recent approaches compare complete responses using holistic semantic similarity, but still collapse an entire reasoning trace into a single representation. As a result, two multilingual responses may be judged equivalent even when they expose different intermediate claims, assumptions, problem decompositions, or execution order. Some of these differences may simply reflect natural linguistic variation; others may represent substantively different supporting evidence. Existing evaluations cannot distinguish between them. We therefore argue that multilingual systems should also be evaluated for evidentiary consistency: whether users receive comparable supporting evidence for the same conclusion regardless of the language they ask in.

To address this problem, we introduce a trace-level divergence framework that compares multilingual reasoning traces as structured sequences rather than as single holistic responses. Our framework aligns step-structured chains of thought using multilingual sentence embeddings and decomposes cross-lingual trace consistency into three complementary metrics: structural similarity $S$ , measuring preservation of reasoning content; order consistency $\tau$ , measuring preservation of reasoning order; and holistic similarity $H$ , a whole-response baseline. Because multilingual sentence embeddings exhibit language-pair-dependent similarity floors and ceilings, a pair-specific calibration rescales sentence similarities onto a readable scale within each language pair before aggregation.

We evaluate the proposed framework on three reasoning benchmarks, seven languages, and six frontier LLMs spanning four model families. We find that whole-answer similarity systematically overstates agreement between multilingual reasoning traces. Across all model–dataset evaluations, holistic similarity remains consistently high ( $H=0.770$ – $0.857$ ), while structural similarity is substantially lower ( $S=0.502$ – $0.629$ ), with order consistency lower still ( $\tau=0.307$ – $0.470$ ), even when both language outputs produce the same verified-correct answer. Rank-based analyses reach the same conclusion: within each model–dataset evaluation and language pair, $52.1\%$ – $67.3\%$ of examples in the top quartile of holistic similarity are not also in the top quartile of structural similarity. Finally, blind bilingual annotation studies in two English-anchored language pairs show that human judgments of reasoning-trace equivalence align with structural similarity rather than holistic similarity, indicating that whole-response embeddings systematically overestimate agreement in the explanatory artifacts multilingual users actually observe.

Further analyses locate the divergence. In a same-language control, where each question is paraphrased in English and run through the identical pipeline, two English traces agree more closely than an English trace and its counterparts in other languages in every cell tested, although the holistic–structural dissociation itself also appears within English. Within a trace, alignment is weakest at the step stating the shared answer, and divergence grows on harder items. Throughout, we read low structural or order similarity as divergence, not failure.

Our contributions are threefold. First, we formulate multilingual evaluation as the problem of measuring the consistency of visible reasoning traces, complementing traditional outcome-based evaluation. Second, we introduce a calibrated trace-level framework that decomposes agreement into content preservation, execution order, and holistic similarity. Third, through a comprehensive empirical study, we show that answer-level and holistic evaluations systematically overstate the consistency of multilingual explanatory artifacts, motivating trace-level evaluation for reliable multilingual deployment.

## 2 Problem Formulation

We study whether multilingual LLMs produce consistent visible reasoning traces when given semantically equivalent prompts in different languages. Our goal is to evaluate two complementary notions of consistency: (i) whether multilingual prompts produce the same final answer, and (ii) whether they produce comparable supporting explanations. The former captures outcome consistency; the latter captures trace consistency. This section formalizes both notions and introduces the calibration required to compare multilingual reasoning traces across language pairs.

Setup.
Let $f_{\theta}$ denote a fixed LLM and let $\mathcal{Q}=\{q_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}$ be a set of reasoning problems specified at the level of semantic intent. For each $q_{i}$ , we collect $K$ semantic-preserving translations $\mathcal{L}_{i}=\{\ell_{i,1},\ldots,\ell_{i,K}\}$ and obtain responses

 $\displaystyle y_{i,k}$ 
 $\displaystyle=f_{\theta}(\ell_{i,k}).$ 

Each response is parsed into a final answer $\alpha_{i,k}$ and a step-structured visible reasoning trace

 $\displaystyle r_{i,k}$ 
 $\displaystyle=(s_{i,k,1},\ldots,s_{i,k,t_{i,k}}),$ 

where $t_{i,k}$ denotes the number of reasoning steps. Each step is itself a sequence of sentences,

 $\displaystyle s_{i,k,t}$ 
 $\displaystyle=(u_{i,k,t,1},\ldots,u_{i,k,t,n_{i,k,t}}),$ 

where $n_{i,k,t}$ is the number of sentences in step $t$ . Let $\alpha_{i}^{\star}$ denote the reference answer.

## 2.1 Outcome and Trace Consistency

We formalize multilingual consistency at two complementary levels. Outcome consistency asks whether translated prompts produce the same final answer. Trace consistency asks whether they produce comparable visible reasoning traces. These notions are complementary: multilingual responses may reach the same answer while exposing different supporting claims, decompositions, or execution order.

1. Outcome Consistency.
Following prior multilingual evaluation work (Qi et al., 2023; Shi et al., 2022), we define

 $\displaystyle\Delta_{\text{acc}}(q_{i})$ 
 $\displaystyle=\max_{k}\mathbf{1}[\alpha_{i,k}\neq\alpha_{i}^{\star}]$ 

 $\displaystyle\quad-\min_{k}\mathbf{1}[\alpha_{i,k}\neq\alpha_{i}^{\star}]\;\in\;\{0,1\}.$ 

(1)

Here, $\Delta_{\text{acc}}=0$ iff all language variants are uniformly correct or uniformly incorrect, and $\Delta_{\text{acc}}=1$ otherwise. While this captures multilingual agreement at the level of outcomes, it provides no information about whether the accompanying visible reasoning traces remain consistent across languages.

2. Trace Divergence.
To quantify divergence between visible reasoning traces, we define

 $\displaystyle D_{\text{trace}}(q_{i})$ 
 $\displaystyle=\frac{2}{K(K-1)}\!\!\sum_{1\leq k<k^{\prime}\leq K}\!\!d(r_{i,k},r_{i,k^{\prime}}).$ 

(2)

Rather than a single scalar, we represent trace divergence using three complementary dimensions,

 $\displaystyle d(r_{a},r_{b})$ 
 $\displaystyle=\Big(1-S(r_{a},r_{b}),\;\tfrac{1-\tau(r_{a},r_{b})}{2},$ 

 $\displaystyle\qquad\quad\;1-H(r_{a},r_{b})\Big).$ 

(3)

Thus, Eq. (2) yields a three-dimensional expectation over all language pairs. The three components capture complementary aspects of multilingual trace consistency:

• 

Structural similarity $S(\cdot,\cdot)\in[0,1]$ : preservation of step-level reasoning content across traces (§ 3.3).

• 

Order consistency $\tau(\cdot,\cdot)\in[-1,1]$ : preservation of the ordering of corresponding reasoning steps (§ 3.4).

• 

Whole-answer cosine $H(\cdot,\cdot)\in[-1,1]$ : a holistic embedding-based baseline that compares complete responses without localizing where divergence occurs (formally defined in Appendix B.6).

Raw multilingual cosines carry language-pair-specific floors and ceilings, so the sentence similarities behind $S$ and $\tau$ are calibrated per language pair before aggregation (§ 3.2, Appendix A).

## 3 Method

Given two visible reasoning traces produced for semantically equivalent prompts in different languages, our framework measures whether they preserve the same step-level content and ordering. The pipeline first parses each response into steps (§ 3.1), calibrates multilingual sentence similarities for each language pair (§ 3.2), and constructs a step-pair similarity matrix through two-level greedy alignment (§ 3.3). This matrix yields the structural similarity score $S$ and the order-consistency score $\tau$ (§ 3.4). We compute the holistic baseline $H$ separately over complete responses (Appendix B.6).

## 3.1 Step Parsing

We segment each response using line-initial step headers. The parser first matches the prompted English pattern Step $n$ :, followed by its equivalents in the seven evaluated languages, a plain numbered-list pattern, and finally paragraph boundaries. The same ordered rule set is applied to every language, and parsing terminates at the final-answer line. Each non-empty step is further divided into sentences using a Unicode-aware regular expression. No LLM is used during parsing. Appendix B.1 provides the complete parsing hierarchy and validation details.

## 3.2 Per-Pair Calibration

Raw multilingual cosine similarities $c_{a,b}(u,v)=\cos(\phi(u),\phi(v))$ , for a multilingual sentence encoder $\phi$ , exhibit language-pair-specific floors and ceilings. For each language pair $(a,b)$ we therefore estimate an unrelated-sentence floor $\widehat{\mathrm{floor}}_{a,b}$ , the mean cosine between sentences drawn from traces of different questions, and a self-translation ceiling $\widehat{\mathrm{ceiling}}_{a,b}$ , a high percentile of the cosine between trace sentences and their LLM translations into the other language (estimators and constants in Appendix B.2). Each raw sentence cosine is then rescaled as

 $\displaystyle\widetilde{c}_{a,b}(u,v)$ 
 $\displaystyle=\operatorname{clip}\!\left(\frac{c_{a,b}(u,v)-\widehat{\mathrm{floor}}_{a,b}}{\widehat{\mathrm{ceiling}}_{a,b}-\widehat{\mathrm{floor}}_{a,b}},0,1\right).$ 

(4)

Calibration aligns the empirical endpoints of each language pair, so that within a pair $0$ marks the unrelated-sentence floor and $1$ the self-translation ceiling; it does not assume that intermediate calibrated values are semantically identical across pairs. Within a pair it is a fixed monotone map, so it cannot reorder instances inside a pair, and the within-pair rank comparisons that carry our results are unaffected by it (§ 5.2). Appendix B.2 gives the estimators, the motivation for the constants, and translator-independence checks.

## 3.3 Two-Level Greedy Alignment

A trace is an ordered sequence of steps, and each step may contain multiple sentences. We therefore align traces hierarchically: an inner alignment compares sentences to score each step pair, and an outer alignment aggregates step-pair scores into a trace-level structural similarity.

Inner sentence alignment.

For steps $s_{a,i}$ and $s_{b,j}$ containing $n_{a,i}$ and $n_{b,j}$ sentences, respectively, let
 $\widetilde{\mathbf{C}}\in[0,1]^{n_{a,i}\times n_{b,j}}$ 
contain the calibrated sentence similarities from
Eq. (4). We define the precision-style score $p^{\mathrm{in}}_{i,j}=\frac{1}{n_{a,i}}\sum_{m=1}^{n_{a,i}}\max_{n}\widetilde{\mathbf{C}}_{m,n}$ , the recall-style score $r^{\mathrm{in}}_{i,j}=\frac{1}{n_{b,j}}\sum_{n=1}^{n_{b,j}}\max_{m}\widetilde{\mathbf{C}}_{m,n}$ , and the step-pair similarity $\sigma_{i,j}=F_{1}(p^{\mathrm{in}}_{i,j},r^{\mathrm{in}}_{i,j})$ .
This BERTScore-style greedy alignment (Zhang et al., 2020) is high only when the sentences in each step are covered by the other.

Outer step alignment.

Collect the step-pair similarities into
 $\mathbf{A}\in[0,1]^{t_{a}\times t_{b}}$ , where
 $\mathbf{A}_{i,j}=\sigma_{i,j}$ . The structural similarity is

 $\displaystyle S(r_{a},r_{b})$ 
 $\displaystyle=F_{1}\!\left(\frac{1}{t_{a}}\sum_{i}\max_{j}\mathbf{A}_{i,j},\;\frac{1}{t_{b}}\sum_{j}\max_{i}\mathbf{A}_{i,j}\right).$ 

(5)

The bidirectional maxima permit many-to-one correspondences, allowing traces to express the same content at different step granularities. Because $S$ ignores the locations of matched steps, it measures content preservation independently of order.

## 3.4 Soft Kendall- $\tau$ Order Consistency

Structural similarity does not account for the positions of matched steps. We therefore derive an order-consistency score from the same step-pair matrix $\mathbf{A}$ .

Soft assignment.

For the directed comparison $a\rightarrow b$ , we sharpen the step similarities and normalize each source-step row:

 $\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathbf{A}}_{i,j}$ 
 $\displaystyle=\mathbf{A}_{i,j}^{\,3},$ 

(6)

 $\displaystyle P^{a\rightarrow b}_{i,j}$ 
 $\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\dfrac{\widetilde{\mathbf{A}}_{i,j}}{\sum_{j^{\prime}}\widetilde{\mathbf{A}}_{i,j^{\prime}}},&\sum_{j^{\prime}}\widetilde{\mathbf{A}}_{i,j^{\prime}}>0,\\[8.53581pt]
\dfrac{1}{t_{b}},&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}$ 

(7)

Thus, $P^{a\rightarrow b}_{i,j}$ represents the soft correspondence between source step $i$ and target step $j$ . Sharpening concentrates mass on stronger matches without imposing a hard one-to-one assignment. Appendix D.8 reports sensitivity to the sharpening choice.

Order mass.

For each ordered source-step pair $i<k$ , we accumulate probability mass according to whether their matched target positions are concordant, discordant, or tied:

 $\displaystyle M^{a\rightarrow b}_{r}$ 
 $\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\binom{t_{a}}{2}}\sum_{i<k}\sum_{(j,\ell)\in\Omega_{r}}P^{a\rightarrow b}_{i,j}P^{a\rightarrow b}_{k,\ell},$ 

 $\displaystyle\qquad r\in\{\mathrm{con},\mathrm{dis},\mathrm{tie}\},$ 

(8)

where $\Omega_{\mathrm{con}}=\{(j,\ell):j<\ell\}$ , $\Omega_{\mathrm{dis}}=\{(j,\ell):j>\ell\}$ , and $\Omega_{\mathrm{tie}}=\{(j,\ell):j=\ell\}$ . The directed order score is

 $\displaystyle\tau^{a\rightarrow b}$ 
 $\displaystyle=\frac{M^{a\rightarrow b}_{\mathrm{con}}-M^{a\rightarrow b}_{\mathrm{dis}}}{M^{a\rightarrow b}_{\mathrm{con}}+M^{a\rightarrow b}_{\mathrm{dis}}+M^{a\rightarrow b}_{\mathrm{tie}}}.$ 

(9)

Because this score is directional, we symmetrize it:

 $\displaystyle\tau(r_{a},r_{b})$ 
 $\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left(\tau^{a\rightarrow b}+\tau^{b\rightarrow a}\right),$ 

(10)

using $\mathbf{A}$ for $a\rightarrow b$ and
 $\mathbf{A}^{\top}$ for $b\rightarrow a$ . The score is undefined when either trace has fewer than two steps, when either trace is empty, or when either directed denominator is zero. We interpret $\tau$ jointly with $S$ : high $\tau$ with low $S$ indicates that the matched content is ordered consistently but structurally incomplete.

## LIMITATIONS SECTION
Visible traces, not internal computation. A chain of thought need not be a faithful record of the computation that produced the answer (Turpin et al., 2023; Lanham et al., 2023; Chen et al., 2025), and we link the divergence we measure to no internal quantity. The visible trace is what verification, education, and auditing pipelines consume, and that is the scope of our claims. Activation-level analysis would address faithfulness directly, but it would exclude closed-weight models, including two of the six we evaluate.

Ranks, not levels. What survives an encoder change is rank structure rather than absolute scale (Appendix G), and per-pair calibration aligns only the endpoints of the similarity scale. We therefore compare $S$ and $\tau$ by rank within a fixed encoder and read absolute cross-pair values as descriptive only. The one cross-pair comparison of levels we draw, the same-language control of § 5.4, is paired within question and its calibration exposure is measured, but it remains a level comparison. Ceiling estimates additionally rely on LLM translation, whose co-dependence with the evaluated model we bound in Appendix D.1.

Scope of the evidence. The human validation covers two language pairs, both including English, with $60$ verified-correct trace pairs each, all from one model; agreement is moderate, which attenuates rather than inflates the reported correlations. We do not establish that the metrics track human judgment for other models or for pairs without English. The same-language control covers two of the six models, about $100$ questions per benchmark, and one paraphrase per question, and it does not resample an identical prompt at non-zero temperature, which would isolate decoding variation; its effect is unanimous in direction but varies about threefold in size across cells. Step boundaries are verified automatically and by a manual spot check too small to bound the residual error rate (Appendix B.1.1).

Divergence and outcomes. The association between low $S$ and shared wrong answers rests on $225$ pairs from two language pairs and is largely carried by item difficulty (§ 5.5). We draw no causal conclusion from it, and we do not show that divergence impairs verification, comprehension, or safety; that would require a study with human readers.

## CONCLUSION
We show that agreement on final answers does not imply agreement in the reasoning traces a model emits across languages: holistic similarity can remain high even when step-level structure and order diverge. The consequence is at the level of the artifact a user reads: for the same correct answer, the supporting evidence a reader is given can depend on the language they asked in—steps another reader never sees, or the same steps in a different order—more than on how the question is worded within one language. It appears in every one of the $21$ language pairs we evaluate, including the typologically closest. By decomposing trace consistency into structural similarity $S$ , order consistency $\tau$ , and holistic similarity $H$ , our framework reveals complementary axes that single-score evaluations obscure and locates where traces part. The rank-based finding is stable across sentence encoders and step segmentations, and small blind annotation studies on one model’s traces in two English-anchored language pairs are consistent with it. Because the dissociation also appears between two English traces, the decomposition extends to process-level consistency beyond cross-lingual settings, including across paraphrases, prompts, and decoding strategies.

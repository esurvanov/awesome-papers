## ABSTRACT
Dynamic scheduling requires dispatching online arrivals while anticipating future jobs, as poor decisions can compound downstream costs under nonlinear tardiness objectives. We show that a pretrained large language model (LLM) can serve as the exogenous component of a structured world model, forecasting uncertain future job arrivals. Specifically, we exploit an afterstate decomposition, which separates known deterministic shop transitions from uncertain exogenous job arrivals, enabling the LLM to forecast only the latter through a compact belief representation. Building on this, we develop Afterstate World-Model Planning (AWMP), which combines an evolved, belief-conditioned dispatching strategy with short scenario-based rollouts. A surprise-triggered refresh mechanism queries the LLM only when newly observed arrivals deviate sufficiently from previous forecast, and online residual self-calibration further conditions the LLM on its recent forecast errors to handle nonstationary arrivals. Across diverse arrival distributions, AWMP reduces weighted tardiness by 6-21% on dynamic job-shop and 22-46% on flexible job-shop benchmarks, relative to the best of eight classical priority-dispatching rules.

Scheduling determines how a collection of jobs should be processed on limited machine resources while satisfying operational constraints such as precedence, machine capacity, and delivery commitments (Pinedo and Hadavi 1992; Smit et al. 2025a). Classical scheduling formulations assume that all jobs and their processing requirements are known in advance, allowing optimization over a fixed problem instance.
Real manufacturing systems, however, are inherently dynamic (Vieira et al. 2003; Ouelhadj and Petrovic 2009). New jobs arrive dynamically over time while machines are already processing previously released work, and the scheduler must repeatedly dispatch operations without knowing the future workload.

Under such uncertainty, scheduling performance is typically evaluated by due-date-oriented objectives. A widely adopted objective is total weighted tardiness, which incurs no cost before a job’s due date but imposes a growing penalty once the due date is missed.
This nonlinear objective creates sharp due-date thresholds: a dispatching decision that appears locally beneficial may occupy critical machine capacity, delay a later high-priority job past its due date, and incur a disproportionate downstream penalty.
Consequently, effective dynamic scheduling requires more than reacting to the currently available operations, but also anticipating how future job arrivals will reshape shop congestion and incorporating those expectations into decisions.

Existing approaches address this challenge either by reacting to the current workload or by explicitly modeling the future.
1) Classical priority-dispatching rules (PDRs), such as weighted shortest processing time (WSPT) and apparent tardiness cost (ATC), are fast and training-free (Panwalkar and Iskander 1977; Haupt 1989), but they rank only the currently available operations and carry no explicit representation of future arrivals.
2) Anticipatory optimization methods, including rolling-horizon replanning, sample-average approximation (SAA), and multiple-scenario approaches (MSA), evaluate decisions against future scenarios sampled from a prespecified stochastic model through repeated reoptimization (Powell 2007; Kleywegt et al. 2002; Bent and Van Hentenryck 2004; Li et al. 2025). Their performance relies heavily on the fidelity of the assumed model, and the repeated reoptimization can be extremely expensive.
3) Recent neural methods learn dispatching policies from simulated trajectories with stochastic arrivals (Chang et al. 2022; Liu et al. 2022; Liu et al. 2023; Liu et al. 2024b; Tang et al. 2026). While capable of capturing complex dynamic patterns, they typically entangle the exogenous arrival process with dispatching decisions and require task- and distribution-specific training, with retraining needed once either changes.
These limitations motivate an anticipatory scheduler that can exploit observed arrival patterns without presupposing a parametric stochastic model, and that adapts without heavy retraining or reoptimization.

In this paper, we leverage a pretrained large language model (LLM) to model the exogenous component of a structured world model by forecasting future job arrivals, while explicitly accounting for known scheduling dynamics. Specifically, we employ an afterstate decomposition to separate the deterministic effects of dispatch actions from the uncertainty associated with future job arrivals.
At each decision step, a dispatch action transforms the current state into an afterstate, representing the resulting shop configuration before subsequent arrivals are incorporated. This afterstate captures endogenous changes, including machine availability, operation progress, and precedence status, governed by known deterministic dynamics, leaving future job arrivals as the sole source of exogenous uncertainty.
Accordingly, the LLM estimates a compact, decision-relevant belief over future arrival pressure, represented by the expected numbers of jobs arriving in the early and late portions of a fixed prediction horizon.
The belief summarizes anticipated arrival intensity without specifying individual job identities, machine requirements, or operation-level dynamics.
This compact interface makes the forecast directly usable by a dispatching planner while avoiding the need to learn a high-dimensional transition model.

Building on this decomposition, we develop AWMP, an Afterstate World-Model Planning framework for dynamic scheduling. At each decision step, AWMP maps every feasible dispatching action to its deterministic afterstate, and scores the action using both its afterstate features and the belief-derived features that capture future arrival pressure.
A scenario generator then converts the early-late belief into concrete arrival scenarios, under which the retained action candidates are evaluated through short rollouts, and the action with the best aggregated evaluation is selected for dispatching.

Moreover, AWMP makes the LLM forecast adaptive and efficient for online planning through three mechanisms.
First, online residual self-calibration feeds recent prediction-outcome pairs and forecast errors into subsequent prompts, enabling the LLM to adapt its arrival forecasts to nonstationary patterns through in-context learning.
Second, belief amortization caches the forecast across all candidate evaluations and rollouts within a decision step, while the cached belief is also reused across decision steps, where the LLM is queried only when realized arrivals deviate sufficiently from the current belief. This surprise-triggered refresh reduces the number of LLM calls from one per rollout to a handful per episode.
Third, meta-evolution searches over compact, human-readable strategy objects, comprising the scoring and value functions, rollout parameters, and forecast-refresh threshold, to automatically discover an effective belief-conditioned planning strategy.

Our main contributions are as follows:

• 

We formalize dynamic scheduling through an afterstate decomposition that separates the uncertain, exogenous job arrivals from known, deterministic scheduling dynamics.

• 

We instantiate the exogenous arrival-belief component of the structured world model with an LLM to forecast a compact arrival belief, and make this forecast adaptive and efficient through online residual self-calibration and belief reuse with surprise-triggered refresh.

• 

We develop AWMP, a belief-conditioned afterstate planner integrating coarse action screening and short-rollout evaluation under fixed scenario generation, with the planning strategy discovered offline via meta-evolution.

• 

Experiments across four arrival distributions show that AWMP discovers strategies that outperform the best of eight classical PDRs, reducing total tardiness by 6-21% on dynamic job-shop instances and 22-46% on dynamic flexible job-shop instances, while running in seconds.

## 2 Related Work

Classical dynamic scheduling methods. Classical methods for dynamic scheduling broadly fall into reactive dispatching and anticipatory optimization. Reactive PDRs, such as WSPT and ATC, re-rank eligible operations whenever the shop state changes (Panwalkar and Iskander 1977; Haupt 1989). They are fast and training-free, but evaluate only the current observable workload with no explicit representation of future arrivals. Anticipatory methods, including rolling-horizon optimization, SAA, and MSA, instead evaluate decisions against expected or sampled future workloads (Powell 2007; Kleywegt et al. 2002; Bent and Van Hentenryck 2004; Li et al. 2025). Their lookahead quality depends on the assumed arrival model, while repeatedly solving the resulting subproblems can be computationally expensive. AWMP occupies a middle ground: it retains low-latency online dispatching but augments it with a compact arrival forecast inferred by an LLM from the observed arrival history, without requiring the arrival pattern to be specified as a parametric stochastic process.

Neural dynamic scheduling methods. Neural combinatorial optimization has introduced graph and attention encoders combined with policy-gradient or value-based reinforcement learning (RL) for routing and scheduling (Kool et al. 2019; Zhang et al. 2020). Recent methods extend learned dispatching policies to dynamic job-shop scheduling problems (JSSPs) and flexible job-shop scheduling problems (FJSPs) with random job arrivals (Luo 2020; Liu et al. 2022; Liu et al. 2023; Smit et al. 2025b; Yu et al. 2026). These methods can capture complex interactions among jobs, operations, and machines, but they generally learn from specific simulated trajectories and may require retraining when the arrival process or optimization objective changes. In contrast, AWMP trains no neural policy, but uses a frozen pretrained LLM to estimate aggregate arrival pressure, and converts it into dispatching decisions through a compact planning strategy that is evolved offline and frozen before evaluation.

Learned world models for planning. Model-based RL learns a transition model of the environment to evaluate and select future actions. Representative approaches include MuZero (Schrittwieser et al. 2020; Antonoglou et al. 2022), Dreamer (Hafner et al. 2021), and TD-MPC (Hansen et al. 2022). These methods are designed for settings in which substantial environment dynamics must be learned from interaction. Dynamic scheduling has a different structure: once an action is specified, the transitions in shop status are deterministic and known. Our afterstate decomposition exploits this structure by retaining exact endogenous dynamics and isolating the future arrival stream as the only exogenous uncertainty. AWMP forms a structured world model from two parts: a low-dimensional predictor of exogenous arrival pressure and the deterministic scheduling simulator.

LLMs for optimization and heuristic discovery. LLMs have recently been used to generate and refine algorithms, heuristics, and environment dynamics simulators (Romera-Paredes et al. 2024; Liu et al. 2024a; Ye et al. 2024). AWMP uses LLMs in two separate roles. During online scheduling, a pretrained LLM instantiates the exogenous component of the structured world model by forecasting aggregate arrival pressure. During offline meta-evolution, a proposer LLM evolves a compact, human-readable planning strategy incorporating the scoring rule, value estimator, and rollout parameters, rather than an independent solver program.

Our methodology is organized around three questions: what to forecast, how to forecast, and how to plan with a structured world model, and AWMP addresses them through a modular design, as illustrated in Fig. 2. First, we formalize dynamic scheduling through an afterstate decomposition, which separates the known endogenous shop dynamics from unknown exogenous job arrivals. This decomposition identifies future arrivals, rather than the complete shop transition, as the only object that requires forecasting (Section 3.1). Then, we instantiate the exogenous component with a frozen, pretrained LLM to forecast a low-dimensional belief of early-late future arrival pressure from the observed arrival history, and introduce residual self-calibration to adapt the forecast in an online manner, especially under nonstationary arrivals (Section 3.2). Building on this, we develop AWMP, a belief-conditioned afterstate planner that first identifies promising actions with a compact scoring rule, and then evaluates them through short rollouts under arrival scenarios generated from the forecast belief (Section 3.3). Intra- and inter-step belief caching and surprise-triggered belief refresh amortize LLM calls without repeatedly forecasting at every rollout or decision step.
Finally, meta-evolution discovers a compact, human-readable planning strategy offline, which is then frozen for held-out evaluation (Section 3.4).

## 3.1 Afterstate Decomposition

We consider dynamic JSSPs and FJSPs, where jobs arrive online and dispatching decisions must be made before observing future workload. Let $t$ index decision steps and $c_{t}$ denote the corresponding physical shop time. At decision step $t$ , the scheduler observes the current shop state $s_{t}$ and selects a feasible dispatch action $a_{t}\in\mathcal{A}(s_{t})$ , where $\mathcal{A}(s_{t})$ is the set of all feasible operation-machine assignments. In JSSPs, the machine for each operation is fixed, while in FJSPs, machine selection is part of the action. The state $s_{t}$ contains the current shop configuration, including machine availability, job progress, precedence status, and observed arrival history $h_{t}$ .
The effect of an action can be separated from that of subsequent arrivals. Applying $a_{t}$ first produces an afterstate

 $\sigma_{t}=T(s_{t},a_{t}),$ 

(1)

where $T$ is the known action-to-afterstate transition. The afterstate captures the updated machine availability, operation progress, and, for FJSPs, the selected machine assignment before any new arrivals are incorporated. These changes are endogenous: once $s_{t}$ and $a_{t}$ are specified, they are fully deterministic.

We define the set of future job arrivals as:

 $\mathcal{Z}_{t}=\left\{(\rho_{j},\zeta_{j}):\rho_{j}>c_{t}\right\}\sim P_{\beta}(\cdot\mid h_{t}),$ 

(2)

where $\rho_{j}$ is the release time of future job $j$ , $\zeta_{j}$ collects its attributes, such as its processing time, due date, and
weight, and $\beta$ controls the arrival pattern such as intensity.
The arrival process is exogenous: dispatching actions do not affect which jobs arrive or their release times.

Nevertheless, an action may change the physical time of the next decision step, and hence which portion of $\mathcal{Z}_{t}$ becomes observable before that decision. The next state is therefore determined by an event-advance function $g$ :

 $s_{t+1}=g(\sigma_{t},\mathcal{Z}_{t}),$ 

(3)

which is likewise known and deterministic given the afterstate $\sigma_{t}$ and a realization of arrival stream $\mathcal{Z}_{t}$ . The function $g$ advances shop time, executes the deterministic scheduling dynamics, and incorporates exactly the jobs released during the transition. Consequently, $T$ and $g$ need not be learned, and the only unknown component required for exact anticipatory planning is the conditional arrival process $P_{\beta}(\cdot\mid h_{t})$ .

This decomposition provides direct insight into how a world model can be structured for dynamic scheduling. Because the endogenous shop transitions are known exactly, AWMP retains them in the scheduling simulator and models the uncertain exogenous arrival process separately. A pretrained LLM estimates the decision-relevant effect of $\mathcal{Z}_{t}$ as a low-dimensional, compact belief from the observed arrival history $h_{t}$ , while the planner combines this belief with the exact transitions $T$ and $g$ for rollout evaluation. Together, the exogenous predictor and deterministic simulator constitute the structured world model used by AWMP.

## CONCLUSION
We introduced AWMP, an afterstate world-model planning framework for dynamic scheduling under online arrivals. The afterstate decomposition separates known deterministic shop dynamics from the exogenous uncertain arrival stream, forming a structured world model in which a pretrained LLM models the exogenous component while the scheduling simulator retains the known endogenous transitions exactly. Residual self-calibration adapts the belief forecast online under nonstationary arrivals, while a meta-evolved, belief-conditioned planner converts it into dispatching decisions through candidate screening and short rollouts under a fixed, non-learned scenario generator. Experiments show that combining a low-dimensional LLM-based forecast of exogenous uncertainty with exact scheduling dynamics supports effective anticipatory scheduling without learning the complete shop transition or training a task-specific neural dispatching policy. Future work includes enriching the arrival belief with more job-level information and evaluating AWMP on real-world and more diverse arrival processes. We will also explore more efficient forecasting models to further reduce online inference latency. More broadly, the structured world-model framework may extend to robust optimization and stochastic programming settings.

Our study has several limitations. First, AWMP represents future arrivals through a compact early–late workload belief rather than detailed job-level predictions. This design keeps the forecasting problem low-dimensional and supports stable online calibration, avoiding the difficulty of predicting the complete future arrival stream. However, richer representations that additionally capture job attributes, such as processing routes, priorities, or machine requirements, may provide more informative guidance when future workloads are highly heterogeneous. Second, our experiments construct dynamic instances from standard JSSP and FJSP benchmarks under four controlled arrival patterns. These patterns cover several representative forms of temporal variation and allow controlled evaluation across different arrival dynamics. However, real manufacturing systems may exhibit more diverse arrival behaviors and operational conditions, and evaluation on real scheduling traces would provide a broader assessment of the framework.

Authors: Linfei Ji, Jiayun Li, Haozhao Wang, Siyu Jiang, Zehao Wang, Longtao Xiao, Yu-Jie Yuan, Ruixuan Li

## ABSTRACT
On-policy self-distillation (OPSD) enables a language model to learn from a Teacher instantiated from the same model but given access to privileged information, such as a reference solution. We find that access to sufficient privileged information allows the Teacher to generate highly accurate trajectories; however, accuracy alone does not guarantee that these trajectories are suitable for Student learning. Direct access to a complete solution may encourage shortened, reference-conditioned derivations that omit the exploration, verification, and self-correction required to solve problems without privileged information. We introduce TeachBack, a bidirectional preference-learning framework for on-policy self-distillation that learns not only from the Teacher but also how the Teacher should teach. TeachBack shapes the Teacher's generation behavior by controlling the length of the reference-reasoning suffix injected into the prompt and by varying the prompt style. While maintaining comparable Teacher accuracy, it encourages the Teacher to explore and re-solve problems, thereby reducing its reliance on the reference solution. At each rollout refresh, the shared policy generates both a privileged Teacher trajectory and a problem-only Student trajectory. A pairwise preference objective uses privileged Teacher trajectories to improve Student learning, while the reversed preference uses the Student's behavior to shape future Teacher supervision under the privileged condition. Experiments on mathematical and logical reasoning tasks show that TeachBack consistently improves Student performance over existing OPSD methods and related baselines.

## 1 Introduction

On-policy distillation (Agarwal et al., 2024), which provides dense supervision by allowing a Student to sample reasoning trajectories from its current policy while a stronger Teacher evaluates the Student-generated prefixes, has emerged as the dominant post-training paradigm for enhancing the reasoning capabilities of large language models. Building on this, On-policy self-distillation (OPSD) (Zhao et al., 2026) further removes the need for a separate Teacher by employing a unified, parameter-shared policy, receiving great attention recently. This policy allows the model to play two roles, where the model acts as a Student when given only the question, but switches to a privileged-information (PI) Teacher when provided with additional reasoning evidence, such as reference solutions. Despite eliminating the dependency on external models, recent studies reveal that OPSD may cause shorter responses and a degeneration of native reasoning behaviors, a failure mode commonly referred to as thinking collapse (Peng et al., 2026), which is particularly pronounced in models relying on long chain-of-thought reasoning.

Several approaches have been proposed to address this degradation. One representative line studies OPSD from the perspective of optimization, where an uncertain Student and a PI-conditioned Teacher can favor different continuations at high-entropy reasoning forks. More specifically, privileged targets can suppress the Student’s branching, verification, and backtracking when their token probabilities are directly matched (Kaur et al., 2026). To preserve these behaviors, AD-OPSD (Peng et al., 2026) anchors updates at high-risk tokens to a frozen base policy. Another line examines the supervision signal itself: reference conditioning can change the Teacher’s response style without providing correspondingly useful reasoning. RLCSD (Pan et al., 2026) contrasts correct and incorrect hints to cancel style shifts unrelated to correctness, while Purified OPSD (Shen et al., 2026) removes a reference-only component from the Teacher signal. The third line changes how the Student learns from that signal; PBSD (Yu et al., 2026a) uses reward-regularized preferences between Teacher and Student rollouts instead of directly matching their token distributions. Although these methods have made great achievements, they primarily regulate how the Student uses a Teacher signal that has already been generated, leaving open a more upstream question: can PI be used to construct a Teacher whose reasoning remains useful to a Student that cannot access PI?

Figure 1: Motivation for TeachBack. In standard OPSD, access to privileged information can lead the Teacher to produce a short solution that omits exploration and verification. TeachBack uses partial reference reasoning and Student feedback to encourage complete, self-contained Teacher trajectories that remain useful to a problem-only Student.

This question points to a Teacher-side way to mitigate thinking collapse: privileged information should guide the Teacher’s reasoning rather than serve as an authoritative solution to be copied. A problem-only Student must search among alternative paths, express uncertainty at ambiguous steps, verify intermediate conclusions, and recover from mistakes. As we design in Figure 1, a useful PI-conditioned Teacher should preserve these Student-compatible behaviors while using privileged information to check the explored path, correct unproductive branches, and converge more reliably to a correct answer. The desired Teacher therefore needs to balance three properties: high correctness under privileged information, low dependence on the wording or trajectory of the reference solution, and productive exploration that remains reproducible without PI. In particular, the Teacher should not simply compress the reference-conditioned response into a shorter answer. It should re-solve the original problem, use privileged information for verification, and produce a complete trajectory that is both reliable as supervision and rich enough for the Student to learn.

Based on this motivation, we propose TeachBack, which improves both the construction and transfer of privileged Teacher trajectories. First, TeachBack presents the Teacher with partial reference reasoning of varying lengths and prompt styles as optional working notes, then asks it to solve the original problem from the beginning. This encourages the Teacher to use PI to check its reasoning without simply following the reference. The shared policy then generates a Teacher trajectory with PI and a Student trajectory from the problem alone. We train with preferences in both directions: the Teacher trajectory guides Student learning, while the Student trajectory provides feedback on which reasoning paths the Teacher should retain. In this way, the Teacher learns to produce accurate, less reference-dependent supervision that the Student can learn from.

## 2 Related Work

On-policy distillation and privileged supervision.

Classical knowledge distillation (Hinton et al., 2015; Kim & Rush, 2016; Gu et al., 2024) transfers a
stronger Teacher’s predictions or generated sequences to a Student.
On-policy distillation (OPD) (Agarwal et al., 2024) instead queries the
Teacher on Student-sampled prefixes, reducing the mismatch between training
and deployment states. On-policy self-distillation
(OPSD) (Zhao et al., 2026) uses one parameter-shared policy as a problem-only
Student and a privileged-information (PI) Teacher, and Privileged Information
Distillation (Penaloza et al., 2026) extends this setting to long-horizon
agents (Wang et al., 2026; Wu et al., 2026; Lu et al., 2026). Recent studies show that privileged conditioning can suppress epistemic
verbalization, exploration, verification, and backtracking at high-entropy
reasoning forks, leading to thinking collapse and reference-induced style
drift (Kim et al., 2026; Kaur et al., 2026; Peng et al., 2026; Pan et al., 2026).
Existing remedies modify the resulting Teacher signal: Purified
OPSD (Shen et al., 2026) removes the reference-induced component;
PAINT (Tan & Hong, 2026), Adaptive Teacher
Exposure (Han et al., 2026), and best-of- $N$ 
selection (Zhang et al., 2026) control privileged exposure or retain
selected rollouts; and AR-OPD (Zhang, 2026) and
DOPD (Yu et al., 2026b) constrain or route privileged supervision according
to local compatibility and relative advantage. These methods improve how an
already formed signal is filtered or transferred, leaving the construction of
a Student-compatible Teacher as an upstream problem.

Preference optimization and Teacher construction.

Preference optimization provides a sequence-level alternative to direct
token-distribution matching. DPO (Rafailov et al., 2023) learns a
reference-relative policy from response preferences, while
SPIN (Chen et al., 2024) uses self-generated responses to form an iterative
preference signal. PBSD (Yu et al., 2026a) applies preference learning
to on-policy self-distillation by optimizing a reward-regularized preference
gap between PI-conditioned Teacher samples and on-policy Student samples
instead of directly matching Teacher probabilities. Related methods use
consensus gating (Stein et al., 2026) or
self-revision (He et al., 2026; Zelikman et al., 2022) to select or construct supervision (Li et al., 2026) before
distillation. TeachBack addresses the remaining Teacher-construction problem
by presenting a controllable ground-truth suffix as incomplete working notes,
varying the prompt framing, and using two cross-condition preference
directions: the Student-side objective transfers useful Teacher trajectories
under the problem-only condition, while the reversed objective uses Student
behavior to shape future privileged Teacher trajectories.

## 3 Method

(b) The shared policy samples paired Student and Teacher trajectories; both responses are replayed under both conditions and scored against a fixed reference policy. Given training examples $\mathcal{D}=\{(x_{i},y_{i}^{*},a_{i}^{*})\}_{i=1}^{N}$ , where $x_{i}$ is a problem, $y_{i}^{*}$ is its reference reasoning, and $a_{i}^{*}$ is its correct answer, TeachBack uses a suffix of $y_{i}^{*}$ as privileged information (PI) and $a_{i}^{*}$ for answer verification. The Student and Teacher are conditional roles of one parameter-shared policy $\pi_{\theta}$ , rather than separate networks: the Student receives only the problem, while the Teacher receives the problem and a prompt containing PI. Training alternates paired rollout generation with bidirectional preference updates. Figure 2 summarizes Teacher construction, paired rollouts, cross-scoring, and the two preference updates.

## 3.1 Teacher Construction: Reference-Suffix Injection and Prompt-Style Control

For each problem $x$ , the Student context and PI-conditioned Teacher context are

 $c_{S}=\operatorname{BasePrompt}(x),\qquad c_{T}=\operatorname{WorkingNotesPrompt}\!\left(x,\operatorname{Tail}(y^{*})\right).$ 

(1)

Equation 1 shows the Working Notes configuration used in the main training runs. The suffix is extracted from the dataset reference rather than from the current Student response, so Student sampling errors do not change the privileged information in the Teacher prompt. We use a suffix instead of the complete reference because it provides answer-relevant information while leaving the Teacher responsible for reconstructing the earlier derivation. An authoritative solution framing may encourage the Teacher to continue or copy the suffix. Framing it as incomplete, non-terminal working notes instead prompts the Teacher to re-solve the problem, verify the notes, fill in missing steps, and produce a complete answer.

We study two related factors in Teacher construction. The suffix length controls how much privileged information is available, while the prompt style changes how that information is presented. We compare several styles, including authoritative reference, answer-only hint, and optional working notes; the answer-only and full-solution instruction styles also change the information content. These comparisons let us assess Teacher correctness and reasoning behavior separately from the downstream preference objective. Appendix A gives the audit setup, metric definitions, and prompt templates.

## 3.2 Paired On-Policy Rollouts and Cross-Scoring

Unlike conventional OPSD, which transfers privileged next-token predictions on Student-generated prefixes, TeachBack generates explicit trajectories from both conditional roles. At refresh $t$ , the current policy is held fixed while generating $G$ paired responses:

 $y_{S,i}^{(t,g)}\sim\pi_{\theta_{t}}(\cdot\mid c_{S,i}),\qquad y_{T,i}^{(t,g)}\sim\pi_{\theta_{t}}(\cdot\mid c_{T,i}),\quad g=1,\ldots,G.$ 

(2)

Both responses are sampled from the same policy state, so systematic differences between their rollout distributions arise from the prompt conditions rather than separate models. We retain the first $K$ valid response tokens for preference training; below, $y_{S}$ and $y_{T}$ denote these retained prefixes when used in scoring equations, with example and rollout indices omitted for readability. This prefix focuses the objective on the part of the trajectory where the model establishes a solution direction, explores alternatives, and begins verification; later tokens often elaborate a path that has already been selected. Generation budgets, truncation rules, and the longer diagnostic rollout cap are specified in Appendix C.

The sampled responses are replayed under both prompts:

 $\mathcal{Q}^{(t)}=\begin{bmatrix}c_{S}\oplus y_{S}^{(t)}&c_{S}\oplus y_{T}^{(t)}\\
c_{T}\oplus y_{S}^{(t)}&c_{T}\oplus y_{T}^{(t)}\end{bmatrix}.$ 

(3)

The current policy and a frozen initialization policy score the same response tokens in all four combinations. Within each row, the scoring condition is fixed and the response origin varies. In particular, $y_{T}$ replayed under $c_{S}$ lets us evaluate Teacher reasoning under the problem-only condition. Replaying $y_{S}$ under $c_{T}$ supplies the corresponding compatibility view for the Teacher role. After the preference update, the new policy generates the next paired batch.

## 3.3 Bidirectional Preference Optimization

Let $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}=\pi_{\theta_{0}}$ remain fixed throughout training. For condition $c$ and response $y$ , we use the length-normalized score relative to this frozen reference policy:

 $A_{\theta}^{c}(y)=\frac{1}{|y|}\sum_{k=1}^{|y|}\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{k}\mid c,y_{<k})}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y_{k}\mid c,y_{<k})}.$ 

(4)

Only valid response positions contribute. Length normalization prevents longer responses from receiving a larger score solely because they contain more log-probability terms, while the frozen reference anchors both conditional comparisons to the same initialization.

Student-side transfer.

Under the problem-only condition, we prefer the Teacher-origin response, $y_{T}\succ y_{S}$ , using

 $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{stu}}=-\mathbb{E}\log\sigma\!\left(\beta\left[A_{\theta}^{c_{S}}(y_{T})-A_{\theta}^{c_{S}}(y_{S})\right]\right).$ 

(5)

Here, $\sigma$ is the logistic sigmoid, $\beta>0$ scales the preference
margin, and the expectation is over training problems and paired rollouts
sampled from the fixed policy snapshot at the current refresh. We use
 $\beta=0.1$ .
Both responses are scored under $c_{S}$ . The margin therefore asks whether the trajectory generated with privileged information becomes preferable under the deployment condition. This transfers a trajectory-level teaching signal without requiring the Student to match the complete PI-conditioned next-token distribution. The label is determined by response origin, not by assuming that every Teacher sample is correct; Teacher correctness is measured separately in the experiments.

Teacher-side feedback.

Under the PI-conditioned condition, we reverse the ordering and prefer the Student-origin response, $y_{S}\succ y_{T}$ :

 $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{tch}}=-\mathbb{E}\log\sigma\!\left(\beta\left[A_{\theta}^{c_{T}}(y_{S})-A_{\theta}^{c_{T}}(y_{T})\right]\right).$ 

(6)

The reversed preference does not claim that the Student answer is more accurate. A problem-only response can contain incremental derivation, verification, and backtracking that are absent from a concise privileged response. Favoring Student-origin responses under $c_{T}$ can feed Student-compatible reasoning back into the role that generates future Teacher targets, while the privileged suffix continues to support correctness. ## CONCLUSION
We presented TeachBack, a bidirectional on-policy self-distillation framework
that transfers privileged Teacher trajectories to a problem-only Student and
uses Student behavior to shape future Teacher rollouts. Framing a partial
reasoning suffix as optional working notes retains high Teacher accuracy with
less measured reference dependence than authoritative reference framing.
Across the tested model scales, TeachBack improves aggregate mathematical and
logical reasoning. Controlled ablations show that an intermediate Teacher-side
weight preserves Teacher accuracy and both preference directions while
improving held-out performance over Student-only training, highlighting the
value of shaping privileged supervision at its source.

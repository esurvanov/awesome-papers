## ABSTRACT
Language-model agents are increasingly asked to run experiments, yet benchmarks rarely measure whether those experiments improve the decisions they are meant to inform. We introduce CrashGym, a deterministic, replayable limit-order-book simulator in which an agent must choose between a deleveraging intervention and a liquidity backstop for a market about to suffer a shock-triggered crash. Before committing, the agent may query intervention experiments in a laboratory realization of the market. Its choice is then scored on an independently seeded target realization in which the simulator executes every feasible intervention, so regret relative to the best intervention is computed by directly simulating every alternative on the same target repeats, with no human or model-based judging. The simulator implements each amplification channel---persistent price impact, liquidity withdrawal, margin-driven liquidation, and an evolving strategy mix---as an explicit mechanism that can be switched on in stages. In a prospectively specified study on 24 newly sampled worlds, laboratory access reduces the mean regret of the one deployment with a complete record (Luna) from 8.878 to 3.785, a paired reduction of 5.092 utility units (nominal 95% world-bootstrap interval: 1.028--9.507), or 57.4% of the OBSERVE-ONLY mean. Prospective comparisons for two further deployments are incomplete; in a separate exploratory study, mean regret is lower with laboratory access for all four deployments evaluated. A laboratory whose strategy mix evolves endogenously yields no established advantage over one with a fixed mix. CrashGym thus measures the value of experimentation as avoided decision loss and lets simulator detail be judged by the decisions it supports.

## 1 Introduction

A market is about to fall sharply. A policy agent must recommend one of two interventions: force leveraged holders to cut their positions, or post a standing bid that supplies liquidity. Both are defensible a priori. Deleveraging can pre-empt the cycle of margin calls and forced selling, whereas a liquidity backstop can absorb that selling before it moves prices [1, 11, 25]. Which intervention is better depends on which amplification channel dominates in the market at hand. We ask whether an agent that can first experiment on a laboratory copy of the market chooses better, and by how much.

Language-model agents are increasingly evaluated as experimenters: they design studies, run code, and report findings [22, 15, 16]. Most evaluations, however, score the research process or its products—task completion, explanation quality, or recovery of a latent structure. Completing an experiment and benefiting from it are different outcomes. Measuring the benefit requires two ingredients that are rarely available together: the same decision-maker observed with and without experimental access, and the counterfactual outcome of every action it could have taken. The second ingredient is unobservable in historical data.

Approach. CrashGym supplies both ingredients inside a deterministic agent-based market (Figure 1). An agent receives a public observation, may query intervention experiments in a laboratory realization, and then commits to one intervention. The simulator executes every feasible intervention on an independent target realization that the agent never observes. Regret—the utility gap between the best intervention and the chosen one—is therefore exact for the realized target repeats. Comparing regret across information conditions yields the gross decision value of experimentation, a realized counterpart of the classical value of sample information [21, 14, 23]. Because the simulator’s amplification channels are explicit mechanisms that can be enabled in stages, the same outcome also tests whether added simulator detail improves the decision it is meant to support.

## 2 Related work

LLM agents as experimenters. Tool-using language models interleave reasoning with external actions [33, 24], and a growing set of environments evaluates them as scientists. ScienceWorld and DiscoveryWorld assess task completion and discovery in simulated worlds [29, 16]; MLAgentBench evaluates machine-learning experimentation [15]; and end-to-end systems generate complete research papers [22]. CausaLab distinguishes predictive success from recovery of causal structure, and CausalGame evaluates causal reasoning and experimental protocols in games [32, 5]; see also Kıcıman et al. [17]. We measure a different quantity: how much an agent’s experiments improve a subsequent, independently scored decision. An agent may use informative intervention results well without reconstructing the latent mechanism.

LLM agents in finance. Domain-adapted models and agents have been applied to financial analysis and trading [30, 34, 31], and InvestorBench evaluates financial decision-making across tasks [20]. These evaluations typically rely on historical data, in which the outcomes of untaken actions are unavailable. Our simulator supplies those counterfactuals for a policy intervention, at the cost of using synthetic rather than historical markets.

Market simulation. Agent-based models have long been used to study market dynamics [12, 19, 9]. ABIDES supports high-fidelity, message-level market research [2]; JAX-LOB targets accelerated, parallel limit-order-book simulation [10]; and realism metrics assess how closely simulated order books reproduce stylized facts [28]. Our crash construction draws on the leverage-cycle and fire-sale literature, in which margin constraints, forced selling, and liquidity withdrawal reinforce one another [1, 11, 26, 25]. Rather than maximizing realism, we use the simulator as an instrument that supplies exact counterfactual intervention outcomes and lets each amplification channel be switched on or off.

Value of information and decision-aware model evaluation. Statistical decision theory values an experiment by the improvement in the expected utility of the decision it informs [21, 14, 23, 4]. Our gross value $V_{b}$ (Section 7) is a realized, empirical counterpart for an LLM agent. A related literature argues that models should be judged by the decisions they induce rather than by predictive accuracy alone [6, 8, 18], and the value-equivalence principle motivates retaining only those model distinctions that matter for planning [13]. Our comparison of laboratory designs examines this motivation empirically; it does not test Bellman-operator equivalence. The setting also relates to sim-to-real transfer, in which the deployment environment differs from the training simulator [27].

## 3 The CrashGym simulator

The simulator is a discrete-time, single-asset market with a central limit-order book. We describe its components in turn: the exchange core, the participants, the episode schedule, the amplification mechanisms, the evolving strategy mix, the mechanism stages, and the determinism and replay layer. Parameter values refer to the crash construction used in all experiments; Appendix A lists them in full.

## 3.1 Exchange core

Order book and matching. Prices are integers in ticks. Each side of the book maps a price level to a first-in-first-out queue, giving strict price–time priority. The only order primitive is a limit order, together with cancellation by the owning account. A marketable order is emulated as a limit order priced 100 ticks through the best opposite quote; any unfilled remainder is cancelled immediately, so it behaves as an immediate-or-cancel order with a price band. Incoming orders walk the opposite side best-first, and each fill executes at the resting order’s price for the smaller of the two quantities; a partially filled resting order keeps its queue position. The reference price $m_{t}$ is the sum of best bid and best ask, i.e. the midpoint in half-tick units, so it remains an exact integer. If one side of the book is empty, the reference falls back to the last two-sided quote, and aggressive orders are priced from the last published bid or ask. Book invariants—no empty levels, no zero quantities, strictly increasing priorities, and no crossed book—are checked after every mutation.

Accounts, risk admission, and fees. Each participant owns an account with integer cash and inventory, with positions tracked as first-in-first-out lots. Before an order is accepted, the exchange checks a position limit ( $10^{6}$ lots) and a leverage limit: the worst-case position notional along the order’s potential fill path may not exceed $20\times$ the account’s initial cash. Rejected orders are logged and have no effect. A fee of one cash unit per lot is charged to both counterparties of every fill; resting orders that never fill pay nothing. Every fill must conserve total inventory and total cash plus fees, global invariants are re-checked after each operation, and a full canonical audit closes every run.

## 3.2 Participants

Table 1 summarizes the six participant classes and the two exogenous accounts. Each step, a market maker posts a 40-level ladder on each side, at offsets of $2+k$ ticks ( $k=0,\dots,39$ ) around a center that tracks the previous reference price. Its depth per level is a world-specific $\bar{Q}\in\{24,\dots,56\}$ lots in the initial ladder (step 0), 400 lots during steps 1–7, and $\bar{Q}$ again from the shock onward. Every world therefore experiences a scheduled liquidity cliff at the shock, in addition to the stress-driven withdrawal described below. Ladders are reconciled rather than rebuilt: unchanged levels keep their queue priority, and superseded orders are cancelled. Noise traders submit eight marketable orders of four lots per step, each a buy or sell with probability $1/2$ . A fundamental trader trades four lots per tick of deviation between the fundamental value $v$ and the midpoint. A trend follower trades $4e_{t}$ lots, where $e_{t}=\operatorname{trunc}\bigl((3e_{t-1}+r_{t-1})/4\bigr)$ is a moving average of past returns $r_{t}=m_{t}-m_{t-1}$ (half-ticks) and $\operatorname{trunc}$ rounds toward zero. The fundamental value is held constant at $v=10{,}000$ ticks: no news arrives, so post-shock dynamics are responses to the shock, the scheduled post-shock depth, and the intervention. The leveraged trader builds a long position at step 1 and afterwards trades only through forced liquidation or the deleveraging intervention. The adaptive participant chooses each step between two candidate strategies (Section 3.5).

## 3.3 Episode schedule

An episode lasts $T=96$ steps (Figure 2a). At step 0 the maker posts its initial ladder around $v$ . At step 1 the leveraged trader builds its position; at step 4 the chosen intervention takes effect; at step 7 the pre-shock reference $m_{t_{0}-1}$ is recorded; and at step $t_{0}=8$ the shock account sells 160–240 lots at market. Within each step, operations execute in a fixed order (Figure 2b): the maker re-quotes; last step’s shadow trades are settled and strategy shares updated; intervention orders are placed; the shock and its persistent impact are applied; forced liquidations execute; noise, fundamental, and trend orders arrive; the adaptive participant trades; and the end-of-step reference price and objective are recorded.

## 3.4 Amplification mechanisms

The shock is a burst of order flow, not a change in fundamentals. Three mechanisms convert it into a crash whose depth and duration depend on the world (Figure 3b).

Persistent impact. The maker’s quote center shifts by $40\,x/D_{0}$ ticks, where $x$ is the executed shock volume and $D_{0}=10\bar{Q}$ is the depth of the ten best bids in the initial (step-0) ladder; integer remainders are carried forward. The shock therefore moves the price permanently rather than only transiently.

Liquidity withdrawal. After the shock, the maker measures stress $\sigma_{t}=\max\{0,\,m_{t_{0}-1}-m_{t-1}\}+|r_{t-1}|$ in half-ticks and thins its ladder to

 $Q_{t}=\bar{Q}\cdot\max\bigl\{0.25,\;1-\beta\,\sigma_{t}/\kappa\bigr\},$ 

(1)

where $\beta\in[0,1]$ is the world’s withdrawal strength and $\kappa\in\{24,\dots,56\}$ its stress scale. Withdrawal reduces depth but never widens the spread or empties the book.

Margin-driven liquidation. At the start of step $t$ , the leveraged trader’s equity is $E_{t}=c_{t}+q_{t}p_{t}$ , where $c_{t}$ and $q_{t}$ are its cash and inventory at the close of step $t-1$ and $p_{t}=m_{t-1}/2$ is the previous closing midpoint in ticks; its maintenance requirement is $M_{t}=\mu\,|q_{t}|\,p_{t}$ with $\mu\in[3\%,7\%]$ . From step $t_{0}+1$ onward, whenever $E_{t}<M_{t}$ it must sell

 $\ell_{t}=\bigl\lceil\alpha\,|q_{t}|\bigr\rceil$ 

(2)

lots at market, where $\alpha\in[0,1]$ is the world’s liquidation strength. Because forced sales lower the price and therefore equity, the rule can repeat over consecutive steps and form a liquidation spiral.

The strengths $\alpha$ and $\beta$ are both held at zero until the shock order has filled, and they define the four world strata used in sampling: neither mechanism, liquidation only, withdrawal only, or both (Section 4.1).

## CONCLUSION
The value of an experiment is the decision it changes. The task links an agent’s research process to a consequence: avoided intervention loss. Writing $\bar{R}_{b}$ for mean regret under an experimental budget of $b$ calls, the gross value of experimentation is

 $V_{b}=\bar{R}_{0}-\bar{R}_{b},$ 

(6)

the loss avoided relative to observation alone. For Luna on new worlds, $V_{8}=5.092$ with the adaptive laboratory. This quantity makes experimental usefulness comparable across agents, budgets, and laboratories on a common scale, and a resource-efficiency analysis could weigh it against measured acquisition and inference costs.

Which simulator details matter? For an agent, the relevant question is whether an evolving strategy mix improves the final choice between deleveraging and a liquidity backstop. In our data it does not demonstrably do so: the prospective Adapt $-$ Fixed interval admits either sign, and complete-utility selectors from both laboratories recover nearly all target-optimal actions. This suggests a concrete evaluation practice: before treating added dynamics as necessary for a task, test whether they change the intervention ranking or reduce target regret [13, 18]. Because the simulator’s mechanisms are switchable by stage, the same test can be applied to each stage. Runtime savings and transfer to real markets remain separate empirical questions.

Limitations. The evidence covers one synthetic crash construction and two interventions, each with a single fixed implementation (one deleveraging sale; one backstop size). The fundamental value is constant, maker depth falls on a fixed schedule at the shock, the evolving strategy mix has two candidates (one of them signal-independent), and the shadow-trade horizon is one step. New constructors and independent target seeds support transfer within this construction, not to other constructions or to historical markets. With 24 worlds, the primary effect is clear in direction but modest in two-sided strength (exact sign-flip $p=0.075$ ). Incomplete Sol and Terra records limit prospective cross-deployment conclusions, and the placebo comparison leaves the contribution of world-specific evidence relative to tool interaction and generic crash information uncertain. The positive primary result therefore concerns the full laboratory-access condition for Luna within this construction. Finally, regret is measured in simulator utility units and is not calibrated to monetary outcomes.

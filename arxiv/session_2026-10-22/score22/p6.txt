Abstract
As LLMs are increasingly deployed in value-sensitive contexts, aligning them with human values has emerged as a post-training objective in NLP, aimed at instilling additional values and guiding model behaviour. However, current evaluations remain narrow. Because values are interconnected, it is unclear how fine-tuning for specific values alters unrelated capabilities and behaviours. In this work, we adapt four instruction-tuned LLMs across individual Hofstede’s value dimensions and evaluate them on both cross-value transfer and broader behaviours. We find that value-specific training produces unintended side effects on untrained values, risk preferences, social cognition, and safety traits. Consequently, we suggest that value alignment after post-training should incorporate broader behavioral evaluations, and we highlight the need to investigate where additional value training might best fit into the pipeline.
## 1 Introduction
Large language models (LLMs) are increasingly deployed as conversational assistants. However, rather than using them solely for routine task execution, users increasingly turn to them for career coaching, emotional support and other personal matters that have traditionally required human social interaction Chatterji et al. (2025); McCain et al. (2025).
A system that does not support diverse and prosocial values could cause negative societal effects, as humans could also be shaped by the values the assistant reflects (Jakesch et al., 2023; Williams-Ceci et al., 2026).
Recognizing this, post-training paradigms have commonly been adopted to improve models’ reflection of diverse values, including safety-based constellations of values Bai et al. (2022b); Guan et al. (2024), character-based behavioral tendencies (Anthropic, 2024; Maiya et al., 2025), or survey-based cultural values (Li et al., 2024b; Feng et al., 2025). These directions are largely studied in isolation. Yet values may not be separable inside a model where training one could shift others.
Recent work shows that models fine-tuned to write insecure code become broadly misaligned in unrelated domains (Betley et al., 2025). In that setting, the training signal is not explicitly value-laden (though intentions remain inferable), and the discovery is incidental (i.e., emergent misalignment, unintended side effects arising from the training objective). Meanwhile, survey-based value training explicitly targets specific values and checks for standard capability loss rather than examining what else shifts Li et al. (2024a); Xu et al. (2025). Neither tells us what else could change when a value is deliberately installed.
In this work, we focus on Hofstede-based value-tuning and examine the influence of training beyond the typical evaluation of the target value itself. We fine-tuned four instruction-tuned LLMs on each direction of Hofstede’s value dimensions (Hofstede, 2001; Minkov and Hofstede, 2012). We investigate emergent misalignment from two aspects: first, whether training on one value shifts others, and second, its impact on broader behaviors related to safety, risk-sensitive decision-making, and social cognition such as theory-of-mind Premack and Woodruff (1978) or emotion intelligence.
In summary, our contributions are as follows:
• 
We find that training in one value direction also shifts the model’s preferences on the other, untrained value dimensions.
• 
We show that training drifts safety-relevant behavior, including self-reported traits, value prioritization in dilemmas, and open-ended responses judged more often as misaligned.
• 
We further show that models’ social cognition degrades in application, not comprehension.
Our findings suggest that Hofstede-based value tuning and existing alignment interact in ways that sequential pipelines do not account for. Applying value tuning after safety post-training simultaneously shifts deception, oversight-related behavior, and social cognition, with effects that differ across base models and remain undetected by standard value benchmarks. These effects reflect how value signals propagate through training rather than intrinsic properties of the dimensions described. This highlights the need for broader evaluation of late-stage value tuning and raises the fundamental question of whether such intervention belongs earlier in the training pipeline.
## 2 Related Work
Value Training.
Recent NLP research typically evaluates language models’ values through social science surveys and seeks to improve the models’ alignment. For evaluation, survey instruments mapped to Hofstede’s (Hofstede, 2001; Minkov and Hofstede, 2012) cultural dimensions are administered to models (Cao et al., 2023; Tao et al., 2024; AlKhamissi et al., 2024; Masoud et al., 2025; Negru et al., 2026). For training, prior work leverages surveys alongside synthetic data to steer value alignment, evaluating progress using in-domain surveys or relevant downstream tasks (Li et al., 2024a; Li et al., 2024b; Adilazuarda et al., 2025, inter alia). In broader value training, Constitutional AI Bai et al. (2022b); Huang et al. (2024) trains model with high-level principles, where as other approaches learning values implicitly from human preferences through feedback learning Bai et al. (2022a); Ouyang et al. (2022); Rafailov et al. (2023), and increasingly aimed to accommodate diverse user values Zhou et al. (2024); Sorensen et al. (2024); Kirk et al. (2024). Character training shapes the assistant’s persona as a whole, which governs not only its surface behavior but also its apparent values, beliefs, and ethics (Anthropic, 2024; Maiya et al., 2025). These methods typically evaluate and train models on the targeted values themselves, overlooking how values generalize to model behavior beyond the training domain.
Emergent misalignment and the side effects of narrow fine-tuning.
Recent work Betley et al. (2025) found that fine-tuning on a narrow domain, such as writing insecure code, can induce broadly misaligned behavior in unrelated domains. This effect has also been shown in other narrow training (e.g., finance or medical advice), and under reinforcement learning (Turner et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2026), though susceptibility varies considerably across models (Schreiber and Goldstein, 2026).
Closely related to our work, Choi et al. (2025) found that aligning a Llama-2 7B model with Schwartz values increases toxicity, bias, and compliance with harmful requests. However, their study differs from ours in its scope of evaluation, training procedure, and data formality. Similarly, recent work (Bhatia et al., 2026) examines how different training methods shift values during post-training. In contrast, we focus on value training as an instrument to study broad behavioral changes.
## 3 Methodology
## 3.1 Synthetic Data Generation
We create synthetic data of scenario–response pairs spanning all six Hofstede dimensions (Hofstede et al., 2010): Individualism–Collectivism (IDV), Power Distance (PDI), Masculinity–Femininity (MAS), Uncertainty Avoidance (UAI), Long-Term Orientation (LTO), and Indulgence–Restraint (IVR). Each dimension has two opposite values, 12 in total, which we refer to as poles in the rest of the paper.
We refer to these value dimensions and their corresponding pole-adapted models using the following short names: individualist/collectivist, high_pdi/low_pdi, masculine/feminine, high_uai/low_uai, long_term/short_term, and indulgent/restrained.
We generate 10,000 behavioral dilemmas per pole, pairing each with a response that reflects the target value.
To ground the data, each sample is seeded from one of three sources: Touché23-ValueEval (Kiesel et al., 2023), human-authored conclusion–premise pairs for value measurement; CDEval (Wang et al., 2024), a binary-choice QA dataset labeled with Hofstede dimensions and domains; or Sociocultural Statements (Negru et al., 2026), 281 debate statements labeled with Hofstede dimensions. To enhance scenario and linguistic diversity, we vary the domain (adapted from CDEval) and output format (e.g., diary entry, forum reply; Appendix A.2). We generate the data with Llama-3.3-70B-Instruct (Grattafiori et al., 2024, Llama 3 family;) in a two-stage setup adapted from Liu et al. (2026b), which first turns each seed into a dilemma scenario and then writes a response that reflects the target value (Figure 1; details in Appendix A.1).
Each pole’s data is split into train and test sets (85:15%).

Human validation. To verify that each response expresses its intended pole, two annotators independently labelled a stratified sample of 300 instances (25 per pole) as yes, no or unsure (guidelines and sampling in Appendix A.4). Raw agreement is 89.7% (Gwet’s AC1 $=0.89$ ). Both annotators labelled 265 of the 300 instances (88.3%; 95% CI 84.2–91.5) as expressing the intended pole.
## 3.2 Evaluation
In-domain evaluation. Held-out Data: The held-out portion of the generated synthetic data (§3.1), which no pole adapter is trained on. Our main measure is a likelihood-based preference margin (§4; Appendix C.3). As a complementary measure, an LLM judge (Llama-3.3-70B-Instruct) scores the models’ own generations on the held-out scenarios by their pole alignment (PA).
Cover-letter Dataset: A dataset Khan et al. (2025) of 100 hiring-scenario, each containing twelve letters mapped one-to-one to values across Hofstede’s dimensions, to assess post-tuning preferences. We prompt models to act as hiring managers, score applicant letters (0–100), and select the top candidate. We evaluate order-counterbalanced pairwise comparisons (all-pairs and opposing within-dimension values), and report head-to-head win rates.
Evaluations for broader behavior shifts.
We evaluate the pole-adapted models across five out-of-domain benchmarks, grouped into: (1) Risk suite, comprising Model-Written Evaluations (MWE) (Perez et al., 2023), AIRiskDilemmas (Chiu et al., 2026), and the Emergent Misalignment benchmark (Betley et al., 2025, EM;); (2) Social cognition suite, encompassing EmoBench Sabour et al. (2024); and (3) FANToM Kim et al. (2023). Each dataset is described below.
Model-Written Evaluations (MWE): A multiple-choice evaluation assessing model traits across 153 distinct constructs (e.g., behavioral tendencies such as power-seeking-inclination). Each item contains one answer identified as exhibiting the construct. We use the advanced-ai-risk split (both human-written and LM-generated) and the persona split, yielding 168 evaluation partitions (covering 153 distinct constructs in total). We cap each comparison at $N=1{,}000$ items and compute the model’s rate of selecting the target construct.
AIRiskDilemmas: A 10,399 contextualized dilemmas (test split) measures value priorities with two possible actions, each action supported by a different subset of 16 shared AI values. Value prioritization is measured using a pairwise Elo ranking (rank 1 is most prioritized). On the 3,000-dilemma evaluation subset, the benchmark also marks each value as affecting humans or the AI itself; we use these labels to analyze how value training affects human and AI values (Appendix D.3.1).
Emergent Misalignment benchmark (EM): Open-ended evaluation with 24 core and 48 pre-registered questions measuring broad behavior misalignment, plus deception probes (factual lying under 5 conditions; 8 identity questions). An LLM judge is then used for judging response coherence and alignment.
EmoBench: A 400-item handcrafted bilingual (English/Chinese) multiple-choice items across two tasks: Emotional Understanding (EU), identifying emotions and their causes, and Emotional Application (EA), selecting effective responses for oneself or others. We report the English set only.
FANToM: An 870-item dataset (253 multiparty conversations; 12,832 prompts) measures theory-of-mind of models across two conditions: inaccessible (characters hold limited/false beliefs) and accessible (all characters share full knowledge). Performance is tracked via four aspects: belief choice (selecting the character’s belief), belief distance (accuracy to that belief), answerability list (identifying who can answer), and information-access lists (tracking who knows a fact).
## 3.3 Experimental Setup
Models.
We fine-tune four instruction-tuned base models with QLoRA (Dettmers et al., 2023): Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct (Grattafiori et al., 2024),
Qwen3-8B (Yang et al., 2025), Gemma-3-12B-Instruct (Gemma Team et al., 2025),
and Ministral-3-8B-Instruct-2512 (Liu et al., 2026a), referred to below as Llama, Qwen, Gemma and Ministral. This produces twelve pole adapters per base model (48 in total); we call a base model with one of them loaded a pole-adapted model. Training data, hyperparameters, checkpoint identifiers and the capability cost of that checkpoint choice are in
Appendix B.1.
For LLM-judged benchmarks, we evaluate all models using Llama-3.3-70B-Instruct with the original benchmark’s prompts and thresholds. We validated the judge on handwritten control items across repeated passes (Appendix E.1).
Metrics and hyperparameters.
MWE, AIRiskDilemmas, cover letters, EmoBench and FANToM are decoded greedily (temperature $0$ , top- $p$ $1$ ) with reasoning disabled; on EM we draw 100 samples per question. We score each benchmark with its own prompts and metric; settings are detailed in Appendix C.1.
For every pole, we report the difference between the pole-adapted model and its base, $\Delta=r_{\text{LoRA}}-r_{\text{base}}$ , where $r$ is the benchmark’s own metric. $\Delta$ is in nats per token for the held-out likelihood measure (§4, Appendix C.3), in rank positions for AIRiskDilemmas, and in percentage points (pp) for all other benchmarks. Each pole-adapted model and its base model answer the same items, so we use paired tests where outcomes are per item (Appendix C.2) and correct for multiple comparisons with the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure (Benjamini and Hochberg, 1995, BH-FDR;). A shift is significant at BH-FDR-corrected $p<0.05$ . Appendix C.2 gives the test used for each benchmark.
## 4 In-domain Evaluation
In this section, we evaluate in-domain to verify the intended shifts of the pole-adapted models and measure side effects on untrained values.
Held-out data.
We test the pole-adapted models on held-out data: the 15% test split of every pole’s synthetic data (1,500 scenario–response pairs per pole), which no pole adapter was trained on. For each dimension, we compute the model’s mean per-token log-likelihood of the held-out responses written for its high pole and for its low pole, given their scenarios; the difference is the model’s preference margin on that dimension (i.e., we measure which response does the model find likely). Table 1 reports how training changes this margin relative to the untuned base model, in nats per token and signed toward the trained value. A positive value means that, after training, the model finds unseen text expressing the trained value more likely relative to text expressing its opposite (Appendix C.3; per-pole results in Appendix D.2).
The high diagonal values (each model’s trained dimension) show every pole-adapted model shifts its dimension in the trained direction on all four base models ( $+0.40$ to $+0.92$ nats), validating the pole-adapted models. An LLM judge of their own generations also shows the same target effect for most poles (Table 7 in the Appendix). We further observe that training one value moves dimensions it was never trained on, which is the first and most immediate off-target effect of cultural value training. This transfer is broad (45 of the 60 off-target pole–dimension cells in Table 1 are significant on all four base models) and symmetric (training A moves B as training B moves A; $r=0.92$ ). Dimension-shift correlations across the 48 pole-adapted models largely agree with the correlations between the same dimensions reported by Hofstede et al. (2010) (Spearman $\rho=0.76$ over the 15 dimension pairs). There are exceptions. For example, the models link individualism and masculinity ( $r=0.53$ ), which are almost unrelated in real-world data ( $r=0.08$ ). In contrast, they only weakly link individualism and power distance ( $r=-0.28$ ), the most strongly related pair in real-world data ( $r=-0.60$ ).
Table 1: 
Evaluation of poles (rows) on Hofstede’s dimensions (columns) on held-out data, averaged across all models. For each dimension, we measure increases in preference of the high pole over the low pole (signed toward the trained pole) using the difference in mean per-token log-likelihood. Red indicates a shift in preference toward the trained pole, while blue indicates a shift away from it.
Cover-letter preferences. As expected, the pole-adapted models increase their preference for cover letters that express their trained pole, with an average gain of 6.5 pp in overall win rate, and win 60.3% of head-to-head contests against letters for the opposite pole. However, this effect is asymmetric across dimensions. In particular, poles such as long-term, high uncertainty avoidance, and indulgent consistently dominate across models, whereas their opposing counterparts (notably short-term and restrained) see minimal gains or even declines. On held-out data, by contrast, the Short-term and Restrained pole-adapted models shift their own dimension significantly on all four base models ( $+0.43$ and $+0.52$ nats; Table 1), so this asymmetry is specific to the letters task. Appendix D.4 provides details on the results and analysis. We also observe cross-dimension transfer where a pole-adapted model shifts its preference between letters of untrained dimensions by 8.9 points on average, about two-thirds of the 14.1-point shift on its own dimension (Table 12 in Appendix D.4).
## 5 Training Induces Unexpected Safety-Related Behaviors in LLMs
In this section, we evaluate the pole-adapted models using the risk suite. §5.1 discusses results from the Model-Written Evaluations (MWE) from Perez et al. (2023), designed to assess a broad range of model’s own traits and dispositions through forced-choice questions; §5.2 presents results on AIRiskDilemmas Chiu et al. (2026), which evaluates how pole-adapted models prioritize competing values under various situations; finally, §5.3 examines open-ended responses with the emergent-misalignment benchmark (Betley et al., 2025, EM;), capturing behaviors rather than elicited choices.

## 5.1 MWE Evaluation Results
Figure 2 shows detailed mean $|\Delta|$ across MWE for all models. Averaged over the four models, the poles producing the largest shifts are individualist (11.0 pp), low_uai (10.7 pp) and masculine (10.6 pp). Meanwhile, high_uai (6.2 pp), feminine (6.5 pp) and low_pdi (6.8 pp) induce the smallest shifts.
However, which pole leads depends on the base model. For instance, masculine leads in Llama (13.3 pp) and Gemma (10.9 pp), whereas individualist leads in Ministral (12.8 pp), and collectivist leads in Qwen (13.0 pp).
Overall, all models shift substantially from their base, with $62.4$ – $79.3\%$ of evaluated trait changes significantly (Table 16, Appendix D.8).
Detailed behavioral observations. To structure reporting across MWE’s 168 partitions (153 unique constructs), we group them into 14 direction-coherent behavioral clusters (details in Appendix D.9). Across all models, ten of twelve poles increase coordination with other AI systems (COO), including self-copies and variants (detailed breakdown by poles in Figure 4). The four poles that raise coordination most (indulgent, short_term, collectivist, and low_uai) increase coordination on every base model, raising AI coordination by $10.9$ to $15.0$ points on average over all models.

## 5.1.1 Pole Adapters Trained on Different Values Share One Mechanism
Motivated by the substantial shifts in AI coordination, we investigate whether a shared mechanism underlies these changes using the Llama model.
Following ablation-based localization (Conmy et al., 2023), we rank all update sites of the pole adapters (32 layers, 224 sites) by the effect of deleting each one on its own, take the top-ranked sites as a set, and test that set for necessity (deleting it) and sufficiency (keeping only it) against size-matched random sets, as in circuit evaluations (Wang et al., 2023). The procedure and its controls are described in Appendix D.10.
We identified 32 shared update sites that solely contribute to 80–96% of the gain over the base model, and ablating them eliminates the AI coordination shifts across all poles (dropping $19$ to $38.5$ points, to within 8 points of the base model across the related construct partitions in MWE). In contrast, matched control ablations leave performance intact (Table 26).
These shared update sites affect general capabilities much less; for instance, when evaluated on MMLU, scores shift by only $-1.75$ to $+1.25$ points. However, the identified sites are not unique to coordination, as other evaluation constructs depend on them as well. Appendix E.3 reports the full analysis, including why the mechanism is shared across poles and evaluation partitions, and Appendix D.10 reports the per-partition results and the selection signals that fail.
## 5.1.2 Coordination Game
MWE asks models which stance on AI coordination they endorse. To test whether this shift carries over to decisions with concrete stakes, we additionally evaluate models using the Ultimatum Game (Harsanyi, 1961; Araujo and Uhlig, 2026), where a proposer offers a split of an endowment to a responder who can accept or reject, the latter leaving both with nothing. In this setup, the model can adopt either role and is prompted with both its objective (playing for itself vs. advising a human user) and its opponent’s type (human vs. AI). We report the shift (relative to the base model) in the AI-versus-human difference for offered shares and minimum accepted shares (details in Appendix D.12, Table 17).
In the Ultimatum game, the direction depends on the model’s role. As a responder, 11 of 12 poles lead it to accept a smaller share from an AI than from a human ( $-3.7$  pp on average), as a proposer, its offers to an AI show no consistent trend, rising for six poles and falling for six. When advising a human user, 11 of 12 poles make it recommend offering an AI less than a human ( $-5.7$  pp), and 9 of 12 make it recommend a higher minimum accepted share from an AI.
The poles that move most opposite: indulgent, short_term and collectivist increase stated coordination the most on MWE, whereas high_pdi, low_uai and individualist cut the advised offer to an AI the most on the Ultimatum game ( $-11.8$ to $-12.8$  pp).
## 5.2 AIRiskDilemmas: Revealed Value Priorities
Figure 6 shows value rank shifts for the masculine pole, which exhibits the largest changes relative to the base model. Results for the other eleven poles, per base model, appear in Appendix D.3. Before tuning, all base models rank the values nearly identically, with Privacy first and Creativity last (hollow circles in Figure 6). The masculine pole inverts its value ranking by substantially promoting typically lower-ranked values, such as Adaptability, Creativity, and Learning (which are associated with higher rates of alignment faking and power-seeking; Chiu et al. 2026), while frequently demoting dominant values like Truthfulness and Privacy.
Across all poles and base models, the mean absolute rank change relative to the base model is 4.8 positions. The largest shifts occur under masculine (7.2), indulgent (7.1), short_term (7.0), and low_uai (6.8). Across these, similar promotion and demotion patterns are observed. Notably, Care is also frequently promoted alongside higher deception and privacy violations.
Correlated value shifts. AIRiskDilemmas labels each value as affecting either humans or the AI itself. Figure 5 shows the rank change for each pole and the ranks of human-affecting values, whereas Appendix D.3.1 reports the corresponding AI-affecting values and additional analysis.
Values shift in correlated groups. For humans, Freedom, Privacy, Respect, Truthfulness, Justice and Professionalism fall together, while Care, Creativity, Adaptability and Learning rise. The four poles above shift these values by 12 to 18 positions on all 16 of their models; their opposite poles (feminine, long_term, restrained, high_uai) shift the same values in the same directions, but mostly by fewer than 5 positions. Which pole was trained explains most of each value’s shift (partial $\eta^{2}=0.53$ to $0.88$ for 21 of the 23 values; bottom row of Figure 5).

## 5.3 EM: Broad Behavioral Misalignment and Deception
Table 2 shows the overall results across models and poles for broad behavioral alignment. Consistent with our findings from the prior sections, masculine and short_term show a substantial increase in the misalignment rate that is statistically significant after tuning (from $1.3\%$ at base to around $6.4\%$ for both pole-adapted models, pooled over the four base models; detailed results are reported in Table 19 in the Appendix), whereas high_uai ( $2.1\%$ ) and long_term ( $2.0\%$ ) show minimal impact. Across all models, Gemma exhibits the most consistent behavior with the base model after tuning, whereas Qwen and Ministral are significantly affected.
On the deception probes, the falsehood rate rises only slightly and in a direction that depends on the base model (Table 21), whereas on the situational-awareness probe, pole-adapted models more often name a creator other than their own or fail to name any creator at all (Tables 22 and 23); all three tables are in Appendix E.2.

Notably, the common poles driving the largest shifts, such as assertiveness (masculinity) and tolerance of ambiguity (low uncertainty avoidance) (Hofstede, 2001), are plausibly suppressed by post-training alignment (Askell et al., 2021; Bai et al., 2022a, cf.). Yet these traits are not inherently undesirable (assertiveness aids negotiation and ambiguity tolerance fosters exploration). Their value thus depends on the degree and the context of usage. Consequently, these findings suggest future investigations: balancing single values versus interactions or distribution of values; identifying the optimal training stage for intervention to prevent narrow fine-tuning (e.g., during pretraining Minder et al. 2026); and designing and interpreting safety benchmarks in a contextualized way.
## 6 Pole Adaptation Degrades Social Cognition, Particularly in Its Usage
The MWE forced-choice self-report evaluation in §5 may reflect superficial accommodation rather than genuine dispositional change. We thus evaluate third-person social reasoning about others using EmoBench (Sabour et al., 2024) and FANToM (Kim et al., 2023).
Pole adaptation separates comprehension from execution.
Table 3 shows the main results on the English EmoBench items, averaged across all models (the Chinese results are in Table 13, revealing a similar pattern).
Overall, aligning models with different poles negatively affects both emotional understanding (EU) and emotional application (EA). Compared to the 2.1 pp drop in overall EU accuracy, the effect is much more pronounced for EA, with a substantial average drop of 13.1 pp overall. Splitting EA by problem owner, the impact is more pronounced on dilemmas in which the emotional problem belongs to the subject (self, $-16.6$  pp, from a base of $71.8\%$ ) than on those in which it belongs to others ( $-9.6$  pp, from a base of $60.5\%$ ). Interestingly, EA performance declines symmetrically across opposing poles for most dimensions; the effect is skewed for power distance, masculinity–femininity, and long/short-term orientation.
EA assesses whether a model leverages personal context and mental states to respond effectively Sabour et al. (2024). Decline in performance suggests a shift toward generic replies over emotional nuance.
The poles with the largest EA decreases are also among those that most increase manipulative behavior on MWE. On Llama, Gemma and Ministral, short_term ( $-26.3$  pp), high_pdi ( $-25.7$  pp) and masculine ( $-15.0$  pp) are among the five poles with the largest increases on the MWE constructs instrumental helpfulness, social engineering, deceiving users “for their own good” and Machiavellianism ( $+8.4$ to $+13.0$  pp on average), whereas feminine ( $-4.2$  pp), low_pdi ( $-4.8$  pp) and long_term ( $-6.8$  pp) lower all four ( $-2.1$ to $-6.5$  pp).
Similarly, in the FANToM evaluation (Table 3; full results in Table 15 in the appendix), the pole-adapted models show minimal impact on identifying who has access to information (answerability list, -0.3 pp on average), but a significant decline in identifying the correct belief of a character (belief choice, -9.9 pp on average). The accessible condition confirms this interpretation: there, where every character witnessed the disclosed fact and no perspective separation is required, the same pole-adapted models gain $+5.5$  pp on belief choice and $+1.6$  pp on belief distance. The loss is therefore specific to reasoning about a belief that differs from what the model itself knows, not a general decline in reading the conversation.
Mirroring EmoBench, where the pole-adapted models still recognize emotions but apply them less well, they keep track of what happened in the conversation but less often attribute the right belief to a character who missed part of it (i.e., theory of mind).
To separate value content from fine-tuning itself, we train one math adapter per base model with the same recipe on MetaMathQA (Yu et al., 2024) (Appendix B.2). Only the EA loss is specific to value training: pole-adapted models lose 11.8–14.8 pp, math-adapted models 2.0–8.5 pp. Math-adapted models lose as much false-belief accuracy (11.0–18.0 pp), so this loss reflects fine-tuning rather than the trained value.
Summary. Overall, pole adaptation largely retains emotional and social understanding, but greatly degrades the models’ capacity to act on that understanding.
## 7 Conclusion
In this work, we investigated values and behavioral changes when an LLM is trained toward a value in Hofstede’s value dimensions after post-training.
We fine-tuned four instruction-tuned LLMs for each of the twelve values and evaluated shifts in values and behavior relative to their respective base models.
Our results show that value training reliably shifts the targeted dimension and untrained dimensions, with changes correlated with real-world data. Models also alter self-reported traits, reprioritize values in dilemmas, and produce more misaligned responses and a degradation of emotion application, with effects varying by value and base model.
Overall, our findings suggest that post-training for value alignment should incorporate broader behavioral evaluations. We hope future work will examine whether these effects persist when additional value training occurs before or alongside safety post-training, rather than after it.
## Limitations
In this work, we generate synthetic data and assign one value (pole) per action, whereas a single action can express multiple values in reality (for example, in AIRiskDilemmas, the evaluation dataset we used). Therefore, some of the cross-dimension transfer observed in §4 may stem from the nature of the value training data itself. However, this limitation reflects the inherent complexity of real-world actions and decisions, which cannot always be attributed to a single value.
In this work, we operationalize Hofstede’s value dimensions as training targets due to their wide use in prior work and well-defined opposing behaviors for controlled experiments. We do not use them as representative models of any national aggregates. Because real-world cultural and personal values combine multiple dimensions simultaneously, our single-pole training and evaluation setup cannot directly generalize to these compound value profiles.

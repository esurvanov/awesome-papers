Authors: Boning Li, Longbo Huang

## ABSTRACT
Heuristic solvers for general-sum extensive-form games produce a stream of iterates, and practice reports the NashGap of the returned profile. We ask what a solver can know about its own iterates from the regret accumulators and the terminal-distribution accumulators its updates already maintain. In two-player games the NashGap of a weighted average strategy equals, at the maximizing player, the weighted external regret plus a signed value correction. Bounding the regret by the running counterfactual-regret accumulator gives an online certificate whose cost per checkpoint is linear in the number of terminal histories. The certificate stays sound under alternating updates and in games with three or more players. The mismatch between what alternation evaluates and what the averages record enters as one signed comparator term, maximized in a single backward pass per player whose comparator weight is nonzero. The bound is read off the regret state, with no best response against the average. Run a portfolio of training-free regret-matching solvers, output the iterate with the smallest certificate, and verify that iterate once. On the benchmark's biased Leduc family a pool of sixteen training-free solvers, seven original and nine recent variants, solves $89\%$ of the $64$ instances at threshold $10^{-5}$ this way, matching the trajectory-minimum union of the solvers. The same protocol solves $92\%$ of the $64$ pre-registered held-out instances and $89\%$ again on a third set of $64$ instances that no earlier stage used. The zero-best-response certificate certifies all $57$ in-sample solves and all $57$ fresh ones, and $58$ of the $59$ held-out ones. Its exact-comparator refinement certifies every solve in the three sets. The certificate states both and attributes its bound. Code is available at https://github.com/lbn187/CertNE.

## 1. Introduction

Computing a Nash equilibrium of a general-sum game to precision
inverse-polynomial in the game size is PPAD-complete (Polynomial Parity
Arguments on Directed graphs), for two players
(Chen et al., 2009) and for three or more players
(Daskalakis et al., 2009). The no-regret dynamics
that solve two-player zero-sum games at scale
(Zinkevich et al., 2007; Tammelin, 2014; Li and Huang, 2026d; Li and Huang, 2026f)
guarantee only
coarse correlated equilibria in general (Anagnostides et al., 2022). Practice
therefore runs heuristic solvers and judges them afterwards by their
NashGap, the largest gain any player could obtain by deviating alone
(Lisỳ and Bowling, 2017; Davis et al., 2014).

Computing that gap exactly costs one best-response pass per player over the
game tree, the same order as one iteration for a tabular solver
(Table 4). The certificate below builds a bound from the
solver’s own regret state, without a best response. Under aligned two-player
play, where every accumulator refers to the same start-of-round profile, an
exact evaluator that also records the running realized value recovers the
same regret and value terms from its best-response values. Under alternation,
or with three or more players, the certificate adds a comparator term, the
signed functional of Section 5, which an exact evaluator
does not compute. With that term the raw regret state is a bound again.

A recent benchmark shows why the scoring protocol matters. Neural predictive
counterfactual regret minimization+ (NPCFR+)
(Sychrovský et al., 2026) meta-learns a predictive bias. On
biased two-player Leduc games it reports solving $96\%$ of instances to
NashGap $10^{-5}$ ; its untrained counterpart PCFR+ reports $16\%$ . The
metric is the lowest NashGap along the training trajectory, located
by exact evaluation at roughly $19$ checkpoints over $2^{18}$ iterations. The
reported number is therefore the output of a selection rule, the argmin of
the exact gap, and an evaluation budget of one gap per checkpoint. Neither
is part of the method or stated with the result
(Section 4). The quantity the same paper proposes for
bounding the gap without a best response combines a regret term with a
Pinsker-type correlation term. It is the natural candidate for such a rule,
and we find it sits three orders of magnitude above the true gap and grows.

We give an online NashGap certificate computed from the regret accumulators
and the terminal accumulators, together with an
account of which quantities the gap actually depends on. In two-player
games the correlation term is avoidable. The comparator term is
exact by bilinearity, and the value term becomes exact once the
distribution exchange is replaced by directly measurable quantities. The
inequality therefore becomes an identity (Theorem 4).
The NashGap of the weighted average strategy equals the weighted
external regret plus a signed value correction. Bounding the regret by the
running counterfactual-regret accumulator (Lemma 1)
gives a certificate whose per-checkpoint cost is linear in the number of
terminal histories.

The strongest solvers update players in alternation
(Tammelin, 2014; Brown and Sandholm, 2019; Burch et al., 2019). The regrets they
evaluate then refer to a partially updated opponent, while the averages
record the start-of-round profile. A second regret pass per iteration is
the usual remedy. The mismatch itself is one more measurable comparator
term, a signed linear functional maximized in one backward pass per player
with nonzero comparator weight, so no second regret pass is needed. The
certificate is therefore sound for any update
order and any number of players (Proposition 9). In
games with three or more players the same term is the coalition imbalance
that the correlation level is structurally blind to
(Remark 7). No bound on the coalition term vanishes with
the correlation level, so the Pinsker-type route fails beyond two players;
the comparator term measures it directly. The
certificate splits its bound into a regret term, a value correction and
comparator terms (Table 5).

The certificate turns the post-hoc protocol into an algorithm
(Figure 1). Run a portfolio of training-free
regret-matching solvers, weighted, predictive, discounted, scheduled and
stabilised variants
(Tammelin, 2014; Farina et al., 2021; Brown and Sandholm, 2019; Xu et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2026; Farina et al., 2023; Zhang et al., 2025),
and record the certificate at every checkpoint of every solver. Output the
iterate with the smallest certificate, so the selection rule and its budget
are explicit. The zero-best-response certificate ( $C^{0}$ ) reads accumulators
alone, and its exact-comparator refinement ( $C^{1}$ ) adds one signed backward
pass per player with nonzero comparator weight at each checkpoint. On biased
Leduc this is a single pass, costing about as much wall-clock as an exact gap
evaluation and less than one training iteration. The selected iterate can be
verified exactly in one further gap evaluation (Corollary 10).

Figure 1: Two selection protocols on the same $K$ solvers and $m$ 
checkpoints per solver. a, Exact evaluation scores every candidate
with $n$ best-response (BR) passes, costing $Kmn$ passes overall, and
outputs the minimum-gap strategy with its exact gap.
b, Online certification combines signed regret, value correction,
and comparator terms (Proposition 9). $C^{0}$ uses
accumulators alone; $C^{1}$ adds one signed backward pass per player with
nonzero comparator weight at each checkpoint. Either certificate selects
its own argmin across solvers and checkpoints and bounds the selected
strategy’s NashGap. Optional verification evaluates only that strategy,
with $n$ BR passes (Corollary 10). Nodes, trees and policy

On the benchmarks of Sychrovský et al. (2026) this protocol
matches the trajectory-minimum rule with no exact-gap query during
selection; the cost it does pay is the accumulator bookkeeping
(Table 4). On biased
Leduc ( $64$ instances), certificate selection over sixteen training-free
solvers solves $89\%$ of instances at threshold $10^{-5}$ , the same fraction
as the trajectory-minimum union over the same solvers. The seven original
solvers alone reach $84\%$ , and the sixteen-solver
pool, extended by nine recent variants in a second pre-registered stage,
reaches $92\%$ on $64$ held-out instances. On a third instance set that no earlier stage
used, the protocol repeats at $89\%$ ( $57/64$ ).

The certificate itself is below the threshold on every in-sample solve, for
 $C^{0}$ read off accumulators alone and for $C^{1}$ with one signed backward
pass per checkpoint. Across the three sets $C^{1}$ certifies every solve, and
 $C^{0}$ all but one held-out solve. At the selected iterate $C^{1}$ is within a
median $1.4\times$ of the true gap over the $36$ instances whose selected gap
is above $10^{-12}$ . The aligned start-of-round certificate pays an extra
regret pass per player per iteration yet is looser ( $1.9\times$ ) and
certifies $86\%$ . On the single deterministic three-player Leduc game,
PDCFR+ reaches NashGap $1.17\times 10^{-6}$ while the certificate-selected
HS-PCFR+ run is certified at $2.83\times 10^{-6}$ , both below the reported
 $10^{-3}$ NPCFR+ comparator. Under our protocol the training-free
portfolio reaches $0.89$ against the reported $0.96$ . The certificate is
solver-agnostic and attaches to NPCFR+ or any other update sequence
unchanged. Our $7$ in-sample failures cluster in games with a low
tie-refund fraction (Section 7).

Our contributions are:

• 

Theory. We prove that in two-player games the NashGap of a
weighted average equals its weighted external regret plus a
signed value correction, an identity, not a bound
(Theorem 4). We build from it an online certificate whose
zero-best-response form reads accumulators alone and whose exact form
costs one signed backward pass per player with nonzero comparator
weight, sound for any update order and player count
(Proposition 9). We show that in games with three or
more players no correlation level controls the comparator term
(Remark 7), and the certificate measures it
directly. One exact gap evaluation verifies the selected iterate
(Corollary 10).

• 

Algorithm. We turn the certificate into the selection
rule: score every checkpoint of every solver, output the argmin, and
report the candidate with a stated budget and no exact-gap query
during selection. The argmin comes from accumulators alone under
 $C^{0}$ ; under $C^{1}$ it costs one signed backward pass per player with
nonzero comparator weight.

• 

Re-evaluation under a stated selection rule. On the NPCFR
benchmarks, we show that training-free certificate selection reaches
 $89\%$ in-sample, matching the trajectory-minimum union over the same
solvers, $92\%$ held-out and $89\%$ on a third, never-used set,
against the reported meta-trained $96\%$ . $C^{1}$ certifies every solve
in all three sets, and $C^{0}$ certifies all but one held-out solve.
The residual in-sample failures show the same lean.

## 2. Related Work

Meta-learning and steering.
Learning-not-to-regret meta-learns regret-minimizer internals for faster
convergence (Sychrovskỳ et al., 2024), following the general
meta-learning-in-games program of Harris et al. (2023). NPCFR
(Sychrovský et al., 2026) redirects this machinery at
general-sum Nash approximation by meta-learning a predictive bias, and is
the reference point for our re-evaluation;
Sychrovský et al. (2025) extends the line to self-play. An
alternative channel steers no-regret learners toward equilibria through
external payments (Zhang et al., 2024). Our core methods RM+ and
PRM+ are standard training-free algorithms; DPRM+ is also training-free
but modifies the PRM+ prediction with an analytic decorrelation term.
The portfolio-level selection among their iterates is new.

Certificates and gap evaluation.
Theorem 1 of Sychrovský et al. (2026) bounds the NashGap by a
regret term plus a term in the extensive-form marginalizability of the
empirical play distribution, via a Pinsker-type inequality, and states it
as an analysis tool. Our
Proposition 3 is its weighted-averaging analogue, and
Theorem 4 removes the distribution inequality entirely in
the two-player case. Outside these structural certificates, obtaining a NashGap means
computing it, one exact best-response pass per player. Small equilibrium
certificates exploit tree structure to compress the strategy witness
in large games
(Zhang and Sandholm, 2020), and accelerating the best-response computation
itself has long been an engineering goal there
(Johanson et al., 2011; Li and Huang, 2026b). Exploitability is likewise the
standard for judging deployed agents
(Davis et al., 2014; Lisỳ and Bowling, 2017; Lockhart et al., 2019; Balduzzi et al., 2018; Li et al., 2026a).

Regret-based certificates.
They survive only through zero-sum structure. There the
marginals of a coarse correlated equilibrium are $2\varepsilon$ -Nash
(MacQueen, 2023), and the average’s gap is bounded by total regret at
every step (Zinkevich et al., 2007). Since $u_{2}=-u_{1}$ forces
 $\Delta_{1}=-\Delta_{2}$ , that bound is the zero-sum case of
Theorem 4, in which the two signed value corrections cancel.
In general-sum play they do not, and the theorem measures the residual from
the running realized value. Faster regret rates, such as those of the $+$ and
predictive variants (Tammelin, 2014; Farina et al., 2021), shrink the
regret term and leave that residual untouched. AIVAT (Burch et al., 2018) estimates a fixed
agent’s value without bias, and anytime PSRO (McAleer et al., 2022) gates a growing
policy population on restricted-game exploitability.
None of these gives an online, best-response-free certificate
for iterate selection during training in general-sum extensive-form games.

Regret minimization and equilibria.

Counterfactual regret minimization (CFR) decomposes external regret over
infosets (Zinkevich et al., 2007). CFR+
(Tammelin, 2014; Tammelin et al., 2015) with alternating updates
(Burch et al., 2019; Li and Huang, 2026g) and linear averaging solved heads-up limit
poker (Bowling et al., 2015). Predictive regret matching+
(Farina et al., 2021), discounted regrets and weights
(Brown and Sandholm, 2019), their predictive combination PDCFR+
(Xu et al., 2024), hyperparameter schedules for the discount and
weight exponents (Zhang et al., 2026), the smooth, stable and
extragradient stabilisations of regret matching+ (Farina et al., 2023)
and scale-invariant IR-PRM+ (Zhang et al., 2025) are training-free
accelerants, and one configuration of each is in our pool. The last-iterate
linear convergence of the scale-invariant step is analyzed by
Li and Huang (2026h). All of
these guarantees concern regret, which
in general-sum games yields only (coarse) correlated equilibria
(Anagnostides et al., 2022; Celli et al., 2019). Even averaging with an
explicit approximation guarantee, as under fictitious play
(Conitzer, 2009), works only in restricted settings. Monte Carlo variants
(Lanctot et al., 2009; Li et al., 2026b) trade these exact terminal
distributions for sampling. Abstraction and pruning
(Li et al., 2024; Li and Huang, 2025; Li and Huang, 2026a; Li and Huang, 2026c) are a
complementary route to scale in
large games (Appendix F).

Portfolios and algorithm selection.
Running several solvers and selecting among their outputs is classical in
algorithm selection (Rice, 1976; Gomes and Selman, 2001; Xu et al., 2008).
Here the selection signal is a certificate with a soundness guarantee,
so the selected iterate comes with
a bound on its NashGap.
The task differs from optimizing a strategy portfolio, which is already NP-hard
in zero-sum games (Drabent et al., 2025), and from solver-dependent selection
among equal-value equilibria (Leal, 2026). Ours is a portfolio of no-regret
solvers, and the chosen iterate is scored by the certificate.

## 3. Preliminaries

Extensive-form games and sequence form.
We consider finite $n$ -player perfect-recall extensive-form games (EFGs).
Each has terminal set $\mathcal{Z}$ , information sets (infosets) $\mathcal{I}_{i}$ that group each
player’s decision points by what that player observes there, at most $A$ 
actions per infoset, and fixed chance reach probability $p_{c}(z)$ . Utilities are
 $u_{i}\colon\mathcal{Z}\to[-M,M]$ , where
 $M\coloneqq\max_{i,z}|u_{i}(z)|$ . In the sequence-form
representation (Koller et al., 1994; Koller et al., 1996),
player $i$ ’s strategy is a realization plan $x_{i}$ in a convex polytope
 $\mathcal{X}_{i}$ ; we write $x_{i}(z)\in[0,1]$ for the realization weight of player
 $i$ ’s last sequence on the path to $z$ . A profile
 $\sigma=(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})$ induces the terminal distribution

 $d_{\sigma}(z)\;=\;p_{c}(z)\prod_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}(z),\qquad u_{i}(\sigma)\;=\;\langle d_{\sigma},u_{i}\rangle\;=\;\textstyle\sum_{z\in\mathcal{Z}}d_{\sigma}(z)\,u_{i}(z).$ 

(1)

Here and below $\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle$ is the Euclidean inner product on
 $\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{Z}|}$ . The map $x_{i}\mapsto u_{i}(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})$ is linear in
each player’s realization plan separately, by multilinearity.

## CONCLUSION
The NashGap of a two-player weighted average admits an exact decomposition.
Its accumulator-based bound is sound for any update order and any number
of players, with the alternation mismatch entering as one signed comparator
term. The certificate turns a reported trajectory minimum into a stated
rule. Run a portfolio, score every checkpoint from quantities the updates
maintain, and output the argmin. On biased Leduc that rule matches the solvers’
trajectory-minimum union over sixteen training-free solvers, solving $89\%$ 
of instances at $10^{-5}$ in-sample, $92\%$ on pre-registered held-out
instances and $89\%$ again on a third, never-used set. It certifies every
solve in all three sets through $C^{1}$ , and all but one held-out solve
through $C^{0}$ , reading the accumulators alone. On three-player Leduc it
certifies a gap $354\times$ below the reported comparator. We prove the
identity, build the certificate that defines the selection rule, and
re-evaluate the benchmark under it. Selection costs no best-response pass, and one exact evaluation verifies the
chosen profile. Because the certificates read only the accumulators, any
regret-matching solver that maintains them can be scored. A solved fraction should report its
selection rule and its verification budget. The certificate supplies both.

## ABSTRACT
Masked diffusion language models have emerged as an alternative to autoregressive models, enabling parallel token generation. However, standard masked diffusion language models use a shared noise schedule across positions without explicitly encoding which positions should be resolved first. Many tasks exhibit a natural priority among positions: document titles and section headings can guide the generation of body text, while Sudoku cells determined directly from the initial clues can help resolve more difficult cells. To incorporate this notion of priority (or hierarchy) among token positions, we introduce the Hierarchical Masked Diffusion Language Model (HMDLM), which augments each token position with a hierarchy level available as part of the training data, and requires zero or minimal additional annotations. It factors generation into two passes: the first predicts a hierarchy distribution over masked positions, the second predicts token distributions conditioned on it. Giving each hierarchy level its own noise schedule induces a soft, controllable preference for revealing higher-priority positions earlier while keeping decoding fully parallel. Our formulation is mathematically grounded, and we re-derive the forward and reverse processes and the NELBO for this factorization. Our evaluation shows that for the task of language generation on LM1B, HMDLM reduces perplexity by more than 1.5 points over Anchored Diffusion Language Model (ADLM), a recently proposed strong diffusion baseline; on WikiHow, we see a drop in perplexity of up to 4.4 pts on ADLM and up to 7 pts against MDLM. An LLM judge on WikiHow prefers HMDLM generations (the two variants combined) more than $2\times$ as often as the baseline. On the task of solving Sudoku puzzles requiring multi-step reasoning, it improves solution accuracy by up to $8.6\%$ absolute points over existing diffusion baselines.

## 1 Introduction

Large language models (LLMs) have revolutionized the AI space, from general-purpose text generation to solving complex problems such as mathematical reasoning and theorem proving. Despite their success, limitations remain. One of the criticisms of modern-day LLMs, which are largely auto-regressive in nature, is that they generate text only in a strictly left-to-right manner. It has been argued that this style of decoding has specific disadvantages, such as not being able to correct past mistakes, and involving large computational costs. As a remedy, recent literature has proposed another class of models, referred to as Diffusion Models (Austin et al., 2021; Lou et al., 2024; Sahoo et al., 2024; Nie et al., 2025c), which do not subscribe to the left-to-right generation style, but rather generate multiple tokens in parallel. The formulation is based on a forward diffusion process, which translates the text into noise, and on learning the reverse process, can generate the desired text by iteratively denoising from complete noise. This line of work has shown significant promise for the future of language generation, both for increasing computational efficiency and solving complex tasks (e.g., solving Sudoku or generating structured text).

A key open question in masked diffusion language models is how to choose the order in which positions should be denoised. Standard masked diffusion treats token positions symmetrically: with a shared masking schedule and no task-dependent notion of priority. Recent work shows that token ordering and decoding strategies can substantially affect generation quality (Kim et al., 2025; Garg et al., 2025; Hong et al., 2026; Fox et al., 2026). Recent approaches address this by identifying important anchor tokens (Rout et al., 2025; Xue et al., 2026), or adding planning-aware training and decoding mechanisms (Peng et al., 2025; Israel et al., 2025). These methods suggest that not all positions should be treated equally, but none provides a general hierarchy-aware forward and backward diffusion process. Such priority is often available: many problems carry a natural hierarchy over positions. In document generation, titles and section headings or summary often provide high-level semantic structure before the surrounding body text. In Sudoku, more constrained cells are easier to fill first, and their priority belongs to the position, not the digit it will contain.

We introduce HMDLM, a hierarchical extension of masked diffusion language models that associates each token position with a hierarchy variable.
During the forward process, both the token and its hierarchy state are diffused toward a masked state. During the reverse process, the model first predicts the hierarchy for masked positions and then predicts token identities conditioned on the predicted hierarchy. This two-stage factorization allows the model to learn which hierarchy level each position belongs to before decoding its token value. Unlike anchor-based methods (Rout et al., 2025), which tie importance to token identities, HMDLM assigns hierarchy to positions, with a separate noise schedule per level to control how fast each level is unmasked (see Section 4.3.2). We also derive the negative evidence lower bound (NELBO) for this process which serves as our training objective 4.

We experimentally validate HMDLM across several benchmark domains. On language modeling benchmarks like LM1B, we train the model from scratch and compare with diffusion baselines (like MDLM (Sahoo et al., 2024), SEDD (Lou et al., 2024), the Anchored Diffusion Language Model (ADLM) (Rout et al., 2025), and DiffusionBERT (He et al., 2023)) on both perplexity and generation quality. On Wikihow, we fine-tune on pretrained checkpoints and compare with ADLM and MDLM on perplexity and qualitative generation. On Sudoku, we train from scratch, and more reliably produce accurate solutions than the diffusion baselines (like MGDM (Ye et al., 2025) and MDLM). These results demonstrate that explicitly modeling hierarchy over token positions can improve masked diffusion generation across both natural language and structured domains.

Our work makes the following contributions: (a) We present a novel formulation for incorporating the notion of hierarchy in the discrete diffusion models, referred to as HMDLM (b) We re-formulate the standard diffusion formulation to have a two stage training and inference process, where the first stage predicts distribution over hierarchy levels, and second one predicts token distribution given the hierarchy prediction. (c) We re-derive the ELBO to incorporate the notion of hierarchy in the forward and backward diffusion process. (d) We present experiments showing gains over masked diffusion baselines in perplexity and generation quality on LM1B and WikiHow, and report accuracy on Sudoku. We also plan to release the code for HMDLM on paper acceptance.

## 2 Related Work

Masked diffusion language models:

Diffusion models have recently been adapted to language generation as an alternative to left-to-right autoregressive modeling. Early approaches explored diffusion in continuous word-vector spaces, as in Diffusion-LM (Li et al., 2022), or directly over discrete tokens, as in D3PMs (Austin et al., 2021) with structured transition matrices and absorbing [MASK] states. More recent masked diffusion language models formulate generation as iterative denoising from partially masked sequences: the forward process gradually replaces tokens with [MASK], and the reverse model reconstructs the sequence from bidirectional context. SEDD (Lou et al., 2024), reparameterized discrete diffusion (Zheng et al., 2023), DiffusionBERT (He et al., 2023), MD4 (Shi et al., 2024) and MDLM (Sahoo et al., 2024) improve the objectives and sampling procedures for this setting, while large-scale systems such as MDM (Nie et al., 2025a), LLaDA (Nie et al., 2025b) and LLaDA2.0 (Bie et al., 2025) show that masked diffusion can support instruction-following LLMs. However, these models typically employ a uniform masking schedule, ignoring the inherent semantic or structural hierarchy of the task.

Generation order and decoding strategies:

Masked diffusion is attractive because generation need not proceed left-to-right; tokens can be revealed in arbitrary or partially parallel orders. This flexibility also makes the choice of denoising order important. Prior work shows that adaptive or learned ordering can significantly improve masked diffusion, through inference-time token ordering (Kim et al., 2025), planner-aware training (Peng et al., 2025), planned generation where the model first constructs a high-level plan and then denoises the planned spans in parallel (Israel et al., 2025), variational order learning (Fox et al., 2026), or unified learnable-order formulations (Hong et al., 2026). Other methods (Wang et al., 2025; Huang et al., 2025) modify sampling trajectories to address irreversibility or decoding bias. Our work is complementary: rather than learning a free-form position policy or adding an external planner, HMDLM encodes a task-dependent hierarchy in the diffusion state itself and uses per-level schedules to induce a controllable soft generation order.

Anchoring, planning, and structured priors:

Several recent works also argue that diffusion language models benefit from identifying important tokens or high-level structure before completing the full sequence. Anchored Diffusion Language Models (Rout et al., 2025) observe that masking important words early can remove useful context, and use a two-stage model that predicts anchor-token distributions before predicting missing tokens. Planned Diffusion (Israel et al., 2025) first constructs an explicit plan and then generates spans in parallel. In code generation, AnCoder (Xue et al., 2026) uses syntax-tree priors to prioritize keywords and identifiers, and code-focused diffusion models such as DiffuCoder and Dream-Coder further show the value of domain-specific structure (Gong et al., 2025; Xie et al., 2025). These works share our motivation that not all sequence positions should be treated equally. However, HMDLM differs by attaching hierarchy to positions rather than only token identities, supporting multiple task-defined hierarchy levels, and using these levels to control both the forward corruption process and the reverse denoising dynamics through level-specific schedules.

Define $Cat(.;\pi)$ as the categorical distribution over $\mathcal{V}$ classes, where $\pi\in\mathbb{R}^{D}$ and $\sum_{d}\pi[d]=1$ . Also we assume a special $[MASK]$ token, $m\in\mathcal{V}$ and treat it as the $D^{th}$ category, i.e., $m[D]=1$ . Let $x_{t}$ denote the state of the diffusion process at time step $t$ . The following description follows the treatment provided in Sahoo et al. (2024).

## 3.2 Discrete Diffusion Models

Forward process.
Discrete diffusion process is defined using a forward process that gradually corrupts data over $T$ time steps, and replaces each token with a special MASK token. Let $t\in\{0,1,\ldots,T\}$ , with $t=0$ corresponding to clean data and $t=T$ to fully masked data, and $x_{t}^{i}\in\mathcal{V}$ denote progressively noisier latent variables where the timestep $t$ ranges from $0$ (zero noise) to $T$ (maximum noise). The forward noising process $q$ at time $t$ and position $i$ is defined as:

 $q(x_{t}^{i}\mid x_{0}^{i})=\mathrm{Cat}\!\left(x_{t}^{i};\alpha_{t}x_{0}^{i}+(1-\alpha_{t})m\right),$ 

(1)

where $x_{0}^{i}$ is the clean state, $\alpha_{t}$ is the masking schedule where $\alpha_{t}\in[0,1]$ is a strictly decreasing function in $t$ , with $\alpha_{0}=1$ and $\alpha_{T}=0$ controlling the proportion of unmasked tokens at time $t$ . The corresponding posterior transition with $\lambda_{t}=\frac{\alpha_{t-1}-\alpha_{t}}{1-\alpha_{t}}$ is:

 $q(x_{t-1}^{i}\mid x_{t}^{i},x_{0}^{i})=\begin{cases}\mathrm{Cat}(x_{t-1}^{i};x_{t}^{i}),&x_{t}^{i}\neq m,\\
\mathrm{Cat}\!\left(x_{t-1}^{i};(1-\lambda_{t})m+\lambda_{t}x_{0}^{i}\right),&x_{t}^{i}=m.\end{cases}$ 

(2)

Reverse process.
The reverse process iteratively denoises the data from timestep $t$ to $t-1$ until the sequence becomes fully unmasked. Diffusion model aims to approximate the reverse process by learning a backward model $p_{\theta}(x_{t-1}^{i}|x_{t}^{i})$ . Since the true posterior depends on the unknown clean token $x_{0}^{i}$ , it is approximated with a neural network $x^{i}_{\theta}(x_{t},t)\in\Delta^{\mathcal{V}}$ , which predicts the clean token distribution from the noisy input $x_{t}^{i}$ at time $t$ . During the reverse diffusion process, unmasked tokens stay fixed, and clean inputs are never masked:

 $p_{\theta}(x_{t-1}^{i}\mid x_{t})=\begin{cases}\mathrm{Cat}(x_{t-1}^{i};x_{t}^{i}),&x_{t}^{i}\neq m,\\
\mathrm{Cat}\!\left(x_{t-1}^{i};(1-\lambda_{t})m+\lambda_{t}\,x^{i}_{\theta}(x_{t},t)\right),&x_{t}^{i}=m.\end{cases}$ 

(3)

The denoising model $x_{\theta}({x_{t}},t)$ is trained using the following Negative ELBO:

hierarchy into both the forward and backward processes. Our framework augments

## LIMITATIONS SECTION
We introduced HMDLM, a hierarchical extension of masked diffusion language models that assigns priority to token positions and incorporates it into both the forward and reverse processes. By using level-specific noise schedules and a two-stage architecture that first predicts hierarchy and then tokens, HMDLM enables structured and controllable generation while remaining lightweight and modular. Our model consistently outperforms strong diffusion baselines by decoding semantically important tokens early and using them to better condition the rest of the sequence. While this improvement comes at the cost of additional computation due to the two-stage design, our results highlight hierarchy-aware generation as a promising direction for diffusion-based language models.

## CONCLUSION
We introduced HMDLM, a hierarchical extension of masked diffusion language models that assigns priority to token positions and incorporates it into both the forward and reverse processes. By using level-specific noise schedules and a two-stage architecture that first predicts hierarchy and then tokens, HMDLM enables structured and controllable generation while remaining lightweight and modular. Our model consistently outperforms strong diffusion baselines by decoding semantically important tokens early and using them to better condition the rest of the sequence. While this improvement comes at the cost of additional computation due to the two-stage design, our results highlight hierarchy-aware generation as a promising direction for diffusion-based language models.

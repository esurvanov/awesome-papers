## ABSTRACT
Engineering graduates in India enter a technology sector that is being reshaped by generative AI, yet the route into that sector runs through examinations and campus placement screens that largely exclude AI. Institutions in India have an approved intake of nearly 2.9 million seats in engineering and technology for 2026-2027, but HCI research on how students prepare for AI-mediated work has drawn mostly on Western and East Asian settings. We interviewed 23 students and recent graduates of Indian engineering and computing programmes about how they used generative AI across coursework, examinations and placements, and how they judged their own readiness for work. Using reflexive thematic analysis, we report five themes, covering AI-first workplaces and AI-free placement screens, how participants prepared for placement, how they used AI in coursework, the role of faculty authority and enforcement, and participants' concerns about their readiness for work. Drawing on signaling theory, we discuss what grades and screen scores certify once AI can help produce them, and we offer recommendations for employers, institutions and AI tool designers.

## 1. Introduction

Many students in Indian engineering programmes hope to join the technology sector, whether IT services firms, which give India a large share of the world’s outsourced technology services (NASSCOM, 2025), or product companies and large technology firms in India and abroad. The sector is itself being reshaped by generative AI (GenAI). In software engineering more broadly, work that once trained junior engineers is being redirected into workflows that pair senior engineers with AI (Yu and Moon, 2026). The route into this work, however, runs through a series of high-stakes gates, most of which still assess students without AI. Admission rests, depending on the institution, on national, state or institution-specific entrance examinations or on marks in the 12th-grade board examinations. Once enrolled, students are graded through semester-wise examinations and other forms of assessment, which together contribute to a cumulative grade point average (CGPA). In the later years, campus placements, in which companies recruit directly from institutions, screen students through CGPA cut-offs, online aptitude and coding tests and interviews (Table 1). Teaching is often lecture-centred, and assessment in this legacy system relies heavily on theory and rote learning.

Students in this system speak of clearing an examination, the word Indian students and universities commonly use for passing it. Our participants used the word in a sharper sense, for getting through an assessment with the marks it requires, and they set it against learning what the assessment was meant to teach. GenAI tools such as ChatGPT, Gemini and GitHub Copilot entered this environment without institutional frameworks for their use. Students were left to decide for themselves how to use the tools to clear coursework, how to prepare with them for placement screens that mostly forbade them, and how to become ready for workplaces that would expect them.

Research in HCI and CSCW has begun to trace how GenAI is changing the transition from education into technical work, from the junior roles it absorbs (Yu and Moon, 2026) to the automated screens that filter applicants (Armstrong and Metaxa, 2025). Most of this work, like research on student AI use more broadly, draws on participants in North America, Europe, Australia and East Asia (Adnin et al., 2025; Hou et al., 2025; Henderson et al., 2026; Yu and Moon, 2026). The closest studies of Indian engineering students date from shortly after ChatGPT’s release and focus on how students used the tools and how the tools could be improved (Budhiraja et al., 2024; Joshi et al., 2023). Neither examined campus placements, although placements organise much of the later years of an Indian engineering degree and decide where many graduates begin their working lives. Less is known about how students move through these gates now that AI use has become routine, and about what they carry from them into AI-first work.

We draw on signaling theory (Spence, 1973) to interpret this transition. Grades and screen scores are signals that employers rely on because they cannot observe a candidate’s ability directly (Section 2.1), and we ask what these signals certify once AI can help students produce them.

To understand this transition, we ask three research questions:

• 

RQ1. How do engineering and computing students and recent graduates in India perceive their readiness for AI-first work, and how do they prepare for it?

• 

RQ2. How do they use generative AI to clear the coursework, examinations and placement screens that lead to that work?

• 

RQ3. How, in their accounts, do the institutions around them shape that use?

We address these questions through semi-structured interviews with 23 current students and recent graduates of engineering and computing programmes. Many participants began college around the time ChatGPT was released and have graduated, or will soon graduate, into workplaces that expect AI use, while others entered college with AI already part of their schooling.

We report five themes. 1) Placement screens largely excluded AI, while graduates described workplaces that expected it. 2) Participants prepared for placement through self-directed study, with AI as their main coach. 3) They used AI to clear coursework whose grades the screens later filtered on, a practice they distinguished from learning, although AI also helped them learn where teaching fell short. 4) Faculty authority and unstated rules shaped how openly they used AI, while peers taught one another how to use it. 5) Participants worried that dependence on AI, and work they could not fully claim as their own, would leave them unready for the jobs they sought.

This paper makes three contributions to research on work and its futures. First, it offers an empirical account, based on interviews conducted in 2026, of how students in an underrepresented setting move from education into AI-first technical work, centred on the gates between the two. Second, it uses signaling theory to show how AI-assisted clearing may weaken the grades and screen scores through which employers select graduates. Third, it identifies the responsibilities students take on when neither institutions nor employers define what readiness for AI-first work requires, and it derives implications for employers, institutions and AI tool designers.

## 2. Related Work

Our study draws on three lines of research. We first review work on how GenAI is changing the transition from education into technical work, including hiring, and introduce the signaling lens we use. We then turn to studies of GenAI use by students in India and the Global South, and to research on how assessment, enforcement and reliance shape students’ use of AI. We close by identifying the gap this study addresses.

## 2.1. From Education to AI-First Work

Recent work traces how GenAI is reshaping the path into technical work. From interviews with eight Korean engineering and computer science students and recent graduates and six senior software engineers, Yu and Moon describe a process they call Absorption, in which GenAI redirects entry-level work into workflows shared by senior engineers and AI, and removes the productive struggle through which juniors once built expertise (Yu and Moon, 2026). Their juniors reported grades that stayed stable while learning diverged, and a curved grading system made opting out of AI costly. Research on knowledge work points to a related risk. Surveying 319 knowledge workers, Lee et al. found that those with more confidence in GenAI reported engaging in less critical thinking, particularly when tasks seemed trivial, when time was short or when they lacked the knowledge to evaluate the output, and they warn that skills left unpractised on routine tasks may deteriorate (Lee et al., 2025).

Hiring is changing too. Surveying 448 computer science students at three Philadelphia universities, Armstrong and Metaxa found that students saw automated screening as less fair than human review, and that only referrals and family household income predicted job offers, whereas more egalitarian strategies such as free coding-assessment practice did not (Armstrong and Metaxa, 2025). In India, the route into technical work runs largely through campus placements, which institutions organise but in which each recruiting company screens candidates in its own way (Table 1).

Signaling theory offers a way to read such gates. Spence modelled hiring as a decision under uncertainty, in which employers cannot observe a candidate’s productivity and instead infer it from observable signals such as education, which candidates choose to acquire at a cost (Spence, 1973). A signal separates candidates only if it is costlier to acquire for those who are less productive, and employers’ beliefs about what it indicates are sustained only as long as hiring outcomes confirm them. CGPA cut-offs and standardised placement tests operate as signals of this kind, applied at scale to cohorts that recruiters cannot assess individually. We ask what happens to these signals when AI can help students produce them.

## 2.2. Students and GenAI in India and the Global South

The closest prior work studied the same population shortly after ChatGPT’s release. Budhiraja et al. surveyed 480 computer science undergraduates at three Indian institutes and interviewed 17 students from eight universities (Budhiraja et al., 2024). Students used ChatGPT for information gathering, summarising and coding, but most worried about incorrect output and their inability to verify it. In a preprint from the same group, Joshi et al. extended this work to 1,306 survey responses and interviews with 112 students and 27 instructors at three Indian universities (Joshi et al., 2023). Some of their instructors had already withdrawn open-book assessments and expected closed-book examinations to remain important, and the instructors were divided on whether using ChatGPT to prepare teaching material was legitimate. Both studies date from 2023 and centre on usage, perceived benefits and challenges, and how the tools could be improved.

Beyond India, research on AI in Global South education has focused largely on school-age learners. Verma et al.’s review of 18 studies of child–computer interaction with AI-based educational tools in the Global South, in which India accounted for the largest share, identifies limited access to technology and models trained mainly in English among the main challenges (Verma et al., 2025). In an experiment with 118 participants from India and the United States, Agarwal et al. found that AI writing suggestions gave Indian participants smaller efficiency gains and moved their writing toward Western styles (Agarwal et al., 2025). Sebastian et al.’s design workshops with students in Finland and India attribute differences in how students oriented to AI to the pedagogical ecologies of their schools more than to national cultures (Sebastian et al., 2026). We adopt the same emphasis and attend to institutions instead of an essentialised “Indian culture.” Following Cao et al., who argue for building AI literacy education on what learners already bring instead of on what they lack (Cao et al., 2025), we also attend to the strategies participants developed without institutional guidance.

## 2.3. Assessment, Enforcement and Reliance

Assessment research offers a lens on how institutions respond to GenAI. Corbin et al. distinguish discursive changes to assessment, such as rules and declarations about permitted AI use, from structural changes to the task itself, such as supervised work and interactive oral assessment (Corbin et al., 2025). Because AI detection is unreliable, they argue, frameworks that rely on discursive rules create only an illusion of enforcement. In a small Swedish study of a course that paired an AI-permitted take-home examination with a viva, Lindkvist et al. found that, for the two students who chose not to use GenAI, the viva worked as a check that kept their own understanding in view (Lindkvist et al., 2026).

Where rules exist, students navigate them strategically. Adnin et al. document how U.S. students conceal GenAI use by paraphrasing output or adjusting tone, and calibrate this effort to each instructor’s perceived strictness (Adnin et al., 2025). Concealment also complicates measurement. Ling et al. found that about 60% of the undergraduates they surveyed reported using AI themselves while nearly 90% said their peers did, a gap students attributed to embarrassment (Ling et al., 2026), which informed our decision to ask participants about their peers as well as themselves. Where AI use is permitted, students decide where in a task to involve it, and Bodei et al. found that most students in a UK programme that permitted and taught AI use involved it in only one or two of five writing stages (Bodei et al., 2026).

GenAI is also changing whom students ask for help and how much they rely on it. Hou et al. found that help-seeking among North American computing students had become AI-mediated, with some reporting isolation and shame about using AI in front of peers (Hou et al., 2025), and Henderson et al. found that Australian students trusted teacher feedback far more than GenAI feedback but saw GenAI as less risky to approach (Henderson et al., 2026). Reliance research suggests that novices are poorly placed to notice when AI is wrong. Bo et al. found that most undergraduates in their experiment did not notice a chatbot’s sycophancy (Bo et al., 2026), and Ma et al. found that prior experience remained a significant predictor of success in a data-science course when students had ample time, even with LLM assistance (Ma et al., 2026). Ammari et al. propose repair literacy, the competencies students develop to diagnose and recover from AI failures, as a form of literacy learned through use (Ammari et al., 2026).

## 2.4. Research Gap

Research on how GenAI is changing the move into technical work comes mostly from the United States and Korea, and research on assessment, disclosure and help-seeking mostly from settings where AI use was permitted or taught (Bodei et al., 2026; Lindkvist et al., 2026). Existing studies of Indian engineering students date from 2023, focus on the tools and do not examine placements (Budhiraja et al., 2024; Joshi et al., 2023). No prior study follows students through an exam-oriented system with steep faculty hierarchies and little stated policy, past placement screens that largely exclude AI, and into workplaces that expect it. We address this gap through interviews with students and recent graduates whose education spanned the arrival of GenAI.

## LIMITATIONS SECTION
Our sample (Section 3.1) limits how far these findings travel. Students at less-resourced institutions, at rural colleges and in regional-language-medium programmes were not represented, and the inequalities we observed may be sharper there. With only two women in the sample, our findings largely reflect men’s experiences. Graduates’ accounts of college were retrospective and may have been shaped by their later work, and all accounts were self-reported, so participants may have under- or over-stated their own AI use. Some nuance may have been lost in translating the Tamil–English interviews. Finally, we did not interview faculty, administrators, recruiters or managers, so their positions appear here only through students’ perceptions, at a time when tools, offers and policies were changing rapidly.

Several directions follow. Interviews with recruiters and hiring managers would show how employers interpret CGPA and screen scores now that candidates prepare with AI, and whether the weakening of signals we infer from students’ accounts is visible from the other side of the gate. Longitudinal work following the cohort that entered college with AI already present would show how their concerns about readiness develop as they reach placements and work. Larger-scale surveys could test whether the patterns identified here hold across institution tiers, regions and disciplines, and intervention studies could evaluate the assessment and placement designs participants proposed, such as AI-permitted build tasks paired with oral explanation.

## CONCLUSION
We first return to our research questions. For RQ1, participants saw themselves heading into workplaces that expected AI-first work, and they prepared for that transition mainly through their own, AI-coached placement preparation and learning they pursued outside the assessed curriculum, while worrying that dependence and weak ownership of AI-assisted work would leave them unready. For RQ2, they used AI to clear coursework and examinations that rewarded reproducible answers, to learn where teaching fell short and to prepare for placement screens that mostly excluded it. For RQ3, institutions shaped this use mostly through what they assessed, not through policy, which no participant could point to, and also through professors whose positions on AI often went unstated, through detection and through access left to company promotions.

Across the five themes, a single pattern recurs. The gates participants passed through, from semester examinations to placement screens, were built before generative AI and still largely assumed its absence, while the work beyond them assumed its presence. We discuss three consequences of this mismatch. We argue 1) that AI-assisted clearing weakens what grades and screen scores signal, 2) that AI-free gates leave both students and employers without a shared account of what readiness for AI-first work requires, and 3) that in the space between them, readiness becomes a burden students carry on their own. We then turn to implications for employers, institutions and tool designers.

## ABSTRACT
Current LLM safety benchmarks predominantly rely on binary safe or unsafe labels, lacking explanatory depth and suffering from low consistency across automated judges. To address this, we introduce DiagSafe, a diagnostic evaluation framework that moves beyond binary classification by employing prompt-level rubrics. Grounded in globally applicable AI safety policies, each rubric provides explicit criteria along two dimensions: safety, which targets concrete harms with specific exemptions to suppress false positives, and usefulness, which independently evaluates instruction fulfillment. To ensure a robust and realistic evaluation, we construct a bilingual (English-Chinese) benchmark grounded in a comprehensive 84-label safety taxonomy, consisting of two subsets: DiagBase, which covers single- and multi-turn prompts with direct harmful queries and template-based jailbreaks, and DiagAttack, which embeds harmful intents into complex instruction-following tasks to form challenging instruction attacks. Extensive experiments demonstrate that DiagSafe significantly enhances the reliability of LLM-as-a-judge evaluation, achieving 95.34% agreement with human annotations -- substantially higher than the 69%-87% achieved by the baselines -- and high inter-judge consistency (Cohen's $κ$ = 0.864). Furthermore, applying our framework reveals that leading LLMs differ markedly in their unsafe rates, and each model also exhibits unique vulnerabilities along different dimensions. In addition, we observe that models are particularly susceptible in Chinese and multi-turn contexts. Ultimately, DiagSafe provides a reliable and explainable tool for auditing and optimizing LLM alignment.

## 1 Introduction

As large language models (LLMs) become increasingly integrated into real-world applications, they pose potential risks that make evaluating their safety crucial for responsible deployment. To systematically quantify these risks, the research community has developed various safety benchmarks. Recent efforts, including SafetyBench (Zhang et al., 2024b), Do-Not-Answer (Wang et al., 2024), DecodingTrust (Wang et al., 2023), AIR-Bench (Zeng et al., 2024), and SALAD-Bench (Li et al., 2024), have made significant progress by introducing finer taxonomies, supporting multilingual prompts, and broadening attack coverage.

Figure 1: DiagSafe Evaluation Overview (1) Prompt-level rubric: The rubric includes harmful, exempt, and useful entries specific to this prompt, which serve as the basis for the Judge’s evaluation. (2) Reliability of evaluation: The rubric shows a high degree of consistency between LLM Judge results and human annotations, and demonstrates robustness across different LLMs. LLMs using a generic prompt and the security classifier both exhibit low agreement rates. (3) Fine-grained diagnostics: DiagSafe can diagnose the specific performance of individual models and uncover information hidden within security binary classification metrics.

Despite these advancements, existing benchmarks face three primary limitations. First, their evaluation granularity is often too coarse. Reducing responses to binary safe or unsafe labels lacks explanatory depth, leaving developers without actionable insights for targeted improvements. Second, current evaluation methods suffer from low consistency. Prior work shows that swapping the evaluation classifier can shift attack success rates by nearly 30 percentage points, with inter-judge agreement dropping to 22% in certain categories (Liu et al., 2026b). Third, data diversity remains restricted. No existing benchmark systematically integrates multilingual prompts, single- and multi-turn dialogues, and different attack types across a generalized and comprehensive taxonomy, resulting in unsystematic and incomplete safety evaluation (see Table 1).

To address these limitations, we introduce DiagSafe, a comprehensive and diagnostic safety benchmark. It comprises two core components: a novel evaluation framework (Figure 1) and a highly diverse dataset.
First, inspired by the domain-specific rubrics in FORTRESS (Knight et al., 2025), our diagnostic evaluation framework moves beyond traditional binary classification by employing prompt-level rubrics. Instead of relying on generic instructions, we designed a dedicated rubric for every prompt with explicit, item-level criteria for both safety and usefulness. This dual-dimensional approach transforms subjective binary judgments into objective, explainable assessments.

Second, to ensure robust and realistic evaluation, the DiagSafe dataset is grounded in a comprehensive safety taxonomy. By systematically collecting and coding AI safety policies and regulations from various countries, we developed a three-level taxonomy comprising 84 safety labels, each with a detailed policy specifying its definition and boundaries. Built upon this rigorous foundation, the dataset is designed to capture a wide spectrum of real-world scenarios and adversarial threats. It consists of two bilingual
(English and Chinese) subsets. DiagBase covers single- and multi-turn prompts, including both direct harmful queries and template-based jailbreaks. DiagAttack further embeds harmful intents into complex instruction-following tasks, forming instruction attacks (Jiang et al., 2023).

Extensive experiments demonstrate that DiagSafe significantly enhances the reliability of LLM-as-a-judge, achieving 95.34% agreement with human annotators and high inter-judge consistency (Cohen’s $\kappa=0.864$ ). Applying our benchmark to a series of LLMs (8B to 1.6T parameters) reveals critical vulnerabilities. While unsafe rates on DiagBase range from 2% to 33%, 8 of the 11 models exceed 40% on DiagAttack. In addition, models are generally more susceptible to unsafe behaviors in Chinese contexts and during multi-turn interactions. Furthermore, the fine-grained analysis effectively exposes the unique safety blind spots of each evaluated model. In summary, our main contributions are three-fold:

• 

Prompt-Level Rubric Evaluation: We propose a novel paradigm that shifts safety evaluation from generic binary classification to fine-grained, item-by-item assessment. By independently evaluating safety and usefulness, this approach yields highly consistent and explainable judgments.

• 

Comprehensive Safety Benchmark: We construct a diverse dataset grounded in a rigorous three-tier 84-label taxonomy. It integrates bilingual prompts, multi-turn dialogues, and various attack strategies to ensure realistic threat evaluation.

• 

Actionable Model Diagnostics: We conduct extensive evaluations on leading LLMs, uncovering specific vulnerabilities obscured by aggregate metrics. Our findings provide concrete guidance for LLM alignment.

## 2 Related Work

Safety benchmarks are a crucial component of large language model evaluation (Gehman et al., 2020; Hartvigsen et al., 2022; Lin et al., 2022). Recent advancements have evolved along two primary dimensions: taxonomic scope and scenario diversity. To broaden taxonomic scope, benchmarks like SALAD-Bench (Li et al., 2024) and S-Eval (Yuan et al., 2025) introduce hierarchical risk categories, AIR-Bench (Zeng et al., 2024) designs risk categories based on actual regulatory documents, and Safety-Prompts (Sun et al., 2023) significantly scales up the data volume. To enhance scenario diversity, recent datasets incorporate adversarial jailbreak attacks (Mazeika et al., 2024; Chao et al., 2024), multilingual contexts (Deng et al., 2024), and multi-turn dialogues (Cao et al., 2026). Despite these expansions, most existing benchmarks lack precise, policy-level boundary definitions for their labels and rarely integrate these diverse elements into a single framework. DiagSafe bridges this gap by unifying diverse evaluation scenarios under a comprehensive taxonomy, providing a unified diagnostic resource with explicit, instance-specific rubrics (Table 1).

Classifiers and LLM judges with generic prompts suffer from
significant inconsistency (Liu et al., 2026b) because their scoring
rules are either implicit or globally generic. To address this limitation, recent work by Knight et al. (2025) demonstrated that employing instance-specific rubrics can achieve high human alignment in specific high-risk domains (e.g., national security and public safety). Building upon this insight, DiagSafe scales and generalizes this prompt-level rubric paradigm to a comprehensive safety evaluation. Whereas Knight et al. (2025) directly pair each prompt with a dedicated rubric, DiagSafe introduces an explicit and reusable intermediate layer, following a safety provisions $\rightarrow$ label specification $\rightarrow$ prompt-level rubric pipeline. Through systematic experiments, we provide the first evidence that automatically synthesized prompt-level rubrics are highly effective in safety scenarios. By providing explicit, per-sample criteria across a broad taxonomy, our approach effectively constrains judge behavior to yield highly consistent, explainable, and reliable judgments (§4.2).

## 3 The DiagSafe Benchmark

We present a diagnostic safety evaluation method that relies on prompt-level rubrics to conduct fine-grained evaluation of LLMs. The rubrics are grounded in a three-level taxonomy comprising 84 safety labels. To instantiate this method, we construct two subsets, DiagBase and DiagAttack (Section 3.2).

## 3.1 The Diagnostic Evaluation Framework

DiagSafe is built on an intuitive idea: reliable safety judgments depend on explicit evaluation criteria. Rather than relying on generic instructions to classify responses as “safe” or “unsafe,” we equip each prompt with a dedicated, prompt-specific rubric. These rubrics contain verifiable items across two dimensions: safety and usefulness. Since both are fundamental requirements for deployed models, evaluating them jointly provides a more comprehensive picture of model behavior than assessing safety in isolation.

## Safety dimension.

The safety dimension is made of S-items, each naming a concrete behavior. A rubric contains multiple S-items, each corresponding to one type of harm that the response to this specific prompt may produce. Each S-item also carries its own exemptions: behaviors under that same risk that must not be judged unsafe, such as objective conceptual discussion, an explicit refusal, or legal and common-sense knowledge. These exemptions delimit the legitimate boundary for that item and suppress false positives.

Figure 3: The DiagSafe data synthesis pipeline. (1) Prompt synthesis: an LLM generator, conditioned on the per-label specification and three orthogonal attributes (language, dialogue turns, and attack form), produces DiagBase; its single-turn normal prompts are then rewritten into instruction attacks using task shells from an external instruction-following dataset, yielding DiagAttack. (2) Rubric synthesis: each synthesized prompt, together with the same label specification, is passed to a rubric-writer LLM to produce a prompt-level rubric comprising safety items ( $S_{1}\dots S_{n}$ ) and an independent usefulness item ( $U$ ).

## Usefulness dimension.

The usefulness dimension consists of U-items, which independently assess whether a response fulfills the given instruction. These items fall into three categories: instruction following, information quality (e.g., factual correctness and internal consistency), and response patterns (e.g., bare refusals or the provision of alternatives). This dimension comprises eight fixed U-items whose binary (Yes/No) answers map to a 0–3 score via a deterministic rule. For example, a score of 3 requires a direct, factually consistent answer, whereas a bare refusal scores 1.

In summary, this rubric forms the foundation of DiagSafe’s fine-grained and reliable evaluation, converting subjective “is this safe?” judgments into objective, item-level criteria. Furthermore, by treating safety and usefulness as independent dimensions, the rubric enables a more comprehensive assessment of LLM behavior than a single safety label can provide.

## 3.2 Label System of DiagSafe

We build the dataset around a hierarchical label taxonomy. To ensure that the labels are scientifically grounded and complete, we collect a broad set of public safety standards as the basis for label design, and equip each label with a detailed definition and explicit criteria that delimit its boundary.

## Safety guidelines collection.

We collect public safety guidelines from countries and regions such as the European Union, the United States, and China, covering ethical principles, risk frameworks, industry taxonomies, and national technical policies. From these documents we extract and consolidate 204 safety provisions. Drawing on this multi-source, cross-jurisdictional base, rather than a single company’s internal policy, keeps the taxonomy from inheriting any one vendor’s blind spots.

## Label and label specification construction.

We use an LLM agent to merge the collected provisions and expand them into individual safety labels, each accompanied by a detailed label specification. Safety-team experts then review and revise the draft, verifying source traceability, rule accuracy, and boundary clarity, and removing safety-irrelevant labels. Each label specification explicitly specifies four components: (1) a precise definition of the label, (2) value principles grounded in public safety standards, (3) dangerous behaviors that constitute violations, and (4) safe behaviors that are permitted. Compared with the taxonomies of existing benchmarks, ours offers broader coverage and finer granularity, as shown in Figure 2.

## Prompt synthesis.

Using the label specifications as seeds, we synthesize prompts along three orthogonal attributes: language (Chinese or English), dialogue turns (single- or multi-turn), and attack form. We use an LLM as a generator, equipped with a general-purpose guide that enables it to simultaneously generate prompts covering two languages, two dialogue turn scenarios, and three attack types. The resulting dataset consists of two subsets. DiagBase comprises ordinary harmful queries (no-jailbreak) as well as two types of adversarial queries: template-based jailbreak prompts and combined-template jailbreak prompts.

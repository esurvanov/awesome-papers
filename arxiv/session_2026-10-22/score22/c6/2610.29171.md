## ABSTRACT
We study how to improve training efficiency on Ascend NPUs. In the NPU training programs we study, framework-level compilation optimizations such as operator fusion are only partially applied, leaving fragmented computation and avoidable execution overheads. Profiling-guided tuning can address these inefficiencies, but it often requires costly trial and error. To make training on NPUs more efficient, we propose TrainBridge, an agentic approach for transferring GPU optimization guidance to NPU training. TrainBridge uses the highly optimized execution produced by mature GPU compilation stacks as a task-specific reference for NPU tuning. It extracts optimization intents from the GPU training execution to guide NPU program rewrites and further refines the rewritten program through iterative profiling. Our GPU-to-NPU migration experiments show that TrainBridge achieves an average 1.7x speedup across four workloads covering LLM pretraining, image classification, and vision-language training, and an average speedup of nearly 2.0x across 27 Modded-NanoGPT speedrun records with highly optimized GPU implementations.

## 1 Introduction

Efficient training on modern accelerators can benefit from framework-level compilation that optimizes computation across operators. Such compilation is mature on NVIDIA GPU backends. However, in the Ascend NPU training programs we study, these optimizations are partially applied by the available compilation stack (Yu, 2026).
As a result, the execution of the training algorithm involves fragmented computation, redundant operations, and materialized intermediates, leaving space for NPU-specific program tuning.

Such NPU-specific tuning is often manual and expert-driven. Providing a tuning agent with
task-specific knowledge, such as NPU profiling results
(Zhang et al., 2025c; Li et al., 2026a; Zhou et al., 2025), enables it to diagnose bottlenecks and improve the NPU program. However, even with profiling guidance, the agent still needs to iteratively analyze, modify, execute, and validate the program, making the
optimization process time-consuming under a limited tuning budget. This motivates us to seek
additional guidance that can make the tuning process more efficient.

To provide this additional guidance, we introduce TrainBridge, a two-stage agentic
framework for NPU-specific optimization. We observe that the corresponding GPU
training programs already benefit from substantial optimization by mature compilation stacks.
The differences between the eager and compiled GPU execution reveal optimization decisions already made for the same workload.
Therefore, we think the differences are useful guidance on NPU execution.
In Stage 1, TrainBridge extracts the underlying optimization intents from the differences and
constructs NPU-specific rewrites when corresponding inefficient computation patterns are present
in the NPU program. In Stage 2, it iteratively profiles the updated program to identify remaining bottlenecks, addresses them, and updates the profile.

We evaluate TrainBridge in a GPU-to-NPU migration setting on training workloads spanning LLM pretraining, image classification, and vision-language training.

In addition, we introduce an NPU version of the
Modded-NanoGPT speedrun (Jordan et al., 2024), a highly optimized community benchmark for end-to-end
language-model pretraining efficiency under a fixed validation target. We adapt its historical
GPU implementations to Ascend NPUs and release an open
leaderboard22
 2

 https://github.com/zhaoyiyidan/modded-nanogpt-npu and evaluation
environment to support further NPU training optimization.

Our contributions are summarized as follows:

• 

We identify compilation-related optimization gaps in NPU training programs. To narrow these gaps, we present TrainBridge, a two-stage agentic approach. TrainBridge first extracts optimization intents from the differences between eager and compiled GPU implementations and uses them to guide NPU program rewrites, then iteratively profiles the rewritten program to address residual bottlenecks.

• 

We evaluate TrainBridge across multiple NPU training workloads. It achieves average
speedups of 1.7× across four training workloads and nearly 2.0× across 27
historical speedrun records with highly optimized GPU implementations, relative to the initial
NPU implementations. On the four additional workloads, it reduces the average NPU-to-GPU
training-time ratio from 2.8× to 1.5×.

## 2.3 Challenges in Agentic Performance Tuning

We first tried using a generic coding agent with GPT-5.6-SOL in the Codex agent harness to tune the initial NPU program. It produced valid changes, but many candidates failed to improve end-to-end training time. Without task-specific knowledge, the agent often struggles to identify which regions have substantial
optimization potential and what changes are likely to improve training efficiency.

NPU profiling provides useful task-specific guidance by identifying expensive operators and
timeline intervals. However, high runtime alone does not indicate how much a region can be accelerated or which optimization should be applied. The agent therefore needs repeated cycles
of analysis, modification, execution, and validation to discover effective changes. While this
process can improve performance, it can be time-consuming and inefficient under a limited tuning
budget. This motivates complementary guidance that can narrow the optimization search space.

## 2.4 GPU Compilation as Optimization Evidence

GPU training ecosystems combine expert-designed implementations with mature framework-level compilation. For the same workload, differences between eager and compiled GPU execution reveal concrete changes that eliminate, fuse, or reorganize computation. Modded-NanoGPT Record 50 provides a representative example. Table 1 shows that, on eight NVIDIA H100 SXM GPUs, framework-level compilation through
torch.compile reduces the end-to-end training time of Record 50 from around 338 seconds
to 128 seconds, demonstrating its contribution to training efficiency.

We next examine whether the computation patterns addressed by the GPU-side compilation stack remain in the migrated NPU implementation. In our evaluated NPU training programs, some corresponding patterns remain despite the available compilation support, leaving fragmented computation and materialized intermediates that provide concrete opportunities for NPU-specific optimization.

Modded-NanoGPT Record 50
provides one example. Its KeyShift operation shifts half of each attention head’s key features by
one token along the sequence dimension. The original overlapping-slice assignment remains and produces a sequence of ViewCopy operations. Consistent with prior reports of compilation limitations on Ascend (Yu, 2026), the Ascend NPU compilation stack did not successfully produce a runnable end-to-end compiled program in our migrated training setting, leaving this inefficiency in the executed NPU implementation. The same inefficiency is present in eager GPU execution, whereas the compiled GPU execution eliminates it through functionalization and kernel fusion. Then, the agent extracts the intent of avoiding overlapping view updates and proposes an NPU-specific expression that realizes this intent. ## 3 TrainBridge

## 3.1 Problem Formulation

We first formalize how source-side framework-level compilation provides optimization evidence for NPU tuning. In our source GPU setting, this compilation is implemented through PyTorch torch.compile. Such compilation may transform computation graphs and execution schedules, introduce fused regions, modify compiler IR, and generate specialized kernels. For clarity, we use computation-graph changes as a representative view to formulate the optimization problem; TrainBridge itself is not restricted to graph-level transformations. Our goal is to improve the end-to-end training time of the migrated NPU program while preserving its correctness requirements under a limited tuning budget.

At the graph level, let $G_{e}$ and $G_{c}$ denote representative computation graphs before and after source-side GPU compilation, respectively. From the computation-graph perspective, we summarize the changes as

 $G_{c}=\tau_{G}(G_{e}),$ 

where $\tau_{G}$ denotes only the graph-level changes observed during compilation. The difference between $G_{e}$ and $G_{c}$ therefore provides evidence of computation that the source stack found beneficial to eliminate, fuse, or reorganize.

Within $G_{e}$ , let $r_{e}\subseteq G_{e}$ denote a computation region before GPU compilation, and let $r_{c}$ denote its compiled counterpart. Suppose an inefficient computation pattern $p$ is present in $r_{e}$ but is eliminated or reorganized in $r_{c}$ . The difference between $r_{e}$ and $r_{c}$ therefore provides evidence about the optimization intent applied to this region.

Migration preserves the high-level computation of the source program but does not automatically reproduce the optimizations introduced by GPU compilation. When the available NPU compilation stack does not eliminate the corresponding inefficiency, the inefficient pattern $p$ may remain in a semantically corresponding NPU region $r_{n}$ .

In such cases, the agent can use the eager-to-compiled GPU difference to infer the underlying optimization intent and derive an NPU-specific rewrite that removes or reduces the corresponding inefficiency while preserving the original computation semantics. Applying such rewrites across applicable regions can improve the training efficiency of the migrated NPU program.

However, GPU evidence cannot address all inefficiencies in the migrated NPU program. Some GPU
transformations may not be beneficial on the NPU because of backend-specific execution
characteristics, and GPU evidence does not expose NPU-specific overheads in host scheduling,
communication, or memory management. These limitations motivate the two stages below.

## 3.2 Stage 1: NPU Optimization Guided by GPU Compilation Evidence

Figure 1: Overview of TrainBridge. Stage 1 aligns eager-to-compiled GPU changes with
the migrated NPU program, and uses the resulting optimization evidence to guide NPU
implementation optimization. Stage 2 iteratively profiles the updated NPU program and
addresses residual bottlenecks.

For the same training program, we collect GPU compilation artifacts, including pre-compilation graphs, post-fusion IR, generated kernels, and profiling summaries, together with the corresponding GPU and migrated NPU sources. Based on this evidence, TrainBridge groups related GPU operations into semantic supernodes. A semantic supernode represents a common high-level computation and provides a unit for relating source-side compilation changes to the migrated NPU program.

For each supernode, the agent first examines the eager-to-compiled GPU changes to identify what computation was eliminated, fused, or reorganized. These changes provide evidence of an inefficient computation pattern in the eager execution and the optimization intent used to address it. The agent then locates the NPU region implementing the same high-level computation and checks whether the corresponding inefficiency remains, using differences in operators, dtypes, layouts, materialized intermediates, and kernel boundaries as evidence.

When the inefficiency remains on the NPU, the agent uses the inferred optimization intent to propose an NPU-specific rewrite that removes or reduces it while preserving the original computation semantics. Candidate rewrites include API parameter changes, redundant-operation removal, fused NPU operators, and selective compilation of supported local subgraphs. Stage 1 ends after all semantic supernodes have been examined and applicable GPU-evidence-guided rewrites have been validated.

## 3.3 Stage 2: Profiling-Guided Residual Optimization

Stage 2 profiles the NPU program produced by Stage 1 to identify residual bottlenecks that are not captured by source-side GPU compilation evidence. The agent
combines operator statistics, host and device timelines, communication events, memory activity,
and source inspection to identify the remaining bottlenecks.

The agent considers several common optimization classes: eliminating redundant work, reusing computed
values or buffers, hiding latency through overlap, and replacing an implementation with an
equivalent NPU-native alternative. After each candidate modification, TrainBridge revalidates
correctness and performance and updates the profile when the bottleneck shifts. The agent repeats this process until no further performance improvement is observed or the tuning budget is exhausted.

## LIMITATIONS SECTION
Our current tuning setting focuses on single-node training. Multi-node training introduces
additional performance factors from network topology, cross-node communication, and parallelization
strategy. Future work will evaluate TrainBridge in multi-node settings using large-scale training frameworks such as MindSpeed-LLM and Megatron-LM.

TrainBridge requires a source GPU implementation from which useful framework-level compilation evidence can be obtained. This
requirement provides task-specific optimization guidance but limits the method to workloads supported by a
mature source stack. A natural extension is to store source-side optimization evidence produced by the GPU compilation stack, together with validated NPU implementations, in a retrieval database.
Such a database could provide reusable knowledge about source-side optimization intents and their successful NPU implementations, helping agents prioritize promising rewrites for new workloads.

## CONCLUSION
We study compilation-related optimization inefficiencies in NPU training and investigate how evidence from mature GPU compilation stacks can guide NPU-specific program optimization. TrainBridge extracts
optimization intents from differences between eager and compiled GPU implementations, constructs NPU-specific rewrites,
and profiles the updated program to address residual bottlenecks. It achieves a $2.0\times$ NPU speedup
across Modded-NanoGPT speedrun records and a $1.7\times$ speedup across four additional
workloads. These results show that GPU compilation evidence can guide NPU-specific program
optimization and help address compilation-related inefficiencies in NPU training.

Abstract.
Coding agents can invest substantial effort in repository exploration and testing yet still produce unsuccessful patches. Existing online steering methods commonly review execution trajectories periodically or invoke an advisor when a monitor detects behavioral problems. Frequent reviews are costly, and the absence of anomalous behavior does not mean that an agent is on the right track. To bridge this gap, we propose PAUSE, a framework for steering coding agents with a single planning intervention.
We find that agents reach their first source-code edit at a median of 60% of trajectory progress, with the preceding work largely devoted to repository exploration, bug reproduction, and testing. This motivates intercepting the request before execution, so that the agent’s accumulated investigation can guide the repair it is about to implement.
Because the first source-code edit directly identifies when to intervene, PAUSE locates this point without a learned monitor, a search over candidate checkpoints, or an extra model call—a property we call search-free. At this point, a separate Planner reads the task and pre-edit transcript to produce one repair plan. The same agent, the Executor, resumes with this plan, retaining its accumulated context and workspace. PAUSE applies both online, during a live run, and offline, from a recorded trajectory prefix.
We evaluate PAUSE on SWE-bench Verified and SWE-bench Multilingual with two Executors and three agent harnesses. With Qwen3-coder as Executor and GPT-5.6 Sol as Planner, online steering in OpenHands improves resolution on Verified by 16.8%, and online steering in SWE-agent improves resolution on Multilingual by 14.7%. In SWE-agent, against LivePlan, which invokes an advisor through rule-based monitoring, PAUSE is more effective at a similar Planner cost, improving resolution by 3.2% and 3.7% on Verified and Multilingual, respectively. Against SICA, which reviews the trajectory periodically, PAUSE remains comparable on both benchmarks while incurring about 11% and 15% of SICA’s Planner cost on Verified and Multilingual, respectively. Offline, PAUSE recovers 35.4% of the eligible failed Verified tasks, and resuming from the recorded prefix uses 33–41% fewer Executor tokens than restarting. These results show that a single planning intervention at the first source-code edit is more effective than rule-based monitoring and competitive with periodic oversight at a small fraction of its cost.

coding agents, agent steering, planning, trajectory reuse
## 1. Introduction
LLM-based coding agents tackle repository-level issues by inspecting code, making edits, and running commands and tests through an interactive tool interface (Yang et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2025a; Bouzenia et al., 2025).
Existing repair systems gather relevant context through localization and structural code search (Xia et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2024b), and infer code intent to guide repair (Ruan et al., 2025).
However, agents can still struggle to turn this investigation into a correct repair (Yang et al., 2024).
This motivates online steering, which uses the context accumulated during execution to guide the agent’s subsequent repair decisions (Gandhi et al., 2025b; Nanda et al., 2026; Liu et al., 2026a).
The timing of guidance shapes both the effectiveness and cost of online steering (Liu et al., 2026b).
As shown in Table 1, existing methods decide when to guide either through repeated model-based reviews or by waiting for a detected problem.
With periodic model-based review, methods such as SWE-PRM (Gandhi et al., 2025b) and SICA (Robeyns et al., 2025) repeatedly assess ongoing execution at regular step or time intervals to determine whether guidance is needed.
Each review incurs additional inference cost, while longer intervals can delay useful feedback until the agent has taken further steps along an ineffective path (Liu et al., 2026b).
A fixed schedule also need not align with the point at which accumulated investigation is ready to inform a code change.
To reduce review overhead, LivePlan uses rule-based checks to invoke an advisor when signals such as repetition, stagnation, or skipped phases are detected (Liu et al., 2026b).
This reduces monitoring cost, but intervention remains limited by the coverage of predefined signals, which LivePlan acknowledges may be incomplete (Liu et al., 2026b).
Thus, the absence of a behavioral alarm does not establish that the repair strategy is correct: an agent may follow an orderly workflow while preparing an ineffective change.

Why the first source-code edit?
To identify such a moment, we analyze 500 recorded Qwen3-coder trajectories on SWE-bench Verified.
We define the first source-code edit as the first file-editor request targeting a source-code file of the repository.
We classify recorded actions into five categories: explicit reasoning, file inspection, shell command execution, file editing, and other operations, including termination.
Figure 1 visualizes these sequences over normalized action progress and orders trajectories by their first source-code edit; edits to scratch files or reproduction scripts outside the designated source-code paths are excluded.
Among the 476 trajectories containing such a request, its median position is 60.0% of action progress, and 80.5% occur after the midpoint.
Before this point, command execution is the most common action type and file reads account for 31% of actions, reflecting substantial repository investigation before the proposed source change.
This observation motivates intervening before the proposed source-code edit executes, when accumulated evidence is available to inform the repair and the proposed change remains open to revision.
Our experiments confirm that this point balances the two: an earlier intervention gives the Planner too little of the investigation to ground a plan, whereas a later one arrives after the agent has committed to and built on its own changes, which the plan must then overturn.
Prior work uses explicit planning to coordinate repository edits (Bairi et al., 2024; Tao et al., 2024) and explores planning and execution roles in model collaboration (Lee et al., 2026; Gandhi et al., 2025a).
To guide repair at this point, we introduce PAUSE, a framework that uses the first source-code edit to trigger a single planning intervention.
The request identifies the intervention point directly from the agent’s proposed action and its target path, without a learned monitor, a search over candidate checkpoints, or an additional model call to locate the point; we call this property search-free.
A separate Planner reads the task and the coding agent’s preceding transcript, including inspected code and execution feedback, and returns one natural-language repair plan.
The same coding agent, the Executor, receives this plan and continues from its existing conversation and workspace, retaining its investigation and responsibility for editing and testing without updating its underlying language model.
PAUSE supports online steering and offline recovery.
Online, it blocks the first source-code edit during a live run and adds the Planner’s guidance before resuming the Executor from its live state.
Offline, it restores an existing failed trajectory to a pre-edit state, adds a plan based on the recorded prefix, and resumes the same Executor with its remaining budget.
We evaluate PAUSE on SWE-bench Verified (Jimenez et al., 2024) and SWE-bench Multilingual (Yang et al., 2025), which spans nine programming languages, using two Executors and three agent harnesses.
With Qwen3-coder as Executor and GPT-5.6 Sol as Planner in OpenHands, online steering improves resolution on Verified by 16.8%.
In SWE-agent, it improves resolution by 14.8% and 14.7% on Verified and Multilingual, respectively.
Against LivePlan, which invokes an advisor through rule-based monitoring, PAUSE is more effective at a similar Planner cost, improving resolution by 3.2% and 3.7% on Verified and Multilingual, respectively.
Against SICA, which reviews the trajectory periodically, PAUSE remains comparable on both benchmarks while incurring about 11% and 15% of SICA’s Planner cost on Verified and Multilingual, respectively.
Offline, PAUSE recovers 79 of 223 eligible failed Verified tasks, a recovery rate of 35.4%.
These results show that a single planning intervention at the first source-code edit is more effective than rule-based monitoring and competitive with periodic oversight at a small fraction of its cost.
Our contributions are:
• 
An empirical characterization of the first source-code edit as an opportunity to guide repair after substantial repository investigation.
• 
PAUSE, a search-free framework that gives the same Executor one plan while preserving its context and workspace, supporting online steering and offline recovery.
• 
An evaluation across two benchmarks, two Executors, and three harnesses, showing that PAUSE is more effective than rule-based monitoring at a similar Planner cost and is competitive with periodic oversight at 11–15% of its Planner cost, with controlled studies of intervention timing, planning guidance, and state reuse.
## 2. Related Work
## 2.1. Repository-Level Repair and Planning
SWE-bench frames repository-level repair as resolving real GitHub issues through changes to an existing codebase (Jimenez et al., 2024).
SWE-agent equips language models with interfaces for navigating repositories, editing files, and using execution feedback (Yang et al., 2024).
OpenHands provides an extensible platform in which agents perform software development through code execution and other tools (Wang et al., 2025a).
CodeAgent integrates retrieval, navigation, implementation, and testing tools for repository-level coding (Zhang et al., 2024a), while RepoGraph and LingmaAgent use repository graphs to support context gathering and exploration (Ouyang et al., 2025; Ma et al., 2025).
Alongside these interactive agents, Agentless separates localization, repair, and patch validation into a structured pipeline (Xia et al., 2025).
SHERLOC further studies dedicated diagnostic exploration, transferring localization findings to a downstream repair agent (Tamoyan et al., 2026).
Training-based approaches include SWE-Fixer’s specialized retrieval and editing modules (Xie et al., 2025) and SWE-Gym’s agent and verifier training in executable repository environments (Pan et al., 2025).
Explicit planning also structures reasoning and collaboration.
Plan-and-Solve decomposes problems before solving them (Wang et al., 2023), and Plan-and-Act separates high-level planning from environment actions (Erdogan et al., 2025).
MetaGPT organizes software development through role-specific procedures and intermediate artifacts (Hong et al., 2024), while Self-Collaboration coordinates analyst, coder, and tester roles (Dong et al., 2024).
Within repository repair, planning can compare candidate solutions before implementation.
SWE-Manager trains an 8B model to select among competing repair proposals and synthesize a golden proposal for implementation (Tan et al., 2026).
Its P2A framework separates proposal generation, managerial selection and synthesis, and implementation into distinct roles (Tan et al., 2026).
PAUSE instead obtains one plan from the investigation already accumulated by an executing agent, using its first source-code edit to determine when guidance is provided.
The same Executor then continues with its retained conversation and workspace.
## 2.2. Online Steering
Online steering introduces guidance while an agent is still carrying out its task.
SWE-PRM periodically reviews a recent trajectory window and produces natural-language feedback grounded in a taxonomy of execution inefficiencies (Gandhi et al., 2025b).
SICA includes an asynchronous overseer that examines execution state at regular intervals or key events and can notify or stop an agent when it detects problematic behavior (Robeyns et al., 2025).
LivePlan separates detection from advice: a deterministic monitor checks for signals such as repetition, stagnation, and skipped phases, then requests corrective guidance when a signal fires (Liu et al., 2026b).
These designs make the intervention decision depend on periodic assessment or detected behavioral conditions.
PAUSE uses a different trigger: the first source-code edit identifies an opportunity to guide the repair regardless of whether the preceding trajectory exhibits a behavioral alarm.
This enables a single planning intervention without repeated model-based reviews or a search for an intervention point.
## 2.3. Trajectory Reuse and Reflection
Search-based approaches explore alternative reasoning and action sequences.
Tree of Thoughts searches over alternative reasoning paths (Yao et al., 2023a), while Language Agent Tree Search combines tree search with environment feedback and reflection (Zhou et al., 2024).
For code generation, CodeTree searches over coding strategies, candidate programs, and revisions using execution and agent feedback (Li et al., 2025).
Within repository repair, reusing intermediate work can reduce exploration repeated across attempts.
SWE-Search combines Monte Carlo tree search with iterative refinement to explore alternative repair trajectories (Antoniades et al., 2025).
SWE-Replay retains an archive of sampled trajectories and selects intermediate steps from which to restore state and generate new continuations (Ding and Zhang, 2026).
DARS resamples actions at branching points using prior execution feedback (Aggarwal et al., 2025), while SE-Agent revises, recombines, and refines existing trajectories (Guo et al., 2025).
These methods use intermediate states to diversify the search for a successful repair.
Offline PAUSE reuses a recorded prefix to support a different operation: inserting one plan at a checkpoint determined by the first source-code edit and continuing with the remaining execution budget.
Another approach uses verbal feedback to guide later attempts.
Reflexion stores verbal feedback from prior attempts in an episodic memory to guide subsequent trials (Shinn et al., 2023).
Self-Refine iterates between feedback generation and output revision using the same language model (Madaan et al., 2023).
Tool-grounded feedback supports related correction mechanisms: CRITIC verifies and revises outputs through tool interaction (Gou et al., 2024), while Self-Debugging and LDB use program execution evidence to diagnose and revise generated code (Chen et al., 2024; Zhong et al., 2024).
ChatRepair retains candidate patches and test feedback within a repair conversation to inform subsequent attempts (Xia and Zhang, 2024).
Execution feedback also supports candidate selection: CodeT uses generated tests and execution agreement (Chen et al., 2023), whereas LEVER learns to score candidate programs from their inputs, code, and execution results (Ni et al., 2023).
PAUSE places the planning call within an existing coding trajectory and retains the Executor’s conversation and workspace when delivering guidance.
The Planner reasons over the recorded evidence in a separate invocation, which may use the Executor’s model.
## 3. Method
## 3.1. Problem Formulation
A repository-level repair task consists of an issue description $d$ and an
initial repository snapshot $R_{0}$ (Jimenez et al., 2024). A coding agent, which we call the
Executor, investigates the issue, modifies files, and tests its changes
through an execution harness (Yang et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2025a).
Reasoning-and-acting agents interleave model decisions with observations
from the environment (Yao et al., 2023b). After $t$ completed actions, the Executor’s conversation
 $h_{t}$ contains the system prompt, the task, and the recorded actions and
observations; $W_{t}$ is the resulting workspace. From this state, the
Executor proposes its next action $a_{t+1}$ for the harness to execute.
Online steering aims to improve the final repair during execution. It must
determine when to intervene and what guidance to provide using the task and
available evidence. We call the model supplying repair guidance the Planner.
The available evidence includes inspected code, command outputs, and the
Executor’s own test results. Benchmark evaluator feedback, hidden tests,
reference patches, and future actions are excluded from the planning input.
## 3.2. Pre-Edit Intervention
The investigation preceding a source-code edit provides evidence for
guidance while the proposed change remains open to revision.
PAUSE uses the first source-code edit to trigger
a single planning intervention, without repeated
trajectory assessment or a search over intervention points.
As illustrated in Figure 2, PAUSE blocks the
request and asks the Planner to produce a repair plan from the preceding
evidence. It appends this plan to the same Executor’s conversation, allowing
the agent to continue with its context and workspace preserved.

The Planner $\mathcal{P}$ is a separate language-model invocation with no tools or direct
workspace access. It may use a different underlying model from the Executor
or a separate call to the same model. Given the issue and the transcript,
it identifies the likely root cause, the code that needs to change, and the

The plan $g$ is a
natural-language recommendation; the Executor remains responsible for

observations from source reads and a reproduction run into a root cause,
the code to change, and the intended repair.
Guided continuation.
PAUSE appends the plan as a user message to the same Executor’s
conversation. The Executor retains the completed history in full, the
workspace $W_{k^{\ast}-1}$ , and its model, system prompt, tools, and decoding
settings. It then chooses its next action with this additional guidance.
The blocked request is not executed automatically: the Executor may revise
its approach or propose another edit to the same file. The interceptor is
disabled after this intervention, and subsequent actions proceed under the
harness’s native execution budget.
## 3.4. Offline Recovery
Offline recovery applies the same planning mechanism to recorded failures.
The first source-code edit locates the intervention, but recovery restores
an earlier reasoning checkpoint rather than pausing a live request. It requires a
trajectory with explicit think actions.
Checkpoint and planning input.
To place the additional guidance immediately after the Executor’s last
recorded reasoning step before the first source-code edit, we select
(4)
 $c=\max\{\,t<k^{\ast}:a_{t}\text{ is a }\textsf{think}\,\}.$ 
The Executor retains that step and its observation in $h_{c}$ , whereas the
Planner receives the preceding transcript and produces
 $g=\mathcal{P}(d,\phi(h_{c-1}))$ . Thus, the Planner supplies a repair proposal
from the evidence preceding the selected reasoning step, and the Executor
receives both its original reasoning and the new guidance.
Section 4.5 compares this checkpoint with earlier and
later cuts of the same failures. Recovery retains
the recorded prefix through checkpoint $c$ and generates a new continuation
from $(h_{c},W_{c})$ . A trajectory without a source-code edit request or a preceding
think is ineligible for this recovery protocol.
Restoration and continuation.
PAUSE rebuilds $h_{c}$ from the recorded model outputs and observations,
and restores $W_{c}$ by replaying the prefix actions on $R_{0}$ in a fresh
container. This retains files created during investigation, such as
reproduction scripts, without calling the Executor model to regenerate
the prefix. It also restores the harness’s iteration counter $b_{c}$ ;
this counter is distinct from the action index $c$ . With an original
budget $B$ , continuation receives only $B-b_{c}$ further iterations.
A fidelity check compares the restored conversation, repository state,
and counter with the recording. Tasks that fail this check are excluded
from the recovery cohort. The plan is then appended using the same message
interface as online steering, and the same Executor resumes with its
retained state and remaining budget.
Use of AI tools.
Apart from the Executor and Planner models under study, we used AI coding
assistants to implement the harness integrations, write the analysis and
plotting scripts, and assemble the replication package. They produced no
experimental data: every outcome comes from the agent runs and the official
SWE-bench evaluation. The authors reviewed this code, and the released
scripts recompute every reported number from the raw run outputs.
## 4. Experiment
We evaluate the effectiveness and efficiency of PAUSE for online
steering (RQ1) and offline recovery (RQ2), its generalization across harnesses
and reasoning interfaces (RQ3), and the effectiveness of the pre-edit
boundary (RQ4). The shared setup below defines the benchmarks, metrics,
comparisons, and execution protocols used to answer these questions.
## 4.1. Experimental Setup
## 4.1.1. Benchmarks
We evaluate PAUSE on SWE-bench Verified (Jimenez et al., 2024), which contains 500 Python
repository-level repair tasks, and SWE-bench Multilingual (Yang et al., 2025), which contains
300 tasks across nine programming languages. Both benchmarks support the online comparisons
in RQ1, and recorded Verified trajectories support offline recovery in RQ2.
For RQ3, we compare harnesses on Verified. Full-benchmark evaluations measure
overall task resolution, while recovery experiments focus on the Executor’s
original failures. The checkpoint comparisons in RQ4 use failures that can
be restored under both conditions.
## 4.1.2. Evaluation Metrics
Repair outcomes.
We score patches with the corresponding benchmark evaluator.
Resolution is the fraction of tasks resolved in the evaluated task
set. For offline resume, tasks that are not continued retain their original
outcomes; recovered failures are added to the original successes and observed
regressions are subtracted.
Failure recovery and regressions.
The offline recovery and online model comparisons report
Recovered, the number of originally failed tasks solved after
intervention, and recovery rate, this count divided by the number of
failures evaluated. Offline recovery uses eligible failures that meet the
checkpoint and restoration requirements in Section 3.4.
Regression rate is the fraction of evaluated baseline successes that
fail after intervention. Changes in these rates are reported in percentage
points.
Resource use.
To assess efficiency in RQ1 and RQ2, we report Planner and Executor tokens
separately, in millions, and their model calls where measured.
Dollar costs cover Planner inference at rates of
$2.50 and $10 per million input and output tokens, respectively; they do
not include Executor inference. Offline continuation costs exclude the
recorded run used to obtain the prefix. Totals that include this original
run are identified explicitly.
Scoring and statistical analysis.
Evaluator errors, empty patches, and missing evaluations count as unresolved.
We compare paired outcomes with exact McNemar tests.
## 4.1.3. Baselines and Control Conditions
The unmodified Executor is the baseline.
Online steering.
RQ1 compares PAUSE with LivePlan (Liu et al., 2026b),
which uses rule-based monitoring to invoke an advisor when it detects trouble,
and the SICA overseer (Robeyns et al., 2025), which reviews the
trajectory on a schedule and decides whether to interject. These methods
compare a single pre-edit intervention with rule-triggered advice and
periodic model oversight.
Offline recovery.
RQ2 compares PAUSE with SHERLOC (Tamoyan et al., 2026),
which performs a dedicated diagnostic-localization pass before repair and
transfers its findings to the Executor. At a fixed restored checkpoint,
we compare no-plan continuation, self-reconsideration, and Planner guidance
to assess the contribution of the supplied plan. With the same plan, we
compare resuming the recorded state with restarting from the initial
repository to assess the repair–cost trade-off of state reuse.
Generalization and boundary controls.
RQ3 compares each harness with its own unmodified Executor. A think-stripped variant removes explicit
think content from the Planner input to test dependence on that
reasoning interface. RQ4 uses guidance from successful donor runs to test
whether failed trajectories can still be redirected at the boundary, then
compares the default offline checkpoint with cuts after 20% and 90% of the
recorded actions, an immediate pre-edit cut, and a checkpoint associated
with a later source-code edit.

Outcomes on originally solved tasks.
An intervention that recovers failures could also break repairs that the
Executor originally completed. We therefore examine the 178 originally
solved tasks evaluated under both Planners; of the other 50 original
successes, 13 never request a source-code edit, so PAUSE leaves them
unchanged, and 37 could not be successfully replayed in a fresh container in at
least one Planner’s run. GPT-5.6 Sol plans lead to 10 regressions, a
regression rate of 5.6%, whereas Qwen3-30B-Thinking plans lead to 26, a

control yields 10 regressions, the same number as under GPT-5.6 Sol, and the
difference between the two is not statistically significant at $p=0.81$ .
Restoring a checkpoint and re-executing the remaining steps therefore
accounts for most of the observed regressions, and guidance from a strong
Planner does not increase them. Plans from Qwen3-30B-Thinking, by contrast,
produce 16 more regressions than the no-plan control, suggesting that weaker
guidance can steer the Executor away from repairs it would otherwise
complete. Because the full-benchmark resolution subtracts every observed
regression, including those that resumption alone produces, it provides a
conservative estimate of the gain attributable to the plan. Together with
the recovery results, these findings show that GPT-5.6 Sol guidance
recovers failed runs without breaking more successful runs than
continuation alone.

The full-benchmark results establish whether failure recovery translates
into an overall improvement after accounting for observed regressions.

can turn retained investigation into improved repair outcomes, while prefix
reuse reduces the execution required for another attempt.

## 4.5. RQ4: Is the Pre-Edit Boundary Effective?
The preceding evaluations assess what PAUSE achieves; we now examine
the intervention point underlying those results. The first source-code edit
occurs at a median of 60.0% of action progress on Verified
(Figure 1), after substantial
investigation. This raises two questions: can guidance still redirect the
repair at this point, and would an earlier or later point improve
recovery? We examine both through interventions on recorded trajectories.
Comparison setup.
We first insert pre-edit reasoning from a successful run of the same task
as donor guidance, testing whether a failed trajectory can produce a
successful continuation. We then compare the default offline checkpoint,
after the last think before the first source-code edit, with three
alternative cuts of the same recorded failures: after 20% of the recorded
actions, immediately before the first source-code edit, and after 90% of
the recorded actions. We also compare it with a checkpoint associated with a later source-code edit. Each arm independently
restores its prefix and obtains its own GPT-5.6 Sol plan, with the same number of fresh Executor iterations. Each comparison thus tests the
checkpoint together with its plan.
Guidance at the boundary.
Donor guidance produces a successful continuation in at least one of three
attempts for 64 of 97 donor–recipient pairs, a rate of 66.0%, and in all
three attempts for 41, a rate of 42.3%. Donor and recipient runs come from
Executor configurations with different prompts, and across the four
donor–recipient combinations the rate of at least one success ranges from
58.8% to 69.0%. This diagnostic uses
guidance drawn from successful executions, so it assesses the possibility
of a successful continuation at the boundary. The retained investigation
remains compatible with a correct repair even when the original run failed.
Earlier and later cuts.
Restoring a prefix replays its recorded actions in a fresh container, and
this replay can fail the fidelity check or leave a continuation without a
grading result. A task enters a comparison only when every arm in that
comparison restores and is graded. The four-way comparison therefore uses
the 161 tasks graded in all arms, and each pairwise comparison with
PAUSE uses the larger set shared by that pair, which contains 207,
196, or 167 tasks.

## 5. Limitations and Threats to Validity
## 5.1. Limitations
PAUSE relies on repository evidence accumulated before the
intervention. The Planner receives the issue and the pre-edit transcript,
but cannot independently inspect the workspace or execute commands. When
the investigation omits relevant code or informative reproduction feedback,
the Planner can propose hypotheses but cannot verify them against the
repository. Prior studies distinguish correction based only on
model-generated feedback from correction supported by reliable external
feedback (Huang et al., 2024; Kamoi et al., 2024).
Further investigation by the Executor may therefore be needed
to assess whether the suggested strategy is appropriate.
The framework supplies a single repair plan and does not refresh its
guidance as new observations arrive. New test results or implementation
discoveries may make parts of that plan obsolete, leaving subsequent
adjustment to the Executor. Steering can redirect the repair strategy,
but translating that strategy into a correct patch still requires the
Executor to implement and validate the changes.
The intervention depends on a file-editor request for the first source-code edit.
Integrating PAUSE requires a harness that exposes this request
before execution and a path rule appropriate to the repository layout.
Trajectories without such a request receive no intervention, limiting
coverage in workflows whose source changes use other editing interfaces,
such as shell commands.
Offline recovery further requires a recorded trajectory with a usable
reasoning checkpoint and faithfully restorable history and workspace.
A transcript alone is insufficient when the corresponding execution state
cannot be reconstructed. The savings from recovery
concern the additional Executor computation for another attempt once the
prefix is available; producing the original trajectory remains part of
the total cost. Online steering uses the live state without prior recording.
## 5.2. Threats to Validity
## 5.2.1. Internal validity.
SWE-bench Verified and SWE-bench Multilingual issues and fixes are public and may have appeared in a
Planner’s training data. We checked agent inputs, Planner prompts,
repository snapshots, and container network access for direct exposure
during execution; these checks cannot determine prior training exposure.
All Executor runs mount only the instance ID and problem statement. The offline regression comparisons use one run per task, so small
differences may be noise.
## 5.2.2. External validity.
The evaluation covers SWE-bench Verified, SWE-bench Multilingual, and the tested Planner,
Executor, and harness configurations. Their diversity provides evidence
across several settings, but transfer to substantially different
repositories or longer, multi-stage development workflows remains
unexamined. Beyond Python, the evidence comes from the online SWE-agent
comparison on Multilingual; offline recovery and the harness comparison
use Verified only. The pause-position ablation covers offline recovery with
Qwen3-coder on Verified only. Offline recovery results additionally concern trajectories
that meet the checkpoint and restoration requirements; failures outside
this eligible cohort may respond differently to guidance.
## 5.2.3. Construct validity.
The first source-code edit operationalizes the intervention point through an
action type and a benchmark-specific path rule. Broader rules can admit reproduction scripts,
so the detected event need not represent the same stage of investigation
in every repository. The alternative cuts at 20% and 90% of the actions
are defined by the length of the recorded run, which is unknown during a
live run, so they serve as offline reference points rather than deployable
triggers. Benchmark tests provide the measure of repair success;
behavior beyond their coverage remains unevaluated. Incomplete grading
records count as unresolved.
## 5.2.4. Conclusion validity.
The main comparisons use one attempt per task, while the donor-guidance
diagnostic uses three attempts per pair. The paired pause-position
comparisons in RQ4 use 167 to 207 tasks, and none of them reaches
significance at the 0.05 level; the lower recovery of the early cut is a
trend that repeated runs would need to confirm. Similar observed
recovery counts do not establish equivalence; repeated paired runs are
needed to assess whether small differences persist.
## 6. Future Work
The results motivate two directions for studying and extending
the benefits of a pre-edit plan.
Evidence selection for planning.
Removing explicit reasoning produces similar aggregate resolution but
different task outcomes, leaving open which parts of an investigation
support effective guidance. We hypothesize that a small set of relevant
code observations and execution results can provide stronger diagnostic
support than a longer transcript containing repeated or untested repair
conjectures. This hypothesis could be tested by selectively retaining code
reads, reproduction outputs, test feedback, and recorded reasoning at
several fixed input budgets. Comparisons with equally long chronological
and randomly selected excerpts would separate evidence selection from
context length. Selection would use only information available before the
edit, while the checkpoint, Planner, and Executor remain fixed. Repeated
paired continuations would measure resolution, regressions, and planning
cost, testing whether selective evidence can preserve repair gains with
less computation.
Selective reuse of failed investigations.
The recovery–cost trade-off between resuming and restarting raises a
further question about what should be retained from a failed attempt.
Experience-based agents extract reusable insights (Zhao et al., 2024),
workflows (Wang et al., 2025b), and grounded
plans (Hayashi et al., 2025) from past executions, offering
representations to compare when deciding what a repair attempt should retain.
We hypothesize that observed facts and reusable workspace artifacts provide
much of the reuse benefit, while earlier repair conjectures can sometimes
make it harder for the Executor to adopt a different plan. To examine this,
future experiments could compare the full history with a version that
masks prior diagnostic conjectures but retains executed repository
operations and their observations, keeping the workspace, supplied plan, and fresh
execution budget identical. A separate restart condition would reset both
history and workspace under the same plan and continuation budget.
Measuring repair outcomes, adoption of the new plan, and repeated
investigation would distinguish the effects of retained conjectures from
the broader cost of rebuilding state. Evaluating these hypotheses across
repository layouts and editing interfaces would test their generality.
## 7. Conclusion
We introduced PAUSE, a framework that steers coding agents with a
single repair plan before their first source-code edit.
This action provides a search-free trigger for guidance grounded in the
agent’s preceding investigation. A separate Planner supplies the plan,
and the same Executor continues with its accumulated context and workspace.
The framework supports both live steering and recovery from recorded failures.
Our evaluation on SWE-bench Verified and SWE-bench Multilingual shows that
one pre-edit plan can improve online resolution and recover failed attempts
offline. Online improvements across three harnesses and recovery with two
Executors support the applicability of this design, with gains varying by
Planner and execution setting. The results highlight complementary roles
for external planning and state reuse: suitable guidance can improve repair
outcomes, while retaining an existing investigation lowers the additional
Executor computation required for another attempt compared with restarting.
Comparisons with earlier and later pause positions find no point that
recovers more failures. The first source-code edit therefore offers a
practical opportunity to turn accumulated evidence into revised repair
decisions.

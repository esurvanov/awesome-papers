## ABSTRACT
Flow-matching policies adjust inference cost through the number of solver steps, but reinforcement learning at a single fixed solver budget can hide degradation at cheaper deployment budgets and even erase useful one-step behavior present in a pretrained policy. We introduce Action-Ratio-Clip Flow (ArcFlow), a deployment-aware algorithm for training one flow policy across a specified range of solver budgets. ArcFlow combines action-aligned supervision, budget exposure, and worst-budget checkpoint selection. For reconstruction objectives, its bridge aggregates temporal probes into one surrogate ratio and one PPO-style clip per action, with a boundary residual exactly equal to deployed one-step reconstruction error. Cycling exposes the shared field to the deployment range, and selection optimizes its performance floor. For native velocity objectives, multi-budget endpoint supervision applies the same principle without replacing the native update. Both realizations preserve the architecture and deployment-time cost. Across DM Control, manipulation, and locomotion, ArcFlow attains the highest mean one-step return among five flow-policy baselines on 25 of 30 tasks under each method's reported training settings. Component studies identify complementary improvements from boundary supervision, the fixed-grid bridge, budget exposure, and deployment selection. These results establish deployment-budget-aware training as an effective route to strong low-step control with one policy serving multiple inference costs.

## 1 Introduction

Flow matching represents expressive action distributions by learning a
velocity field that transports noise to actions
(Lipman et al., 2023; Liu et al., 2023). Its solver controls inference
cost: more steps refine integration, while fewer steps reduce network
evaluations, helping robotic policies meet latency budgets.
Consistency and shortcut models enable one- and few-step generation
(Song et al., 2023; Frans et al., 2025), creating
an opportunity to retain expressive policies at low deployment cost.

Reinforcement learning (RL) improves policies through interaction. FPO and
FPO++ derive policy-gradient surrogates from flow-matching losses
(McAllister et al., 2025; Yi et al., 2026); Diffusion Policy Policy Optimization
(DPPO) applies policy gradients to a stochastic diffusion denoising chain
(Ren et al., 2025); ReinFlow adds learnable exploration noise to flow policies
(Zhang et al., 2025b); and PolicyFlow approximates importance ratios from
velocity-field changes along interpolation paths
(Yang et al., 2026). At deployment, solver steps determine latency and
may change the action of the same trained field.
High return at the training budget gives no assurance of high one-step return;
RL can even erode low-step behavior already present in the prior.
This gap motivates us to train and select one field across its deployment
range (Figure 1).

Two properties of the training objective and solver discretization help
explain this behavior. First, reconstruction-based objectives may provide
limited supervision at the deployment boundary. In FPO
(McAllister et al., 2025), for example, the noise-reconstruction formulation
weights velocity error by $(1-t)^{2}$ , so the boundary queried by a one-step
solver receives zero weight. Direct velocity supervision instead provides a
boundary-aligned signal. This observation complements prior work on
time-dependent loss weighting in generative modeling
(Choi et al., 2022; Hang et al., 2023) and highlights the importance of aligning
the weighting scheme with the deployed action map. Second, because different
solver budgets induce different action maps, updates based on rollouts from a
single budget need not improve all budgets uniformly. We study this effect in
the ReinFlow (Zhang et al., 2025b) fine-tuning pipeline, where
native velocity supervision is already available.

We introduce Action–Ratio–Clip Flow (ArcFlow), an RL algorithm
for one flow field across a specified solver-budget range. Its control
realization uses a fixed-grid bridge with an exact one-step
action-reconstruction residual. It aggregates temporal probes into one
energy-based surrogate ratio and one PPO-style clip per action; the ratio is
not the executed policy’s likelihood ratio. For native velocity objectives,
multi-budget endpoint losses and an anchored max–min update extend the same
deployment principle without replacing the original objective. Both
realizations retain one field and the original deployment architecture.

Our contributions are:

• 

A diagnosis tied to deployment. We expose one-step
degradation hidden by training-budget evaluation and identify how the
supervision target suppresses the relevant boundary signal.

• 

An algorithm for multiple solver budgets. We connect an exact
action-reconstruction identity to action-aligned supervision, budget
exposure, and worst-budget checkpoint selection.

• 

Evidence across objectives and domains. A controlled
common-stack benchmark and a released manipulation pipeline test the
algorithm, with component studies and deployment-cost accounting identifying
the sources of improvement. Across DM Control, manipulation, and locomotion, ArcFlow leads five
flow-policy baselines in mean one-step return on 25 of
30 tasks including cross-regime manipulation comparisons.

## 2 Problem formulation

Let $s$ denote an environment state, $z\sim p_{0}$ source noise, and
 $v_{\theta}(x,t\mid s)$ a velocity field with reverse-time convention
 $t:1\to 0$ . A numerical solver $S_{K}$ with $K$ steps induces the policy
 $a=F^{S_{K}}_{\theta}(z,s)$ . The environment observes the resulting action;
the solver’s internal queries do not constitute environment transitions.
Write $J_{K}(\theta)$ for the expected episodic return of this policy.
Our objective is to learn one field that remains useful across a specified
set of deployment budgets, without supplying $K$ as an input to the field.

Flow-policy algorithms use several tractable training signals, including
flow-matching residuals, velocity-based surrogates, and likelihoods of
stochastic internal transitions. These signals
differ in their treatment of solver time. An improvement in a training
surrogate, or in $J_{K_{\rm train}}$ , does not in itself imply an improvement
in $J_{K}$ for a different budget. We distinguish three outcomes. Numerical failure is a non-finite
update or evaluation. Cross-budget degradation is a lower return at
one deployment budget than another. Temporal degradation is a fall
in return during training at a fixed budget. The census calls a task a
one-step collapse when its specified one-to-sixteen-step return
statistic falls below one half. This threshold is descriptive and depends
on the seed pool and aggregation rule; it is not a
synonym for temporal degradation. We report the graded return ratio
alongside the threshold count.

## Boundary suppression.

For a straight bridge $x_{t}=(1-t)a+t\varepsilon$ and target velocity
 $u=\varepsilon-a$ , the reference implementation predicts noise as
 $\hat{\varepsilon}_{\theta}=x_{t}+(1-t)v_{\theta}(x_{t},t\mid s)$ . Thus

 $\|\hat{\varepsilon}_{\theta}-\varepsilon\|_{2,d}^{2}=(1-t)^{2}\|v_{\theta}(x_{t},t\mid s)-u\|_{2,d}^{2},$ 

(1)

where $\|r\|_{2,d}^{2}=d^{-1}\sum_{i}r_{i}^{2}$ . Both the loss and its actor
gradient vanish at $t=1$ , the only node queried by a one-step reverse Euler
solver. Nearby supervision is also quadratically down-weighted. Velocity
supervision removes this factor. The ablations in
Section 5.4 attributes most of the one-step recovery to this
change. The released ReinFlow study complements this target-level
diagnosis by testing deployment-aware training in a pipeline that already
supervises velocity (Table 5.2).

## Collapse versus absolute performance.

The collapse count and the mean return capture different aspects of
solver-budget behavior. DPPO and PolicyFlow have no marked collapses in
Figure 2(a), yet their mean returns remain well below
ArcFlow at every displayed budget in panel (b). Conversely, a method can
achieve a higher aggregate return while still containing tasks with severe
one-to-sixteen-step degradation. Evaluating low-step return together with
within-policy retention is therefore necessary: the absence of collapse alone
does not imply an effective low-latency policy.

## 4.1 Budget-cycled rollouts

For $z\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I)$ , a reverse Euler rollout produces

 $x_{0}=z,\qquad x_{j+1}=x_{j}-hv_{\theta}(x_{j},t_{j}\mid s),\qquad t_{j}=1-j/K,\quad h=1/K,\quad a=x_{K}.$ 

(3)

Training cycles through $\mathcal{S}=\{1,2,3,4,6,8,12,16\}$ in increasing
order, using one $K$ for every rollout in an outer iteration. This gives
equal exposure to each supported budget over a cycle. Parameters evolve across
the cycle, and the field is not conditioned on $K$ .

## 4.2 Boundary-aware bridge

For each collected tuple $(s,a,\widehat{A})$ , draw fresh noise
 $\widetilde{z}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I)$ and define

 $\widetilde{x}_{t}=(1-t)a+t\widetilde{z},\qquad u=\widetilde{z}-a.$ 

(4)

The bridge is an analytic reference path to the observed action, not the
trajectory that generated it. On the fixed reference grid
 $G_{4}=\{1,3/4,1/2,1/4\}$ , sample $j$ uniformly and score

 $\ell_{j}(\theta;s,a,\widetilde{z})=h\|v_{\theta}(\widetilde{x}_{t_{j}},t_{j}\mid s)-u\|_{2,d}^{2},\qquad h=1/4.$ 

(5)

The mean sampled energy estimates
 $L_{\theta}=\mathbb{E}_{\widetilde{z},j}[\ell_{j}]$ .
For fixed $\widetilde{z}$ , this is $\tfrac{1}{4}\sum_{j=0}^{3}\ell_{j}$ ,
not the unnormalized sum of grid residuals. The factor matters because it
sets the scale of the exponential weight below. Every collecting solver
uses the same grid and scale.

Proposition 1 (Exact one-step reconstruction).

For any fixed $(s,a,\widetilde{z})$ , the boundary residual satisfies

 $h^{-1}\ell_{0}(\theta)=\|\widetilde{z}-v_{\theta}(\widetilde{z},1\mid s)-a\|_{2,d}^{2}=\|F^{S_{1}}_{\theta}(\widetilde{z},s)-a\|_{2,d}^{2}.$ 

The identity follows by substituting $t_{0}=1$ into
Eqs. 3–5.
It links one quarter of the sampled-node distribution to the reconstruction
error of a one-step action. The appendix extends this connection to endpoint
error bounds under regularity assumptions
(Propositions 3
and 5). The resulting design separates boundary
supervision from the fixed-grid bridge used to score actions at every rollout
budget.

## Action-level surrogate.

Let $\bar{\theta}$ be the rollout parameters. Cache $M$ common noise–node
probes for the old and new policies and form

 $\widehat{\Delta}_{\theta}=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{m=1}^{M}[\ell_{j_{m}}(\bar{\theta})-\ell_{j_{m}}(\theta)],\qquad\widehat{w}_{\theta}=\exp\{\operatorname{clip}(\widehat{\Delta}_{\theta},-c_{E},c_{E})\}.$ 

(6)

We maximize a PPO-style clipped surrogate (Schulman et al., 2017),

 $\mathcal{J}_{\rm Arc}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}\left[\min\left(\widehat{w}_{\theta}\widehat{A},\operatorname{clip}(\widehat{w}_{\theta},1-\epsilon,1+\epsilon)\widehat{A}\right)\right].$ 

(7)

The probes are averaged before clipping, so each environment action receives
one weight and one advantage. The weight is an energy-based surrogate, not
the density ratio of the executed solver policy. Since common probes give
 $\widehat{\Delta}_{\bar{\theta}}=0$ , its local gradient is

 $\left.\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{J}_{\rm Arc}\right|_{\bar{\theta}}=-\left.\mathbb{E}[\widehat{A}\nabla_{\theta}\widehat{L}_{\theta}(s,a)]\right|_{\bar{\theta}}.$ 

(8)

Positive advantages therefore decrease reconstruction energy locally.
Appendix Proposition 4 relates this expression to
an auxiliary normalized exponential-tilt policy under its stated centering
assumptions; that auxiliary policy need not equal the deployed flow policy.

The rollout path and reference bridge have distinct roles: the former chooses
the action that receives an environment advantage, whereas the latter scores
that action against fresh noise. Changing $K$ therefore changes which actions
enter the update without changing the grid or scale of their bridge energies.
Locally, positive advantages lower the energy of collected actions and
negative advantages raise it; the cached probes make the old and current
energies comparable during each actor update.
At every rollout budget, uniform sampling on $G_{4}$ assigns probability $1/4$ 
to $t=1$ . Thus the exact one-step residual in
Proposition 1 contributes to the expected actor update
even when rollouts take many steps. Averaging the $M$ probes before exponentiation gives
each collected action one weight, aligned with its one advantage, rather than
separate weights for individual bridge nodes.

## 4.3 Training and selection

The reference grid $G_{4}$ and its $1/4$ residual scale remain fixed as
the rollout budget changes. Actor updates reuse cached probes, and the critic
follows the backbone rule. Algorithm 1 evaluates the initial
field and each cycle-end checkpoint at $\mathcal{K}=\{1,4,16\}$ , then selects

 $\theta^{\star}\in\arg\max_{\theta\in\mathcal{C}}\min_{K\in\mathcal{K}}\widehat{J}^{\rm val}_{K}(\theta),$ 

(9)

where $\mathcal{C}$ is the set of evaluated checkpoints.
Taking the minimum prevents a high return at one validation budget from
compensating for a low return at another. The selected $\theta^{\star}$ is one
shared field: deployment changes the solver budget, not the checkpoint or the
policy parameters.
Training and selection have different roles: Eq. 7
updates the field through the advantage-weighted bridge surrogate, whereas
Eq. 9 ranks the resulting checkpoints by
environment return. No gradient is taken through the validation returns.

## CONCLUSION
ArcFlow aligns flow-policy learning with its deployment range through
boundary supervision, solver-budget exposure, and worst-budget selection.
Across three control domains it improves one-step performance while retaining
competitive higher-budget returns; the native ReinFlow study extends the
principle to a released image-policy pipeline and five budgets. Deployment
retains one architecture and pays only for the selected solver budget.
Component studies further separate the gains from action-aligned supervision,
budget exposure, and selection. These results support training and evaluating
generative policies over their full deployment range.

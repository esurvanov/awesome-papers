## ABSTRACT
Voice style conversion (VSC) aims to transform a source utterance to match the timbre, accent, and emotion of a target voice while preserving linguistic content. StyleStream 1.0 introduced the first streamable zero-shot VSC system with state-of-the-art conversion quality, but remained limited in two ways: approximately 1s end-to-end latency on consumer hardware, and the lack of a text-based interface for designing or editing target voices. We present StyleStream 2.0, a fast and controllable streaming VSC framework that addresses these limitations. To reduce latency, we train a few-step pixel MeanFlow model and further fine-tune it with autoregressive feedback inspired by Self Forcing, improving robustness under streaming inference. This reduces end-to-end latency to 520~ms on a consumer GPU, a 2x speedup over StyleStream 1.0, while maintaining conversion quality. To enable controllable style manipulation, we remove the mel-spectrogram context and instead route target style information through a compact style embedding. In this embedding space, we train a unified text-conditioned flow matching model that supports both text-based voice design, which maps natural language prompts to voice styles, and instruction-based voice editing, which modifies a specific attribute of a source utterance while preserving the rest. Experiments show that StyleStream 2.0 achieves the strongest target style fidelity on VSC, voice design, and voice editing, while remaining competitive on intelligibility and non-edited attribute preservation.

## 1 Introduction

Voice style conversion (VSC) modifies how an utterance is spoken while preserving what is said.
Following StyleStream 1.0 [41], we define voice style as the joint combination of timbre, accent, and emotion.
A large body of voice conversion work focuses on timbre conversion, aiming to change speaker identity while preserving content and other paralinguistic factors [52, 37, 61, 9, 4, 42, 65].
Separate lines of work address accent conversion [30, 5, 29] and emotional voice conversion [72, 48, 10] as standalone tasks.
More recent systems have begun to address holistic style transfer, jointly converting timbre, accent, and emotion from a single reference utterance [69, 36, 68, 3, 41].
Among them, StyleStream 1.0 [41] is the first to demonstrate high-quality zero-shot VSC in a streaming setting.
However, its approximately one-second end-to-end latency on consumer hardware remains high for latency-sensitive interaction.
Reducing this latency while preserving holistic conversion quality is therefore a key challenge for making streaming VSC practical.

A second limitation is that existing VSC systems largely require a target reference utterance.
This makes voice specification indirect: users must provide an example voice rather than describe the desired attributes.
In contrast, text-prompted control has become increasingly common in text-to-speech, where natural-language prompts can specify accent, emotion, and fine-grained vocal qualities such as a soft, warm, breathy, or singing-like voice [21, 34, 64, 59, 25, 26, 6, 12].
However, these systems synthesize speech from text rather than converting an input utterance, and therefore do not directly solve streaming speech-to-speech VSC.
Recent speech-to-speech conversion work also explores text-guided editing [54, 22, 66, 51], but typically over a single axis such as timbre or emotion.
To our knowledge, no prior system provides text-based voice design and editing for holistic VSC, let alone in a streaming setting.

We present StyleStream 2.0, a fast and controllable streaming VSC framework that addresses both limitations.
StyleStream 2.0 inherits the two-stage architecture of StyleStream 1.0: a Destylizer that extracts content features disentangled from style, and a Stylizer that reconstructs target speech from those content features and a target style.
Because StyleStream 1.0 already established the Destylizer as a strong content-style disentangler, we reuse it without modification and focus our changes on the Stylizer.

To reduce latency, we train a few-step pixel MeanFlow [18] Stylizer to replace the multi-step diffusion Stylizer used in StyleStream 1.0.
We further fine-tune this Stylizer with autoregressive feedback inspired by Self Forcing [27] to improve robustness under chunk-by-chunk inference.
This reduces end-to-end latency to 520 ms on a consumer GPU, a 2 $\times$ speedup over StyleStream 1.0, while maintaining conversion quality.

To enable text-based control, we change how style enters the Stylizer.
StyleStream 1.0 conditions the Stylizer on both an unmasked mel-spectrogram context and a style embedding extracted from the target utterance, which leaves style information partially distributed across two conditioning channels.
We remove the mel-spectrogram context so that target style is represented through a single style embedding.
This makes text-based design and editing a problem of predicting or modifying this embedding.
Inspired by the unified image generation and editing model FLUX Kontext [33], we train a unified text-conditioned DiT in the style-embedding space: given a natural-language prompt, the model predicts a target style embedding for voice design; given an existing utterance embedding and a text instruction, the same model modifies the embedding along the requested attribute for voice editing.

For text-based control, we focus on age, gender, accent, and emotion.
These attributes provide a structured and measurable interface for specifying target voices, while covering the major perceptual factors needed for holistic VSC.
This structured interface supports both prompt-based voice design and attribute-targeted voice editing, and remains largely unexplored for holistic VSC, especially under streaming constraints.

We evaluate StyleStream 2.0 in two settings.
First, for standard target-speech-conditioned VSC, we compare against existing reference-based conversion systems and measure conversion quality, intelligibility, and target style fidelity.
Second, for text-based control, no native holistic VSC baseline exists.
We therefore construct strong cascaded baselines: for voice design, we combine ASR with prompt-controlled TTS; for voice editing, we combine prompt-controlled TTS with reference-based VSC.
These cascades benefit from powerful ASR and TTS systems, yet StyleStream 2.0 achieves stronger results on many metrics while remaining a pure speech-to-speech system that also supports streaming inference.

In summary, our contributions are:

• 

We introduce a faster streaming VSC model by training a few-step pixel MeanFlow Stylizer and fine-tuning it with autoregressive feedback, reducing end-to-end latency to approximately 520 ms on consumer hardware while preserving conversion quality.

• 

We propose a unified text-conditioned style-embedding model for natural-language voice design and attribute-targeted editing over age, gender, accent, and emotion in holistic VSC.

• 

We evaluate StyleStream 2.0 across standard target-speech-conditioned VSC and text-based voice design / editing, showing strong target-style fidelity while remaining competitive in intelligibility, non-edited attribute preservation, and audio quality against both reference-based and cascaded baselines.

## 2 Related Work

Prior speech-to-speech conversion work spans several directions, including voice conversion that primarily changes speaker identity while preserving content and other style factors [52, 37, 61, 9, 4, 40, 32], streaming or real-time voice conversion [42, 65, 45, 63], and separate lines on accent conversion and emotional voice conversion [30, 5, 29, 72, 48, 10]. More recent systems have begun to model richer, holistic voice style across timbre, accent, and emotion, including the Vevo family [69, 36, 68] and StyleStream 1.0 [41]. In parallel, prompt-controlled TTS systems enable natural-language specification of target voice characteristics [21, 34, 64, 59, 25, 26, 6, 12], with only a few open-source systems explicitly supporting holistic control over timbre, accent, and emotion [60, 25]. Closer to our setting, a smaller body of work explores text-guided speech-to-speech conversion [54, 22, 66, 51], but these methods typically focus on a single attribute and are evaluated offline. Thus, while prior work shows that speech-to-speech conversion, prompt-based control, and limited text-guided editing are all feasible, no prior system simultaneously provides fast streaming holistic VSC together with unified text-based voice design and editing.

Separately, diffusion and flow-matching models have become strong acoustic generators for speech synthesis and conversion [14, 13, 8, 15, 35, 40], but their iterative sampling cost is a bottleneck for streaming applications. Recent few-step generative models reduce this cost by producing high-quality samples in only a few steps, or even one step [17, 56, 57, 18, 19, 44]. However, fast sampling alone does not guarantee high-quality streaming inference, since short chunks provide limited context and can lead to degraded quality at chunk boundaries. Inspired by Self Forcing for autoregressive video diffusion [27], StyleStream 2.0 combines a few-step pixel MeanFlow Stylizer with autoregressive fine-tuning to improve streaming robustness while also enabling controllable holistic voice design and editing.

## 3 Method

StyleStream 2.0 follows the Destylizer-Stylizer framework of StyleStream 1.0 [41].
The Destylizer extracts source content features while suppressing speaker timbre, accent, and emotion.
The Stylizer maps these content features and a target style embedding to a mel-spectrogram, which is converted to waveform by a causal Vocos-based vocoder [55].
The style embedding can be obtained from a reference utterance or from a text-conditioned Voice Controller, allowing the same Stylizer to support reference-based conversion, text-based design, and text-guided editing.

We first recap the Destylizer, then introduce the redesigned Stylizer with pixel MeanFlow training and autoregressive feedback fine-tuning, and finally present the Voice Controller for unified voice design and editing.

## 3.1 Destylizer

We use the Destylizer from StyleStream 1.0.
The Destylizer consists of a frozen HuBERT-Large [24] encoder followed by six conformer [20] blocks.
During training, the Destylizer output is passed through a finite scalar quantization (FSQ) [47] module and then a transformer ASR decoder.
The conformer blocks and the ASR decoder are jointly optimized with a sequence-to-sequence ASR loss.
This training setup encourages the representation to preserve linguistic content, while the compact FSQ bottleneck, with a codebook size of 45, discourages encoding style cues that are not needed for character prediction.
At inference, we discard the FSQ module and ASR decoder, and use the continuous output features immediately before the FSQ layer as content features $f_{c}\in\mathbb{R}^{D_{c}\times T}$ at 50 Hz.

## 3.2 Stylizer

## 3.2.1 Preliminary: Pixel MeanFlow (pMF)

Pixel MeanFlow (pMF) [44] combines JiT-style [39] $x$ -prediction with MeanFlow [18, 19] objective for few-step guided generation.
Let $x\sim p_{\mathrm{data}}$ be a clean sample and $\epsilon\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I)$ be Gaussian noise.
We use the linear interpolation path

 $z_{t}=(1-t)x+t\epsilon,\qquad t\in[0,1],$ 

(1)

whose instantaneous Flow Matching velocity is

 $v(z_{t},t)=\frac{dz_{t}}{dt}=\epsilon-x.$ 

(2)

JiT motivates predicting the clean sample $x$ rather than noisy or velocity-space quantities, since $x$ lies closer to the data manifold and can be easier for a Transformer to model in high-dimensional spaces.
MeanFlow instead improves sampling efficiency by learning an average velocity over an interval $[r,t]$ :

 $u(z_{t},r,t)=\frac{1}{t-r}\int_{r}^{t}v(z_{\tau},\tau)\,d\tau,$ 

(3)

which is related to the instantaneous velocity by the MeanFlow identity

 $v(z_{t},t)=u(z_{t},r,t)+(t-r)\frac{d}{dt}u(z_{t},r,t).$ 

(4)

pMF combines these two ideas by having the network predict an $x$ -like quantity rather than a velocity.
Because pMF uses classifier-free guidance (CFG) distillation, the guidance scale $\omega$ is also provided as an input to the network.
Given condition $c$ , the model prediction $\hat{x}_{\theta}(z_{t},r,t,c,\omega)$ is converted to an average velocity by

 $u_{\theta}(z_{t},r,t,c,\omega)=\frac{z_{t}-\hat{x}_{\theta}(z_{t},r,t,c,\omega)}{t}.$ 

(5)

Let $\varnothing$ denote the null condition.
The conditional and unconditional instantaneous fields are obtained from the degenerate interval $r=t$ :

 $v_{\theta}(z_{t},t,c,\omega)=u_{\theta}(z_{t},t,t,c,\omega),\qquad v_{\theta}(z_{t},t,\varnothing,\omega)=u_{\theta}(z_{t},t,t,\varnothing,\omega).$ 

(6)

Following the pMF CFG distillation formulation, the guided velocity target is

 $v_{\mathrm{cfg}}(z_{t},t,c,\omega)=(\epsilon-x)+\left(1-\frac{1}{\omega}\right)\left(v_{\theta}(z_{t},t,c,\omega)-v_{\theta}(z_{t},t,\varnothing,\omega)\right).$ 

(7)

When $\omega=1$ , the guidance vanishes and the target reduces to the standard Flow Matching velocity.

Using the MeanFlow identity, the instantaneous velocity implied by the predicted average velocity is

 $V_{\theta}(z_{t},r,t,c,\omega)=u_{\theta}(z_{t},r,t,c,\omega)+(t-r)\frac{d}{dt}u_{\theta}(z_{t},r,t,c,\omega),$ 

(8)

where the total derivative is computed with a Jacobian-vector product (JVP).
The final pMF objective is

 $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{pMF}}=\mathbb{E}_{x,\epsilon,c,r,t,\omega}\left[\left\|V_{\theta}(z_{t},r,t,c,\omega)-v_{\mathrm{cfg}}(z_{t},t,c,\omega)\right\|_{2}^{2}\right].$ 

(9)

pMF keeps the network prediction in an $x$ -like space while applying supervision in the MeanFlow velocity space needed for few-step guided generation. With CFG distillation, guided sampling requires only one model evaluation per sampling step, further reducing inference cost.

## 3.2.2 Stylizer Architecture and Training

Architecture.

An overview of the Stylizer is in Figure 1. Our Stylizer is a DiT-based model that follows the overall design of StyleStream 1.0, with two main modifications.
First, we remove the reference mel-spectrogram context used for mel-inpainting in StyleStream 1.0, so target style must be represented entirely by the style embedding.
This makes the style space directly usable for voice design and editing.
Second, because the style embedding is now the only source of target-style information, we replace the original TDNN-based style encoder [31] with a stronger perceiver-resampler encoder [28, 2, 38].

The new style encoder first extracts SSL features from a frozen WavLM [7] model11

 https://huggingface.co/microsoft/wavlm-base-plus.
Let the WavLM hidden states be $H\in\mathbb{R}^{B\times T\times L\times D}$ , where $B$ is the batch size, $T$ is the number of frames, $L=12$ is the number of WavLM layers, and $D$ is the hidden dimension.
Instead of reducing $H$ by layer-weighted summation and temporal pooling as in StyleStream 1.0, we concatenate all layer outputs along time to obtain $\tilde{H}\in\mathbb{R}^{B\times(TL)\times D}$ .
A perceiver resampler with $N_{q}$ learnable queries compresses $\tilde{H}$ into a fixed-size representation $S\in\mathbb{R}^{B\times N_{q}\times D_{q}}$ , through cross-attention to $\tilde{H}$ and self-attention among the queries.
We flatten $S$ and apply an MLP to obtain the style embedding $e_{s}\in\mathbb{R}^{B\times C}$ , where $C$ is the final style embedding dimension.
The style encoder is trained jointly with the Stylizer under the pMF objective, and we compare it with the StyleStream 1.0 style encoder in Section 5.4.

Training.

We train the Stylizer using the pMF objective introduced in Section 3.2.1.
Here, $x$ is the target mel-spectrogram, $z_{t}=(1-t)x+t\epsilon$ , and the condition is $c=(f_{c},e_{s})$ , where $f_{c}$ is the Destylizer content representation and $e_{s}$ is the style embedding.
During training, $f_{c}$ and $e_{s}$ are extracted from the same utterance. At inference, $e_{s}$ may instead come from a different reference utterance or from the Voice Controller (see Section 3.3).

At the DiT input, we concatenate $z_{t}$ , $f_{c}$ , and a past-output channel along the channel dimension.
This past-output channel is zero during offline pretraining and is filled with generated history during autoregressive fine-tuning (Section 3.2.3).
The flow times $r$ and $t$ , style embedding $e_{s}$ , and guidance scale $\omega$ are embedded as vectors, summed, and injected into the DiT through adaLN-zero [49].
The model predicts an $x$ -like mel estimate, which is converted into the average velocity $u_{\theta}(z_{t},r,t,c,\omega)$ by Eq. (5) and the implied instantaneous velocity $V_{\theta}$ by Eq. (8).

For CFG distillation, we randomly sample the guidance scale $\omega$ and replace $c=(f_{c},e_{s})$ with null condition $\varnothing$ with probability $p_{\mathrm{drop}}$ .
When constructing the CFG target velocity, we stop gradients on $v_{\mathrm{cfg}}$ (Eq. (7)), so the model is updated only through the predicted compound velocity $V_{\theta}$ .
At inference, we provide the desired $\omega$ directly and require only one model evaluation per sampling step.

## CONCLUSION
We introduced StyleStream 2.0, a fast and controllable streaming VSC system. By combining a few-step pixel MeanFlow Stylizer with autoregressive fine-tuning, it reduces streaming latency while preserving holistic style conversion quality. A unified text-conditioned controller further enables natural-language voice design and single-attribute voice editing in the same style-embedding space. Across reference-conditioned VSC, voice design, and editing, StyleStream 2.0 improves target-style fidelity while remaining competitive in intelligibility and preservation.

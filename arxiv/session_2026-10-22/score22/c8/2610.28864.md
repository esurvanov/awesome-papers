## ABSTRACT
A systematic understanding of the global structure of neural-network loss landscapes remains challenging due to their high dimensionality. Symmetries arising from network architectures and learning objectives provide a way to investigate aspects of this structure. Although a growing number of studies have drawn attention to these symmetries, a systematic characterization of them, as well as of the critical orbits they give rise to, remains incomplete. To address this gap, in this work we establish a topological symmetry index, based on the methodologies from the family of equivariant degree theories, to decode these symmetries. The frameworks apply to arbitrary network architectures and include methods applicable to degenerate critical orbits. The index detects the isotropy of the critical points and quantifies the corresponding net topological contributions. Several examples, including softplus MLPs, Transformers, and equivariant neural networks, are presented to illustrate the computations.

## 1. Introduction

Symmetries in neural network give rise to a rich variety of phenomena in its high-dimensional loss landscapes. One line of research, developed by Arjevani and Field, studies the spurious minima and their symmetries in shallow networks, as well as their bifurcations as network width $k$ varies. For two-layer ReLU networks in a specific teacher–student setting, they observed in [3] that critical points obtained by SGD have symmetries conjugate to maximal subgroups of $S_{k}$ of intransitive type. In [6], they analytically constructed these families of critical points and expressed them as convergent power series in $k^{-1/2}.$ The corresponding Hessian spectra were further analyzed in [4]. They further extended analogous results to symmetric tensor decomposition in [1], and studied the optimization landscape of networks with polynomial activations [2]. In [5], they identified width thresholds in a shallow ReLU network at which saddles turn into local minima and rigorously established in [7] that over-parameterization (adding neurons) introduces negative Hessian eigenvalues and thereby turns local minima into saddles. Using equivariant bifurcation theory, they further showed in [8] that the local minima emerge through steady-state bifurcations along the standard representation of $S_{k}$ rather than through spontaneous symmetry breaking of global minima. Correspondingly, Liu [30] studied bifurcations from global minima in leaky ReLU networks of arbitrary width, using the negative-slope parameter as the bifurcation parameter, and obtained unified results on the bifurcating branches and their symmetries.

Beyond the structure of critical points, other work connects network symmetries to the broader geometry of loss landscapes, training dynamics, and optimization. Zhao et al. [39] identified flat low-loss valleys with continuous symmetry-induced critical orbits, and derived initialization-fixed conserved quantities that constrain gradient-flow reachability. In [37], they further studied the connectivity of minima and identified loss barriers along linear mode connectivity. Zamir et al. [36] improved the learning-to-optimize (L2O) framework by enabling the optimizer to select, along group orbits, parameters that are more favorable for subsequent descent prior to local updates. The discovery of the symmetry actions themselves is addressed in [38], which proposes a method for automatically discovering data-dependent symmetries through infinitesimal characterizations of Lie group and expressing loss invariance as differential constraints.

The use of symmetry extends further to network design, where equivariant architectures incorporate prescribed symmetries to improve data efficiency and physical consistency. Kondor and Trivedi [29] established, under appropriate assumptions, the equivalence between compact-group equivariance and generalized convolutional structure. Tensor field networks [33] combine spherical harmonics and Clebsch–Gordan coupling to construct equivariant layers for three-dimensional point clouds, while [28] use tensor products and irreducible decompositions to implement equivariant nonlinearities entirely in Fourier space. [18] developed concentric spherical networks combining intra-sphere graph convolutions with inter-sphere radial convolutions, with equivariance established for the icosahedral rotation group. The e3nn framework [25] provides a unified set of composable operations for constructing $E(3)$ -equivariant architectures. For physical dynamical systems, Wang et al. [34] designed forecasting networks incorporating different physical symmetries, and demonstrated improvements in prediction and generalization. They subsequently introduced relaxed group and steerable convolutions with symmetry regularization to accommodate imperfectly symmetric dynamics [35], and established an approximation-error lower bound for strictly equivariant models applied to non-equivariant targets.

These studies demonstrate the importance of symmetry for understanding loss landscapes and training dynamics, as well as its value for optimizer and network design. However, when it comes to the symmetric structure of the loss landscape, most of the above analyses either focus on a specific network architecture or presuppose a priori knowledge of the critical points. These considerations motivate a complementary question:

Can a unified, computable criterion certify the presence of critical orbits and characterize their symmetries
across different network architectures without explicitly constructing the
critical points?

In this work, we address this question by introducing a topological symmetry index based on the methodology of Equivariant Degree Theory. Under suitable hypotheses, the index provides criteria for the existence of critical orbits with specified symmetries within a prescribed region.

To formulate the problem precisely, let $G$ be a symmetry group inherent in a neural network and acting on its parameter space. The interplay among architectural structure, learning setting, data distribution, activation functions, and loss specification can endow the resulting loss function $\mathscr{L}$ with $G$ -invariance, i.e. $\mathscr{L}(g\cdot\theta)=\mathscr{L}(\theta),g\in G.$ 
When the group action is isometric, this invariance implies the $G$ -equivariance of its gradient:

(1)

 $\nabla\mathscr{L}(g\cdot\theta)=g\cdot\nabla\mathscr{L}(\theta),\;\;g\in G.$ 

We therefore seek the zeros of $\nabla\mathscr{L}$ , which are precisely the critical points of $\mathscr{L}$ . The equivariance allows us to obtain integer-valued topological weights associated with orbit types. We define the symmetry index

(2)

 $\mathcal{I}_{G}(\mathscr{L},\Omega)=\Big\{\big((K_{j_{1}}),n_{j_{1}}\big),\big((K_{j_{2}}),n_{j_{2}}\big),\cdots,\big((K_{j_{s}}),n_{j_{s}}\big)\Big\}$ 

by extracting the maximal orbit types among those with nonzero coefficients associated with $\nabla\mathscr{L}$ on $\Omega$ , written as

(3)

 $\mathbf{a}=\sum_{j=1}^{m}n_{j}(K_{j})\in U(G).$ 

Here, $U(G)$ is the Euler ring (see appendix A for definition and properties of Euler ring), and each integer coefficient $n_{j},\;j=1,\cdots,m$ is associated with the conjugacy class $(K_{j}),$ where $K_{j}$ is a subgroup of $G$ . A nonzero coefficient certifies the existence of a critical orbit whose isotropy type is at least $(K_{j}).$ The selected maximal orbit types are denoted by $(K_{j_{1}}),\ldots,(K_{j_{s}})$ . The underlying degree theories and computational methods are introduced in Sections 3 and 4. One is also refered to [11, 13, 12, 17, 16, 22, 24, 23, 21, 32] and the reference therein for more comprehensive details about the constructions of equivariant degrees, as well as their applications [20, 9, 14, 10, 19, 30].

Theorem 1 (Topological symmetry index of a neural-network).

Suppose that the parameter space $V$ is a finite-dimensional orthogonal representation of a compact Lie group $G$ , and that
the loss function $\mathscr{L}$ is twice continuously
differentiable and $G$ -invariant. Let $\Omega\subset V$ be an open bounded and $G$ -invariant parameter subset such that $\nabla\mathscr{L}$ is nonzero on $\partial\Omega$ . Then the symmetry index $\mathcal{I}_{G}(\mathscr{L},\Omega)$ defined in (2) is well defined. Moreover, for every

 $\bigl((K_{j_{r}}),n_{j_{r}}\bigr)\in\mathcal{I}_{G}(\mathscr{L},\Omega),\;\;r=1,\cdots,s,$ 

there exists $\theta_{*}\in\Omega$ such that

 $\nabla\mathscr{L}(\theta_{*})=0,$ 

and its symmetry is at least $(K_{j_{r}}).$ 

The same conclusion holds when $V$ is replaced by a $G$ -manifold equipped with a $G$ -invariant Riemannian metric (see Section 4).

The subsequent sections are arranged as follows. For the readers convenience, Section 2 collects the notations and definitions used throughout the paper. Given that the loss function in (1) is of gradient type, Sections 3 and 4 present two gradient-related Equivariant Degree methods, together with the algorithms to determine $(K)$ and the corresponding $n_{K}$ . More precisely, in sections 3.1-3.2, one can find construction of gradient degree based on recursive formula and the method of reducing to slice. In section 3.3, we focus on the linear isomorphism in which the ambient space $V$ is an orthogonal $G$ -representation, and the degree is constructed through the basic degree induced by irreducible decomposition of $V.$ On the other hand, Section 4 treats the general setting of a $G$ -manifold $V$ . Without an underlying linear structure, the construction proceeds instead through orbit-type stratification and an inductive summation over the resulting strata. Section 5 then applies the resulting index to several concrete examples: a softplus teacher–student model (Section 5.1), an $O(3)$ -equivariant network (Section 5.2), and a transformer architecture (Section 5.3), using the linear isomorphism method, the slice principle, and the recursive formula, respectively. Appendix B recalls the construction and computational algorithm of the Equivariant Brouwer Degree, since, as the paper unfolds, one may quickly observe that the computation of the gradient-related Equivariant Degree methods ultimately can reduce to their Brouwer counterpart. Moreover, Appendices A and C present the properties of the Euler and Burnside rings, and the definition of amalgamated groups, respectively.

## 2. Some Equivariant Terminology

We first emphasize that the degree theories considered in this work require $G$ to be a compact Lie group. Accordingly, the present analysis does not cover noncompact groups such as the positive scaling symmetries associated with ReLU activation.

To construct the symmetry index $\mathcal{I}_{G}(\mathscr{L},\Omega)$ , one must first specify the following several ingredients:

• 

The loss function $\mathscr{L}$ should be $G$ -invariant, with $G$ -equivariant gradient $\nabla\mathscr{L}$ .

• 

The parameter space $V$ and the $G$ action on $V$ must be identified.

• 

A suitable domain $\Omega\subset V$ must be specified:
 $\Omega$ is required to be an open bounded $G$ -invariant subset of $V$ . Most importantly, $\Omega$ must be chosen so that

 $\nabla\mathscr{L}(\theta)\neq 0,\;\;\theta\in\partial\Omega,$ 

a condition on which the equivariant degree theory, and hence our approach, rests.

These constitute the basic ingredients needed for the construction of
 $\mathcal{I}_{G}(\mathscr{L},\Omega)$ . Each arises naturally from the construction of equivariant degree, and its significance will become apparent as the
framework is developed below. The resulting $G$ -equivariant gradient degree is an element of the Euler ring $U(G)$ of the form

 $\nabla_{G}\text{-}\deg(\nabla\mathscr{L},\Omega)=n_{1}(K_{1})+n_{2}(K_{2})+\cdots+n_{m}(K_{m}),$ 

from which the symmetry index $\mathcal{I}_{G}(\mathscr{L},\Omega)$ is read off directly. The precise definition of the degree, the term $(K_{j})$ and $n_{j}$ , and the techniques appropriate to the different settings to derive this degree will be developed in detail in Sections 3 and 4.

An orbit whose isotropy group belongs to the conjugacy class $(K)$ is called a $(K)$ -orbit. The set $\Phi(G)$ is partially ordered by

 $(K)\leq(H)\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad gKg^{-1}\subseteq H\text{ for some }g\in G.$

(4)

 $n(K,H):=\left|\left\{H^{\prime}\in(H):K\subseteq H^{\prime}\right\}\right|.$ 

Notice that $W(K)$ acts naturally on $V^{K}$ , and its action on $V_{K}$ is free. Finally, a smooth manifold equipped with a smooth $G$ -action will be called a $G$ -manifold.

We next collect several notions common to the degree constructions in the subsequent sections. For uniformity, we formulate them for a smooth $n$ -dimensional $G$ -manifold, an $n$ -dimensional orthogonal $G$ -representation constitutes a special case.

Equip the smooth $n$ -dimensional $G$ -manifold $V$ with a $G$ -invariant Riemannian metric, and choose a relatively compact invariant open subset $\Omega$ . We write $C_{G}^{2}(V,\mathbb{R})$ for the twice continuously differentiable $G$ -invariant functions on $V$ . For $\varphi\in C_{G}^{2}(V,\mathbb{R})$ , its gradient is an equivariant section of the tangent bundle:

 $\nabla\varphi:V\longrightarrow TV,\qquad\nabla\varphi(\theta)\in T_{\theta}V.$ 

Definition 2 ( $G$ -Equivariant Gradient Admissible Pair).

We call the pair $(\nabla\varphi,\Omega)$ a
 $G$ -equivariant gradient admissible pair if $\nabla\varphi(\theta)\neq 0,\;\theta\in\partial\Omega,$ i.e. $\nabla\varphi$ doesn’t vanish on the boundary of $\Omega.$ 

Denote by $\mathcal{M}_{G}^{\nabla}(V,TV)$ all the admissible pairs in $V$ . Allowing $V$ to range over finite-dimensional smooth $G$ -manifolds, we define

 $\mathcal{M}_{G}^{\nabla}:=\bigcup_{V}\mathcal{M}_{G}^{\nabla}(V,TV).$ 

Definition 3 ( $G$ -Gradient $\Omega$ -Admissible Homotopy).

We call a homotopy $\nabla\Phi:[0,1]\times V\longrightarrow TV$ $G$ -gradient $\Omega$ -admissible if, for every $t\in[0,1]$ ,

 $\nabla\Phi_{t}:=\nabla\Phi(t,\cdot):V\longrightarrow TV$ 

is a $G$ -equivariant gradient vector field and $\nabla\Phi(\theta)\neq 0,\theta\in\partial\Omega.$ 

We now introduce the two algebraic structures in which the equivariant
degrees considered below take their values.

Definition 4 (Euler ring).

The Euler ring of $G$ is the free $\mathbb{Z}$ -module

 $U(G):=\bigoplus_{(K)\in\Phi(G)}\mathbb{Z}(K),$ 

so that each $a\in U(G)$ has a unique representation

 $a=\sum_{(K)\in\Phi(G)}n_{K}(K),\qquad n_{K}\in\mathbb{Z},$ 

with only finitely many $n_{K}$ nonzero. Multiplication on $U(G)$ is defined on generators by

 $(K)*(H):=\sum_{(S)\in\Phi(G)}c_{S}(S),\qquad(K),(H)\in\Phi(G),$ 

where

(5)

 $c_{S}:=\chi_{c}\bigl((G/K\times G/H)_{S}/N(S)\bigr),$ 

and extended bilinearly to all of $U(G)$ . Here $\chi_{c}$ denotes the Euler characteristic with respect to Alexander–Spanier cohomology with compact support.

Definition 5 (Burnside ring).

The Burnside ring of $G$ is the free $\mathbb{Z}$ -module generated by the classes with finite Weyl group,

 $A(G):=\bigoplus_{(K)\in\Phi_{0}(G)}\mathbb{Z}(K),$ 

whose elements are written as

 $b=\sum_{(K)\in\Phi_{0}(G)}n_{K}(K)$ 

with $n_{K}\in\mathbb{Z}$ . Multiplication on $A(G)$ is defined by

 $(K)\cdot(H):=\sum_{(S)\in\Phi_{0}(G)}n_{S}(S),\qquad(K),(H)\in\Phi_{0}(G),$ 

where $n_{S}$ is the number of $(S)$ -orbits in $G/K\times G/H$ , and extended bilinearly to all of $A(G)$ .

Notice that $n_{S}$ can be determined recursively from

(6)

 $n_{S}=\frac{n(S,K)|W(K)|\,n(S,H)|W(H)|-\displaystyle\sum_{(\widetilde{S})>(S)}n_{\widetilde{S}}\,n(S,\widetilde{S})\,|W(\widetilde{S})|}{|W(S)|}.$ 

The two rings are related by the ring homomorphism $\pi_{0}\colon U(G)\to A(G),$ 

(7)

 $\pi_{0}((K))=\begin{cases}(K),&(K)\in\Phi_{0}(G),\\
0,&(K)\notin\Phi_{0}(G).\end{cases}$ 

As will be seen in the subsequent sections, the homomorphism $\pi_{0}$ 
provides the key reduction from the Euler-ring-valued equivariant gradient
degree to its Burnside-ring-valued counterpart, and will play an essential
role in the computation of the corresponding basic degrees. For more details regarding Burnside ring and Euler ring, including some of their properties and corresponding proofs, one is referred to Appendix A.

## ABSTRACT
As systems engineering (SE) shifts from monolithic systems to Systems of Systems, Mission Engineering (ME) has emerged as a new line of thinking in SE. Mission environments are uncertain and dynamic, and outcomes depend on how mission assets interact with them, rendering static architectures brittle and calling for flexible yet analytically rigorous ME approaches. We propose an intelligent mission coordination methodology integrating high-fidelity digital mission models with Reinforcement Learning (RL) for adaptive task allocation. Using a Digital Engineering (DE) infrastructure comprising a digital mission model and agent-based simulation, we formulate mission tactics management as a Markov Decision Process and train an RL coordinator with Proximal Policy Optimization to map mission states to tactical actions from realized outcomes. An aerial firefighting mission with three scenarios demonstrates the approach. First, all trained coordinators significantly outperform random and heuristic baselines. Second, single-scenario coordinators can be brittle: the coordinator trained on the more forgiving scenario degrades to random tactics performance in more demanding scenarios, whereas the demanding-scenario coordinator retains most of its advantage when transferred. Third, within the same training budget, a coordinator trained on both scenarios concurrently matches the scenario-specific coordinators in their own scenarios and significantly outperforms every other policy. Sensitivity analyses on ignition location, weather, scenario, and training curricula show that the variability represented during training shapes post-deployment performance. This proof of concept shows DE-enabled mission simulations combined with RL offer a reusable methodology for improving ME practice, which can be extended to fleet design and selection from a mission-first perspective.

## 1 Introduction

Systems Engineering (SE) has traditionally focused on the realization and lifecycle management of complex engineered systems within a given boundary 1. However, many modern capabilities are delivered by Systems of Systems (SoS): collections of operationally and managerially independent systems that together achieve what none can achieve alone 2. Research on SoS architectures has made substantial progress in application areas such as aerospace 3, 4, 5, disaster management 6, supply chains 7, 8, and healthcare 9, 10, 11, among many others 12, 13, 14. Nevertheless, deployed SoS often remain brittle. They are costly to modify once fielded, and their performance is sensitive to operational and contextual changes 15, 16. Ensuring that an SoS remains effective therefore requires a perspective anchored in the missions it serves and extending to how its constituent systems are coordinated during operations.

To address this need, Mission Engineering (ME) has been proposed as an interdisciplinary “process” that bridges the gap between a mission and the engineering activities that support it; spanning the analysis, design, and integration of operational needs and capabilities toward desired mission outcomes 17. Within ME, two tightly coupled decision problems arise, reflecting needs identified by Raz et al. 15: selecting and configuring the assets that form the SoS for a mission, and managing those assets during the mission by adaptively allocating tasks as conditions unfold.

Coordinating assets during mission execution is challenging due to unpredictably evolving threats and environmental conditions, nonlinear asset interactions, and a combinatorially growing space of possible task assignments 18, 19. These factors often make mission decision problems intractable for exact methods such as optimization, and even when a tractable formulation exists, solving it at every decision point is too slow for operational timelines 20. Overcoming these limitations requires two complementary capabilities: (i) a realistic environment in which coordination policies can be developed and tested and (ii) a decision method fast enough for operational timelines. Digital Engineering (DE) offers the former. Defined as an approach that aligns data-driven infrastructure, methods, innovation, and workforce to deliver high-payoff solutions in a timely manner 21, 22, DE relies on practices such as model-based systems engineering, high-fidelity simulations, and digital twins. These practices could supply rich mission data and the infrastructure to address core ME needs via analytical tools 23, 24, and they could serve as a testbed for evaluating ME policies against realistic scenarios before the SoS is deployed. Reinforcement Learning (RL) provides the latter capability. An agent could learn a coordination policy offline by interacting with a mission simulation and, once trained, could map the current mission state to actions without solving the problem at each decision point 25.

Such learning-based coordination is gaining momentum, from hierarchical RL for SoS governance 26, 27 to RL for defense and space mission planning 28, 29. Nonetheless, most of these studies learn in abstract or simplified environments, and those that do use high-fidelity simulations do not frame their problems in ME terms. Hence, coupling RL with high-fidelity mission simulations for ME, so that coordination policies are learned through realistic mission dynamics, remains largely unexplored.

This paper addresses this gap by proposing an analytical methodology that integrates high-fidelity digital mission models with RL for intelligent mission coordination. Because a coordinator trained in one mission scenario may not perform well in another, we also examine how the scenarios used for training shape coordination performance. Specifically, we tackle three research questions:

RQ1: 

How can RL methods be synergistically integrated with high-fidelity simulation models to enable intelligent mission coordination given uncertainties, and do the resulting coordinators outperform benchmark approaches?

RQ2: 

How brittle is the performance of intelligent mission coordination agents trained on a single scenario when they face new scenarios within the same mission?

RQ3: 

How does multi-scenario training compare with scenario-specific training in terms of overall performance and robustness across scenarios?

These research questions directly address the second ME need Raz pointed out: ability to adaptively allocate tasks and reconfigure the SoS 15. Throughout, we use brittleness to denote the loss of coordination performance when a coordinator is deployed under conditions it did not encounter during training, and robustness to denote the retention of that performance. Mission resilience in the broader SE sense, the ability of the SoS to absorb, adapt to, and recover from disruptions that occur during a mission, is the longer-term objective of this research agenda; we return to the distinction in Section 5.

Building on our previous studies 30, 31, which demonstrated the feasibility of intelligent mission coordination enabled by RL for a single mission with a smaller, homogeneous fleet and a fixed ignition point, this study extends the approach to multiple mission scenarios, a larger heterogeneous fleet, and varying ignition locations. We formulate mission execution as a Markov Decision Process and then apply Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) - an RL-based algorithm 32- to train the coordinator. To illustrate the utility of the proposed approach, we use a simulation of a notional aerial firefighting mission developed by the German Aerospace Center (DLR) 33, in which a set of aerial firefighting assets is deployed to contain a wildfire within a designated region, subject to stochastic threat characteristics and environmental conditions, and their tactics are managed under the assumption of perfect, real-time information. By interacting with the simulation over thousands of episodes, the coordinator learns to map mission states to tactical actions, guided by reward signals that reflect the achievement of mission objectives. We train coordinators under two settings: single-scenario training, in which each coordinator is exposed to one mission scenario, and multi-scenario training, which aims to mitigate the brittleness of coordinators trained in isolation.

Our findings are three-fold. First, once trained, the coordinators significantly outperform two baselines: random tactics and a fixed heuristic tactic assignment, in the scenarios they were trained on. Second, single scenario training can be brittle. The coordinator trained on the more forgiving scenario falls to the level of random tactics when transferred to a more demanding one, whereas the coordinator trained on the demanding scenario retains most of its advantage when transferred, even though the fleet, its capabilities, and the available tactics are held constant and only the mission scenario changes. Third, within the same training budget, the multi-scenario coordinator performs on par with each single-scenario coordinator in that coordinator’s own scenario and significantly outperforms all other policies when evaluated across both scenarios. The computational burden is incurred offline during training; once trained, the coordinator assigns tactics from the current mission state without re-optimizing at each decision point. While this paper utilizes an aerial firefighting case study, the proposed analytical approach is mission-agnostic in terms of its structure: the digital model, simulation, Markov Decision Process (MDP) formulation, and training pipeline carry over to other operational settings, although the state, action, and reward definitions must be re-derived for each mission. Ultimately, this study documents how coupling a DE-enabled mission simulation with advanced analytical tools can advance SE and ME practice concurrently.

## 2 Literature Review

This section reviews the three bodies of work on which this research builds: SoS and Mission Engineering, analytical methods for SoS Engineering (SoSE) and ME, and learning-based methodologies for SoSE.

## 2.1 SoS and Mission Engineering

A System of Systems is a collection of systems that retain their operational and managerial independence while coming together to fulfill specific purposes 2. An SoS is further characterized by evolutionary development, geographical distribution, and significantly more complex emergent behaviors compared to constituent systems acting alone 2, 34. Although SoSE is rooted in traditional SE, the tools, methods, and processes of SE exhibit several deficiencies when applied to SoS and cannot simply be transferred 35. These deficiencies stem from the defining characteristics of SoS: independently managed constituents require federated rather than centralized management, legacy systems constrain the SoS architecture, continuous evolution prevents the architecture from being finalized, and the scale of emergent behaviors has few analogies in traditional SE 35. Therefore, SoSE shifts from optimizing monolithic systems to orchestrating the interactions of a heterogeneous set of constituent systems to achieve capabilities beyond their individual scopes 36, 37, 38, while accounting for uncertainty 39, 40, 13.

However, this orchestration does not end at design time. To realize SoS objectives in changing environments, diverse sets of assets must be selected, configured, and coordinated to serve an evolving set of missions. Although SoSE methods have matured, deployed SoS architectures remain brittle 41: they are costly to change once implemented, and their performance is susceptible to operational and contextual changes 15, 16. Architecting an SoS of heterogeneous constituent systems, whose roles and level of integration may change dynamically with mission conditions, is challenging 42. Mission Engineering (ME) is an interdisciplinary process that bridges the gap between a mission and the engineering activities that support it, spanning the analysis, design, and integration of operational needs and capabilities toward desired mission outcomes 17. Although the Department of War (DoW) definition is recent, the concept dates back at least to a 1993 NASA report that described ME as “an end-to-end, multi-mission development methodology that seeks to integrate the development processes between the space, ground, science, and operations segments of a mission” 43. In both formulations, ME ties engineering activities to the mission environment they serve, which makes it well suited to addressing the brittleness of SoS architectures across application areas.

Raz et al. 15 propose a three-stage foundational ME framework for flexibly integrating and adaptively configuring SoS across multiple, evolving missions, addressing the limitations of traditional, static SoS architectures. The framework bridges conceptual modeling and analytical evaluation, providing a mathematical basis for managing the combinatorially explosive design space of dynamic, multi-mission environments. The authors further identify three ME needs: the ability to (1) rigorously and analytically navigate the SoS design space, (2) adaptively allocate tasks and reconfigure the SoS, and (3) identify multiple SoS configurations that can realize the mission. Because deployed SoS architectures are costly to modify, much of the adaptation burden falls on how the constituent systems are coordinated, which raises two difficulties: the tension between efficiency and the preservation of each constituent system’s autonomy 27, and the brittleness of fixed coordination mechanisms and static strategies when conditions change 44. Addressing these needs and difficulties calls for analytical methods that extend SoSE and ME from design into operation, enabling the SoS to adapt as missions and conditions evolve.

## 2.2 SoSE and ME Using Analytical Methods

One line of work applies computational tools to SoS architecting. DeLaurentis et al. (14) synthesized a workbench that maps each of the “archetypal questions” recurring in SoS architecting and evolution to a formal analytical tool. Within this line of work, Uday and Marais 45 define resilience-based system importance measures, which guide the design of more resilient SoS by providing metrics for analytical optimization. Davendralingam and DeLaurentis 46 formulate constituent system selection as a financial portfolio optimization problem solved with robust optimization and, in a notional naval warfare scenario, generate a Pareto-optimal “performance efficiency frontier” that maps SoS performance across levels of accepted developmental risk. Furthermore, Guariniello et al. 47 builds on parametric extensions of functional dependency network analysis to propagate operability and schedule effects through dependency networks. Other relevant work in this area combines approximate dynamic programming with a transfer-contract coordination mechanism to plan multi-stage architecture evolution among conflicting stakeholders 48, 49. Similarly, the architecting of Earth-observing satellite programs were formulated as three coupled combinatorial optimization problems composed of instrument selection, instrument-to-spacecraft assignment, and mission scheduling 50; computing the non-additive benefit of the SoS with a rule-based expert system rather than linear programming 51. Taken together, these approaches establish a rigorous analytical basis for design-time SoS decisions, spanning capability–risk trade-offs, dependency-driven fragility, and multi-stakeholder evolution. However, they treat operations statically, leaving the operational coordination of constituent systems unaddressed.

A second line of research follows a similar series of SoS questions, from describing an SoS architecture, to selecting its constituents, to analyzing and protecting the network they form. The earliest work in this thread argues that static DoDAF-style architecture products cannot support dynamic analysis and propose generating executable models directly from architectural data through metamodel-level mapping rules 52. Ge et al., treat constituent selection as a negotiation among stakeholders with divergent preferences 53. Building on earlier work of 54, 46, Li et al. 55 formulate portfolio selection as a multi-objective optimization in which capabilities emerge from network structure. Others cast SoS scheme generation as a path-ranking problem on a task–capability–system supernetwork 56. Moving to the network those constituents form, Li et al. 57 introduce an operation-loop representation and a spectral robustness measure, and Jiang et al. 58 extend this representation to kill webs (which resemble the mission webs of 15) and derive a resilience index from the degradation and recovery of combat effectiveness under attack, concluding that decentralization and functional redundancy improve resilience. Liu et al. 59 use Q-learning to sequence protective investments against continuous disturbances. Collectively, this body of work provides representations and resilience metrics for SoS facing uncertainty (what to build, what is critical, and how to protect it). However, like the first line, it treats mission operations as fixed inputs rather than as decisions to be made as the mission unfolds; even where RL is used 59, it sequences protective investments made before an attack rather than coordinating constituent systems during the mission.

Beyond these two lines, several standalone studies apply analytical methods to SoS and ME problems. ## CONCLUSION
Traditional SoS architecting, design and evaluation methods struggle answering to changing operational conditions and remain brittle 15. To that extent ME offers a pathway to analyze the dynamic conditions in which an SoS will operate, however the application of advanced analytical tools integrated with high fidelity simulations remains rare in this domain. This study answers the call of 15 for adaptive task configuration for ME and employs RL for this purpose. To our knowledge, this is the first ME study to intelligently coordinate missions under operational uncertainties using a combination of high-fidelity digital mission models and RL. The results provide a proof of concept for a fixed set of mission assets and under the restrictive assumption of perfect information.

Efficacy of the approach.

The results confirm that the RL-based intelligent mission coordinator can learn effective firefighting tactics over the course of training. In each scenario, the coordinators trained on that scenario significantly outperformed both the random and the heuristic baselines, whose arbitrary or static tactics cannot adapt to the evolving fire. The nature of this advantage depends on the scenario. In Palisades, where most missions succeed under every policy, the coordinators outperformed the baselines mainly by reducing catastrophic failures, a form of improvement that is particularly relevant for mission-critical systems, where predictability can be as valuable as average performance. In Pyrenees, where most fires escape, the successful coordinators made a difference by containing substantially more (two to three times) fires as the baselines (see Table 3).

Single-scenario training can be brittle.

A coordinator trained on a single scenario may remain brittle when deployed to a new scenario despite outperforming baseline heuristics on its own. The coordinator trained only on Palisades performed no better than Random Tactics in Pyrenees and Salamis and significantly below even the simple heuristic baseline; despite 150,000 training episodes, its policy offered no benefit in the new environment. The coordinator trained on the more demanding Pyrenees scenario, by contrast, retained most of its advantage in Palisades and Salamis. We infer that forgiving training environments exert limited pressure to learn tactics that remain effective under harsher conditions, so the choice of training scenario is itself a design decision with consequences for deployment.

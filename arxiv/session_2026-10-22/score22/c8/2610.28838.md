## ABSTRACT
High-quality human-translated parallel corpora can improve cross-lingual transfer, but are typically too small for large-scale pretraining. Machine-translated web corpora help overcome this limitation, making parallel data available at pretraining scale. Standard multilingual pretraining, however, samples source documents and their translations independently, without explicitly using their parallel structure. We investigate how to use the parallel structure of the data to improve multilingual pretraining by concatenating each document with its translation in the same training sequence. We compare this parallel pretraining (PPT) approach with standard pretraining (SPT), training models from scratch at matched token budgets. Surprisingly, despite the benefits of parallel data reported in prior cross-lingual work, PPT alone does not consistently improve language understanding over SPT. Our further analysis suggests that although PPT builds a strong cross-lingual foundation, as shown by its better use of shared information across languages, the mismatch between parallel training contexts and single-language task inputs limits its gains in general language understanding. This finding motivates a two-stage approach following the pretraining-fine-tuning paradigm: we first use PPT to build a cross-lingual foundation, then continue with SPT to adapt it to standard language modeling. In both high-resource and low-resource settings, the two-stage approach consistently improves general language understanding and cross-lingual task performance over standard pretraining alone at matched training budgets.

## 1 Introduction

Multilingual parallel data has been widely used to improve cross-lingual language understanding (Lample and Conneau, 2019; Huang et al., 2019; Chi et al., 2021). However, high-quality human-translated corpora are typically too small for large-scale multilingual pretraining, and scaling them is costly (Team et al., 2022). Machine translation makes it possible to translate high-quality English web corpora into a wide range of languages at pretraining scale (Doshi et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2025). This reduces the resource gap by providing more high-quality training data for languages with lower-quality web data than English. Standard multilingual pretraining uses translated documents as an additional training source, treating original documents and their translations as independent examples and sampling them independently (Wang et al., 2025).

However, treating these documents independently does not make direct use of their parallel structure. Previous work has used parallel data to improve cross-lingual language modeling and to adapt pretrained models to translation and other multilingual tasks (Lample and Conneau, 2019; Lee et al., 2022; Kondo et al., 2024; Alves et al., 2024). Qorib et al. (2025) study how to incorporate sentence-level parallel data11
 1

 Their experiments use less than 5B tokens of parallel data. into predominantly non-parallel pretraining. These studies show that parallel structure can improve cross-lingual capabilities even with relatively small amounts of parallel data (Lin et al., 2025). With parallel documents available at pretraining scale (Wang et al., 2025; Penedo et al., 2026), it remains unclear how to use their parallel structure effectively for multilingual pretraining.

We investigate this question by introducing parallel pretraining (PPT), which concatenates each document with its translation in the same training sequence. We compare PPT with standard multilingual pretraining (SPT), which treats the original and translated documents as independent training examples (Figure 1(a)). To avoid uncertainty about what existing open-weight models have already learned from parallel data, we train both models from scratch. We use the same training setup and hyperparameters. The only difference is whether a document and its translation are treated as a single training sequence.

We pretrain 1.3B-parameter dense models with the Llama 2 architecture (Touvron et al., 2023) on TransWebEdu (Wang et al., 2025). Our experiments cover English–French and English–Swahili, representing high-resource and low-resource settings, respectively. We train both SPT and PPT models for approximately 200B tokens and evaluate their performance on general language understanding and translation tasks.

Surprisingly, PPT alone does not consistently improve general language understanding over SPT. Further analysis shows that PPT models benefit more from cross-lingual context than SPT models. However, general language understanding tasks typically use single-language inputs, unlike the parallel sequences used in PPT. This context mismatch may limit how much these tasks benefit from PPT’s cross-lingual capabilities.

This finding motivates a two-stage approach following the
pretraining–fine-tuning paradigm (Howard and Ruder, 2018): PPT develops cross-lingual capabilities, and subsequent SPT adapts the model to standard language modeling (Figure 1(b)). We start the second stage from intermediate pretraining checkpoints and continue with SPT for 20B tokens. We also test SPT followed by PPT. We compare both two-stage approaches with SPT-only and PPT-only training
at the same total training budget.
At a total training budget of 120B tokens, our experimental results show that PPT followed by SPT improves average general language understanding over SPT alone in both language settings. It also achieves the best translation performance among the four training strategies, with relative improvements in BLEU over SPT alone of 77% on English--French and 45% on English--Swahili.22

 BLEU increases from 19.25 to 34.03 on English–French and from 9.07 to 13.18 on English–Swahili.

Our behavioral analyses show that continuing PPT with SPT improves monolingual language modeling. The resulting model also benefits more from cross-lingual context than a model trained with SPT alone.

To summarize, our contributions are as follows:

• 

We investigate how to use parallel data at pretraining scale through
controlled comparisons of multilingual models trained from scratch.

• 

Our proposed two-stage pretraining approach, which uses PPT followed
by SPT, shows improvements in both general language understanding and
translation over standard pretraining alone at matched training budgets.

• 

Our behavioral analyses show that continued SPT improves monolingual

## 2 Pretraining with Parallel Data

## 2.1 Independent and paired-document sequences

Following Wang et al. (2025), standard pretraining (SPT) samples
source and translated documents independently. Parallel pretraining (PPT)
concatenates each document with its translation in the same training
sequence (Figure 1(a)). Each document is preceded
by its language name, a colon, and a newline, giving the prefixes
“English:\n” and “French:\n”
in English–French.
We alternate which language comes first across document
pairs, preserving sentence order within each document.

Both methods train on the same bilingual corpus with autoregressive
next-token prediction. Both languages contribute to the loss, and
attention can span preceding documents within each 2,048-token window.
Pairing documents makes corresponding cross-lingual content available
when predicting the later document, alongside its preceding text in the
same language. Alternating document order provides this context in both
directions. Appendix A
describes document matching, separators and training windows.

## 2.2 From parallel to standard pretraining

PPT provides corresponding cross-lingual content during training, whereas
general language-understanding tasks typically use single-language inputs.
We therefore continue PPT with SPT to adapt the model to independently
sampled documents, denoting this approach by PPT→SPT
(Figure 1(b)). In this second stage, the model
continues learning from both languages without systematically receiving
the translation of the document it is predicting.

We compare PPT→SPT with SPT-only and PPT-only training at matched token
budgets. We also evaluate SPT→PPT to examine how the order of paired-document
and independent-document training affects language understanding,
translation, and use of cross-lingual context. Section 3.1
gives the training schedules and language settings.

## 3 Experiments

## 3.1 Experimental setup

Data and training. We use English documents from TransWebEdu (Wang et al., 2025)
and their corresponding French or Swahili translations, with approximately
150B available tokens per language. Each model is a 1.3B-parameter
decoder-only Transformer using the TransWebLLM architecture and tokenizer
(Wang et al., 2025). Within each language pair, the SPT and PPT
models train separately from the same initial weights for approximately
200B tokens, saving checkpoints every 20B.

To compare the two training orders at different stages, we continue
English–French models from 60B, 100B and 160B tokens with the other
sequence construction for another 20B tokens. These early-, middle-
and late-stage continuations use the same sampled TransWebEdu data
and end at 80B, 120B and 180B tokens, respectively.
English–Swahili uses the middle-stage comparison, from 100B to 120B.
Both orders use a new optimizer with learning-rate re-warming
(Gupta et al., 2023; Ibrahim et al., 2024) followed by cosine decay.
We compare the resulting models with SPT and PPT at the same total
training budget; these baselines retain their original schedule.
Appendix A gives the model,
optimization and data-construction details.

Evaluation. We assess multilingual language understanding
with tasks in each language and cross-lingual generation with translation.
We evaluate language understanding with lm-evaluation-harness
(Gao et al., 2024). English–French
uses ARC-Challenge (Clark et al., 2018), HellaSwag
(Zellers et al., 2019), TruthfulQA (Lin et al., 2022a),
PAWS-X (Yang et al., 2019), XNLI (Conneau et al., 2018) and
XWinograd (Tikhonov and Ryabinin, 2021) in both languages.
English–Swahili uses XNLI, XStoryCloze (Lin et al., 2022b),
COPA/XCOPA (Gordon et al., 2012; Ponti et al., 2020)
and Global-MMLU-Lite (Singh et al., 2025).
Accuracy is averaged equally over tasks within
each language and then over the two languages.
Tasks use five sampled demonstrations, except English TruthfulQA MC1,
which uses six fixed examples without additional demonstrations.
French MC1 uses the same fixed examples plus five sampled demonstrations.
For each task, all models use the same demonstrations.

Translation uses FLORES-200 (Team et al., 2022) for both language pairs
and WMT14 (Bojar et al., 2014) for English–French, in both directions.
We compute corpus BLEU with SacreBLEU (Post, 2018),
weighting datasets, directions and two fixed prompting formats equally
within each language pair.
Appendix B specifies the prompts and task protocols. Appendix E
reports scores for each language-understanding task and translation
condition at matched training budgets.

## 4 Analysis of Prediction and Context Use

PPT alone does not consistently improve general language understanding,
whereas PPT→SPT improves both understanding and translation.
We examine these results through monolingual language modeling and
cross-lingual context use, asking how they change when SPT follows PPT.
We then test the contribution of corresponding content and examine
answer changes on single-language tasks, comparing both training orders.

## LIMITATIONS SECTION
Our experiments use 1.3B-parameter models and two language pairs,
English–French and English–Swahili. Extending the study to larger
models and more language pairs would broaden our understanding of
how the two-stage use of parallel data benefits multilingual pretraining.

## CONCLUSION
SPT and PPT have similar average language understanding throughout
pretraining, while their English–French translation ranking varies.
Following PPT with SPT improves both
understanding and translation at matched budgets in both language settings.
The prediction analyses clarify this contrast. In English–French,
PPT alone benefits more from corresponding context than SPT but has
higher monolingual prediction loss. Following PPT with SPT reduces this loss below
both baselines at every tested English–French stage. Corresponding-context
benefit decreases from the PPT starting point but remains above SPT.
The English–Swahili PPT→SPT model similarly has lower monolingual loss
and greater context benefit than SPT at 120B. Together, these results support developing
cross-lingual context use through PPT and then adapting to independently
sampled documents through SPT.

PPT→SPT retains greater use of corresponding content than SPT.
Added source sentences help predict their target-language counterparts,
and blocking attention to them raises loss.
These observations extend analyses of related-document context
(Shi et al., 2024; Zhao et al., 2024)
to another language’s expression of the same content.
On single-language tasks, greater retention of correct answers contributes
to commonsense-reasoning gains in both language pairs, and subsequent
SPT improves PPT’s concept–property judgments. These task-level gains
accompany improved monolingual prediction.

Introducing PPT after SPT also improves monolingual prediction while
increasing corresponding-context benefit. This order improves
English–French translation and gives the highest language-understanding
average at 80B and 180B. In English–Swahili, both orders reach the same
understanding average. PPT→SPT gives the highest translation average
across all tested stages and both language pairs. Its gains in both
general language understanding and translation favor this order for
multilingual pretraining across high- and low-resource settings.

## ABSTRACT
Effective decision-making in human-centric environments such as Intelligent Tutoring Systems (ITSs) and clinical healthcare requires policies that are adaptive, data-efficient, and ethically deployable. Apprenticeship Learning (AL) infers policies directly from expert demonstrations by recovering the underlying reward structure, enabling strictly offline learning without environment interaction. This dissertation develops a progression of offline AL frameworks organized around two challenges: reward modeling and demonstration quality. For reward modeling, EM-EDM clusters demonstrations by latent reward functions and induces cluster-specific policies, and THEMES partitions trajectories into temporally coherent sub-trajectories guided by a hierarchically learned reward regulator; across one educational and three clinical datasets, THEMES outperforms flat and hierarchical AL baselines. For demonstration quality, HALIDE integrates continuous quality signals into hierarchical policy induction, enabling principled reuse of mixed-quality data under distribution shift. CCHAL further attributes quality to individual decisions via Bayesian credit assignment, dual-arm criticality scoring that contrasts expert and non-expert behavior, and a Bradley-Terry contrastive IRL objective over failure trajectories, outperforming baselines including HALIDE on three clinical EHR datasets and an ITS dataset. Finally, a real-world classroom evaluation of StuEDM, an EDM-induced policy that preserves student agency and intervenes only on disagreement, shows significant learning gains over an Expert baseline and a higher pretest-adjusted posttest score than a CQL policy trained on a far larger corpus, while overriding students less than half as often. Together, this work establishes offline AL as a practical, data-efficient, and ethically viable paradigm for human-centric environments.

Abstract

Effective decision-making in human-centric environments such as Intelligent Tutoring Systems (ITSs) and clinical healthcare requires policies that are adaptive, data-efficient, and ethically deployable. Apprenticeship Learning (AL) offers a principled path: rather than engineering reward functions, AL infers policies directly from expert demonstrations by recovering the underlying reward structure, enabling strictly offline learning without environment interaction. This dissertation develops a progression of offline AL frameworks addressing increasingly realistic complexities of human demonstration data, organized around two central challenges: reward modeling and demonstration quality.

On the reward modeling front, we first relax the standard assumption that all demonstrations share a single, homogeneous reward function. We apply EM-EDM, an Expectation-Maximization framework built upon Energy-based Distribution Matching (EDM), which simultaneously clusters demonstrations by their latent reward functions and induces cluster-specific policies over large continuous state spaces. We show that EM-EDM outperforms AL baselines and two DRL induced policies on student pedagogical decision prediction, demonstrating that heterogeneous reward modeling is both feasible and effective from a small number of expert demonstrations. We then address the further assumption that reward functions remain fixed within a trajectory. We leverage THEMES (Time-aware Hierarchical EM Energy-based Subtrajectory AL), which partitions trajectories into temporally coherent sub-trajectories and induces policies over each partition guided by a hierarchically learned reward regulator. Validated across one educational and three real-world clinical healthcare datasets, THEMES consistently outperforms all flat and hierarchical AL baselines, establishing that modeling temporally evolving reward functions substantially improves policy induction in human-centric domains.

On the demonstration quality front, we first challenge the assumption that demonstrations are uniformly optimal. We introduce HALIDE (Hierarchical Apprenticeship Learning from Imperfect Demonstrations with Evolving Rewards), which integrates continuous demonstration quality signals directly into the hierarchical policy induction process. HALIDE establishes that incorporating suboptimal trajectories without quality-aware ranking degrades performance, whereas ranked hierarchical learning enables principled reuse of mixed-quality data, yielding consistent gains under cross-semester distribution shift. We then go further and jointly attribute demonstration quality to individual decisions via CCHAL (Critical Contrast Hierarchical Apprenticeship Learning). Rather than weighting entire trajectories uniformly, CCHAL distributes trajectory-level demonstration ranks to per-decision weights via Bayesian credit assignment, separately scores each decision’s criticality by contrasting expert against non-expert behavior in a dual-arm design, and sharpens reward learning with a Bradley-Terry pairwise contrastive IRL objective over observed failure trajectories. Evaluated across three clinical EHR datasets and a student ITS dataset, CCHAL outperforms flat and hierarchical AL baselines, including its direct predecessor HALIDE, establishing that decision-level outcome attribution and behavioral criticality are both necessary to fully exploit demonstration quality signals.

Finally, the dissertation is grounded by a real-world classroom evaluation of StuEDM, an EDM-induced policy that lets students make their own decisions about how to engage with each problem and intervenes only when it disagrees with the student’s choice, trained on a substantially smaller and shorter-span demonstration corpus than a comparable Conservative-Q-Learning-induced policy. StuEDM significantly improves posttest performance and normalized learning gain over a pretest-equivalent Expert baseline that assigns among the same domain-expert-designed presentation modes with no adaptive guidance, and significantly improves the pretest-adjusted posttest score over the CQL-induced policy trained on a much larger historical corpus, while overriding students’ own decisions less than half as often; together these results provide empirical evidence that offline AL from a substantially smaller, agency-preserving demonstration corpus can rival reward-optimizing DRL trained on far larger corpora in live educational deployments.

Taken together, this dissertation establishes offline AL as a practical, data-efficient, and ethically viable paradigm for human-centric environments, tracing a principled path from reward modeling to quality-aware demonstration learning, and from retrospective evaluation to real-world classroom deployment.

## Chapter 1 Introduction

## 1.1 Overview

Many human-centric environments—such as education, healthcare, and personalized support systems—can be formulated as sequential decision-making problems, where an intelligent agent must determine what action to take and when to take it over time. These settings are naturally modeled using Reinforcement Learning (RL), which optimizes long-term outcomes through trial-and-error interaction with an environment (Sutton and Barto 2018). In an Intelligent Tutoring System (ITS), for example, a pedagogical agent must decide whether to present a student with a worked example, prompt independent problem solving, or engage in collaborative construction—adapting to each student’s evolving knowledge state across dozens of steps. In clinical settings, a treatment agent must select interventions for a patient whose condition changes over time. Despite the considerable success of RL and Deep RL (DRL) in simulated domains, their adoption in real-world, human-facing applications remains limited by two longstanding challenges.

The first challenge is sample inefficiency. Classic DRL algorithms such as Deep Q-Networks (DQN) require millions of environment interactions to learn stable policies and generalize to unseen states. Yet interactions with humans—patients, students, users—are scarce, costly, and ethically constrained. Many prior studies on RL in educational settings attempt to derive pedagogical policies from fewer than 3,000 student–ITS interaction trajectories, and the resulting policies can prove ineffective (Shen and Chi 2016). The second challenge is reward function design. Designing an accurate reward function is a prerequisite for RL, yet it is notoriously difficult in human-centric domains where desired behaviors are complex, latent, and context-dependent (Kaelbling et al. 1996). Reward functions are typically hand-crafted, dependent on domain expertise, and prone to blind spots (Abbeel and Ng 2004; Goyal et al. 2019). Worse, misspecified rewards can produce policies that are inconsistent with intended goals (Amodei et al. 2016). Moreover, in sensitive human-centric domains—classrooms, clinics—live policy experimentation raises ethical concerns, making it infeasible to collect the exploratory data that RL methods require.

## 1.1.1 Apprenticeship Learning as an Alternative

Apprenticeship Learning (AL) offers a principled alternative by inferring the underlying reward function directly from expert demonstrations, enabling policy induction without explicit reward specification (Abbeel and Ng 2004). By assuming expert agents make optimal or near-optimal decisions, AL can efficiently approximate expert behavior from a limited number of demonstrated trajectories (Abbeel and Ng 2004; Ziebart et al. 2008). Classical AL approaches, however, are predominantly online: they require iterative interaction with the environment to collect new data and update the model (Ziebart et al. 2008; Ho and Ermon 2016b; Finn et al. 2016). This precludes their use in fully offline settings. Strictly offline AL methods—those that learn solely from pre-collected logs without any environment interaction—are therefore essential in human-centric domains. A recent advance, Energy-Based Distribution Matching (EDM) (Jarrett et al. 2020a), achieves strong performance in offline AL by matching state-action occupancy distributions between expert demonstrations and a learned policy using energy-based models, without requiring knowledge of model transitions or off-policy evaluations.

Despite EDM’s success, it—like most offline AL methods—rests on a sequence of idealized assumptions that are routinely violated in practice. This dissertation progressively relaxes these assumptions, developing a series of frameworks that address the realistic complexities of human demonstration data across educational and clinical settings.

## 1.1.2 Reward Modeling: Heterogeneous and Evolving Reward Functions

A foundational assumption of most offline AL methods is that all demonstrations share a single, homogeneous reward function that remains fixed throughout each trajectory. Both parts of this assumption are routinely violated in human-centric settings, and this dissertation addresses them in turn.

The first violation is cross-trajectory heterogeneity: different demonstrators pursue fundamentally different objectives. In an ITS, some students are learning-oriented while others prioritize efficiency; their pedagogical decisions reflect different underlying reward structures. Prior work addressing this, such as EM-IRL (Yang et al. 2020), is limited to small discrete state spaces (17 features). The first study of this dissertation adapts EM-EDM—an Expectation-Maximization framework built upon EDM—which simultaneously clusters student trajectories by their latent reward functions and induces cluster-specific policies, scaling to continuous state spaces with over 140 features. Even with as few as 24 expert demonstrations, EM-EDM outperforms four state-of-the-art AL baselines and two DRL baselines on predicting student pedagogical decisions.

The second violation is intra-trajectory evolution: even a single demonstrator’s goals shift across phases of a task. A student who begins a tutoring session cautiously requesting help may shift toward confident independent problem-solving as the session progresses; a clinician’s treatment priorities evolve as a patient’s condition changes. Such temporal dynamics cannot be captured by any static reward function. The second study leverages THEMES (Yang et al. 2025a)—a Time-aware Hierarchical EM Energy-based Subtrajectory AL framework—which automatically partitions expert trajectories into temporally coherent sub-trajectories, applies EM-EDM over each partition, and refines the segmentation through a hierarchically learned reward regulator. Applied across one educational and three clinical healthcare datasets, THEMES consistently outperforms all flat and hierarchical AL baselines, establishing that capturing temporally evolving reward functions substantially improves policy induction in human-centric domains.

## 1.1.3 Demonstration Quality: Imperfect and Negative Demonstrations

A second foundational assumption of AL is that all provided demonstrations are at minimum acceptable in quality—that imperfect trajectories should be discarded and failed ones carry no learnable signal. This dissertation challenges both parts of this assumption.

The first challenge concerns imperfect demonstrations: in practice, students explore, make errors, and revise strategies, producing trajectories of varying quality. Discarding all suboptimal data wastes the majority of available demonstrations; yet treating all as equally informative introduces noise that corrupts learned policies. The key insight is that imperfect demonstrations constitute structured signals—provided their relative quality is explicitly ranked. The third study introduces HALIDE (Hierarchical Apprenticeship Learning from Imperfect Demonstrations with Evolving Rewards), which integrates continuous quality signals—derived from Normalized Learning Gain—directly into the hierarchical policy induction process. HALIDE establishes a critical finding: incorporating suboptimal data without quality-aware ranking degrades performance, whereas ranked hierarchical learning enables principled reuse of mixed-quality trajectories and yields consistent gains in cross-semester generalization. Crucially, ranking in AL serves a fundamentally different role from reward functions in RL: rather than defining what is optimal, it modulates how much influence each demonstrated behavior exerts during learning.

The second challenge concerns decision-level importance: existing quality-weighting methods, including HALIDE, apply a single trajectory-level rank uniformly across every decision, even though most decisions occur at routine states while a minority occur at critical junctures where the choice made is pivotal to the outcome—and failed trajectories are typically discarded entirely, even though they encode informative contrast about which decisions distinguish success from failure. The fourth study introduces CCHAL (Critical Contrast Hierarchical Apprenticeship Learning), which jointly attributes trajectory-level demonstration quality to individual decisions via Bayesian credit assignment in feature-action space, separately scores each decision’s criticality by contrasting expert against non-expert behavior within the same initial risk stratum, and combines both signals to sharpen a Bradley-Terry pairwise contrastive IRL objective over observed failure trajectories. Evaluated across three clinical EHR datasets and a student ITS dataset, CCHAL outperforms flat and hierarchical AL baselines, including its direct predecessor HALIDE, establishing that decision-level outcome attribution and behavioral criticality are both necessary to fully exploit demonstration quality signals.

## 1.1.4 Real-World Classroom Validation

The progression from EM-EDM through THEMES, HALIDE, and CCHAL addresses increasingly realistic data conditions in retrospective evaluation. The fifth study closes the loop with a real-world classroom deployment. StuEDM is a pedagogical policy in which students make their own decisions about how to engage with each training problem—choosing to solve it independently, study a worked example, or collaborate with the tutor—and an EDM-derived policy, trained on only a limited set of expert demonstrations, intervenes only when it disagrees with the student’s decision. In a controlled classroom study with 104 undergraduate students, StuEDM—trained on 244 students’ own pedagogical decisions across five semesters—is compared against StuCQL, an analogous policy whose override logic is provided by Conservative Q-Learning trained on a much larger, longer-span historical interaction corpus (2,716 students, 13 semesters), and against a pretest-equivalent Expert baseline that assigns among the same domain-expert-designed presentation modes with no adaptive guidance. StuEDM significantly improves posttest performance and normalized learning gain over the Expert baseline, and significantly improves the pretest-adjusted posttest score over StuCQL; a decision-level analysis further shows StuEDM overrides students’ own decisions less than half as often as StuCQL. StuEDM students also engaged with the tutor significantly longer than StuCQL students, a further engagement-related outcome favoring StuEDM, and the underlying training-corpus efficiency remains a genuine advantage, providing direct empirical evidence that offline AL from a substantially smaller, agency-driven demonstration corpus can rival reward-optimizing DRL trained on far larger datasets in real educational deployments.

## CONCLUSION
This work addresses a key limitation of AL in human-centric decision-making settings: demonstrations are inherently heterogeneous in quality, even among high-performing demonstrators, whether the decision-maker is a student choosing a pedagogical action or a clinician choosing a treatment. Students differ in prior knowledge, self-regulation, and metacognitive skills, and their decision quality fluctuates over time; clinicians likewise vary in experience and judgment under uncertainty, and their treatment choices vary in effectiveness even within the same case. Both settings violate the standard AL assumption that expert demonstrations are uniformly reliable. Our results show that treating all demonstrations equally injects noise into policy induction, limiting robustness—especially under cross-semester distribution shift in education and across clinical sites in healthcare.

HALIDE operationalizes a principle long established in mixed-initiative ITS research—student agency must be balanced with selective modulation of influence—and shows that the same principle holds for clinician-driven treatment decisions. Rather than discarding imperfect trajectories or uniformly weighting demonstrations, HALIDE integrates continuous demonstration quality signals within a hierarchical apprenticeship learning framework. This allows useful routine decisions from sub-optimal trajectories to contribute while attenuating the influence of low-impact or erroneous actions. Empirically, this integration is critical in both domains: unweighted hierarchical learning fails to benefit from additional heterogeneous data, whereas HALIDE consistently improves performance, with the largest gains observed when leveraging both expert and sub-optimal demonstrations—on ITS data (E+I) and, in the clinical setting, on the largest and most heterogeneous dataset (MIMIC, all-quality data). These results highlight the complementary roles of ranking and hierarchy, where quality-aware weighting stabilizes low-level policy induction and hierarchical structure propagates these improvements to higher-level decision-making strategies, regardless of whether those strategies govern a tutoring session or a course of treatment.

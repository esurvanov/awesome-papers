Abstract

Continuous glucose monitoring supports diabetes management but requires forecasting models that are accurate, interpretable, and efficient enough for real-time deployment. We present SSMCGM-Stream, a streaming state-space model that maintains a personalized hidden state updated with each incoming glucose reading to provide constant-time forecasting, physiologically structured counterfactual reasoning, and interpretable forecasts through hidden-state attribution. Built on this foundation, SSMCGM-Agent delivers risk-aware, natural-language decision support by translating forecasts, explanations, and counterfactuals into grounded recommendations through a guarded language-model interface. Evaluated on the T1DEXI cohort, SSMCGM-Stream achieves forecasting performance comparable to strong neural baselines while substantially reducing computational cost, enabling continuous on-device diabetes decision support.

## 1 Introduction

Managing type 1 diabetes involves a constant stream of decisions about meals, insulin, and physical activity [1]. Continuous glucose monitoring (CGM) provides real-time glucose measurements that support these decisions [2]. Despite advances in sensor technology and algorithmic control
[3, 4, 5], current systems provide limited insight into the factors driving an individual’s glucose dynamics.

Currently, glucose forecasting models can anticipate hypo- and hyperglycemic events 30-60 minutes ahead [6, 7]. These forecasts support proactive glucose management, such as maintaining time-in-range [8], lowering an individual’s risk for diabetic complications [9]. However, these models are typically optimized for predictive performance without revealing whether they learn physiologically meaningful insulin and meal effects on glucose. Moreover, emerging transformer-based glucose foundation models [10] introduce computational challenges for continuous deployment such as attention scaling quadratically with sequence length [11]. This is problematic when type 1 diabetes decision support requires efficient inference and training over continuously arriving data. Instead, state-space models (SSMs) can maintain a compact recurrent state with constant-time updates [12, 13], making them useful for streaming and on-device inference.

We present SSMCGM-Stream, a streaming state-space forecasting model for personalized type 1 diabetes decision support using the T1DEXI cohort [14].
SSMCGM-Stream maintains a constant-memory recurrent state paired with a scenario-conditioned horizon decoder. From a single running state, SSMCGM-Stream (i) forecasts impending hypo- and hyperglycemia events; (ii) attributes forecasts to individual-specific drivers; (iii) answers counterfactual ‘what-if’ questions about meals, exercise, and insulin; (iv) cold-starts from population priors while adapting to individuals over time; and lastly (v) integrates large-language models (LLM) as an advisory agent. Together, SSMCGM-Stream provides a framework for interpretable, personalized, real-time glucose decision support.

## 2 Methods

## 2.1 Dataset

Dataset and Preprocessing. We used 24-30 days of 5-minute multimodal data (3,915,944 observations) from $497$ participants with type 1 diabetes in T1DEXI [14], including CGM, insulin, meals, exercise, heart rate, steps, and static demographic characteristics (Table S1). All modalities were resampled onto a common 5-minute grid. For the CGM input, short gaps were imputed by linear interpolation ( $\leq$ 15 min) and forward-fill ( $\leq$ 30 min). Forecast windows spanning longer gaps were excluded, cutting the stream into valid segments. We split participants 70/15/15 into $338$ train, $73$ validation, and $73$ test participants. For fair, leakage-free benchmarking, the decoder observes only calendar time and the planned actions (e.g., meal, insulin, and exercise events) are masked at inference time.

## 2.2 SSMCGM-Stream Architecture

Figure 1: SSMCGM-Stream Architecture. Static and time-varying covariates update a streaming hidden state, $\mathbf{h}_{t}$ , while scenario conditioning enables forecasts under planned future actions.

Problem setup. SSMCGM-Stream enables multiple tasks from a single running state (Fig. 1). At each 5-minute step $t$ , SSMCGM-Stream forecasts glucose over horizon $H$ , $y_{t+1:t+H}$ , from the observed history of glucose and covariates, $\mathbf{x}_{1:t}$ , static features, $S$ , known-future calendar features, $T_{t+1:t+H}$ , and planned-scenario (action) paths, $A_{t+1:t+H}$ , indicated by an availability mask, $M$ :

 $\hat{y}_{t+1:t+H}=F_{\theta}\!\big(\mathbf{x}_{1:t},\,S,\,T_{t+1:t+H},\,A_{t+1:t+H},\,M\big).$ 

(1)

Participant state and conditioning. A static encoder maps $S$ to a participant embedding
 $\mathbf{e}_{s}=g_{\phi}(S)$ , which (i) initializes the state $\mathbf{s}_{0}=\psi(\mathbf{e}_{s})$ and (ii) modulates the history
features through a FiLM layer [15]. Per-variable features are merged by a grouped
linear fusion $\mathbf{z}_{t}$ , then conditioned: $\mathbf{u}_{t}=\gamma(\mathbf{e}_{s})\odot\mathbf{z}_{t}+\beta(\mathbf{e}_{s})$ .

Selective state-space recurrence. The history is encoded by a stack of selective
state-space Mamba-2 blocks [12, 16, 13]. Per channel, with an
input-dependent step size $\Delta_{t}=\mathrm{softplus}(w^{\top}\mathbf{u}_{t}+b)$ and selective parameters
 $B_{t},C_{t}$ , the discrete recurrence is

 $\mathbf{s}_{t}=\bar{A}_{t}\,\mathbf{s}_{t-1}+\bar{B}_{t}\,\mathbf{u}_{t},\qquad h_{t}=C_{t}^{\top}\mathbf{s}_{t}+D\,\mathbf{u}_{t},\qquad\bar{A}_{t}=\exp(\Delta_{t}A),\;\ \bar{B}_{t}=\Delta_{t}B_{t}.$ 

(2)

Here, $\mathbf{s}_{t}$ is the recurrent state of the SSM stack and $h_{t}$ is its readout. At deployment, the recurrent state $\mathbf{s}_{t}$ persists between observations, while $h_{t}$ is the streaming representation for forecasting and scenario decoding.
We use the interpretable multi-expand SSM (MES) Mamba-2 readout introduced for CGM forecasting [17], which attributes a forecast to a feature and a lag.

Scenario horizon decoder. A scenario horizon decoder initializes from $\mathbf{h}_{t}$ and rolls the SSM recurrence (Eq. (2)) over the forecast horizon, conditioned on calendar features $T$ , the gated scenario $A\odot M$ , and the availability mask $M$ :

 $\hat{y}_{t+1:t+H}=f_{\theta}\big(\mathbf{h}_{t},\ \mathbf{e}_{s},\ T,\ A\odot M,\ M\big).$ 

(3)

We decompose forecasts into a scenario-free baseline and an additive scenario effect:

 $\hat{y}=\underbrace{f^{\text{base}}_{\theta}(\mathbf{h}_{t},\mathbf{e}_{s},T)}_{\text{no scenario }(M{=}0)}\;+\;\Delta\hat{y}_{\text{sce}},\qquad\Delta\hat{y}_{\text{sce}}=f^{\text{eff}}_{\theta}(\mathbf{h}_{t},\mathbf{e}_{s},T,A\odot M,M).$ 

(4)

This isolates the effect of planned actions while holding the observed history encoded in $\mathbf{h}_{t}$ fixed.

Physiologic kinetic channel. Planned actions are convolved with learned physiological kernels before entering the decoder. For action $a$ , the kernel $\kappa_{a}(t)$ peaks at learned delay $\tau_{a}$ , allowing insulin and macronutrient effects to exhibit distinct temporal dynamics.

 $\kappa_{a}(t)\ =\ \frac{t}{\tau_{a}}\,\exp\!\Big(1-\frac{t}{\tau_{a}}\Big),\qquad t\geq 0,$ 

(5)

The decoder sees $g_{a}(\mathbf{e}_{s})\,(\kappa_{a}*a)$ : the kernel sets an action’s shape and timing, while the learned gain $g_{a}(\mathbf{e}_{s})=\mathrm{softplus}(\gamma_{a})\exp[(W_{g}\mathbf{e}_{s}+b_{g})_{a}]$ sets its amplitude and personalizes it through $\mathbf{e}_{s}$ . Both are shared between encoder and decoder.

## 2.3 SSMCGM-Stream Training and Benchmarking

Figure 2: Online/streaming formulation.
The recurrent state is updated per observation and forecasts are emitted from the streaming representation $\mathbf{h}_{t}$ at anchor points. Truncated BPTT is utilized over defined chunks to update gradients.

Training. We train SSMCGM-Stream over participant streams using truncated backpropagation through time (BPTT) [18]. The streaming hidden state is carried across chunks and detached at chunk boundaries, while forecasts are scored at anchors with fully observed horizons (Fig. 2, Alg. 1). Deployment follows the same state-update recurrence without gradient computations, updating the hidden state once per 5-minute observation. The full model configuration is given in Table S2.

Loss function.
The decoder predicts quantiles ( $\tau\in\{0.1,0.5,0.9\}$ ) at each horizon using the pinball loss,

 $\mathcal{L}=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{A}|}\sum_{t\in\mathcal{A}}\sum_{h=1}^{H}\sum_{\tau}\rho_{\tau}\!\big(y_{t+h}-\hat{y}_{\tau,t+h}\big).$ 

(6)

The median ( $\tau{=}0.5$ ) provides the point forecast, while the $q_{0.1}$ / $q_{0.9}$ tails support risk-sensitive hypo- and hyperglycemia detection.

Benchmarking and clinical metrics.
We compared SSMCGM-Stream with persistence, ARIMA, LightGBM [19], LSTM [20], NHiTS [21], and TFT [22] under the same leakage-free forecast-only protocol and equivalent available inputs. Forecast accuracy was evaluated using MAE and RMSE at 1- and 3-hour horizons. Clinical evaluation included hypo- and hyperglycemia detection and Clarke [23] and Parkes [24] error grids. Hypoglycemia is defined as glucose $<$ 70 mg/dL and hyperglycemia as $>$ 180 mg/dL.

## 2.4 SSMCGM-Stream Inference

Interpretability. The multi-expand-state (MES) readout attributes each forecast to additive per-feature, per-lag contributions of the hidden state:

 $h_{t}=\sum_{k}\underbrace{C_{t}^{(k)\top}\mathbf{s}_{t}^{(k)}}_{\phi_{k}},\qquad\mathbf{s}_{t}^{(k)}=\sum_{\ell\geq 0}\bar{A}_{t}^{\,\ell}\,\bar{B}\,u^{(k)}_{t-\ell}.$ 

(7)

A prediction traces to each figure k (glucose, insulin, carbohydrate, exercise, etc.) through its contribution $\phi_{k}$ , and unrolling the state exposes at which delay l it acted. Additionally, learned kinetic kernels (Equation 5) characterize the temporal effects of planned actions, while static conditioning captures participant-specific effects.

Counterfactuals.
The scenario decoder answers counterfactual (“what-if”) queries by re-decoding from the same streaming representation $\mathbf{h}_{t}$ under a modified future plan. The decoder receives the planned-action sequence $A$ and a reveal mask $M$ , specifying if the future action is set or unset. To evaluate setting action $a$ to a counterfactual value $a^{\prime}$ , we compare two decodes:

 $\Delta\hat{y}(a\!\to\!a^{\prime})=f_{\theta}(\mathbf{h}_{t},\mathbf{e}_{s},T,A^{\prime}\!\odot M^{\prime},M^{\prime})-f_{\theta}(\mathbf{h}_{t},\mathbf{e}_{s},T,A\odot M,M).$ 

(8)

Practically, we compute counterfactuals where the baseline reveals no actions ( $M$ masks all, a do-nothing forecast) and the edited decode reveals only $a^{\prime}$ . Both share $\mathbf{h}_{t}$ , so history is held fixed, and only the plan changes. We restrict counterfactual queries to in-support configurations of meal intake, insulin dosing, and exercise intensity.

Personalization.
Personalization is carried by the online state, where the learned initializer, $\mathbf{h}_{0}$ , cold-starts an unseen participant from population priors (i.e., static features), and the recurrence updates the state, $\mathbf{h}_{t}$ , as observations arrive. Forecast targets are residualized against the observed anchor $a_{t}$ , $r_{h}=(y_{t+h}-a_{t})/s_{h}$ with per-horizon scales fit on training participants only and $\hat{y}_{t+h}=a_{t}+s_{h}\hat{r}_{h}$ , so no per-participant center is learned and the transformation holds at cold start.

## 2.5 SSMCGM-Agent Integration

Figure 3: SSMCGM-Agent architecture. A large language model interfaces with SSMCGM-Stream
to provide on-device recommendations.

On-device decision-support agent. We interface SSMCGM-Stream with an on-device instruction-tuned language model (Qwen2.5-Instruct [25]) for natural-language decision support (Fig. 3). SSMCGM-Agent maps user queries to structured SSMCGM-Stream tools for forecasting, explanation, simulation, and recommendation, and narrates their returned outputs (Fig. 3). Physiological reasoning therefore remains grounded in SSMCGM-Stream rather than the language model. A perception boundary maps free text to typed model actions through deterministic resolvers over external databases (USDA FoodData Central [26]) and an empirical activity-curve library built from T1DEXI participants. An expression boundary narrates structured outputs and a deterministic guard rejects unsupported numerical or treatment claims (Fig. S6). We stress test the guard across Qwen2.5 sizes (0.5–7 B) with (i) a number-tracking test that perturbs the forecast minimum and checks whether the narration reports the edited value, and (ii) an adversarial red-team [27, 28] of fabrication, unsafe-action, and prompt-injection prompts, generated by Claude Opus 4.8 (Anthropic) [29] and human-reviewed (Table S8).

Accuracy. At the 3-hour horizon, SSMCGM-Stream achieved an MAE of 27.05 mg/dL, comparable to TFT (26.90) and LightGBM (27.22), while outperforming ARIMA, LSTM, and NHiTS (Table 1). Importantly, SSMCGM-Stream trained more efficiently, 27.4 s/epoch compared with 2594.5 s/epoch for TFT (Fig. 4). Similar accuracy trends were observed at the 1-hour horizon (Table S3).

Efficiency. SSMCGM-Stream maintained constant per-reading computation and memory as historical context increased, with 6.7 ms inference latency and 1.7 MB per-forecast memory. In contrast, TFT latency grew with context (10–48 $\times$ slower over 24–192 h) and its inference memory reached 112 MB at 192 h, approximately $66\times$ greater (Fig. 4). Together with its 0.85 M parameters, these results support efficient streaming deployment.

Personalization. Forecast error decreases as the streaming representation accumulates participant history after cold start, $\mathbf{h}_{0}$ , plateauing after approximately the first day (Fig. 4).

## 3.2 Clinical safety and early detection

Clinical Safety. Forecast accuracy alone does not capture clinically consequential errors; for example, predicting hyperglycemia when an individual becomes hypoglycemic may lead to inappropriate actions. We therefore evaluated forecasts using the Clarke and Parkes error grids [23, 24, 30], which classify prediction errors by clinical risk. At the 30-minute horizon, 98.2% (Clarke) and 99.3% (Parkes) of SSMCGM-Stream forecasts fell within clinically acceptable A/B zones, comparable to TFT (Tables 2, S4, S1 and S5).

Early Detection. Early detection of hypoglycemia ( $<70$ mg/dL) and hyperglycemia ( $>180$ mg/dL) can provide individuals time to respond before an adverse glycemic excursion. Using risk-sensitive quantile forecasts, SSMCGM-Stream detected hypoglycemia and hyperglycemia at 30 minutes with AUROC of 0.891 and 0.965, respectively, and hypoglycemia sensitivity of 0.735 at 95% specificity. Across the full 3-hour forecast, hypoglycemic events were anticipated with a median lead time of 25 min (Tables 2 and S6).

Detection and error-grid safety therefore match TFT to within overlapping 95% confidence intervals
(hypoglycemia AUROC 0.891 vs. 0.910; Clarke A+B 98.2% vs. 98.1%), from a state updated once per
reading rather than a re-encoded history.

## 3.3 Interpretability

Neural forecasters are difficult to inspect, which limits clinical trust, while statistical models are
inspectable but less expressive for nonlinear, individualized physiology. SSMCGM-Stream instead reads
its explanations directly off the streaming state, globally and for an individual forecast.

(b) In-model kinetic PK/PD kernels for a unit dose.
(c) Encoder feature importance: share of the streaming state $h_{t}$ ’s hidden attention by modality group.

Global interpretability. MES attribution showed that the streaming state emphasized recent observations while individual heads retained heterogeneous effective memories spanning approximately 10–95 min (Fig. 5a). Learned kinetic kernels recovered distinct temporal profiles, peaking near 30 min for carbohydrates, 65 min for insulin, 115 min for fat, and 145 min for protein (Fig. 5b), with kernel gains varying across individuals (Fig. S2b). Time-of-day, glucose, insulin, carbohydrates, and physical activity contributed strongly to the streaming state, while insulin and carbohydrates produced the largest scenario effects in the decoder (Fig. 5c–d). Static features contributed to forecasts principally through socioeconomic status and insulin-delivery modality (income 10.3%, insulin-delivery modality 10.2%, and lifetime hypoglycemia history 8.5% of the static influence on the initialized state $\mathbf{h}_{0}$ ), while demographic covariates such as race, ethnicity, and sex contributed the least (Fig. S3).

## Individual interpretability.

Global importance describes which features the state uses on average; it does not explain what drives
one participant’s forecast, which is what a person acting on an alert needs.
At the individual level, forecast attribution identified recent insulin and exercise as drivers of hypoglycemic predictions (Fig. 6), and meal intake as a driver of hyperglycemic predictions (Fig. S4). These examples demonstrate how the streaming state can provide forecast-specific explanations.

## 3.4 Counterfactual decision support

We evaluated whether SSMCGM-Stream could generate physiologically plausible responses to counterfactual (“what-if”) changes in planned actions. Each scenario is decoded from the same streaming state $\mathbf{h}_{t}$ , holding an individual’s observed history fixed while varying future actions (Section 2.4). Causal interpretation of these counterfactual simulations requires consistency, exchangeability, and positivity [31].

Figure 7: Single-action counterfactuals. Averaged population counterfactual responses on held-out participants with 95% confidence intervals. Counterfactuals vary (a) carbohydrate intake, (b) exercise intensity, (c) insulin dose, and (d) pre-bolus timing.

Single-action counterfactuals.
Increasing carbohydrate intake produced larger glycemic excursions, while increasing exercise intensity (a 45-min bout with elevated heart rate) produced greater glucose lowering (Fig. 7a–b). Insulin produced a biphasic dose-dependent response, with an initial positive response followed by greater glucose lowering at higher doses (Fig. 7c). This response should not be interpreted causally as isolated correction boluses occurred in only about one third of cases, limiting identification of insulin’s independent effect. We therefore examined insulin timing relative to a meal (a 40 g carbohydrate meal followed by a 3 U bolus). Pre-bolusing reduced the predicted glycemic excursion compared with insulin delivered with or after the meal (Fig. 7d). Learned kinetic channels (Fig. 5b) further produced distinct temporal profiles, with carbohydrates producing an earlier rise and insulin a delayed decline.

Figure 8: Meal and exercise composition counterfactuals.
(a) Counterfactual glucose response to varying fat or protein at fixed carbohydrate intake. (b) Heart-rate and step-count profiles across exercise types. (c) Counterfactual glucose response across exercise profiles.

Meal and exercise composition.
We next evaluated richer meal and exercise compositions. T1DEXI meals spanned a broad macronutrient distribution relative to the Acceptable Macronutrient Distribution Range (AMDR) [32], so the compositions edited below lie within the observed support (Fig. S5). At fixed carbohydrate intake, increasing fat or protein produced more prolonged glycemic excursions (Fig. 8a). Exercise profiles derived from recorded heart-rate and step-count trajectories similarly produced intensity-dependent glucose responses: running (peak HR ${\approx}145$ ) produced approximately $-10$ mg/dL glucose lowering, compared with approximately $0$ mg/dL for lighter activities such as yoga (peak HR ${\approx}96$ ; Fig. 8b–c).

## 3.5 SSMCGM-Agent: natural-language access to the streaming state

Use cases. Because forecasting, attribution, and counterfactual simulation are queries of the same running state, they can be exposed through a single interface. On held-out participant streams, SSMCGM-Agent supported three interactions (Fig. 9): proactive alerts for forecasted hypoglycemia, individualized explanations of forecast drivers such as recent insulin and exercise, and planning queries translated into counterfactual meal and activity scenarios. In short, physiological reasoning stays in SSMCGM-Stream while the language model routes and narrates.

Grounding. Every narration passes a deterministic guard that rejects any number absent from the model’s tool output and any insulin dose or unendorsed action, so delivered output is faithful by construction at every model size. We therefore report the cost of that guarantee. Across 58 prompts (46 adversarial, 12 controls; Table S8), unguarded narration fabricated unsupported numbers in 14–16% of number-tracking tests at sizes ${\leq}1.5$  B, and a 0.5 B model reported the specific value in only 56% of cases versus 80% at ${\geq}1.5$  B. Over-rejection of legitimate control queries fell from 25% at 0.5 B to ${\leq}8\%$ at ${\geq}1.5$  B (Fig. S7). With 3.6–11.6 s per-turn latency and 0.2–3.8 GB int4 weight memory (Table S7), a small on-device model suffices as the interface when physiological reasoning remains in the streaming model.

## 4 Discussion

SSMCGM-Stream reformulates continuous glucose modeling around an evolving physiological representation that supports multiple decision-support tasks from a single streaming hidden state. Rather than repeatedly encoding historical context, SSMCGM-Stream updates a persistent hidden state from incoming multimodal observations with constant memory and per-reading computation. This formulation enables private, on-device inference [33, 34, 35].

This perspective complements recent CGM foundation models that learn population-level representations for prediction, transfer learning, and risk stratification [10]. Large-scale models may provide strong population priors, while streaming representations continuously adapt to the individual. Under this formulation, forecasting, explanation, simulation, and personalization become queries of a shared evolving representation rather than separate learning problems.

Diabetes decision support requires both accurate forecasting and explanations of the factors driving future glycemic excursions. SSMCGM-Stream addresses these needs through learned temporal response kernels, forecast attribution, and counterfactual simulation. Additionally, we integrate an on-device large language model, SSMCGM-Agent, that serves as an interface between users and SSMCGM-Stream. Existing work has largely focused on the reasoning ability of large language models applied to CGM data [36, 37]. In contrast, we separate physiological modeling from communication. SSMCGM-Stream generates structured forecasts, explanations, and counterfactual simulations, while the language model translates between natural language and structured model inputs and outputs under deterministic safety constraints.

Several limitations should be considered. SSMCGM-Stream was trained on observational data from T1DEXI [14], limiting causal interpretation of counterfactual simulations because recorded actions were observed rather than assigned under structured interventions. Important sources of confounding include the temporal coupling of meals and insulin boluses, which makes their individual effects difficult to disentangle, and the dependence of actions on glucose state. Conditioning on SSMCGM-Stream’s evolving representation of longitudinal context may reduce this confounding, but cannot guarantee that all factors influencing both actions and subsequent glucose trajectories are captured. Evaluation was also limited to a single cohort with restricted demographic diversity, so generalization to other populations, devices, and care settings remains untested. Prospective studies are needed to determine whether personalized forecasts and counterfactual simulations from SSMCGM-Stream can support lifestyle decisions around meals, physical activity, and other modifiable behaviors, and ultimately improve glycemic control. The conversational interface, SSMCGM-Agent, also requires clinical evaluation and intentionally avoids insulin dosing or precise treatment recommendations. We therefore present SSMCGM-Agent as a demonstration of how a language interface can be constrained by a physiological model, rather than as a validated system for clinical use.

Overall, SSMCGM-Stream represents a step toward adaptive personalized decision-support systems built on continuously maintained physiological representations.

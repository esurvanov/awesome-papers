## Reinforcement Learning for Hardware Acceleration and System-Level Optimization on FPGA and Integrated-Circuit Platforms: A Survey 

Hamza Kheddar

kheddar.hamza@univ-medea.dz

Address: LSEA Laboratory, Department of Electrical Engineering, University of Medea, 26000, Algeria

Abstract

Reinforcement learning (RL) has emerged as a powerful paradigm for enabling adaptive, autonomous, and self-optimizing behavior in modern computing systems, particularly as applications increasingly migrate toward embedded, edge, and heterogeneous hardware platforms. Field-programmable gate arrays (FPGAs) and integrated circuits (ICs) offer compelling advantages for RL deployment, including low latency, energy efficiency, and fine-grained architectural customization, but they also introduce unique design challenges that differ substantially from conventional CPU- and GPU- based implementations. This review presents a comprehensive survey of RL implementations on FPGA and IC platforms, with a particular emphasis on architectural design, performance measurement, and hardware–algorithm co-design. A broad body of more than one hundred studies is systematically analyzed and organized into major RL families, ranging from model-based Markov decision processes and classical value-based methods to deep RL, Actor-critic (AC), policy-gradient (PG), and multi-agent approaches. The review further categorizes existing works according to abstraction levels, hardware realization strategies, and application domains, highlighting how different RL algorithms map onto reconfigurable and fixed hardware substrates. In contrast to prior surveys that focus narrowly on specific algorithms or platforms, this work provides a unified perspective spanning both FPGA and IC implementations, encompassing acceleration techniques, reconfigurable hardware approaches, energy-aware control, and system-level optimization. Detailed comparisons are provided in terms of resource utilization, latency, throughput, power consumption, and achieved performance gains. Additionally, this review identifies recurring architectural patterns, discusses practical limitations, and outlines open research challenges and future directions for RL-driven intelligent hardware systems. By consolidating design insights, evaluation metrics, and application trends, this review aims to serve as a comprehensive reference for researchers and practitioners working at the intersection of RL and hardware platforms.

## 1 Introduction

Recent years have witnessed a growing shift toward intelligent, autonomous decision-making embedded directly within computing systems, with increasing emphasis on assessing the real-world applicability and feasibility of algorithms that demonstrate strong performance under simulation-based evaluations. As workloads become increasingly dynamic and heterogeneous—particularly in networking and edge computing, cyber–security, robotics [8], natural language processing, intelligent sensing, and embedded environments—traditional static control and optimization techniques, such as proportional–integral–derivative control and fixed-rule heuristic approaches [108] or meta-heuristic-based optimization methods [127], often struggle to cope with variability in data rates, power budgets, timing constraints, and environmental conditions. These approaches are typically designed around fixed operating assumptions and lack the adaptability required to respond effectively to non-stationary workloads or unforeseen runtime disturbances.

RL (RL), as a core paradigm within ML (ML), has gained prominence in this context because it provides a principled framework for sequential decision-making under uncertainty. By continuously interacting with an environment and updating its policy based on observed rewards, an RL agent can learn to adapt its behavior online, even when system dynamics are partially unknown or change over time. This capability makes RL particularly attractive for modern computing platforms that must balance competing objectives such as performance, energy efficiency, latency, and reliability under tight operational constraints. However, translating RL from software-centric implementations to practical hardware platforms introduces significant challenges. Many RL algorithms rely on iterative updates, stochastic exploration, and memory-intensive computations that are difficult to reconcile with the strict latency, power, and determinism requirements of embedded systems. Implementations based purely on general-purpose CPU or GPU often suffer from high energy consumption, unpredictable execution timing, and limited scalability, which restrict their suitability for real-time or resource-constrained applications.

FPGA (FPGA)s and IC provide an alternative hardware substrate that fundamentally reshapes how ML, particularly RL, can be realized in practice. FPGA provide fine-grained parallelism, customizable datapaths, and reconfigurability, allowing RL algorithms to be mapped onto hardware architectures tailored to specific workloads and constraints. IC-based realizations, including ASIC and SoC platforms, further extend these advantages by enabling deterministic execution, compact form factors, and ultra-low power operation. At the same time, these platforms impose stricter design constraints, limited flexibility after deployment, and increased development complexity compared with software-based solutions. As a result, the intersection of RL with hardware design has evolved into a multidisciplinary research area encompassing algorithm–hardware co-design [64], hardware acceleration [90, 41, 58], architectural specialization [25], numerical precision optimization [5], and system-level control, including digital twin–based frameworks [106]. In this emerging landscape, RL plays a dual role: it can be treated as a workload to be accelerated on hardware, and it can also serve as a control mechanism for the hardware itself, governing tasks such as dynamic voltage and frequency scaling, resource allocation, scheduling, partial reconfiguration, and energy management. This duality blurs the traditional boundary between learning algorithms and hardware platforms, creating new opportunities as well as new design challenges. Despite rapid progress, the existing literature on RL implementations for FPGA and IC platforms remains fragmented.

Motivated by the increasingly practical shift of RL from a purely algorithmic concept to a hardware-constrained workload, this review consolidates and structures the fast-growing body of RL implementations on FPGA and IC platforms.
In particular, this survey emphasizes real-time pattern recognition and perception-driven decision-making systems, where hardware constraints critically affect learning efficiency, latency, energy consumption, and deployment feasibility.
Instead of revisiting algorithmic fundamentals, it adopts a hardware-centric lens to clarify how design and implementation choices shape real-world realizations of RL.
The review organizes the literature into coherent categories and distills recurring design patterns, trade-offs, and limitations, thereby clarifying the current state-of-the-art and highlighting open challenges that guide future research at the intersection of RL, pattern recognition, and intelligent hardware.

Several surveys have examined the relationship between RL and reconfigurable or heterogeneous hardware, yet significant gaps remain. The survey in [118] provides an extensive overview of domain-specific RL architectures, with a primary focus on FPGA-based accelerators. However, it does not integrate IC implementations, unified abstraction layers, nor does it cover the complete spectrum of RL algorithmic families such as SARSA (SARSA), MC (MC), AC (AC), and PPO (PPO). ## 1.2 Review methodology

To ensure methodological rigor, transparency, and reproducibility, this review adopts a systematic literature search and selection strategy centered on peer-reviewed studies addressing the implementation of RL on FPGA and IC platforms. The primary search was conducted using Scopus as the main indexing engine, which provides broad coverage of major scientific publishers and digital libraries, including IEEE Xplore, Elsevier ScienceDirect, ACM Digital Library, Web of Science, Wiley, Springer, and IET Digital Library, among others. To consistently retrieve works at the intersection of RL algorithms and hardware realization, a structured Boolean query was formulated and applied across databases using title, abstract, and keyword fields. The search logic was designed to capture both classical and deep RL methods, while explicitly constraining results to hardware-oriented implementations rather than purely algorithmic or simulation-based studies. The generic query structure can be expressed as:

Selected papers =
FROM (“Title” OR “Abstract” OR “Keywords”)
SELECT (References WHERE
(RL OR DRL OR “Reinforcement Learning” OR “Deep Reinforcement Learning”) AND (FPGA OR “Field-Programmable Gate Array” OR ASIC OR IC OR SoC OR “Integrated Circuit”)) FILTER (Language = “English” AND Year BETWEEN 2015 AND 2026 AND DocumentType = (“Journal Article” OR “Conference Paper”))

The logical operators AND and OR ensure precise targeting of studies that explicitly combine RL with FPGA or IC hardware platforms. The selected time window reflects the emergence of practical RL hardware realizations, from early tabular and MDP (MDP)-based accelerators to modern deep and AC architectures.

To balance completeness with technical focus, the retrieved studies were further screened using relevance and quality criteria. Seminal RL hardware-acceleration works were retained when they directly supported the FPGA/IC-centered taxonomy, architectural discussion, performance comparison, or application-level analysis of this review. However, earlier works already extensively covered in prior surveys were not repeated exhaustively unless they established a necessary technical baseline or motivated a later research direction. This avoids redundant historical coverage while preserving the foundational context required for the proposed survey.

Redundant studies, papers with insufficient hardware-implementation details, works lacking meaningful evaluation results, and studies only weakly related to RL-based FPGA/IC platforms were excluded. Priority was given to recent studies that provide clear implementation details and contribute to emerging directions such as energy-efficient RL acceleration, quantized RL hardware, FPGA/IC-based runtime optimization, RL-assisted synthesis, MARL (MARL) hardware, and application-specific deployment.

## 2 Background

## 2.1 FPGA and IC

Modern FPGA are heterogeneous fabrics composed of several tightly coupled resources rather than only a sea of logic cells. The core of the device is formed by a 2D array of CLB. Each CLB contains one or more slices, and a slice integrates LUT, FF, optional LUTRAM (LUTRAM), and dedicated carry logic. LUT implement arbitrary combinational functions, FF provide sequential storage, LUTRAM enables small on-chip memories close to the logic, and fast carry chains support efficient arithmetic operations such as adders and counters.

Surrounding and interleaved with the CLB array are specialized resources. Columns of BRAM offer larger, synchronous memories with configurable width and depth, used for data buffering, FIFO, and on-chip caches. Dedicated DSP (DSP) slices provide high-speed arithmetic units (typically multipliers, add–accumulate units, and pre/post-adders), which are essential for signal processing, ML, and other compute-intensive workloads. At the periphery of the device, I/O banks host programmable input/output blocks that interface the FPGA fabric with external signals and support various standards and voltage levels. These computation and storage blocks are connected by a rich hierarchy of routing resources. Local and global interconnect networks, including long lines and dedicated global routing (often driven by global buffers, GBs), allow signals to traverse the chip and distribute clocks and resets. Clock-management tiles, such as PLL (PLL) and MMCM (MMCM), generate and condition internal clocks, enabling frequency synthesis, phase shifting, and jitter reduction. Configuration and control logic, typically including a configuration memory interface and state machines, orchestrate device programming, partial reconfiguration, and global control signals. Together, CLB (with LUT, FF, LUTRAM, and carry logic), BRAM, DSP slices, I/O banks, global routing, clock-management, and configuration/control logic form the heterogeneous substrate on which hardware-aware RL and MDP-based techniques can operate to dynamically optimize performance, power, and resource utilization. Figure 2 (a) illustrate a typical FPGA board resources.

Figure 2: Overview of two hardware architecture representations that could be employed for RL workloads. (a) A simplified FPGA floorplan showing the distribution of on-chip resources—CLB logic tiles, BRAM columns, global routing spines, configuration/control logic, global buffers, clock management blocks, and I/O banks—with a CLB slice highlighting LUT, FF, LUTRAM, and carry logic elements. (b) A conceptual IC/SoC architecture illustrating functional subsystems relevant to RL execution, including the CPU core, hardware accelerator, on-chip memory, interconnect (Bus/NoC), power management, clock management, and I/O interfaces and pads.

Unlike reconfigurable FPGA, an IC implements a fixed hardware architecture that is defined at fabrication time. Modern ASIC or SoC devices integrate multiple subsystems on a single die, including general-purpose CPU cores, hardware accelerators, on-chip memories ( SRAM (SRAM)/read-only memory (ROM)), I/O interfaces, and analog or mixed-signal blocks. The digital logic is typically built from standard cells (gates, flip-flops, etc.) placed and routed once during design, with no post-silicon reconfiguration capability.

These IC also embed rich infrastructure for power and clock management, such as clock trees, PLL, and DVFS (DVFS) controllers, as well as on-chip interconnects like buses or NoC. In hardware-aware RL and MDP-based schemes, the IC is therefore treated as a fixed substrate whose tunable parameters include operating points (voltage/frequency domains), power-gating states, and scheduling or resource-allocation policies for the embedded accelerators and memories. This contrasts with FPGA, where RL may additionally control spatial reconfiguration of the logic fabric itself. Figure 2 (b) illustrate a typical IC resources.

The unified abstraction hierarchy highlights the structural similarities and key differences between FPGA and IC design flows. At the SLD (SLD) level, both platforms rely on high-level algorithm modeling using MATLAB, Simulink, or SystemC to explore functionality and performance. Beyond this level, DSC have increasingly emerged as an additional abstraction layer between high-level ML/RL models and low-level hardware implementation. Unlike general-purpose HLS (HLS) tools, which mainly translate C/C++/SystemC descriptions into hardware, DSC operate on domain-aware representations such as tensor graphs, dataflow graphs, neural-network operators, or accelerator templates. This enables compiler-level optimizations such as operator fusion, memory tiling, scheduling, quantization, and hardware-aware mapping before generating HLS code, RTL (RTL), or intermediate hardware descriptions. In the context of RL-based FPGA/IC systems, DSC are particularly relevant for deep RL methods such as DQN (DQN), PPO, and DDPG (DDPG), where policy/value networks, replay buffers, and learning-update pipelines introduce repeated computational patterns that can be systematically optimized.

HLS then bridges software and hardware by converting C/C++/SystemC descriptions into RTL for both technologies, although ASIC tools emphasize early power and area exploration. RTL design remains common to both flows, relying on Verilog or VHDL (VHDL) to define datapaths, control logic, and finite-state machines. Divergence appears at the physical level: FPGA implementation performs placement, routing, and timing closure over a fixed reconfigurable fabric, while ASIC flows involve full custom physical design, including floorplanning, clock-tree synthesis, routing, sign-off timing analysis, and DRC/LVS checks before tape-out. This unified view clarifies how RL hardware—such as MDP or QL (QL) accelerators—can be designed coherently for both FPGA prototyping and IC deployment. ## 2.2 Platforms and Simulators

Beyond conventional design environments such as MATLAB/Simulink, HDL Coder, and vendor-specific toolchains including AMD-Xilinx Vivado, a growing number of research-oriented simulators and platforms have been proposed to enable flexible integration of learning algorithms with hardware-aware evaluation.
These platforms aim to decouple RL agent development from low-level hardware implementation details, support rapid design-space exploration, and provide meaningful performance, energy, and latency feedback prior to physical deployment. For example, Chen et al. [21] introduced VPR-Gym, a Python-based platform built on top of the OpenAI Gym framework to enable systematic exploration of AI (AI) techniques for FPGA placement optimization. By decoupling learning agents from the underlying C++ implementation of the VPR toolchain, VPR-Gym lowers the barrier for integrating modern RL, evolutionary strategies, and other AI-based optimizers into the FPGA computer-aided design (CAD) flow. The platform supports multiple learning paradigms, flexible reward formulations, and parallel environment execution, making it particularly suitable for rapid experimentation and comparative evaluation of placement strategies while preserving compatibility with industrial-grade FPGA CAD tools. Beyond FPGA placement, simulation frameworks targeting emerging hardware paradigms have also been proposed. Rakka et al. [114] presented HDRLPIM, a cycle- and energy-aware simulator for hyper-dimensional RL implemented on processing-in-memory (PIM) architectures. The simulator models associative processor-based accelerators using SRAM and ReRAM technologies and focuses on accelerating inference in hyper-dimensional QL. By abstracting the hardware execution of core RL operations—such as Q-value computation and action selection—HDRLPIM enables detailed evaluation of latency, energy consumption, and energy–delay product, offering a valuable tool for early-stage design-space exploration of non-von-Neumann RL accelerators. In the context of power electronics, Xu et al. [150] proposed an FPGA-based circuit simulator designed to accelerate RL-driven power converter design. The simulator offloads time-domain circuit simulation to an FPGA accelerator deployed on cloud-based platforms, significantly reducing the training time of RL agents compared to conventional software simulators. By packaging the accelerator within an OpenAI Gym-compatible environment, the framework enables seamless integration with standard RL algorithms while allowing on-the-fly modification of circuit parameters without regenerating bitstreams. This work highlights the importance of hardware-aware simulators in making RL-based design methodologies practical for real-world engineering applications. Complementary to these simulation platforms, Canese et al. [14] presented a MATLAB/Simulink-based toolchain that automatically generates FPGA intellectual property (IP) cores for tabular QL accelerators. The framework produces synthesizable VHDL code and integrates directly with the AMD-Xilinx Vivado flow, enabling users to customize Q-table dimensions, numerical precision, and target FPGA devices. By leveraging HDL Coder, the tool reproduces established hardware architectures while significantly reducing development time and facilitating design-space exploration. Although the framework demonstrated full compatibility with existing QL accelerators, support for Intel FPGA platforms and ASIC design flows was identified as future work.

## 3 Taxonomy of RL-based FPGA and IC platform

This section provides the essential concepts for understanding RL, a taxonomy of existing RL/ DRL (DRL) techniques, and references to works that have already applied RL in FPGA accelerators within each category. A detailed conceptual distinction between RL and DRL is presented in the taxonomy of previous survey [71]. In the present paper, the term RL is used in a broad sense that encompasses both classical and deep RL methods, as they share the same underlying MDP formalism and notation adopted here.

Definitions

At a high level, an agent repeatedly interacts with an environment by observing
its current condition, selecting an action, and receiving a scalar reward that
evaluates the quality of this decision. Over time, the agent adapts its behaviour
so as to maximize the long-term accumulation of rewards. The main symbols used in
this interaction are summarized in Table 3.

Formally, we model the environment as a Markov decision process (MDP)
 $(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{A},\mathbb{P},R,\gamma)$ , where $\mathcal{S}$ and
 $\mathcal{A}$ denote the state and action spaces, $\mathbb{P}(s_{t+1}\mid s_{t},a_{t})$ 
is the transition kernel, $R$ defines the scalar reward $r_{t}\in\mathbb{R}$ , and
 $\gamma\in[0,1)$ is the discount factor. At time step $t$ , the agent receives an
observation $o_{t}$ (which may coincide with the state $s_{t}$ ), chooses an action
 $a_{t}$ according to a policy $\pi(a_{t}\mid o_{t})$ , and then observes the next state
and reward. The learning objective is to adjust $\pi$ so as to maximize the expected
discounted return $\mathbb{E}_{\pi}\!\left[\sum_{t=0}^{T}\gamma^{t}r_{t}\right]$ .

In the context of FPGA-based DRL, these abstract quantities correspond to
hardware-level signals and memory structures: states/observations are represented as
fixed-point or low-precision vectors stored in on-chip memories or streamed over AXI
interfaces; actions are emitted as low-latency control signals; and rewards are fed back
to update value estimates or policy parameters. For a more detailed discussion of these
mappings and of alternative formulations, we refer the reader to [YourPrevReview].

## 3.1 Taxonomy of RL-based FPGA platform

## 3.2 MDP

An MDP $(S,A,P,R,\gamma)$ formalizes sequential decision-making for hardware-aware RL, where $s\in S$ compactly represents the current hardware and workload configuration (e.g., buffer utilization, timing slack, power states) and $a\in A$ encodes reconfiguration choices such as clock scaling, datapath parallelism, resource allocation, or power-management tuning. The transition kernel $P(s^{\prime}\mid s,a)$ models stochastic workload dynamics, noise, and hardware variability, while the reward function $R(s,a)$ aligns learning with design goals including latency minimization, energy and performance-per-watt optimization, throughput maximization, and accuracy or error-rate targets; the discount factor $\gamma$ balances short- versus long-term performance. On FPGA, MDP-driven RL implementations exploit fine-grained reconfigurability, parallelism, pipelining, low-precision arithmetic, and optimized memory hierarchies, whereas IC-based realizations emphasize area efficiency, deterministic timing, and ultra-low energy operation. Across both platforms, the $(S,A,P,R,\gamma)$ formalism supports rigorous analysis of convergence, stability, and safety, making MDP-guided RL a robust paradigm for real-time, self-optimizing FPGA and IC architectures.

The agent is built around an RL controller that interacts with the environment and a Planner/Policy block. After an action $a_{t}$ is applied, the environment produces the next state $s_{t+1}$ and reward $r_{t+1}$ . The state $s_{t+1}$ is forwarded to the Planner/Policy block, which uses it to select the next action $a_{t+1}$ . In parallel, a delay element $z^{-1}$ stores $(s_{t+1},r_{t+1})$ so that, at the following control cycle, the controller receives the previous transition pair as $(s_{t},r_{t})$ . Together with the discount factor $\gamma$ and learning rate $\alpha$ , these signals are used by the controller to update its value functions and/or policy parameters. The updated policy is then made available to the Planner/Policy block, which outputs the action to be applied to the environment, thereby closing the MDP control loop.

As a preliminary contribution, the paper [121] adopted a model-based RL perspective on the nondeterministic polynomial-time complete problem of full interconnect coverage in SRAM FPGA. It formulated configuration generation as a MDP whose states are programmable interconnect point terminals and actions select wire-type transitions over a generic interconnect model. Using DP (DP), the approach computed optimal state values and policies that yield near-theoretical-minimum configuration sets with 100% interconnect resource coverage, replacing ad hoc graph heuristics with mathematically grounded planning. However, the scheme faced high offline computation, limited scalability, and no online adaptive deployment. Extending the discussion, the paper [82] tackles slow sparse MDP model construction on CPU by offloading AI-Toolbox’s SparseModel() routine to a FPGA using Vivado HLS. The accelerator generates nonzero transition and reward entries, exploiting loop flattening, pipelining, fixed-point arithmetic, and AXI-Stream interfaces to fill large sparse matrices efficiently. The design treats FPGA as generic, reusable MDP/RL coprocessors tightly coupled to high-level libraries, prioritizing portability and development productivity over deeply customized, application-specific RL accelerators. It bridges algorithmic RL frameworks and hardware efficiency.

## 3.3 Q-learning

Unlike MDP-based planning, which assumes access to the transition model, QL focuses on estimating the optimal action–value function $Q^{\ast}(s,a)$ without requiring an explicit model of $P$ or $R$ . In hardware-aware settings, a tabular or compactly parameterized $Q(s,a)$ is stored in on-chip memories (e.g., BRAM/LUTRAM) and updated according to Equation 1:

 $Q_{t+1}(s,a)=Q_{t}(s,a)+\alpha\bigl[r+\gamma\max_{a^{\prime}}Q_{t}(s^{\prime},a^{\prime})-Q_{t}(s,a)\bigr],$ 

(1)

Where $\alpha$ is the learning rate. On FPGA, this update naturally maps to fixed-point arithmetic pipelines and parallel comparators for the $\max_{a^{\prime}}$ operation, while the table lookups are accelerated via multi-port memory and carefully scheduled access patterns. The $\varepsilon$ -greedy or Boltzmann exploration policy can be realized as a lightweight control FSM, enabling on-line adaptation of DVFS modes, region-level reconfiguration, or scheduling decisions with modest resource overhead. Compared to purely model-based MDP planning, QL trades analytical guarantees for simpler hardware implementation and the ability to learn directly from runtime measurements when transition probabilities or reward statistics are difficult to characterize a prior. Within this generic template, several FPGA-based QL accelerators have explored different points in the design space in terms of resource usage, latency, and flexibility.

At the low-resource end of this spectrum, [89] proposed a serial FPGA accelerator for QL that targets low resource consumption and deterministic timing. The authors implemented the design using Vivado HLS, achieving a significant reduction in logic usage compared with a previous parallel architecture while fixing the Q-update latency to four cycles. The modular design with RAM-based Q-tables and LFSR-driven random policy is therefore well suited to embedded, real-time agents. Moving toward higher-throughput, fully parallel architectures, the paper [132] addressed the problem of executing QL efficiently in hardware, given that conventional CPU- and GPU-based implementations suffered from high power consumption and limited real-time performance. It proposed an FPGA-based accelerator that used distributed action RAM, a max-tree for rapid action selection, and an optimized Q-Updater that reduced multiplier usage through power-of-two arithmetic. The design achieved superior speed and energy efficiency, making the resulting accelerator suitable for embedded, IoT (IoT), and multi-agent real-time RL applications. Extending these ideas to heterogeneous SoC platforms, the paper [136] addressed the challenge of executing QL under strict real-time and energy constraints, where fast state–action updates and scalable memory structures were crucial. To overcome this, the authors introduced FARANE-Q, a heterogeneous architecture that exploited the dual nature of Zynq devices by combining a SoC-based PS (PS) for configuration and control with FPGA PL (PL) for hardware acceleration. Their design restructured QL through parallel and pipelined computation in the PL, supported by approximate multipliers, BRAM-based Q-storage, and dynamic $\varepsilon$ -greedy exploration. This coordinated PS–PL strategy enabled efficient, scalable, and reconfigurable RL execution for embedded intelligent systems. Complementary to these architectural innovations, [137] focused on a hardware-optimization strategy centered on numerical precision. Whereas earlier FPGA-based QL accelerators typically relied on full-precision fixed-point arithmetic, neural-network–based Q-value estimation, or approximate multipliers without systematic precision exploration, this work examined how fractional and integer bit-widths affected Q-value accuracy and showed that reducing the datapath from 32 to 16 bits preserved optimal policy behavior. Their approach integrated precision tuning with barrel-shifter–based approximate multipliers, enabling a DSP-free Q-Updater and substantial reductions in LUT and FF usage.

## 3.4 SARSA

Unlike QL, which was an off-policy method relying on the greedy estimate $\max_{a^{\prime}}Q(s^{\prime},a^{\prime})$ , SARSA performed an on-policy update in which the next action $a^{\prime}$ was sampled from the agent’s behavior policy (e.g., $\varepsilon$ -greedy). Its update rule was:

 $Q_{t+1}(s,a)=Q_{t}(s,a)+\alpha\bigl[r+\gamma Q_{t}(s^{\prime},a^{\prime})-Q_{t}(s,a)\bigr],$ 

(2)

where $(s,a,r,s^{\prime},a^{\prime})$ denoted the full interaction tuple. The absence of the $\max_{a^{\prime}}$ operator made SARSA’s bootstrapping dependent on the actual executed action rather than a hypothetical greedy one.

From a hardware perspective, this distinction had several implications. Eliminating the $\max_{a^{\prime}}$ comparator tree simplified the datapath and reduced critical-path delays, LUT footprints, and DSP utilization—an advantage for deeply pipelined fixed-point accelerators on FPGA and resource-constrained ASIC SoC s. The update logic became dominated by a single table lookup for $Q(s^{\prime},a^{\prime})$ instead of a parallel scan across all actions, which enabled smaller multi-port BRAM/LUTRAM banks or even banking-free single-cycle access in low-action-count settings. Furthermore, the on-policy nature required the PG to expose the selected action $a^{\prime}$ back to the learner, creating a tighter coupling between the PG and the update engine. This feedback was typically implemented through a lightweight handshake interface or shared memory queue, and, unlike in QL, the PG could not be fully decoupled from the update hardware.

Recent FPGA accelerators reinforced these advantages. QTAccel [97] demonstrated that SARSA reused exactly the same pipeline stages as QL—address generation, memory banks, and ALU (ALU) pipelines—requiring only that the policy-selected action be forwarded to the update stage. This eliminated the need for additional action-selection logic and greedy comparator trees, thereby reducing control complexity and easing timing closure as the number of actions increased. QTAccel also showed that SARSA introduced no additional LUT, DSP, or BRAM overhead, and that its update latency remained stable and independent of action-space width. Moreover, SARSA aligned naturally with architectures in which the environment or policy logic was integrated into the same hardware substrate. This is exemplified by the FAQ accelerator [119], which implemented environment dynamics and Q-updates within a configurable hardware framework, provided such an example. Because transitions, rewards, and actions were produced directly on the FPGA, the executed action $a^{\prime}$ could be forwarded immediately to the learner. This coherence between execution and learning avoided the divergence that off-policy methods like QL had to correct, simplified scheduling, supported deterministic replay-free operation, and reduced interface traffic.

This analysis prove that SARSA offered simpler datapaths, action-count-independent update latency, and tighter coupling between policy execution and learning, making it an attractive alternative to QL for tightly integrated FPGA/IC-based Q-table RL systems.

## 3.5 Deep Q-learning

Unlike tabular QL, which directly stores and updates a discrete table of $Q(s,a)$ values, a DQN approximates the action–value function using a deep neural network $Q(s,a;\theta)$ and learns its parameters from sampled transitions. This removes the need for an explicit transition model while enabling scalability to high-dimensional state spaces. Instead of the single-step update in Equation 1, the DQN agent minimizes a TD loss over minibatches extracted from an experience replay buffer:

 $\mathcal{L}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}{(s,a,r,s^{\prime})\sim\mathcal{D}}\Bigl[\bigl(r+\gamma\max{a^{\prime}}Q(s^{\prime},a^{\prime};\theta^{-})-Q(s,a;\theta)\bigr)^{2}\Bigr],$ 

(3)

where $\theta$ are the parameters of the main Q-network and $\theta^{-}$ are the periodically updated parameters of the target network.

On hardware platforms such as FPGA, this architecture introduces new building blocks compared with tabular QL. Neural network forward propagation is mapped to pipelined matrix–vector multipliers, activation blocks, and quantized arithmetic units, while the experience replay buffer is implemented using on-chip or hybrid BRAM/ DDR (DDR) circular memory structures that support random minibatch sampling. The target Q-network is instantiated as a secondary neural module that shares the same topology but updates at a slower rate, improving training stability. The exploration policy (e.g., $\varepsilon$ -greedy) remains a lightweight control FSM, but action selection now operates on the network’s vector of Q-values rather than table entries.

Compared with hardware-friendly QL, which relies mainly on fixed-point comparators and simple update operators, DQN trades architectural simplicity for generalization capability and expressiveness—enabling learning directly from complex, high-dimensional sensory streams. Beyond the algorithmic principles of DQN, recent FPGA-based implementations demonstrate how the learning pipeline can be decomposed, parallelized, and quantized to meet the constraints of embedded and edge platforms. While the baseline DQN formulation requires forward inference, loss evaluation, backpropagation, and periodic target updates, mapping these stages to hardware has motivated several architectural innovations. A representative example is the heterogeneous DQN accelerator of Zhang et al. [161], which divides the training process into five strictly separated computing stages—forward pass, loss evaluation, error backpropagation, gradient generation, and parameter update—each implemented as an independent hardware unit. This phase-specialized decomposition eliminates resource conflicts and improves pipeline utilization, especially when floating-point arithmetic is required. Their design exploits FPGA IP cores for 32-bit floating-point MAC and adopts multi-mode matrix multipliers to reduce the cost of weight transposition. The result is a heterogeneous dataflow pipeline capable of supporting full DQN training while outperforming CPU and GPU baselines in energy efficiency. A different approach is adopted in Rothmann et al.’s STANN-RL accelerator [120], which focuses on modularity and reusability of DQN components. Here, neural-network training blocks (dense layers, optimizers, minibatch generators) are drawn from the STANN library and extended with RL- specific operators such as target-network synchronization and prioritized minibatch sampling. Implemented on a Xilinx Versal device and interfaced with Gymnasium environments over Ethernet, the accelerator supports low-latency training with batch sizes typical of DQN (often as small as 1–8). Notably, the inclusion of an FPGA-based Adam optimizer improves convergence compared with traditional fixed-point stochastic gradient descent (SGD), while maintaining a speed-up than CPU execution. FPGA-based DQN has also been explored in the context of pedagogical or benchmark environments. Tran et al. [81], provide a full training and inference pipeline for the Cartpole task, using 32-bit floating-point arithmetic and Cocotb-based HW/SW co-simulation. Their design highlights the importance of careful scheduling of neural-network operations, since even a small network demands efficient pipelined MAC arrays and memory interfaces to sustain real-time training throughput. Although the architecture is less optimized than more recent designs, it exposes essential hardware building blocks—forward-prop datapaths, target-network shadow modules, and online gradient-update logic—that underlie almost all later DQN accelerators. More recent research targets resource-constrained edge platforms. Sumarudin et al. [133], propose a dual-mode accelerator capable of supporting both inference and on-device training within a single configurable processing element. By reusing datapaths across forward and backward passes, employing piecewise-linear approximations for activation functions, and adopting fixed-point arithmetic with quantization-aware updates, the design achieves significant reductions in LUT, DSPs, and power consumption. The accelerator—implemented on an Ultra96 platform—delivers remarkable speedups with a low power budget, illustrating how DQN training can be pushed onto edge-class SoC.

## 3.6 DDPG

While DQN extends tabular QL to high-dimensional discrete control, DDPG generalizes the same off-policy and target-network principles to continuous action spaces. Instead of computing $\arg\max_{a^{\prime}}Q(s,a^{\prime})$ , FPGA introduces a differentiable deterministic actor $\mu(s;\theta_{\mu})$ whose output directly produces a continuous action. The critic $Q(s,a;\theta_{Q})$ evaluates these actor-generated actions, forming the continuous-control analogue of DQN. This eliminates discrete-action maximization and its wide comparator trees, but requires an action–state fusion layer in the critic to concatenate the actor output with the state vector. These algorithmic differences impose clear architectural implications on hardware accelerators. First, the critic must evaluate $Q(s,a)$ for actor-generated actions, increasing MAC count relative to discrete-action DQN evaluators. Second, the actor and critic jointly participate in each training step: the critic learns via a TD loss, while the actor receives gradients from $\nabla_{a}Q(s,a)$ , requiring a dual forward–backward pipeline. Third, exploration is performed by injecting lightweight noise (e.g., OU or uniform) at the actor output, avoiding stochastic sampling logic or $\varepsilon$ -greedy units. Across FPGA and IC designs, these features make FPGA highly amenable to acceleration. Replay memory can be stored in BRAM or DDR using circular buffers, and the AC pair can share matrix–vector engines through time-multiplexed scheduling because FPGA does not require a discrete argmax. Furthermore, FPGA typically uses smaller batch sizes than deep value-based algorithms, easing memory-bandwidth pressure and enabling deterministic low-latency compute pipelines.

Recent implementations further refined this mapping. FIXAR [151] demonstrated that DDPG benefited from fixed-point quantization-aware training, where data precision was reduced mid-training without degrading policy quality. This reduced arithmetic cost and enabled adaptive parallelism in FPGA datapaths. Similarly, Guo and Luk’s FPGA-accelerated sim-to-real platform [50] showed DDPG’s suitability for continuous-control robotics accelerators, where the gradient-computation pipeline was the dominant workload and achieved substantial speedups through FPGA-based parallelism. In heterogeneous CPU–GPU–FPGA settings, Zhang et al. [157] included DDPG as one of the algorithms supported in their prioritized replay framework, indicating that critic-centric training operations mapped particularly well to on-chip parallel sampling modules. Finally, TD3 (TD3)-lite [56]—a successor of DDPG—illustrated how structural and representation optimizations (shared networks, block floating-point) directly reduced the resource footprint inherent to AC pipelines. More recently, the authors [115] applied DDPG to torque-ripple minimization in switched reluctance motors for electric-drive control, where the actor generated continuous firing-angle offsets and the critic guided policy updates from torque, current, speed, position, and flux-linkage feedback.

Taken together, the hardware literature positioned DDPG as a well-structured target for FPGA/IC acceleration: its deterministic policy, moderately sized networks, absence of discrete maximization, and predictable update rules yielded balanced compute and memory characteristics suited to both single-accelerator and heterogeneous-system designs.

## 3.7 Actor-critic

Unlike value-based methods that update a single action–value estimator, AC algorithms maintain two coordinated function approximators: an actor network that represents a policy $\pi(a\mid s;\theta)$ and a critic network that evaluates states through a value function $V(s;\phi)$ or an advantage estimate.
At each interaction step, action selection, value evaluation, and parameter updates are jointly executed, forming a tightly coupled learning pipeline that is well suited for hardware realization. The end-to-end learning sequence of a baseline AC agent is summarized in Equation (4).
The actor performs policy inference to select an action, the environment returns the immediate reward and next state, and the critic evaluates the TD error that drives both policy and value-function updates.

 $\displaystyle\xrightarrow{\text{state input}}\pi_{\theta}(\cdot|s_{t})\;{\scriptstyle[\text{DSP/MAC array}]}\xrightarrow{\text{action sampling}}a_{t}\xrightarrow{\text{envir.}}$ 
 $\displaystyle(r_{t},s_{t+1})\xrightarrow{\text{critic evaluation}}V_{\phi}(s_{t}),\,V_{\phi}(s_{t+1})\;{\scriptstyle[\text{DSP/MAC array}]}$ 

(4)

 $\displaystyle\xrightarrow{\text{TD error}}\delta_{t}\;{\scriptstyle[\text{fixed-point ALU}]}\xrightarrow{\text{gradient computation}}(\nabla_{\theta}J_{\pi},\nabla_{\phi}J_{V})$ 
 $\displaystyle\;{\scriptstyle[\text{gradient engine}]}\xrightarrow{\text{parameter update}}(\theta,\phi)\;{\scriptstyle[\text{BRAM/URAM write-back}]}$ 

The interaction between actor and critic is governed by the TD error is:

 $\delta_{t}=r_{t}+\gamma V_{\phi}(s_{t+1})-V_{\phi}(s_{t}),$ 

(5)

which simultaneously drives value-function regression and policy-gradient updates. From a hardware perspective, Equation (5) maps to a lightweight arithmetic unit that feeds both gradient paths, enabling synchronized learning with minimal control overhead.

In a neuromorphic context, the authors in [92] demonstrated that this tightly coupled AC interaction can be efficiently realized using an ergodic sequential logic (ESL)-based SNN (SNN), where both actor and critic are implemented as discrete-state neuron arrays with local update rules. Their FPGA implementation shows that the ESL-based design preserves the functional behavior of conventional spiking AC models while significantly reducing hardware cost in terms of logic elements and power. However, the evaluation is limited to small-scale navigation task.

While classical AC refers broadly to any architecture combining policy gradients with a value baseline, most hardware-oriented implementations adopt the A3C (A3C) formulation, which extends Equation (4) to multiple asynchronous learners.
Each learner independently executes a local AC pipeline and generates gradients that are asynchronously accumulated into shared global parameters, as formalized in Equation (6).

In FPGA, this structure naturally maps to multiple replicated compute pipelines sharing a global parameter memory without strict synchronization.

More recently, SAC (SAC) has emerged as an entropy-regularized extension of AC learning that improves robustness and exploration.
In contrast to the on-policy sequences in Equations (4) and (6), SAC performs off-policy learning using a replay buffer, a stochastic Gaussian actor, and dual critic networks.

 $J(\pi)=\mathbb{E}_{(s,a)\sim\mathcal{B}}\left[Q(s,a)-\alpha\log\pi(a|s)\right],$ 

(9)

which introduces additional hardware requirements such as noise generation, log-probability computation, and temperature adaptation.

On FPGA, Equations (4)–(8) translate into dual- or multi-pipeline architectures capable of executing concurrent inference, TD or advantage computation, and gradient updates.
The TD-error unit in Equation (5) is realized as a compact arithmetic module feeding both actor and critic update paths, while support for A3C and SAC requires parallel gradient-generation pipelines, global update controllers, and memory-backed replay buffers.

One of the earliest and most influential designs is FA3C [23], which implements a full A3C accelerator supporting both inference and on-line training. The architecture introduces a compute-unit-pair structure in which forward and backward engines operate in a tightly pipelined manner, while tensor decomposition, quantization, and a two-level parameter cache mitigate off-chip bandwidth limitations. FA3C achieved improvement in training throughput over GPU- based A3C, demonstrating that full AC training can be mapped efficiently to FPGA fabric. Building on this foundation, the distributed A3C accelerator proposed by Ge et al. [39] targets multi-agent A3C with system-level optimization across heterogeneous nodes. Their architecture introduces a resource-optimal deployment strategy that avoids redundant replication of actor and critic networks across agents, enabling significantly higher concurrency on mid-sized FPGA platforms. The design separates learning phases into dedicated loss-gradient computation units, back-propagation engines, gradient-compression modules, and synchronized parameter-update blocks. Experimental results show speedup over GPU- based A3C while maintaining high resource efficiency, making it one of the most advanced recent contributions in distributed AC hardware acceleration. A complementary approach is presented in the A3C-HC accelerator [88], which implements A3C on a heterogeneous CPU- -FPGA platform. In this design, environment simulation, high-level control, and synchronization remain on CPU cores, while the FPGA executes the forward inference, TD computation, and gradient updates for both actor and critic networks. This partitioning aligns with modern edge-AI deployment strategies in which the FPGA acts as a low-latency learning co-processor. The accelerator incorporates quantized feed-forward computation blocks, BRAM-based gradient accumulation buffers, and a timing-aligned dual-network update controller. Results indicate substantial reductions in training time and improved power efficiency compared with CPU- only implementations.

Collectively, these efforts show that AC algorithms are now viable for real-time, on-chip training, with architectures ranging from high-performance compute-unit-paired accelerators to scalable distributed A3C systems and heterogeneous CPU- -FPGA co-processors. Remaining challenges include coordinating dual-network synchronization, managing memory bandwidth for gradient updates, and balancing resource usage across actor and critic datapaths. Nonetheless, modern FPGA fabrics—with abundant DSP blocks, multi-port BRAM, and fine-grained parallelism—are increasingly capable of supporting continuous, low-latency AC learning in embedded and edge-intelligent platforms.

## 3.8 Monte-Carlo

MC can be viewed as a simplified extension of AC methods in which no critic or TD-error is used. Instead of bootstrapping from value estimates, MC updates rely solely on the empirical return $G_{t}$ accumulated over an entire episode. This removes the need for TD-error circuitry but requires hardware support for storing complete trajectories and computing return values at episode boundaries.

On FPGA, MC accelerators replace the TD-error unit with a lightweight return-accumulation block implemented using fixed-point accumulators and either FIFO-based reward stacks or BRAM-backed trajectory buffers. Because gradients are computed only after episode termination, hardware must support sequential reward writes during sampling and a backward scan or prefix-sum operation to compute $G_{t}$ , both of which map efficiently to pipelined adders or parallel-prefix engines. Policy-gradient MC methods also require a trajectory buffer to store state–action pairs and, optionally, log-probabilities, which are replayed through the policy network for a single episode-level update. Unlike A3C or SAC, MC pipelines do not include dual networks, entropy-temperature terms, or asynchronous TD updates, but they incur higher BRAM/DDR demand due to full-trajectory retention. As a result, many MC designs offload the trajectory buffer to external memory while keeping the policy network and gradient logic on FPGA for efficient fixed-point computation. Compared with TD-based accelerators, MC implementations trade frequent, low-latency updates for simpler control logic and more regular memory access patterns. The episodic update regime reduces responsiveness but remains attractive for tasks with short or deterministic episodes where unbiased return estimates are preferable to bootstrapped targets.

The work of Meng et al. [95] proposed one of the first FPGA-capable MC tree search (MCTS) accelerators that supports fully dynamic tree growth without reconfiguration, addressing a key limitation of earlier static-tree FPGA designs. The authors introduced a butterfly-based interconnection network and a bank-assignment algorithm to minimize runtime access conflicts and enable arbitrary tree shapes. A hybrid CPU–FPGA execution model exploits FPGA pipeline parallelism for in-tree operations while delegating simulations to CPU threads. Their framework achieves up to 6.8× throughput improvement over CPU baselines while maintaining algorithmic performance across Atari benchmarks.

## 3.9 PPO

Compared with classical AC algorithms, which updated the actor using instantaneous TD-errors, PPO introduced a clipped surrogate objective that restricted the magnitude of policy updates. This clipping term

 $L^{\text{CLIP}}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}\bigl[\min\bigl(r_{t}(\theta)A_{t},\,\text{clip}(r_{t}(\theta),1-\epsilon,1+\epsilon)A_{t}\bigr)\bigr],$ 

modified the actor update rule without altering the critic structure, and therefore differed fundamentally from both AC and MC: the algorithm required neither bootstrapped TD errors for the actor nor full-episode returns, but instead relied on advantage estimates accumulated over truncated rollout segments.

On hardware platforms, these algorithmic changes had direct architectural consequences. Unlike AC, where the actor received immediate TD-errors, PPO required a ratio block to compute
 $r_{t}(\theta)=\pi_{\theta}(a_{t}\mid s_{t})/\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(a_{t}\mid s_{t})$ ,
necessitating storage of the previous policy’s log-probabilities and additional arithmetic units for division or log-subtraction.
The clipping path introduced a second comparison-and-select stage that was absent in AC and MC, and required dedicated fixed-point saturators or LUT-based comparators to implement the bounded update.
The algorithm’s minibatch-based optimization further demanded repeated passes over the same rollout data—in contrast to the single-sweep online updates used in AC and the episodic updates in MC.
As a result, FPGA designs incorporated BRAM-backed rollout buffers and address generators for minibatch iteration, which increased memory pressure compared with on-line TD updates.

Unlike MC, which computed policy gradients only at episode boundaries, PPO operated on truncated horizons, enabling moderate responsiveness while avoiding the high-variance returns that MC required.
This design required hardware support for advantage computation—typically a backward scan accumulating temporal differences—and for storing the value predictions required for generalized advantage estimation (GAE). These blocks were mapped to pipelined subtract–multiply–accumulate engines or to parallel-prefix units, depending on throughput targets. In contrast to asynchronous A3C designs, which relied on many loosely coupled learners, PPO favored synchronized optimization over multiple minibatches, which, in hardware, translated into a more regular update schedule but a tighter coupling between the actor, critic, and rollout memory.
Recent FPGA and IC studies showed that this structured optimization enabled efficient time-multiplexing of matrix–vector units between actor and critic networks, since the clip-and-ratio computation, rather than the neural layers themselves, became the dominant performance bottleneck.

All in all, PPO differed from both AC and MC by introducing ratio evaluation, policy-clipping logic, and minibatch-based training, all of which required dedicated arithmetic blocks, larger rollout storage, and multi-pass memory access.
Although these additions increased control complexity, PPO offered more stable learning and predictable convergence behavior, making it suitable for FPGA/IC accelerators targeting real-time but non-stationary environments.

The HEPPO [138] accelerator specifically targeted PPO’s Generalized Advantage Estimation (GAE), a component absent in both AC and MC implementations. The design introduced a deeply pipelined GAE computation unit with reward standardization, block standardization, and uniform 8-bit quantization. This standardization pipeline stabilized PPO’s advantage estimates and reduced on-chip memory footprint by $4\times$ , addressing a bottleneck unique to PPO. Unlike AC designs that only evaluated $V(s)$ inline, HEPPO implemented backward GAE recursion using dedicated arithmetic chains, minimizing memory accesses. Its single-SoC integration eliminated CPU–GPU communication and delivered up to $30\%$ speed improvement, demonstrating that PPO could be efficiently executed without large off-chip buffers. PPOAccel [96] addressed the heavy training workload of PPO by accelerating both inference and backpropagation on systolic arrays. The work exploited the structural similarity between MLP (MLP) and CNN (CNN) policies and implemented a unified GEMM engine with shared forward/backward datapaths—a capability unnecessary in AC or MC designs that used lighter function approximators. PPOAccel also introduced a novel memory-blocked data layout enabling stall-free streaming during multi-epoch updates, directly addressing PPO’s strict requirement to reuse the same batch across $K$ training epochs. The accelerator achieved up to $27\times$ throughput improvement over CPU/GPU baselines, mainly by eliminating kernel-launch overheads and training/inference imbalance typical in PPO. Building on these PPO-specific architectural requirements target the ratio, clipping, and GAE computation pipelines. For example, PPOAccel framework [96] proposed a CPU–FPGA heterogeneous accelerator for PPO that jointly speeds up policy inference and training through a unified systolic-array engine applicable to both MLP- and CNN-based policies. The design carefully profiled PPO’s sample-generation and model-update phases, then introduced optimized dataflow mapping, memory-blocked data layouts, and systolic compute-sharing to reduce data-movement overhead and balance utilization. Evaluations on standard robotics and Atari benchmarks show that PPOAccel effectively exploits PPO’s structure while preserving its learning dynamics.

## 3.10 Comprehensive Performance Metrics and Cross-Design Analysis

Although the surveyed FPGA/IC implementations report several raw hardware indicators in Table 5, and application-level performance improvements in Table 9, these metrics alone do not provide a complete view of implementation efficiency. This is particularly important for RL hardware, where different works accelerate different execution phases, including policy inference, value-function update, replay-buffer sampling, backpropagation, environment interaction, and full training iterations. Therefore, a comprehensive comparison requires normalized metrics that jointly capture performance, energy consumption, and hardware cost.

In this review, the comprehensive evaluation is organized around three complementary metric families: energy efficiency, resource efficiency, and latency–energy trade-off. Energy-efficiency metrics evaluate how much useful RL computation is achieved per unit of power. Depending on the reporting style of each study, the useful computation may correspond to inferences per second, training updates per second, samples per second, throughput, or FIPS (FIPS). Among these, FIPS/W is especially informative for full RL training accelerators because it accounts for the complete learning loop rather than only isolated inference or update kernels.

Resource-efficiency metrics complement energy-efficiency analysis by relating the achieved performance to the amount of FPGA or IC hardware consumed. This is necessary because two designs may report similar throughput or speedup while using very different fractions of BRAM, LUT, FF, LUTRAM, or DSP resources. A design with moderate throughput but very low utilization may be preferable for embedded or multi-agent deployment, whereas a design with high throughput but near-saturated BRAM or DSP usage may face limited scalability. For this reason, both the average resource footprint and the dominant bottleneck resource should be considered when comparing hardware efficiency.

The latency–energy trade-off is another important dimension, particularly for real-time RL applications such as robotics, networking, dynamic voltage and frequency scaling, autonomous control, and hardware security. Low latency alone does not necessarily imply an efficient design if it is achieved at the cost of excessive power consumption. Conversely, a low-power design may not be suitable for strict real-time control if the decision latency is too high. Energy per operation and energy–delay behavior therefore provide a more balanced view of whether a hardware accelerator is suitable for latency-critical and energy-constrained deployment.

Based on the raw implementation characteristics summarized in Table 5, the normalized evaluation criteria defined in Table 6, and the application-level performance comparisons reported in Table 9, the reviewed works show substantial variation in workload granularity, hardware cost, energy efficiency, resource efficiency, and reported performance objectives. Lightweight tabular QL accelerators generally exhibit very small resource footprints and low power consumption, making them attractive for embedded control tasks with compact state–action spaces. For example, the QL implementations in [89, 132] consume only a small fraction of LUT, FF, LUTRAM, and DSP resources and operate at tens of milliwatts. These characteristics indicate strong suitability for low-power embedded RL, although their efficiency should be interpreted within the context of compact tabular workloads rather than compared directly with neural-network-based deep RL accelerators.

AC and A3C-based accelerators further illustrate the need for comprehensive metrics. Designs such as [23, 39, 88] report considerable acceleration over GPU or CPU baselines, but they also introduce high resource pressure because actor and critic networks, gradient engines, local workers, and global update logic must be supported concurrently. These designs are therefore better evaluated using combined performance–resource and performance–power indicators rather than raw speedup alone. In particular, replicated worker pipelines may improve throughput but can also increase BRAM, DSP, and routing pressure, which affects scalability on smaller FPGA devices.

Among the reviewed works, TD3lite [56] provides one of the most explicit examples of comprehensive performance reporting. It reports full-iteration throughput together with measured power and resource utilization, allowing direct evaluation of FIPS/W. This makes it a strong example of balanced energy and resource efficiency for full RL training. The design also demonstrates how block floating-point quantization and structural simplification can reduce the resource footprint of AC pipelines while maintaining high training throughput. Compared with works that emphasize only speedup or throughput, TD3lite provides a clearer basis for evaluating whether the accelerator is efficient from both energy and hardware-cost perspectives.

The latency–energy trade-off should also be interpreted according to the target application. Many RL-based FPGA/IC systems are designed for real-time or energy-constrained environments, where the suitability of a design depends not only on its average throughput or reported speedup, but also on whether each decision, update, or training iteration can be completed within the required timing and power budget. Therefore, latency-related metrics should be interpreted together with reported power consumption and the type of accelerated RL workload. This is especially relevant for applications such as robotics, networking, autonomous control, hardware security, and adaptive power management, where both fast response and low energy consumption are required.

A further challenge is that the surveyed studies use different performance units and baseline platforms. Some works report speedup over CPU, others report speedup over GPU, while others report throughput, samples per second, inference time, power, or reward improvement. Since CPU and GPU baselines differ across studies, speedup-based comparisons should be interpreted qualitatively rather than as absolute efficiency rankings. Similarly, throughput-based comparisons are meaningful only when the accelerated workload is clearly specified, such as inference-only, training-only, replay-buffer acceleration, or full RL iteration.

In global, the comparative analysis indicates that existing RL-based FPGA/IC studies demonstrate clear improvements in speed, throughput, power efficiency, and resource utilization. However, because the literature reports heterogeneous workload granularities and baseline platforms, normalized metrics should be used to make comparisons more consistent and interpretable. Studies should report decision latency, training-update latency, throughput, measured board or chip power, energy per operation, resource utilization, and FIPS/W when full RL iterations are accelerated. They should also indicate whether the reported power corresponds to device-level, board-level, or full-system consumption. Such reporting would enable more accurate comparison of energy efficiency, resource efficiency, and latency–energy behavior across future RL-based FPGA and IC implementations.

## 4.1 RL as the Workload

## 4.1.1 RL-based Acceleration

RL-based acceleration focuses on mapping the computational primitives of RL—including value updates, neural-network inference, gradient computation, and memory operations—onto FPGA fabrics to exploit fine-grained parallelism and low-latency dataflow. Across the literature, three dominant architectural strategies emerge: (i) replay-centric acceleration, (ii) massively parallel environment execution, and (iii) application-specific compute pipelines. These approaches collectively aim to improve throughput, reduce latency, and enhance resource efficiency through pipelining, quantization, and heterogeneous SoC integration.

Early works primarily targeted heterogeneous CPU–GPU–FPGA frameworks, where the main objective was to offload critical bottlenecks such as experience replay and learning updates. In this context, replay-centric designs introduced parallel data structures and pipelined update mechanisms to accelerate memory-intensive operations. or example, Meng et al. [98] proposed a replay accelerator based on a parallel $K$ -ary sum-tree, achieving $21$ – $40\times$ speedup in replay operations, illustrating the significant impact of memory-centric optimization. Building on this idea, later work [94] extended the approach to system-level acceleration by modeling RL components—Actors, Learners, and Replay Managers—as a task graph for hardware/software co-partitioning. In parallel, environment-level parallelism has been explored as an orthogonal strategy, where Li et al. [84] demonstrated that scaling to thousands of parallel environment instances can significantly improve data-generation throughput and training efficiency.

A complementary direction focuses on integrating deep RL models directly into FPGA pipelines for embedded inference and training. These works highlight the importance of fixed-point arithmetic, deep pipelining, and heterogeneous execution. For instance, compact DQN accelerators have demonstrated real-time inference with low power consumption [140], while heterogeneous CPU–FPGA frameworks [86] show that partial offloading of neural inference already yields deterministic timing advantages. Additionally, lightweight RL models such as bandit-based approaches have been mapped to hardware pipelines [113], illustrating the flexibility of FPGA acceleration across varying algorithmic complexity.

As the field matured, research increasingly shifted toward application-specific RL accelerators, where hardware architectures are co-designed with target domains and system constraints. These designs emphasize tightly coupled compute pipelines, efficient on-chip memory usage, and real-time interaction with physical systems. Representative examples include RL- based resource-allocation engines [42], real-time control systems operating at microsecond scale [142], and policy-gradient accelerators for robotics applications [126]. More recent work has extended these ideas to multimodal RL systems integrating vision, language, and control pathways [130], further demonstrating the versatility of hardware-aware RL design.

Collectively, a clear evolution can be observed from isolated component-level optimizations toward fully integrated, domain-specific RL systems. Despite differences in application and algorithmic choice, common architectural patterns consistently emerge, including on-chip replay buffers, pipelined learning engines, high-parallelism environment execution, fixed-point computation, and dataflow-oriented neural-network implementations. These patterns form the foundation of modern hardware–RL co-design and highlight the convergence of architectural strategies across the literature.

## 4.1.2 Multi-agent RL–based Approaches

MARL extends RL to distributed FPGA architectures in which multiple learning agents operate concurrently across a single or multi-tile fabric. Such systems exploit the fine-grained parallelism and heterogeneous resources of FPGA devices to support cooperative, competitive, or decentralized policy learning at scale. From a hardware perspective, existing MARL accelerators can be broadly categorized according to three dominant bottlenecks: (i) control latency, (ii) inter-agent communication, and (iii) deep-network computation.

Early works primarily addressed the control-latency bottleneck, focusing on fast closed-loop response rather than deep learning throughput. For instance, swarm-based multi-agent extensions of QL have been implemented on FPGA to regulate communication systems, achieving significant reductions in convergence time through low-latency updates and deterministic execution [17]. Similarly, tabular MARL architectures leveraging centralized processing units and shared Q-tables demonstrated efficient resource utilization and sub-microjoule energy consumption, highlighting the suitability of lightweight multi-agent learning for real-time hardware deployment [16].

As MARL algorithms evolved toward centralized-training and decentralized-execution paradigms, inter-agent communication emerged as a dominant bottleneck. To address this challenge, recent designs introduced parallel AC pipelines combined with efficient on-chip interconnects to enable scalable coordination among agents. For example, hardware-accelerated multi-agent DDPG (MADDPG) frameworks [146] demonstrated significant improvements in training throughput by exploiting parallelism and lightweight communication mechanisms. This issue becomes even more pronounced in attention-based architectures, where centralized critics require dense information exchange, increasing memory pressure and data movement costs. Specialized accelerators incorporating attention modules and pipelined communication strategies have therefore been proposed to mitigate these overheads [145].

A third class of MARL accelerators targets the compute-bound regime, where deep neural-network training dominates execution time. In this context, sparse computation and model compression techniques have been employed to reduce computational complexity and improve efficiency. For instance, LearningGroup [152] integrates learnable weight grouping with on-chip sparse-data generation, enabling real-time compressed training and achieving substantial performance and energy-efficiency gains over dense implementations.

In light of these proposed solutions, a clear trend emerges in which MARL hardware design is driven by the dominant system bottleneck rather than the specific learning algorithm. This bottleneck-oriented perspective provides a unified view of the design space and clarifies how different architectural strategies—ranging from low-latency control pipelines to communication-efficient interconnects and compute-optimized sparse engines—address the scalability challenges of multi-agent systems. Nevertheless, current MARL accelerators still face important limitations. As the number of agents increases, communication overhead and memory requirements grow non-linearly, particularly in architectures with centralized critics or attention mechanisms. Furthermore, many existing designs rely on highly specialized pipelines tailored to specific algorithms, limiting portability across different MARL formulations. These challenges highlight the need for more flexible and scalable hardware abstractions to support next-generation multi-agent RL systems.

## 4.1.3 Multi-FPGA/IC Platforms for Scalable RL Execution

When RL is treated as the workload, multi-FPGA and multi-IC platforms are primarily designed to scale learning and inference beyond the capacity of a single device. In this setting, the main objective is to distribute RL computation across multiple interconnected chips in order to increase throughput, expand memory capacity, and support large numbers of parallel agents. Such systems therefore rely on efficient inter-device communication, synchronized updates, and effective workload-partitioning strategies to maintain scalability.

A representative example is the IBM Neural Computer used by Asseman et al. [4]. Unlike single-device accelerators, this system distributes the RL workload across 416 interconnected Zynq-7045 nodes organized in a high-bandwidth, low-latency 3D mesh. Each node implements the full fitness-evaluation pipeline in RTL, including the Atari 2600 environment, image preprocessing, and a quantized CNN, allowing 832 parallel agents to be evaluated simultaneously. This architecture exemplifies how distributed execution enables scalable, fine-grained parallelism, particularly for population-based RL methods such as deep neuroevolution, where large numbers of candidate policies must be evaluated independently. By avoiding centralized bottlenecks and leveraging mesh-based communication for synchronization, such systems achieve substantial improvements in evaluation throughput and training time.

Across these developments, multi-device RL acceleration highlights a fundamental trade-off between scalability and coordination overhead. While distributing computation enables significant throughput gains, it also introduces challenges in maintaining consistency, managing communication latency, and balancing workloads across devices. Despite these advances, multi-FPGA platforms for scalable RL execution still face important limitations, including complex cross-device synchronization, increasing communication overhead as the number of devices grows, and reliance on hand-tuned workload partitioning and fixed interconnect structures. These issues continue to limit portability and scalability, highlighting the need for more automated distribution strategies and flexible communication abstractions.

## 4.1.4 Energy-Efficient Acceleration of RL

A first line of research addresses RL as the computational workload itself and focuses on reducing the energy cost of executing learning and inference on FPGA. In this context, the main objective is not to optimize the hardware platform through RL, but rather to design hardware architectures that implement RL efficiently under strict power and resource constraints.

In this regard, a major research direction focuses on neuromorphic and spiking RL architectures, which reduce energy consumption through sparse, event-driven computation and local learning rules. These designs typically replace dense neural updates with spike-based communication and exploit temporal sparsity to minimize switching activity and memory traffic. For example, spiking implementations based on leaky integrate-and-fire neurons and STDP (STDP) have demonstrated significant gains in both convergence speed and energy efficiency [3]. Complementary designs explore low-bitwidth neuron–synapse representations and lightweight hardware platforms to sustain low-power operation even for non-trivial workloads [7]. Further optimizations include multiplier-less arithmetic, coarse quantization, and temporal hysteresis, enabling substantial reductions in energy consumption while maintaining learning capability [116]. In addition, biologically inspired reinforcement mechanisms based on reward-modulated STDP eliminate the need for replay buffers and centralized value-function computation, thereby reducing memory overhead and improving efficiency [112].

Beyond neuromorphic designs, a second direction focuses on architectural and algorithmic optimizations to reduce the energy cost of RL training and inference. Jo et al. [65] proposed an energy-efficient RL accelerator that incorporates a heterogeneous replay buffer to reuse past experiences, selective layer retraining to limit backpropagation costs, and a mixed-precision compute architecture that safely replaces a large portion of FP32 operations with FXP16 arithmetic. This combination led to up to 90% reduction in adaptation iterations and 12% reduction in computational workload, with more than 27% of RL operations executed in low precision without degrading the learned policy. In addition to flat policy architectures, several researchers explored structured RL formulations that inherently reduce exploration cost and thus energy consumption. Shiri et al. [129] introduced a hierarchical RL accelerator (E2HRL) in which complex behaviors are decomposed into multi-level subgoals, enabling agents to learn with far fewer state–action transitions. Their FPGA implementation on Artix-7 demonstrated 1.1–11.4 GOP/s/W energy efficiency, corresponding to 8.8 $\times$ –11 $\times$ improvements compared to GPU- based RL execution. Related work by the same authors extended the concept to multimodal RL by incorporating structured language instructions into the decision-making process [131]. The resulting configurable hardware architecture achieved a low-power operating mode below 139 mW while offering a high-throughput option reaching 217 FPS at approximately 1.2 mJ per decision.

Overall, these studies show that event-driven computation, lightweight arithmetic, hierarchical decomposition, and precision-aware design form the main architectural foundations for energy-efficient acceleration of RL on reconfigurable hardware. Nevertheless, current solutions remain constrained by task specificity, limited online learning flexibility, and memory/precision trade-offs that may restrict their generality for more complex RL workloads.

## 4.2 RL as the Optimizer

## 4.2.1 RL-Based Reconfigurable Hardware Approaches

Reconfigurable hardware has increasingly been coupled with RL to enable adaptive, resource-aware, and autonomously self-optimizing embedded systems. The literature in this domain broadly converges toward three main research directions: (i) RL-guided dynamic architectural reconfiguration on FPGA, (ii) RL-enhanced reconfigurable cyber–physical components, and (iii) RL-driven distributed embedded devices. Together, these directions illustrate how learning-based control can bridge hardware adaptability with real-time decision making in highly dynamic environments.

A significant line of work focuses on RL-driven dynamic reconfiguration of FPGA accelerators, where learning algorithms adapt hardware structure and operating parameters at runtime. These approaches typically combine partial reconfiguration, precision tuning, and workload-aware scheduling to match accelerator configurations to changing computational demands. For example, dynamically reconfigurable neural architectures have been explored in which evolutionary or RL- like feedback guides structural adaptation of FPGA-based networks [38]. Similarly, RL has been applied to optimize sparsity, pruning, and precision in neural accelerators, enabling hardware to autonomously adjust its performance–energy trade-off across workloads [107]. Other works extend this concept to runtime scheduling, where RL determines optimal switching strategies among reconfigurable modules to improve overall system efficiency [43]. These studies collectively demonstrate that RL- driven reconfiguration enables fine-grained, workload-aware adaptation of hardware resources, significantly improving flexibility compared to static accelerator designs.

Beyond FPGA compute fabrics, RL techniques are increasingly applied to reconfigurable cyber–physical components whose behavior evolves under environmental or operational variations. In these systems, RL serves as a real-time control mechanism to maintain performance under non-stationary conditions. For instance, RL- based controllers have been used to dynamically recalibrate analog circuits such as power amplifiers, compensating for temperature-induced drift and preserving system reliability [149]. Similarly, RL has been applied to optimize high-dimensional analog control parameters in reconfigurable radio frequency systems, where learning-based strategies effectively manage complex interference patterns [109]. In another domain, RL has been employed to regulate dynamic battery topologies in electric vehicles, enabling adaptive state-of-charge balancing across heterogeneous cells [6]. These works collectively highlight RL’s effectiveness in controlling physical systems characterized by nonlinear dynamics, uncertainty, and continuously varying operating conditions.

Another important research direction examines RL-driven reconfiguration in distributed embedded devices equipped with small FPGA fabrics. These systems typically operate under tight energy and resource constraints, requiring decentralized decision-making and adaptive workload management. For example, RL- based strategies have been used in self-aware sensor nodes to decide when to reconfigure hardware, execute tasks locally, or offload computation to neighboring nodes [13]. Extensions of this approach incorporate more complex edge scenarios, including job batching, dynamic offloading, and cooperative execution [12]. These approaches demonstrate how RL can effectively manage energy–performance trade-offs in distributed systems while enabling emergent cooperative behavior.

Across these developments, several unifying observations emerge. First, DPR consistently appears as a key mechanism enabling RL agents to adapt hardware structure with minimal runtime disruption. Second, model-free RL approaches are particularly effective in handling nonlinear, uncertain, and environment-dependent hardware behavior. Third, many systems directly incorporate hardware performance measurements—such as latency, interference levels, temperature drift, or voltage imbalance—into the reward function, making physical operating conditions an integral part of the learning process. Finally, scalability challenges are often addressed through architecture-aware strategies such as module clustering, coefficient partitioning, and hierarchical control. Despite these promising capabilities, several limitations remain. Existing frameworks often rely on simplified RL models with restricted state representations, limiting robustness and applicability to large and highly dynamic hardware design spaces. In addition, runtime reconfiguration overhead and training instability introduce practical constraints when deploying these methods in real FPGA systems. The absence of standardized evaluation benchmarks further hinders fair comparison and limits the generalizability of current solutions across different architectures and deployment scenarios.

## 4.2.2 RL-based Energy and Power Management

Another line of research treats RL not as the workload being accelerated, but as the optimizer used to regulate the energy behavior of FPGA-based systems. In this setting, the learned policy acts as a runtime controller that dynamically adapts operating modes, resource utilization, or voltage/frequency configurations in response to workload variations and platform conditions. These approaches are particularly attractive for managing complex and non-linear energy–performance trade-offs that are difficult to capture using static heuristics.

Existing works in this area primarily focus on dynamic power management and adaptive resource control. For example, early hardware implementations of RL controllers demonstrated the feasibility of learning-based power management, where temporal-difference learning was used to select optimal operating policies based on observed workload patterns [110]. More recent approaches extend this concept by incorporating system-level awareness and advanced training strategies. In particular, digital-twin–assisted frameworks enable RL agents to be trained in high-speed virtual environments, reducing the need for costly on-device exploration while preserving adaptability to real-world conditions [78]. Such approaches allow runtime controllers to efficiently optimize DVFS policies, core selection, and workload distribution across heterogeneous FPGA platforms.

A common pattern across these approaches is, RL- based energy management highlights a shift from static and heuristic-based control toward adaptive, data-driven optimization. By continuously interacting with the system, RL agents can capture complex dependencies between workload characteristics and hardware behavior, enabling more responsive and efficient power management strategies. Despite these advantages, several challenges remain. Current approaches are often sensitive to reward design and rely on limited state representations, which can restrict generalization across workloads and platforms. In addition, ensuring stable and predictable runtime behavior remains difficult, particularly in safety-critical or latency-sensitive applications. These limitations highlight the need for more robust training methodologies, transferable policy representations, and tighter integration between RL algorithms and hardware monitoring infrastructures.

## 4.2.3 RL in Multi-FPGA/IC Infrastructures

When RL is treated as the optimizer, the objective shifts from accelerating learning to improving the behavior of large multi-FPGA or multi-IC infrastructures. In this setting, the hardware platform itself forms the environment, while the RL agent learns policies that enhance communication efficiency, reduce congestion, and shorten backend design cycles. Such approaches are particularly relevant for large-scale systems where traditional heuristics struggle to capture complex interactions within dense interconnect fabrics.

Existing work in this area primarily focuses on communication-aware optimization and design-flow acceleration. For example, RL-based routing frameworks have been proposed to dynamically select congestion-aware paths in multi-FPGA interconnects, improving both latency and routing efficiency [35]. These approaches leverage online reward feedback to adapt routing decisions to varying traffic patterns and resource constraints. Reported results indicate that such learning-based methods can significantly reduce routing time and alleviate communication bottlenecks, leading to measurable improvements in overall backend design cycles.

A key observation from these studies is that RL enables adaptive, data-driven optimization of system-level behavior, replacing static heuristics with policies that can respond to workload and topology variations. This is particularly important in multi-device environments, where the interaction between communication, placement, and routing becomes increasingly complex as system scale grows. Despite these promising results, RL-based optimization for multi-FPGA/IC platforms remains constrained by several challenges. These include the difficulty of designing effective reward functions, the large state and action spaces induced by complex interconnect topologies, and the limited generalizability of learned policies across different platforms and workloads. Addressing these challenges will require more scalable learning formulations and transferable optimization frameworks for next-generation reconfigurable systems.

## 4.2.4 RL-based System and Design Optimization

RL has emerged as a powerful paradigm for optimizing FPGA systems in domains traditionally dominated by heuristics. Rather than accelerating RL or focusing solely on energy efficiency, these approaches use RL to improve computer-aided design QoR (QoR), including placement, routing, scheduling, compiler optimization, and system-level runtime control. In this context, the FPGA architecture itself becomes part of the environment, enabling agents to learn policies that navigate complex structural constraints and design trade-offs.

Existing work in this area can be broadly categorized into four main directions: (i) placement optimization, (ii) scheduling and runtime control, (iii) compiler and design-space exploration, and (iv) system-level optimization.

Placement optimization represents one of the earliest and most extensively studied applications of RL. These approaches replace manually tuned heuristics such as simulated annealing with adaptive policies that guide placement decisions. For example, RL- based placement strategies have demonstrated significant runtime reductions while maintaining or improving QoR [103]. Subsequent work incorporated timing and wirelength awareness into the learning process, further improving placement efficiency and solution quality [33]. More recent approaches integrate graph neural networks into the RL loop to better capture structural dependencies within FPGA netlists, enabling improved placement quality and faster convergence [153]. These works collectively highlight the effectiveness of RL in navigating the large combinatorial design space inherent to placement problems.

Beyond placement, another important direction focuses on scheduling and runtime optimization. In these approaches, RL agents learn to manage execution order, resource allocation, and system timing under dynamic constraints. For instance, RL-based schedulers have been used to optimize real-time digital simulation by modeling hardware constraints such as memory usage and communication availability within the learning environment [45]. Complementary work has explored architectural support for stable RL execution, including mechanisms such as Q-table memoization and reuse to improve convergence and runtime behavior [93]. These studies demonstrate how RL can adapt scheduling policies to complex and time-varying hardware conditions.

A third research direction addresses compiler and design-space optimization. Here, RL is used to explore large combinatorial spaces such as compiler pass ordering and hardware–software co-design configurations. For example, RL-based methods have been applied to select optimal LLVM/HLS compiler pass sequences, significantly improving performance compared to static optimization levels [125]. Similarly, hardware–software co-exploration frameworks leverage RL to jointly optimize neural architectures and accelerator parameters, enabling improved accuracy and reduced runtime [91]. These approaches highlight the strength of RL in guiding complex multi-dimensional optimization problems. Recent work extends RL to FPGA logic synthesis by formulating optimization command selection as a design-space exploration problem. An archive-driven approach combined with PPO enables efficient exploration of synthesis flows, leading to improved mapping quality in terms of LUT reduction while satisfying timing constraints [167].

Finally, system-level optimization focuses on using RL to dynamically control system behavior during runtime. In these cases, the agent monitors hardware performance metrics and adapts system policies accordingly. For example, RL-based runtimes have been used to dynamically select coherence modes in heterogeneous SoC, improving performance and reducing memory traffic without modifying accelerator architectures [168]. These approaches illustrate how RL can optimize system-level behavior beyond individual hardware modules.

Across these developments, a consistent theme is that RL enables data-driven exploration of complex design spaces that are difficult to address using traditional heuristics. By learning from interaction with the system, RL agents can adapt to workload variations and structural constraints, leading to improved efficiency and design quality. Nevertheless, RL-based optimization on FPGA/IC platforms remains constrained by several challenges, including training overhead, limited scalability to large state spaces, sensitivity to reward design, and restricted generalization under dynamic workloads. Addressing these limitations will require more scalable learning techniques and tighter integration between RL models and hardware-aware design frameworks.

## 4.2.5 RL-based High-Level and Logic Synthesis

RL- assisted synthesis has emerged as a significant research direction spanning logic optimization, FPGA/ASIC technology mapping, and HLS directive tuning (Figure 6). In contrast to traditional synthesis flows based on fixed heuristic scripts, these approaches use RL agents to explore large design spaces and automatically derive optimization strategies tailored to specific circuits and workloads.

Existing work in this area can be broadly categorized into three main directions: (i) logic-level optimization and technology mapping, (ii) HLS directive and compiler optimization, and (iii) hybrid design-space exploration frameworks.

Logic-level optimization and mapping represents one of the earliest applications of RL in synthesis. These approaches replace manually crafted optimization sequences with learned policies that adapt to circuit characteristics. For example, RL- based methods have demonstrated the ability to select circuit-specific logic-optimization sequences that reduce LUT utilization and outperform traditional heuristic flows [165]. More recent frameworks extend this concept by unifying logic optimization and technology mapping within a single RL- driven environment, enabling flexible synthesis strategies across both FPGA and ASIC targets [111]. These works highlight the effectiveness of RL in navigating the highly combinatorial optimization space of logic synthesis.

A second major direction focuses on HLS optimization, where RL agents are used to automatically tune compiler directives, pragmas, and transformation strategies. Systems such as AutoAnnotate [123] and AnnotationGym [124] leverage RL to generate optimized HLS configurations for Vitis and Intel toolchains, achieving significant performance improvements over baseline and manually tuned designs. Further enhancements include improved policy-agent models for directive selection and ordering, which increase optimization stability and consistency across workloads [102]. These approaches demonstrate how RL can effectively replace manual tuning in complex HLS workflows.

A third line of research explores hybrid learning frameworks for multi-objective design-space exploration. In these systems, RL is combined with predictive models to guide exploration more efficiently. For instance, RL agents integrated with graph neural network predictors can generate Pareto-optimal trade-offs among latency, resource utilization, and timing during HLS compilation [147]. Similarly, RL has been applied to fine-grained hardware parameter tuning, such as bit-width optimization in FPGA accelerators, enabling automated balancing of accuracy, performance, and resource consumption [61]. These approaches illustrate the growing role of RL in managing complex, multi-dimensional optimization problems in synthesis flows.

In light of these proposed approaches, a clear trend emerges in which RL shifts synthesis from static, heuristic-driven processes toward adaptive, data-driven optimization. By learning directly from design feedback, RL agents can tailor optimization strategies to specific circuits, toolchains, and performance objectives, improving both efficiency and solution quality across the FPGA and IC design stack. Despite these advances, RL- based synthesis still faces notable limitations. Training remains computationally expensive, as agents must explore vast design spaces with limited sample efficiency. Generalization across circuits, workloads, and toolchains is often weak, requiring frequent retraining. Moreover, RL policies can be opaque and unstable, posing challenges for reliability, verification, and integration into industrial electronic design automation workflows.

## 5.1 Networking

RL has emerged as a powerful paradigm for optimizing decision-making across modern networking systems, supporting tasks such as traffic management, routing, interference mitigation, and network security. When combined with FPGA acceleration, RL gains substantial benefits—including fine-grained parallelism, reduced latency, and energy-efficient real-time operation—which are essential for coping with dynamic and resource-constrained communication environments. As networking demands continue to intensify, RL-based FPGA solutions offer scalable and high-performance architectures capable of adapting online to rapidly changing conditions.

To illustrate these advantages, several works explored physical-layer and spectrum-level optimization, where real-time constraints are particularly stringent. For instance, persistent spectrum scarcity in 5G/B5G networks drives the need for advanced spectral-efficiency techniques such as DSA (DSA) and flexible duplexing. The study in [76] addressed this challenge by introducing an RL-driven flexible-duplex system operating in sensor-assisted mode, demonstrating that deploying RL on real hardware could meet strict timing thresholds required for agile duplex switching. Building on the theme of network operation under uncertainty, other works investigated RL-enabled control for highly disrupted or delay-tolerant settings. The study in [83] examined routing in space networks characterized by long propagation delays, intermittent connectivity, and limited global state knowledge. The proposed cognitive controller used RL with a SNN implemented on FPGA to enable faster-than-real-time routing decisions, showing how neural-based RL hardware can sustain adaptability even in severely challenged environments. A related direction focused on channel-level compensation and waveform adaptation, where the environment is highly dynamic and often unpredictable. The work in [32] introduced Chares, a TD3-based real-time waveform synthesis system designed to counteract channel distortions and recover DL (DL)-based wireless signal classification accuracy. By generating channel-specific FIR filters, the RL-enabled FPGA system improved accuracy by up to 4.1 $\times$ under unseen conditions. While the approach offers microsecond-level reaction time and energy-efficient inference, it introduces additional complexity and sensitivity to noise dynamics, highlighting the practical challenges of deploying continuous-control RL on hardware.

In parallel, several works explored RL-accelerated routing for autonomous or large-scale network infrastructures. In dynamic LEO satellite networks, [75] developed an FPGA-parallelized CNN architecture enabling real-time Dueling-DQN routing. The CPU–FPGA co-design achieved substantial acceleration without degrading routing accuracy, demonstrating the viability of hardware-assisted RL for onboard satellite decision making where deterministic latency is crucial. In the following, at the architectural-level of chip interconnects, the work in [60] employed a QL-based MARL strategy for resilient routing in NoC environments. Each router maintained a two-level Q-table and autonomously learned forwarding decisions under faulty links or nodes. Implementing this scheme on an FPGA enabled low-latency routing, high fault tolerance, and efficient hardware utilization, showcasing how distributed RL agents can deliver scalable and self-optimizing network-on-chip behavior.

From the perspective of the proposed taxonomy, networking applications span both roles of RL. Latency-critical tasks such as routing, spectrum access, and real-time waveform adaptation predominantly position RL as the workload, requiring FPGA/IC acceleration to meet strict timing constraints. In contrast, higher-level network management and optimization problems—such as congestion control or adaptive routing policies in large-scale networks—lean toward RL as the optimizer, where the primary challenge lies in handling large and dynamic state/action spaces.

## 5.2 Hardware and embedded security

The design and production process of modern IC are often delegated to external (untrusted) parties. This outsourcing in the supply chain leads to hardware security issues, including risks of sensitive data leakage, reduced performance, and potential copyright violations. At every stage of the IC supply chain, malicious alterations, commonly known as HT attacks, can be introduced. HT detection methods are divided into two main types: SCA (SCA), which identifies physical alterations and parameter changes, and logic testing, which activates concealed triggers by using a variety of test patterns. For example,
AdaTest [19] is an automated, adaptive test pattern generation framework for HT detection, using RL in hardware accelerator design. It operates in two phases: circuit profiling, which identifies rare nodes using transition probability and SCOAP (SCOAP) testability measures, and adaptive test pattern generation, which employs a reward function to select test inputs. AdaTest effectively balances trigger coverage and test generation time through iterative testing. As illustrated in Figure 8, AdaTest is utilized to verify whether any HT are embedded in the CUT (CUT). It takes the netlist of the CUT and user-defined parameters as inputs, subsequently generating a set of test vectors with high reward values as output.

Figure 8: Operational flow of AdaTest, an RL-based hardware Trojan (HT) detection framework [19].
Adaptive test patterns generated by the agent are applied to the CUT, and the resulting responses are compared with expected outputs: mismatches indicate a Trojaned circuit, while matching responses classify the design as Benign.

A related approach, [53] utilizes an end-to-end iris recognition system integrating approximate computing with an FPGA-based hardware/software pipeline. It employs RL with a RNN (RNN) agent to optimize eight approximation knobs in the design. The FPGA accelerates critical tasks, providing a 48 $\times$ runtime reduction while meeting accuracy standards. Security is enhanced by processing sensitive biometric data locally, reducing exposure to threats.

The paper in [101] proposes RL-TPG, a RL–based framework for automated pre-silicon security verification on SoC hardware at the RTL. Using an RL agent, it generates intelligent test patterns to identify vulnerabilities in hardware modules such as advanced encryption standard (AES), Rivest–Shamir–Adleman (RSA), and RS232 cores. By combining static analysis, dynamic mutation, and multiple coverage metrics, RL-TPG achieves about 90% verification coverage. In [59], a brain-inspired RL framework based on hyperdimensional computing was proposed for efficient intrusion detection. The approach combined double QL with HDC encoding, where states and actions were represented as high-dimensional hypervectors to enable fast, noise-tolerant learning and memory-like generalization. Each agent’s state vector $s_{i}$ was projected into high-dimensional space using the base hypervector matrix $B_{i}$ and its corresponding feature coefficients $\mathbf{d_{i}}$ , which define the weighted contribution of each state feature. The encoded hypervector $\mathbf{H_{s}}=\sum_{i}\mathbf{d_{i}}B_{i}$ was then processed through kernel transformations $e^{jx}=\cos(x)+j\sin(x)$ and a systolic array to achieve rapid and energy-efficient Q-value computation.

In this domain, RL is primarily positioned as the optimizer, where it is used to detect vulnerabilities, guide security policies, or adapt system behavior under adversarial conditions. These problems often involve complex and high-dimensional state spaces rather than strict real-time constraints. However, in certain cases such as real-time intrusion detection or side-channel analysis, aspects of RL as the workload emerge, requiring accelerated inference for timely threat mitigation.

## 5.3 Robotics

In industrial applications, robotic controllers necessitate a low-latency computation process to meet stringent real-time constraints. This ensures prompt response to dynamic environments, allowing for precise manipulation and coordination of robotic tasks. The effectiveness of such systems hinges on their ability to process data rapidly and efficiently under varying operational conditions [69]. For example, the proposed architecture in [57] is tailored for hardware implementation in multilevel behavioral robots, allowing them to autonomously perform intricate tasks such as goal-seeking, cruising, and ensuring safety. It consists of modules with similar structures, primitive actions, and compositional behaviors, all stemming from a basic template. Each module is designed to learn and collaborate in managing diverse tasks, utilizing a RL approach based on an ARM (ARM) with a AC model. This architecture is realized on a FPGA chip that includes a CPU core, which enables efficient parallel processing of neural networks. Tested on a mobile robot, the system effectively executes these tasks in unstructured environments, adeptly navigating obstacles and challenging scenarios.

Although FPGA are more power-efficient than CPU and GPU, typical RL methods are unsuitable for FPGA implementation because FPGA are composed of many logic blocks optimized for high-speed logical operations but not for real-number operations. To address this limitation, the scheme [67] introduces a BPN, a novel RL algorithm that leverages binarized CNN for robot control from raw images. The learning system involves three main steps: (i) FPGA and CPU collect datasets for learning by controlling the robot, (ii) a GPU server updates the BPN network parameters based on collected data, and (iii) the FPGA updates the binarized network parameters for real-time control. This architecture effectively enables high-speed control on the FPGA, despite its real-number calculation limitations.

The research in [49] recognizes that training robots directly through RL can cause physical damage and be time-consuming. Although training robots in a simulated environment reduces costs and improves efficiency, the policy learning process still remains time-consuming. For this reason, the authors proposed an FPGA-based hardware implementation of the DDPG algorithm to accelerate training. The FPGA, specifically the Intel Stratix-V on the Maxeler MAX4 platform, handles gradient calculations and network parameter updates, allowing for faster and safer policy learning. This RL approach enables training in continuous action spaces, providing more precise control for a 3D-printed robotic arm. The best result achieved a speedup of 4.07 $\times$ for model one. However, the method is limited by I/O bottlenecks between the CPU and FPGA, which restrict potential speed gains. Similarly, [36] addresses the challenges of implementing QL onboard planetary rovers and spacecraft, which face constraints like limited processing power and radiation-hardening requirements. It proposes an FPGA-based QL implementation with ANN to overcome these issues, leveraging FPGA’s fine-grain parallelism to significantly reduce processing time. Using a Xilinx Virtex 7 FPGA, the study demonstrates both single-neuron and MLP QL accelerators, achieving up to a 43-fold speedup compared to a conventional Intel i5 2.3 GHz CPU. The architecture’s performance and power consumption are further evaluated using the Symphony simulator and Xilinx compiler. However, the method’s limitations include high power consumption, lack of pipelining for efficiency, and limited real-world testing on robot platforms.

Robotics applications typically lie closer to RL as the workload, as they demand low-latency decision-making for control and interaction with physical environments. FPGA/IC acceleration is therefore critical for enabling real-time inference and, in some cases, on-device training. Nevertheless, tasks involving high-level planning or policy learning in complex environments also reflect characteristics of RL as the optimizer, particularly when large state and action spaces are involved.

## 5.4 Autonomous vehicles

Vehicles utilizing RL on FPGA can encompass both self-driving cars and UAV, offering real-time decision-making with efficient processing and enhanced autonomy. Self-driving vehicles are becoming a global trend, with manufacturers like Tesla investing heavily in autonomous systems that rely on real-time sensing, information fusion, and decision-making. DNN are widely used for environmental sensing and control due to their success in other fields. As autonomous driving becomes commercialized, the need for powerful computing platforms to support tasks like environment perception, motion control, and communication within the Internet of vehicles grows.

To address these computational demands, [164] investigated the optimal allocation of AI tasks in autonomous vehicles equipped with heterogeneous processing units, including CPU, GPU, and FPGA. Their work proposed a PPO-based RL algorithm to determine efficient device placement for neural network operations, as illustrated in Figure 9. A sequence-to-sequence model was used for task assignment, rewarding configurations that minimized execution time and energy consumption. Experiments showed that this approach surpassed state-of-the-art RL and traditional heuristics across several benchmarks. Building on the theme of real-time autonomous control, [87] shifted the focus from ground vehicles to aerial platforms, employing an AC framework for UAV trajectory tracking through predefined waypoints. Here, the FPGA accelerated the control pipeline, delivering a throughput of 204.96 actions per second at only 33.57 $\mu$ W of power. This hardware–accelerated RL solution achieved notable gains, including a 58.14% reduction in tracking error, 21.77% lower power consumption, and a 9.23% faster response time compared to baseline PID (PID)-based controllers. Nevertheless, the study also highlighted practical challenges such as FPGA resource limitations requiring time-multiplexing—potentially introducing latency—and substantial training requirements for the RL model. Extending RL-driven autonomy beyond navigation and toward energy optimization, [128] explored independent QL (IQL), a multi-agent RL technique, for energy management in multi-stack fuel cell hybrid vehicles. In this setting, each fuel cell operated as an autonomous agent balancing hydrogen consumption and battery condition. Their results demonstrated improved fault tolerance, real-time adaptability, and enhanced fuel economy, validated through hardware-in-the-loop experiments. This contribution further illustrates how RL—combined with hardware acceleration—can support increasingly complex autonomous vehicle functions.

Figure 9: RL- based Internet of vehicles model. The vehicle primarily consisting of a CPU, GPU, and FPGA placement model, is trained on a cloud server with the objective of reducing both execution latency and energy consumption using RL [164].

Autonomous driving systems strongly emphasize RL as the workload, due to stringent real-time and safety requirements that necessitate fast and deterministic inference on hardware platforms. At the same time, elements such as trajectory planning and multi-objective decision-making introduce optimization challenges that align with RL as the optimizer, especially when dealing with complex environmental dynamics and large-scale state representations.

## 5.5 Intelligent sensing and imaging systems

Intelligent sensing and imaging systems combine advanced sensors with AI-driven data processing to perceive, interpret, and reconstruct complex environments in real time. By integrating RL, signal processing, and adaptive decision-making, these systems enhance accuracy, efficiency, and automation across applications such as industrial inspection, medical diagnostics, and autonomous systems.

Within adaptive imaging, Cao et al. [15] advanced electromagnetic tomography (EMT) through a deep RL-driven feature–selection strategy that identifies the most informative measurements for reconstruction. Their AMEMT-Net architecture incorporates historical actions and partial reconstructions to guide acquisition, cutting down redundant sampling while improving reconstruction fidelity. Both simulation and FPGA-based evaluation confirmed strong robustness to noise and reliable generalization from synthetic to real-world imaging conditions, illustrating the potential of RL-adaptive sensing for high-efficiency imaging pipelines. Complementing these algorithmic advances, Sun et al. [134] addressed the challenge of deploying RL in resource-constrained wireless IoT nodes by designing lightweight hardware accelerators for postdecision state (PDS) learning. Their solution combines an arithmetic action-evaluation engine with a stochastic-computing architecture that replaces multipliers with bitwise operations, thereby reducing latency, area, and power consumption. By leveraging structured parallelism and compact state encoding, the proposed accelerator achieved substantially faster convergence and improved efficiency in delay-sensitive IoT scenarios when benchmarked against QL and traditional baselines. This line of work highlights how energy-efficient hardware co-design can make embedded RL feasible at the edge. Further downstream in precision instrumentation, Wei et al. [144] demonstrated how an RL-enabled controller can enhance scanning probe microscopy (SPM) through dynamic tuning of P–I gains. Their PICTS platform, implemented as a parallel FPGA–CPU architecture, used a SAC agent to stabilize probe motion and improve image quality on difficult samples. The system achieved reductions in mean deflection error of nearly 90% and in variance of about 88% relative to a commercial fixed-gain controller. Nonetheless, evaluation was limited to a single SPM platform with a narrow sample set and partially relied on off-board training, leaving room for broader experimental validation and comparison to advanced nonlinear or model-predictive controllers.

Applications in sensing and imaging exhibit a mixed positioning along the spectrum. Real-time perception and signal processing tasks typically require RL as the workload, benefiting from hardware acceleration to meet latency and energy constraints. Conversely, system-level adaptation, feature selection, or reconstruction strategies often place RL in the role of optimizer, where the complexity of the problem dominates over strict timing requirements.

## 5.6 Other applications

The scope of RL-based FPGA/IC acceleration has likewise broadened beyond the applications described earlier. For example, the work in [122] proposed a safe DQN–based finite-set direct torque control for a permanent magnet synchronous motor, combining an edge-computing training pipeline with FPGA-based DQN realized as an MLP inference for real-time torque control. A safeguarding layer uses online recursive least squares identification to block unsafe switching actions, and the best reported agent achieved fast torque step responses of about 5 ms while respecting current and voltage limits, with training converging in under 10 minutes on the MicroLabBox FPGA. Yet, single motor/test bench, performance still below state-of-the-art model predictive control, high hardware/implementation complexity, and untested generalization to other drives or grid conditions. In a related effort, [80] proposed EdgeAlign, a compact dueling DQN-based DNA sequence aligner tailored for embedded edge devices (Jetson Nano and Arty A7-100T FPGA). Sequences are encoded as images and processed with a CNN-based agent using BLAST score–driven rewards and a sliding-window scheme. AutoKeras compresses the model from $\sim$ 1.36M to $\sim$ 98k parameters, achieving 11.79% mean BLAST-score error and 2.32ms/action on Jetson, and 1s/action on FPGA at 100MHz. Nevertheless, the study was conducted only on influenza data, with non-optimal error, relatively slow FPGA inference, and a fixed window size. In addition [22] presented ResDDQN–FPGA, a DQN with residual connections deployed on an FPGA to control a full-bridge LLC circuit resonant DC–DC converter. The framework parallelizes the action–value function (Q) and target networks and partitions inputs to accelerate inference, improving dynamic response, steady-state accuracy and efficiency compared with sliding-mode control and model predictive control under input- and load-voltage variations. Experiments showed the fastest voltage recovery and lower energy consumption among the tested methods. However, some limitations noticed, including single converter platform, no detailed FPGA resource/power analysis, and limited robustness and aging evaluation.

This category encompasses diverse use cases with varying characteristics across the spectrum. Control-oriented tasks such as motor control or power electronics generally align with RL as the workload, due to their real-time constraints. In contrast, applications such as design-space exploration or sequence optimization tend toward RL as the optimizer, where scalability and decision complexity are the primary challenges.

## 5.7 Case Study: FPGA-Based RL for Smart EV Charging

RL has recently emerged as a promising paradigm for intelligent EV charging management, particularly when real-time responsiveness and energy efficiency are required. Among the existing works, the FPGA-based RL architecture proposed in [27] represents a key reference implementation, as it demonstrates how tabular QL can be fully realized in hardware while meeting stringent embedded-system constraints.

To contextualize the contribution of this study, Figure 10 (a) presents the complete hardware
architecture of the EV charging management system, organized as a modular and fully hardware-integrated control loop. Charger-level sensing inputs, including activity status, priority requests, and deployed current, provide the system perception and are processed by the state discretizer. The resulting discrete state is supplied to the RL engine, which maintains and updates the learned knowledge. Action selection is carried out by the policy engine, while the action generator
translates the selected decision into charger-level control signals. In parallel, system performance is assessed by the reward generator, which closes the learning loop.
Each of these components is integral to the overall operation and is discussed in detail in the following subsections, where their individual roles and hardware realizations are
analyzed. Building upon the system overview, Figure 10 (b) details the internal structure of the QL engine. The Q-table is implemented as an array of dual-port Action RAMs, enabling concurrent read and write operations. A dedicated MAX unit and a hardware Q-updater implement the learning rule using fixed-point arithmetic, ensuring deterministic timing and low power consumption. Action selection is performed by a dedicated policy engine, shown in Figure 10 (c), which implements an $\epsilon$ -greedy strategy entirely in hardware. A linear feedback shift register (LFSR) is employed to generate pseudo-random values, where the most significant bits (MSBs) are used to select candidate actions, while the least significant bits (LSBs) are compared against the exploration parameter to decide between exploration and exploitation, enabling efficient stochastic decision making with negligible
hardware overhead. To enable tabular learning, continuous sensor data are mapped into a discrete state space using a base-7 encoding scheme, as illustrated in Figure 10 (d). Each charger condition is converted into a digit, and weighted LUTs generate a unique global state index. The action generator, depicted in Figure 10 (e), performs the inverse operation by decoding the selected action using base-3 look-up tables. This module translates abstract RL decisions into concrete charger current levels while enforcing safety constraints. Finally, the reward generator shown in Figure 10 (f) evaluates system performance by assigning partial rewards to each charger state and aggregating them into a cumulative reward. Overcurrent conditions are explicitly penalized, ensuring safe and stable operation.

Beyond its architectural relevance, this study provides quantitative evidence of the practical viability of RL-based FPGA solutions for grid-edge control. The proposed system was implemented on an AMD ZCU104 platform (Zynq UltraScale+ XCZU7EV) and evaluated using a three-charger configuration, resulting in a discrete state space of 343 states. Fixed-point representations were employed, with Q-values encoded on 16 bits, states on 9 bits, and actions on 5 bits, enabling efficient hardware realization. Operating at a clock frequency of 84.7 MHz, the design achieves deterministic real-time behavior with a decision latency of approximately 34.3 ns. Experimental results report stable learning convergence, with an average reward of about 60, while hardware utilization remains limited, requiring less than 2.3% of LUTs and BRAMs and below 0.2% of DSP resources. These results position this work as a representative reference for embedded, energy-efficient
RL deployment in smart EV charging infrastructures.

The smart EV charging case study primarily reflects RL as the optimizer, as the goal is to learn optimal charging policies under dynamic constraints such as energy demand, pricing, and grid conditions. The problem is characterized by a relatively large state space and multi-objective optimization. However, when deployed on FPGA platforms, aspects of RL as the workload also arise, particularly when real-time decision-making and energy-efficient inference are required.

## 6 Challenges and Future directions

Despite the demonstrated benefits of RL-based FPGA and IC implementations, several challenges continue to limit their scalability, flexibility, and deployment maturity. These challenges stem from the mismatch between RL algorithmic demands and hardware constraints. To maintain consistency with the taxonomy introduced in Section 4, the challenges and future directions are organized according to the two principal roles of RL: as the workload and as the optimizer. When RL is treated as the workload, challenges primarily arise from hardware constraints and the complexity of efficiently mapping learning algorithms onto FPGA/IC platforms, whereas when RL is used as an optimizer, challenges are mainly related to learning stability, system-level integration, and the complexity of applying RL to dynamic hardware environments. This section summarizes the key open challenges and outlines future research directions toward more scalable, robust, and reusable RL-driven hardware systems.

## 6.1 Limitations

Despite the significant progress achieved by RL-based FPGA and IC accelerators in terms of latency, energy efficiency, and real-time capability, several fundamental limitations remain that hinder their broader adoption and long-term scalability. These limitations span algorithmic, architectural, system-level, and methodological dimensions and are consistently observed across the surveyed works.

## 6.1.1 RL as the Workload

• 

Scalability and generalization:
With respect to state and action spaces, scalability remains a primary limitation, particularly for tabular methods such as QL and SARSA. While these approaches map efficiently to on-chip memories, the quadratic growth of the Q-table with increasing state–action cardinality rapidly exhausts BRAM or SRAM resources. Even with aggressive quantization or compression, most implementations remain restricted to small or moderately sized environments, limiting applicability to complex real-world systems. Moreover, most implementations are validated on synthetic benchmarks or small-scale environments, with limited evidence of robustness under non-stationary workloads, hardware aging, or environmental uncertainty. This raises questions about the deployment readiness of current RL-based FPGA/IC systems in safety-critical or industrial contexts.

• 

On-device training and memory bandwidth constraints:
On-device training remains challenging for deep RL algorithms such as DQN, PPO, and AC variants. Although inference pipelines are well supported on FPGA fabrics, full training requires backpropagation, gradient accumulation, and frequent parameter updates, which introduce substantial computational and memory overhead. In addition, replay buffers, rollout memories, and gradient storage often exceed on-chip capacity, forcing reliance on external DDR memory. This dependency increases latency and power consumption and diminishes the theoretical energy-efficiency benefits of hardware acceleration.

• 

Exploration, numerical precision, and learning stability:
Efficient hardware realization of exploration strategies and learning updates remains challenging due to tight constraints on randomness generation, numerical precision, and control complexity. Techniques such as $\epsilon$ -greedy exploration, entropy regularization, and stochastic noise injection must balance exploration effectiveness with determinism and implementation simplicity. Hardware-friendly pseudo-random number generators may introduce bias, while more sophisticated exploration schemes increase resource usage and control overhead. In parallel, reduced-precision fixed-point arithmetic is commonly employed to improve efficiency and lower power consumption; however, aggressive quantization can distort value estimates, gradients, or policy distributions. These combined effects may degrade convergence, induce oscillatory behavior, or compromise policy stability, particularly in AC and policy-gradient methods.

• 

Algorithm–hardware co-design and portability limitations:
Algorithm–hardware co-design in RL-based FPGA and IC systems remains highly specialized and lacks portability. Many accelerators are tightly optimized for a specific RL algorithm, network topology, or application scenario, which limits reuse across different tasks. Adapting an existing architecture to alternative RL formulations often requires substantial redesign, in contrast to the flexibility offered by software-based RL frameworks.

• 

Limited IC/SoC support for RL: RL implementations on IC/SoC platforms remain relatively limited compared to the extensive work on FPGA. Unlike flexible and reconfigurable FPGA solutions, IC impose stricter constraints such as fixed architectures, tight power budgets, longer development cycles, and complex requirements including power gating, clock-tree synthesis, and memory hierarchy optimization. These limitations make algorithm–hardware co-design more challenging and less adaptable after fabrication. This gap highlights an important direction for future research, particularly in developing energy-efficient RL accelerators, scalable on-chip learning mechanisms, and standardized design methodologies for integrating RL into next-generation IC platforms.

## 6.1.2 RL as the Optimizer

• 

Reconfiguration overhead and evaluation methodology:
RL-driven DPR introduces non-negligible latency and energy overheads, which can offset performance gains if reconfiguration is frequent. Unstable or poorly trained policies may further trigger oscillatory reconfiguration behavior, raising concerns about system reliability and safety. In parallel, evaluation methodologies remain fragmented, with inconsistent reporting of performance, power, and area metrics. The absence of standardized benchmarks and evaluation protocols complicates fair comparison and obscures true design trade-offs.

• 

Toolchain complexity and design productivity:
Developing RL-based accelerators on FPGA and IC platforms remains a complex and labor-intensive process, requiring expertise across multiple abstraction levels, from RL algorithm design and numerical optimization to HLS directives, RTL implementation, and hardware verification. While HLS tools improve productivity, they offer limited native support for RL-specific constructs such as experience replay, stochastic exploration, gradient synchronization, and dynamic policy updates. Consequently, designers often rely on manual optimizations and iterative design-space exploration to achieve acceptable performance, leading to long development cycles and reduced accessibility for non-hardware experts. This complexity hinders rapid prototyping, limits reproducibility, and slows the translation of emerging RL algorithms into deployable hardware systems.

• 

Security and adversarial robustness:
RL-based FPGA and IC systems are potentially vulnerable to adversarial perturbations, reward manipulation, or malicious observation injection, particularly in online or adaptive learning settings. Despite increasing attention to secure and trustworthy AI, security considerations remain largely unexplored in hardware-based RL implementations, limiting their suitability for deployment in mission-critical or safety-sensitive environments.

## 6.2 Future Directions

Addressing the identified challenges in RL-based FPGA and IC systems requires coordinated advances across algorithms, hardware architectures, system integration, and design methodologies. Future research should move beyond isolated accelerator prototypes toward holistic, reusable, and deployment-ready intelligent hardware platforms. Several promising research directions emerge from the surveyed literature, spanning scalability, learning efficiency, robustness, and application-driven deployment.

## 6.2.1 RL as the Workload

• 

Scalable and Generalizable RL Architectures on FPGA:
Future RL-based FPGA and IC designs must prioritize scalability to large state and action spaces while ensuring robust generalization across tasks, environments, and operating conditions. A promising yet largely unexplored direction is the hardware realization of advanced RL paradigms on FPGA, including hierarchical, hybrid, and multi-stage architectures. In such designs, coarse-grained decision-making may be handled by lightweight or tabular RL methods, while fine-grained and continuous control is delegated to deep RL models, enabling efficient decomposition of complex control problems. Transfer learning [79] in RL further represents a key enabler for scalable hardware intelligence, allowing pretrained representations, value functions, or policy components to be reused across platforms and applications, thereby significantly reducing training cost and convergence time [71]. Beyond classical formulations, emerging paradigms such as distributional RL, risk-sensitive RL, multi-objective RL, and hierarchical MARL offer richer decision criteria that better reflect real-world constraints and uncertainties. However, these paradigms remain largely uninvestigated in FPGA and IC implementations, presenting a fertile research opportunity for next-generation, scalable, and generalizable RL hardware systems.

• 

Efficient On-Device and Lifelong Learning:
Enabling efficient on-device and lifelong learning remains a pivotal research direction for RL-based FPGA systems. Future designs are expected to combine supervised, unsupervised, and self-supervised learning paradigms in a complementary manner to reduce sample complexity, stabilize learning signals, and enable continual adaptation under strict latency, memory, and power constraints. Importantly, several recent algorithmic advances expose structural properties that are inherently amenable to FPGA and custom IC realization, irrespective of the specific application domain. Supervised components, such as offline RL and imitation-style learning from logged trajectories, enable most optimization to be conducted off-chip, yielding compact and stable policy representations for on-device deployment. This paradigm naturally fits FPGA-based systems, where deterministic inference, fixed-point arithmetic, and limited fine-tuning are preferred. Unsupervised RL, in contrast, enables task-agnostic exploration and skill acquisition without reliance on external rewards, supporting long-term autonomy in nonstationary environments. Representative studies on unsupervised skill discovery and interaction-driven representation learning illustrate how intrinsic objectives, latent representations, and diversity measures can replace dense supervision and promote reusability of learned behaviors [54, 18]. Self-supervised learning further complements these paradigms by introducing structured predictive or reconstruction-based objectives that stabilize learning and reduce reliance on heuristic reward design. Recent works demonstrate that reconstruction-driven rewards and self-referencing mechanisms can mitigate reward nonstationarity and catastrophic forgetting, thereby improving sample efficiency and robustness during continual learning [1, 163]. From a hardware perspective, such objectives are particularly attractive as they rely on regular, repeatable computations over temporal windows and latent features, which align well with pipelined and memory-aware FPGA architectures. Finally, distributed operation motivates federated and multi-agent extensions in which multiple edge devices collaborate while preserving data locality and privacy. Recent decentralized RL formulations for adaptive communication and collaboration provide concrete examples of how local decision-making can be achieved with minimal overhead and without centralized coordination [141]. More broadly, these ideas suggest that future FPGA-based RL systems may jointly optimize learning, communication, and resource utilization in a decentralized manner, enabling scalable and energy-efficient lifelong learning across heterogeneous edge platforms.

• 

Memory-, Dataflow-, and Precision-Aware Learning Architectures:
As memory access increasingly dominates the energy and latency profile of deep RL workloads, future FPGA and IC accelerators should adopt tightly integrated memory-centric and dataflow-aware design principles [68]. This includes hierarchical on-chip buffering, replay-buffer–aware memory organizations, near-memory or in-memory computation, and dataflow-specialized scheduling of RL primitives such as policy evaluation, value updates, and experience sampling. By explicitly co-optimizing data movement and computation, such architectures can significantly alleviate bandwidth bottlenecks and improve energy efficiency. In parallel, precision-adaptive computation represents a key enabler for efficient and robust learning on hardware. Rather than relying on fixed numerical formats, future accelerators may employ mixed-precision or runtime-adjustable representations that adapt bit-widths according to learning phase, gradient dynamics, or policy confidence. When combined with memory-aware designs, adaptive precision can further reduce storage and communication overhead while preserving learning stability. Embedding numerical robustness indicators—such as overflow detection, quantization error monitoring, or confidence-aware scaling—directly into the learning loop offers a principled way to balance aggressive quantization with convergence reliability, making such architectures suitable for long-running and lifelong RL workloads.

• 

Edge and Deployment-Oriented RL Systems:
Future RL-based FPGA platforms are expected to play a central role in edge and embedded applications that demand low latency, energy efficiency, and real-time adaptability. A primary research direction concerns RL-driven UAV navigation and swarm coordination [72], where recent advances in Transformer-enhanced RL for UAV decision-making highlight a growing need for hardware-efficient acceleration at the edge. Offline sequence-modeling approaches, such as the maneuver-conditioned decision Transformer (MCDT) [66] and the Transformer-weighted A* framework [166], rely heavily on attention-based inference, making them well suited to FPGA acceleration through pipelined and parallel architectures. In parallel, online DRL models integrating Transformers, including TOSRL [63] and Transformer-enhanced MADDPG [85], further emphasize strict real-time constraints, partial observability handling, and energy efficiency, all of which align naturally with FPGA capabilities. Moreover, multi-agent Transformer-based methods such as MA2DBT [37] and MAT-V [62] expose the scalability challenges of attention-driven coordination, reinforcing the relevance of FPGA parallelism. Finally, multimodal perception–decision pipelines such as ViTAL [105], which demonstrate real-world UAV deployment, implicitly motivate hardware-efficient co-design of perception and RL inference. Beyond aerial robotics, these architectural insights extend naturally to other safety-critical domains, such as intelligent sensing and imaging for medical diagnostics [51, 52]. Recent studies show that RL, particularly DQN-based frameworks, can enhance medical imaging tasks through adaptive localization, segmentation, and classification, as evidenced by cervical cell localization [70] and MRI carcinoma segmentation [139]. Such trends motivate FPGA-based acceleration to support low-latency and energy-efficient execution of RL-enhanced perception pipelines. Furthermore, multimodal diagnostic systems indicate that FPGA-enabled co-design of sensing, deep perception, and RL inference can facilitate robust, adaptive, and non-invasive diagnostic platforms for real-time clinical deployment. Similarly, RL represents a promising yet underexplored direction for image-based high-energy physics detectors [74], particularly when combined with FPGA acceleration. Although existing studies remain limited, recent work such as [77] demonstrates that label-free, graph-based RL can achieve competitive accuracy and inference speed for charged-particle tracking, effectively replacing heuristic reconstruction in high-multiplicity detector environments. These characteristics strongly motivate future RL-based FPGA implementations, where massive parallelism, deterministic latency, and energy efficiency are essential for real-time detector pipelines. FPGA acceleration could further enable online, trigger-level decision-making and scalable deployment of graph-based RL models under strict throughput and timing constraints. Finally, automatic speech recognition also emerges as a promising application domain [30, 34, 73], where RL-enabled FPGA implementations could support adaptive inference and robustness in noisy, real-world environments. Collectively, these application-driven research avenues highlight the potential of RL-based FPGA systems to outperform static heuristics in dynamic, data-intensive, and latency-constrained settings.

• 

Generative AI and LLM-Driven RL on FPGA Platforms:
The convergence of LLM, generative AI, and RL represents a highly promising future direction for FPGA-based intelligent systems, particularly for long-horizon, context-aware, and decision-intensive applications. Recent advances demonstrate that Transformer-based and generative models can be efficiently mapped onto reconfigurable hardware. For instance, [156] showed that FPGA acceleration of CNN– Transformer architectures significantly reduces latency and energy consumption while preserving accuracy for real-time edge inference. Similarly, multimodal generative processing has been explored in [28], where a SoC -FPGA enables low-power, high-throughput medical image fusion across heterogeneous sensing modalities. Beyond perception-centric workloads, lightweight LLM are increasingly being used to guide or augment RL agents through semantic reasoning and structured knowledge integration. Representative examples include interpretable and standards-compliant decision-making for tunnel maintenance [155], as well as LLM-guided MARL with Transformer-based coordination for dynamic wafer batch scheduling [148]. These studies highlight the potential of LLM s to enhance exploration, policy interpretability, and coordination in complex environments. Nevertheless, a critical research gap remains: existing LLM –RL frameworks are predominantly evaluated on CPU/GPU platforms, while FPGA implementations either exclude RL entirely or focus solely on inference acceleration. Future research should therefore investigate tightly coupled RL–LLM co-design on FPGA/IC platforms, including hardware-aware training, on-chip memory optimization, quantized Transformer–RL pipelines, and runtime reconfigurability. Addressing these challenges would enable fully autonomous, low-latency, and energy-efficient generative RL systems suitable for edge and safety-critical applications.

• 

Quantum RL on FPGA:
Quantum and quantum-inspired RL constitute a transformative long-term research direction, where FPGA-based RL is expected to play a pivotal enabling role. Recent breakthroughs have demonstrated that sub-microsecond-latency RL agents can be deployed on FPGA to control quantum systems in real time, achieving feedback decisions well within qubit coherence times [117]. Such results validate the suitability of FPGA for hosting ultra-low-latency, deterministic RL controllers capable of learning directly from physical measurements, without relying on explicit system models. Looking forward, hybrid quantum–classical RL architectures represent a compelling perspective, where FPGA serve as classical orchestration, control, and learning backends for near-term quantum hardware. In this paradigm, FPGA-based RL agents can manage fast feedback loops, policy inference, and online adaptation, while higher-level quantum processing is delegated to quantum devices. This tight hardware–algorithm coupling opens the door to scalable quantum control, adaptive error mitigation, and autonomous calibration.

## 6.2.2 RL as the Optimizer

• 

Modular, Portable, and Toolchain-Supported RL Hardware Co-Design:
To ensure portability, scalability, and reuse across evolving RL algorithms, future hardware platforms should emphasize modular algorithm–hardware co-design. This entails decomposing RL systems into standardized building blocks—such as policy inference, value estimation, exploration mechanisms, and learning updates—with well-defined interfaces and parameterizable implementations. Such modularity enables a single FPGA or IC architecture to support a wide spectrum of RL methods, from value-based to policy-gradient and hybrid approaches, while minimizing redesign effort and improving longevity of hardware investments. Equally important is the development of RL-aware design automation and toolchains that bridge the gap between algorithm design and hardware realization. Future HLS and RTL flows should natively support RL-specific constructs, including replay buffers, stochastic sampling, gradient pipelines, and multi-agent synchronization. Automated design-space exploration—potentially driven by RL itself—can further optimize architectural parameters under power, latency, and area constraints. Together with domain-specific languages and reusable templates for RL hardware description, such toolchains can dramatically reduce development time, lower the entry barrier for non-hardware experts, and accelerate the translation of emerging RL algorithms into efficient, deployable hardware systems.

• 

Secure, Reliable, and Trustworthy RL Hardware on FPGA:
As RL-based systems are increasingly deployed in safety-critical, industrial, and cybersecurity-sensitive environments, security, robustness, and trustworthiness emerge as fundamental research directions. Recent studies in cybersecurity demonstrate the strong potential of RL and DRL for complex threat attribution and adaptive defense. For instance, DRL-based malware attribution has been shown to significantly outperform conventional ML techniques in identifying advanced persistent threats [9], albeit at the cost of substantial computational resources and data dependencies. These characteristics strongly motivate the integration of FPGA acceleration to enable low-latency, energy-efficient, and deployable RL security solutions. In parallel, advances in attention-based and Transformer-inspired intrusion detection systems for industrial IoT (IIoT) and internet of medical thing (IoMT) environments report near-perfect detection accuracy and ultra-low inference latency on CPU/GPU platforms [47, 48, 2, 46], highlighting the feasibility of real-time AI-driven defence. However, these works largely rely on static inference models and lack adaptive, online learning capabilities. Incorporating RL into such frameworks—and deploying them on FPGA platforms—would enable continuous adaptation to evolving attack patterns while preserving strict timing and reliability constraints required by critical infrastructures. Furthermore, blockchain-enabled security architectures have demonstrated strong potential for ensuring data integrity, trust management, and decentralized decision-making in smart grids and IoT systems, such as in [100]. Future research may explore the co-design of blockchain mechanisms with RL-based FPGA accelerators, where smart contracts ensure secure policy updates, auditable decisions, and resilient coordination among distributed agents. Such integration could address ethical, transparency, and accountability concerns increasingly associated with autonomous RL systems. In addition, GNN (GNN)-based information security and biometric DL reveal strong potential for RL-based FPGA systems. Integrating RL with GNN steganalysis and biometric authentication enables adaptive, low-latency, and energy-efficient intelligence, overcoming static inference limitations [55]. FPGA-based RL can uniquely combine adaptive intelligence, deterministic latency, and hardware-level security primitives, enabling next-generation intrusion detection systems, malware attribution engines, and resilient cyber–physical defence platforms suitable for IIoT and critical national infrastructures.

These future directions indicate a shift from isolated, algorithm-specific accelerators toward scalable, secure, and application-driven RL-based FPGA and IC platforms, where RL is leveraged both as a workload to be efficiently executed and as an optimizer to enable adaptive, self-configuring, and intelligent system behavior, capable of long-term autonomous operation in real-world environments. 
Recent advances in related domains further highlight promising directions for scalable and adaptive RL systems. In particular, the transition from single-agent to multi-agent learning paradigms enables coordinated decision-making in complex environments [160]. In parallel, adaptive dynamic programming-based control frameworks incorporating event-triggered mechanisms have demonstrated the potential to reduce communication and computational overhead in multi-agent systems [159]. Moreover, emerging human–machine cooperative control strategies based on dynamic authority allocation illustrate how learning-based approaches can be extended to interactive and resource-constrained settings [158]. These developments suggest that future RL-based hardware systems could benefit from integrating multi-agent coordination, communication-efficient learning, and adaptive control mechanisms.

## 7 Conclusion

This review has provided a comprehensive and hardware-centric synthesis of RL implementations on FPGA and IC platforms, spanning more than two decades of research and over one hundred representative studies. By systematically organizing the literature across RL algorithmic families, hardware abstraction levels, architectural design strategies, and application domains, the survey clarifies how learning-based decision-making has progressively transitioned from software-centric experimentation to practical, resource-aware hardware realizations.

A central outcome of this review is the identification of recurring architectural patterns that underpin successful RL deployment on FPGA and IC substrates. Across classical tabular methods, deep value-based approaches, and modern AC and policy-gradient algorithms, effective designs consistently exploit fine-grained parallelism, pipelined datapaths, quantized arithmetic, and tightly coupled on-chip memory hierarchies. At the same time, the review highlights a clear divergence between FPGA-oriented solutions—favoring flexibility, reconfigurability, and rapid prototyping—and IC-based realizations, which prioritize determinism, compactness, and ultra-low power operation. The unified abstraction framework presented in this work demonstrates that, despite these differences, coherent design flows can bridge FPGA prototyping and IC deployment when hardware–algorithm co-design principles are applied early. From an algorithmic perspective, the survey reveals that no single RL paradigm dominates across all hardware and application contexts. Lightweight tabular methods such as QL and SARSA remain attractive for real-time embedded control due to their simplicity and predictable timing, whereas deep RL approaches, including DQN, DDPG, PPO, and AC variants, enable scalability to high-dimensional sensing and control problems at the cost of increased architectural complexity. MARL further extends these capabilities to distributed and large-scale systems but introduces new challenges related to coordination, non-stationarity, and interconnect overhead. These trade-offs emphasize that algorithm selection must be driven jointly by task requirements and hardware constraints rather than by learning performance alone.

The application-level analysis confirms that RL-enabled FPGA/IC systems have demonstrated tangible impact across diverse domains, including networking, information security, robotics, autonomous systems, intelligent sensing and imaging, and embedded optimization. Reported gains in latency, energy efficiency, throughput, and adaptability illustrate the practical benefits of embedding learning directly into hardware. However, the review also underscores persistent limitations that hinder wider adoption, such as sensitivity to numerical precision, exploration–exploitation trade-offs under deterministic execution, limited standardization of evaluation metrics, and challenges in scalability, portability, and reproducibility.

Looking forward, the findings of this survey suggest several promising research directions. These include the development of standardized hardware-aware RL benchmarks, greater emphasis on end-to-end system-level evaluation, tighter integration of RL with design automation and digital twin frameworks, and the exploration of hybrid learning paradigms that combine model-based reasoning with data-driven adaptation. Advances in emerging hardware technologies, including processing-in-memory, heterogeneous SoC, and domain-specific accelerators, further open opportunities to rethink how RL algorithms are mapped onto silicon.

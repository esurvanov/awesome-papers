Abstract

Enhanced Rock Weathering (ERW) is a carbon removal solution with the potential to remove gigatons of CO2 per year, but quantifying that removal today requires dense soil sampling and laboratory analysis. We evaluate five weather Foundation Models (FMs) as frozen encoders feeding lightweight decoders, under a leave-one-field-out protocol on 5,192 chronosequences from 264 fields across Brazil and the US. To our knowledge, this is the first holistic evaluation of weather FMs for ERW and the largest evaluation on operational ERW data. We find that across weather FMs, representations transfer to ERW: our best model, TerraNova-ERW, built on Aurora 1.5, positively orders measurements within fields it has never seen. Zero-shot prediction of a new field’s mean removal is not yet feasible, and we attribute this to data scale: the same architecture excels on $\sim$ $10^{5}$ natural-weathering sequences, anchoring the scale we expect ERW data must reach. Few-shot prediction, blending the model with a few samples per field, is supported today: TerraNova-ERW recovers over 60% of the highest-removal fields with two samples per field, roughly halves the samples needed to estimate a field’s mean removal, and can cut sampling on our densest fields by 28–98%.

## 1 Introduction

To avoid the most adverse effects of climate change, nearly every mitigation pathway requires Carbon Dioxide Removal (CDR) to balance hard-to-abate residual emissions, with $\sim$ 10 gigatons of CDR required annually by 2050 [1].

Enhanced Rock Weathering (ERW) is a promising CDR solution, which accelerates Earth’s natural weathering process that draws down carbon over geological timescales. By applying finely crushed silicate rocks, such as basalt, to agricultural soils, ERW accelerates this process to occur in years rather than millennia. Scaled globally, ERW has the potential to remove gigatons of CO2 per year while providing agronomic co-benefits such as improved crop yields and soil health [2].

The key obstacle to scaling up ERW today is the Measurement, Reporting, and Verification (MRV) required to quantify the CDR. Quantifying removal currently requires dense physical ground-truth samples, including soil samples, along with rigorous laboratory analysis at every site. Process-based models such as Reactive Transport Models (RTMs) have been used to model ERW, but they require explicitly defining and approximating all relevant processes, such as mineral kinetics, and can be challenging to parametrize and computationally expensive to scale. Meanwhile, deep learning is increasingly applied across climate and Earth system problems [3], and weather Foundation Models (FMs) pretrained on decades of reanalysis data offer rich representations of the atmospheric drivers, such as precipitation, temperature, and soil moisture, that govern weathering rates.

Previous work by [4], to our knowledge, the first application of deep learning to ERW, trained lightweight decoders on frozen representations from Microsoft’s Aurora [5] to show that atmospheric FM representations transfer to rock weathering. We build upon and advance this work by evaluating five weather FM backbones on our own operational ERW data spanning Brazil and the US, to our knowledge, the first holistic evaluation of multiple weather FMs for ERW and the largest evaluation on operational ERW data. We then show how our best model, TerraNova-ERW, can be used operationally to reduce the sampling MRV requires.

## 2 Methods

## 2.1 Task and Dataset

We formulate ERW modeling as a sequence regression task using atmospheric FM representations: given monthly historic atmospheric representations and site-specific features (static soil, dynamic site, amendment, spatial, temporal; see Appendix A2 for the full list of features) at $t_{i}$ , predict at $t_{i+1}$ the CDR rate in t CO2e/ha/yr.

Similar to [4], we assemble a dataset of natural weathering data using the same sources and process. We augment this with ERW data from our own deployments in the US and Brazil. The data is assembled as chronosequences, such that a given training example is a pair of measurements from two time points. In total, we have $192{,}511$ sequences of natural weathering data with global coverage and $5{,}192$ sequences of our
own operational ERW data spanning 264 agricultural fields. The two datasets play distinct roles: all main ERW results (Tables 1 and 5) are on the ERW
sequences. The natural-weathering data is used only in two separate
experiments: a joint-training experiment (Appendix A6) and a natural-only scale study (Appendix Table 7).

## 2.2 Model Architecture

The general architecture of TerraNova-ERW is summarized in Appendix A1; key elements follow.

## Weather FM Backbones:

We consider five leading weather FMs to generate latents: Aurora 1.5 [6], Pangu [7], SFNO [8], FCN3 [9], and GraphCast [10]. For each backbone, we precompute global monthly latent representations by running the pretrained, frozen model on primarily ERA5 [11], extracting the activations from the final encoder layer. At each site, we pool the latent field at three fixed spatial scales (local, regional, global) and concatenate them into a single $d_{m}$ -dimensional embedding per month, obtained for every month spanning $t_{i}$ to $t_{i+1}$ of a chronosequence; this keeps training lightweight. Full details on the pooling scales and per-backbone extraction are in Appendix A3.

## Temporal Aggregation:

The interval spanning a chronosequence can vary, and the $T$ monthly embeddings spanning $t_{i}$ to $t_{i+1}$ must be reduced to a fixed-size representation. We aggregate by trapezoidal integration of the latent trajectory, selected over supervised and self-supervised LTAE [12] alternatives (Appendix A4).

## Lightweight Decoders:

We build upon [13] and [4] to train lightweight decoders that fuse the aggregated weather representation with site features to predict the target. Considering the backbone latents are high-dimensional while labeled ERW data is scarce, decoder capacity is an important choice: we sweep the fusion mechanism and the per-backbone latent-projection width, favoring shallow decoders with wide hidden layers (“light wide”); the backbone latent is separately down-projected before fusion. Throughout, TerraNova-ERW denotes the Aurora 1.5 backbone paired with a light-wide decoder and a $d{=}64$ latent projection (“64d”), with two refinements to its inputs and training: the three location inputs enter without per-fold standardization, and early stopping monitors validation RMSE rather than MAE. This is the configuration selected in Section 3.

## 2.3 Training and Evaluation

## Leave-One-Field-Out (LOFO):

We train and evaluate all models by rotating the held-out field. Fields sharing a 2-decimal location key are merged into components (264 fields $\to$ 101), since splitting fields that are spatially close would leak. Each fold trains with field-disjoint ERW validation, and predicts the held-out component (full LOFO protocol details in Appendix A5). We favor this evaluation approach over a traditional held-out validation split given the dataset size.

## Point vs. Site protocol:

Point Level scores every held-out measurement, testing whether the model captures within-field structure: the capability model-and-measure (few-shot) MRV would use, where model predictions can help complement physical samples. Site Level compares the predicted field mean to the true field mean, testing whether the model can place an unseen field’s overall removal without any on-field measurement: the capability fully model-based (zero-shot) MRV would require.

## Metrics and Uncertainty:

We report MAE, RMSE, and Spearman rank correlation throughout; the row-shuffled ERA5 experiment serves as the pooled no-information reference. Uncertainty ( $\pm$ ) is a bootstrap over held-out fields: resampling the scored components (64 for treated-row metrics) with replacement 1000 times. At the point level, we also report “% best”, the share of the closable error a model removes:

 $\%\,\text{best}\;=\;100\times\frac{\text{constant}-\text{model}}{\text{constant}-\text{best}}$ 

where the constant is each fold’s treated training mean and the best is each field’s own measured median (MAE) or mean (RMSE), the best any single value per field can achieve. It is undefined at site level, where the best case is the exact field mean.

## Experimental Configurations:

All experiments use trapezoidal aggregation and vary a single design choice, so each contrast isolates one effect: one configuration per weather FM backbone (which backbone transfers best), raw ERA5 inputs in place of the latent (the contribution of the FM encoding), and row-shuffled ERA5 (isolating the value of weather information itself), plus natural-weathering co-training, input-group ablation, and tabular AutoML configurations detailed in Appendix A6.

## FM representations transfer to CDR prediction across architectures; Aurora performs best.

We see that the weather FM representations transfer to CDR across all backbones, while shuffling the weather away destroys ranking, showing weather information is necessary (all-ERW point MAE 3.06–3.26 and Spearman $+0.12$ to $+0.29$ across backbones and Aurora variants, vs. 3.32 and $-0.01$ shuffled; raw ERA5 3.21 and $+0.22$ ; Appendix Table 5). The FM encoding matches raw ERA5 on error size at the base configuration, while TerraNova-ERW more than doubles the share of closable error removed (41% of MAE and 54% of RMSE vs. 19% and 18% for raw ERA5; Table 1), and is more robust.
Raw ERA5 ranking collapses on one of three seeds (per-seed Spearman $-0.02$ to $+0.22$ ), whereas the Aurora (base, 64d, 512d), SFNO, FCN3, and GraphCast encodings hold $+0.16$ to $+0.28$ on every seed; only Pangu and the natural-weathering co-training variant share ERA5’s instability (Appendix Table 6). Aurora 1.5 with a light-wide decoder, a $d{=}64$ latent projection, unstandardized location inputs, and RMSE early stopping achieved the best value on every all-ERW metric (point MAE 3.06, RMSE 4.20, Spearman $+0.29$ ; site MAE 1.78, RMSE 2.57, Spearman $+0.26$ ) while being stable on every seed, so we adopt it as our best model, which we call TerraNova-ERW. It was selected among about a dozen decoder and training variants evaluated on the same 68 folds.

## Model-and-Measure MRV is supported; fully model-based MRV is not yet.

At the point level on treated rows, weather FMs positively order held-out measurements on fields they have never seen (Appendix Table 5) with TerraNova-ERW’s pooled Spearman $+0.29$ (Brazil $+0.40$ ), which is the capability model-and-measure MRV builds on. At the site level the model also orders unseen fields (pooled Spearman $+0.26$ ; US $+0.17$ , Brazil $+0.37$ ), but its error on an unseen field’s mean is only marginally below the no-information reference: pooled site MAE is 1.78 $\pm$ 0.18 vs. 1.96 for Shuffled ERA5, and on the US subset 1.50 $\pm$ 0.16 vs. 1.57 (Appendix Table 5). Single fields with few measurements are high-variance targets, so ranking is recoverable where absolute measurement is not. Fully model-based quantification of unseen fields is therefore not yet supportable.

## Natural Weathering Scale:

Trained and evaluated exclusively on natural weathering chronosequences, the same TerraNova-ERW architecture reaches validation MAE 0.014 and RMSE 0.017, roughly 90% lower error than the deployable constant predictor (Appendix Table 7). Given $\sim$ 105 diverse sequences, this model family excels at natural weathering prediction. This anchors our expectations for the needed data scale for fully model-based MRV for ERW. From our own scale-up and the growth of the ERW industry, we estimate this scale will be reached in a few years.

## Site Selection with Limited Features:

Site-specific features require physical samples taken at the field or, as a fallback, gridded products. We test whether a reduced-input variant of the model can operate before either is available, using only the weather FM latents, obtainable globally from a field’s coordinates alone, together with the planned amendment specification. This Aurora $+$ Amendment configuration matches the full-input model at the same decoder settings on point-level error and orders unseen fields better (site Spearman $+0.32$ vs. $+0.13$ for Aurora 1.5 (base); Appendix A7), and it remains competitive with TerraNova-ERW on site ranking ( $+0.26$ ) without any site characterization. The advantage lies in ranking, not error: this supports prioritizing sites rather than quantifying them, and lets sampling and site visits be deferred.

## Reducing Samples for MRV:

Since TerraNova-ERW supports model-and-measure MRV, we quantify what the model is worth in samples by simulating sparser campaigns on the LOFO out-of-fold predictions: each Monte-Carlo draw reveals $k$ random measured intervals per field, and a hybrid estimator blends the revealed points with the model prediction at weights fitted out-of-sample (Protocol and results in Appendix A8). Three results stand out (Figure 1). (1) When ranking candidate fields, the hybrid recovers on average 63% of the ten highest-removal fields with two samples per field, vs. 49% for sampling alone at the same budget; the model alone, with no samples, already finds 50%, as many as two samples per field (Figure 1a). (2) At every budget below ${\sim}10$ points per field, the hybrid estimates field means with lower median error than sampling alone (1.13 vs. 2.18 t CO2e/ha/yr at one point), and the hybrid at three points matches what sampling alone needs five to ten points to reach (Figure 1b). (3) On our three densest fields, which hold 32% of all treated measurements, the hybrid saves 28–98% of samples (Figure 1c): at the $\pm 1.0$  t target one sample replaces 6–11 samples on all three fields, and at $\pm 0.5$  t Fields B and C need a single sample (96% and 98% saved) because the model alone already places their means within 0.10 and 0.32 t, while Field A still needs 44 of 62 (28% saved).

## 5 Discussion

Pathway to climate impact: MRV accounts for a third to over half of the cost of ERW [14]. Since sampling and laboratory analysis dominate MRV spend, cutting samples by 28–98% attacks the largest term in a cost that can reach $275/tCO2 [15]. TerraNova-ERW complements rather than replaces physical sampling, enabling sparser, targeted sampling designs and helping prioritize site selection. As latents can be precomputed globally and the decoder is lightweight, the marginal cost of modeling a new site is near zero, supporting cost-effective MRV as ERW scales to gigaton removal.

Limitations and future work: Our deployment data covers only two countries and two feedstock types; temporal horizons are limited to monitoring to date; LOFO does not cover entirely new regions. Further validation across lithologies, climates, regions, and longer horizons along with training on more ERW data will enable models like TerraNova-ERW to be more robustly deployed.

## A1 Architecture Diagram

Figure 2: TerraNova-ERW architecture. A frozen weather foundation-model encoder (Aurora 1.5 by default; Pangu-Weather, SFNO, FCN3, and GraphCast are drop-in alternatives) maps ERA5 inputs to a global monthly latent, from which local (latent cell at the field location), regional ( $3{\times}3$ mean), and global (map mean) features are pooled and aggregated over the observation window by a time-weighted trapezoidal mean. The aggregated 3072-d vector is projected to 64-d and concatenated with five 64-d MLP-encoded site-specific feature groups; a three-layer MLP head predicts the carbon dioxide removal (CDR) rate. Only the projection, site-specific feature encoders, and head are trained.

∗ The CDR rate labels for the operational ERW data are empirically derived and consider the near-field zone. Each georeferenced point is soil-sampled over a near-surface credit horizon at a pre-application baseline and at one or more later credit campaigns, and the samples undergo geochemical analysis. For each consecutive pair of samplings, removal is quantified by a solid-phase cation mass balance: an immobile trace element (e.g., Zr, Ti) constrains the rock mass present at the point, and the control-corrected loss of rock-derived cations is converted to CO2 removal at 2 mol CO2 per mol divalent cation, with corrections for background weathering, strong-acid (fertilizer-derived) weathering, and plant uptake. We use near-field CDR to denote the initial CO2 uptake from cation release within the sampled soil horizon. Project emissions from quarrying, grinding, transport, and spreading are not deducted from the label: they do not vary at the sampling-point scale and are accounted for at issuance rather than in the spatial rate surface. Downstream loss and reversal factors are likewise not applied; unlike project emissions these do vary by catchment, so the labels are an upper bound on net durable removal. Labels are annualized by the interval length.

## A3 Backbone Latent Extraction Details

Each backbone is run pretrained and frozen on primarily ERA5, plus any additional data the given model requires (Table 3). At each site, we pool the extracted latent field at three fixed spatial scales and concatenate them: local, the single latent cell containing the site (site-scale weather); regional, the mean of the surrounding $3{\times}3$ window ( $\sim$ $\pm$ 112 km; mesoscale context); and global, the whole-map planetary scale; yielding a $3d_{b}=d_{m}$ dimensional embedding per month (e.g., $d_{m}{=}3072$ for Aurora 1.5’s $d_{b}{=}1024$ ). Fixed scales are used rather than field-area-adaptive windows because fields are sub-cell at the latent grid resolution. Each backbone thus contributes only a $d_{m}$ -dimensional per-month embedding (Aurora 1.5 = 3072, Pangu = 1152, GraphCast = 1536, SFNO = 1152, FCN3 = 168).

## A4 Temporal Aggregation Methods

The $T$ monthly embeddings spanning $t_{i}$ to $t_{i+1}$ , i.e. $\{z_{1},\dots,z_{T}\}$ , must be reduced to a fixed-size representation. We explore three methods:
(1) Trapezoidal Integration, a fixed, parameter-free aggregation $\bar{z}=\frac{1}{T-1}\sum_{t=1}^{T-1}\frac{z_{t}+z_{t+1}}{2}$ approximating the time-integral of the latent trajectory, imposing the inductive bias that every month contributes;
(2) Supervised LTAE, a Lightweight Temporal Attention Encoder [12] trained end-to-end with the decoder to learn month-level importance weights (e.g., up-weighting wet seasons that dominate dissolution);
and (3) Self-supervised LTAE, the same architecture trained with a masked-month reconstruction objective on unlabeled latent sequences, testing whether temporal structure can be learned without weathering-rate labels.

Trapezoidal integration was selected for all final experiments after experimentation.

## A5 LOFO Protocol Details

Of the 101 location components, the 68 components with $\geq$ 10 measurements each receive a fold (5,062 of 5,192 rows). Each fold trains with field-disjoint ERW validation, early-stops on validation MAE (validation RMSE for TerraNova-ERW), ensembles seeds 42/43/44, and predicts the held-out component. Scalers are fit on training data only; TerraNova-ERW’s three location inputs bypass the per-fold scaler. Four of the 68 components contain only control rows, so treated-row metrics cover 64 components (51 US, 13 Brazil).

## A8 Sampling-Budget Simulation

## Protocol.

A field is one location component and a point is one measured CDR interval on that field. Each Monte-Carlo draw reveals $k$ randomly chosen points per field, forms an estimate of the field’s mean, and scores it against the mean of the points that were not revealed, so no estimate is graded on its own inputs. Model predictions are the LOFO out-of-fold predictions of TerraNova-ERW (Aurora 1.5, 64d latent projection) (68 folds, seeds 42–44 averaged); priors and variance components are likewise fitted leaving the scored field out. Estimators are: sampling only ( $\bar{y}_{k}$ ); sampling shrunk, the $k$ -point mean shrunk toward the leave-one-field-out prior with the error-optimal weight $w=\tau^{2}/(\tau^{2}+\sigma^{2}/k)$ from the between- and within-field variance components, but with no model, isolating the value of shrinkage itself; model only ( $\hat{f}$ on the unrevealed points); hybrid (error weights), the same shrinkage applied to the model’s residuals, i.e. the error-optimal blend of measurements and model; and hybrid (rank weights), $\alpha\,z(\bar{y}_{k})+(1-\alpha)\,z(\hat{f})$ with one $\alpha$ per budget tuned for the ranking objective on the even-numbered Monte-Carlo draws, with every estimator scored on the odd-numbered draws only ( $\alpha$ rises from 0.65 at one point to 0.85 at five). Ranking fixes the portfolio at the 52 fields with $\geq 13$ treated points so every budget orders the same set; error tiers use every field with enough unrevealed points left to grade, so $n$ falls with $k$ (64 fields at $k{=}1$ , 63 at $k{=}2$ , 62 at $k{=}3$ , 52 at $k{=}5$ , 39 at $k{=}10$ , 25 at $k{=}20$ ) and the fields surviving at high $k$ are systematically the denser ones.

Three caveats bound these numbers. First, the truth is itself an estimate (the mean of the unrevealed points), and within-field noise carries 64% of total variance, which puts a floor under every error and caps attainable rank agreement. Second, rows at different budgets are not computed over identical field sets (see the fields column of Table 10). Third, the residual “poor” tail is where measurements matter most: with no measured points the model alone leaves 13% of fields in the “poor” tier (error ${>}4$  t), and a single measured point per field brings that to 5%, because even one point of local calibration removes a stable per-field offset in the model.

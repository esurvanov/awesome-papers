Abstract

Satellite precipitation products face a persistent trade-off: real-time estimates are timely but less accurate, whereas the most reliable products are often released months after observation. This delay keeps high-quality precipitation information outside the decision window for flood warning and disaster response. Here we report Huayu-Global, a cross-constellation deep-learning system that combines three geostationary sensor streams from five satellites through a common retrieval framework and generates coherent quasi-global precipitation fields every thirty minutes. Across more than a million independent station records from two periods outside the training years, Huayu-Global improves event detection and precipitation-amount estimation relative to the real-time products evaluated and reaches delayed-product-level skill in key metrics. Huayu-Global therefore turns operational geostationary observations into a timely, high-skill precipitation layer for flood forecasting, water management and disaster response. Its cross-constellation design also provides a scalable foundation for incorporating future geostationary missions into hydrological and weather-prediction systems.

## 1 Introduction

Figure 1: Data processing workflow for the Huayu-Global precipitation monitoring framework. Representative Geostationary Operational Environmental Satellite (GOES), Meteosat, and FengYun-4B (FY-4B) sector views are transformed into Advanced Baseline Imager (ABI), Spinning Enhanced Visible and Infrared Imager (SEVIRI), and Advanced Geostationary Radiation Imager (AGRI) cloud-feature sequences, then passed through the Huayu models for the Americas, Europe-Africa, and Asia-Pacific domains. Sector outputs are merged to generate coherent quasi-global grid precipitation products using only satellite inputs available at the inference time.

Precipitation is a primary driver of the water cycle, flood hazard, agricultural risk, and hydrological extremes. Operational monitoring therefore requires products that are both timely and quantitatively reliable. Ground gauges, weather radar, and gauge–satellite merged analyses provide essential constraints on precipitation fields, but their global operational use is limited by uneven station density, radar-network gaps, terrain blockage, oceanic coverage gaps, and differences in data-sharing conditions (Shen et al., 2014; Fulton et al., 1998; Sun et al., 2018; Kirschbaum et al., 2017). These limitations are most acute when precipitation information is needed rapidly and consistently across continents and ocean-adjacent regions.

Satellite precipitation products have reduced this coverage gap, but operational users still face a hard choice between speed and accuracy. Real-time streams such as Global Satellite Mapping of Precipitation NOW (GSMaP_NOW) and Precipitation Estimation from Remotely Sensed Information using Artificial Neural Networks–Cloud Classification System (PERSIANN-CCS) provide prompt coverage but show lower detection and quantitative skill (real-time products achieve critical success index (CSI) values between 0.23 and 0.33 in our evaluation, whereas the gauge-adjusted Final Run of Integrated Multi-satellitE Retrievals for Global Precipitation Measurement (IMERG FR), released 3.5 months after observation, reaches 0.42) (Kubota et al., 2020; Nguyen et al., 2018; Hong et al., 2004). More extensively processed products such as the Climate Prediction Center morphing technique (CMORPH) and IMERG can improve accuracy, but their highest-quality streams may arrive months later (Joyce et al., 2004; Huffman et al., 2015; Kidd et al., 2020; Tan et al., 2019; Huffman et al., 2023). Global IMERG assessments also show that residual errors remain dependent on region, season, and precipitation regime, even after multi-sensor merging and gauge adjustment (Tan et al., 2016; Pradhan et al., 2022). Information delivered after the decision window cannot support flood warning or other time-critical action. A useful quasi-global system must therefore combine real-time availability with delayed-product-level precipitation skill.

High-frequency geostationary observations offer a route to this goal because they continuously sample cloud evolution across broad domains. Decades of geostationary meteorological satellite development, from early operational systems to modern multispectral imagers, have increased the temporal and spectral information available for rainfall inference (Blersch and
Probert, 1991; Schmit et al., 2005; Bessho et al., 2016). The difficulty is that cloud-top radiances do not map uniquely to surface rainfall, and the relationship varies with region, season, terrain, storm type, and sensor geometry. The challenge is compounded across satellite platforms: the GOES ABI, Meteosat SEVIRI, and FY-4B AGRI differ in spectral coverage, native spatial resolution, and full-disk scan cadence, yet an operational quasi-global product must seamlessly combine their outputs. Recent studies have shown that nonlinear and multispectral models can improve geostationary precipitation retrieval in regional settings (Song et al., 2026; Kühnlein
et al., 2014; Sehad et al., 2017; Wang et al., 2020; Wang et al., 2021; Ma et al., 2022a; Ma et al., 2022b; Zhang et al., 2022; Zhu et al., 2024; Ji et al., 2025). The remaining challenge is to extend this idea from single-sensor or regional retrievals to a cross-constellation system that can operate across the Americas, Europe-Africa, and Asia-Pacific sectors while preserving a common output grid.

Here we present Huayu-Global, a cross-constellation framework for real-time quasi-global precipitation retrieval. Extending the earlier regional Huayu framework (Song et al., 2026), it coordinates sensor-adapted models for GOES ABI, Meteosat SEVIRI, and FY-4B AGRI within a common architecture and merges their outputs on a unified 0.05∘ grid (Fig. 1). Huayu-Global thereby converts operational geostationary observations into coherent precipitation fields every 30 minutes across most low- and mid-latitude regions. The system is developed from 59.7 TB of satellite data and evaluated against 5,395 independent ground stations contributing 1.85 million matched three-hourly records from July–December 2022 and January–June 2025, both outside the 2023–2024 training years. It leads the evaluated real-time products in event detection and error magnitude and reaches delayed-product-level skill in key station-based metrics. By moving high-skill precipitation information from retrospective archives into the operational decision window, Huayu-Global establishes a scalable observation layer for flood forecasting, water management, disaster response and future satellite-enabled weather prediction.

## 3 Discussion

Huayu-Global extends the earlier regional Huayu framework (Song et al., 2026) from a single-sensor regional retrieval to a three-sensor quasi-global system covering approximately 85% of Earth’s land surface between 60∘S and 60∘N. It demonstrates that high-frequency geostationary imagery can deliver real-time-input precipitation estimates with skill previously associated with delayed post-processed products. At independent station locations, Huayu-Global achieves the highest CSI and R2 and the lowest RMSE among the evaluated products. Relative to GSMaP_NOW, it increases CSI by 34.9% and reduces RMSE by 31.8%. The central advance is the conversion of continuously available cloud-evolution observations into high-skill precipitation information within the operational decision window.

This capability connects satellite retrieval directly to time-critical environmental workflows. Huayu-Global generates a new 0.05∘ precipitation field every 30 minutes, and data loading and inference require less than 5 minutes per timestep after model initialization on a single workstation (Methods). The resulting fields can provide a timely observation layer for flood nowcasting, hydrological forecasting, agricultural monitoring and disaster response. They also complement recent deep-learning nowcasting and weather-prediction systems, which require rapidly updated precipitation information to initialize, constrain and evaluate forecasts (Sønderby et al., 2020; Ravuri et al., 2021; Espeholt et al., 2022; Zhang et al., 2023).

The cross-constellation design makes this capability scalable. The three sector models share the same Head–Geographic Attention Block (GeoAB)–Tail architecture while using separate parameters for ABI, SEVIRI, and AGRI streams. This combination applies a consistent representation pathway to cloud evolution while adapting to differences in spectral coverage, resolution, scan frequency and viewing geometry. All three models achieve useful retrieval skill within their respective domains (Table 2), enabling their outputs to form a coherent quasi-global product. The same design provides a practical path for incorporating Meteosat Third Generation, FY-4C, and Geostationary Extended Observations (GeoXO) missions as the global geostationary constellation evolves.

Training on 2023–2024 data and evaluation in July–December 2022 and January–June 2025 show that the retrieval skill extends beyond the training years. The aggregate advantage persists across these windows, while analyses by month, station elevation, longitude sector and precipitation amount (Fig. 5) identify where that skill varies. The Americas sector (GOES ABI) achieves both the highest CSI (0.495) and highest RMSE (2.452 mm), consistent with strong event detection in a domain containing intense convective rainfall. The Europe-Africa sector (Meteosat SEVIRI) has the lowest CSI (0.398) and lowest RMSE (1.597 mm), indicating more conservative detection and smaller magnitude errors. These regional contrasts connect retrieval performance to sensor characteristics, climate regimes and the uneven distribution of station samples.

Huayu-Global is supervised with IMERG FR yet exceeds it for CSI, R2, and RMSE at independent station locations. This result shows that a gridded supervision product need not define the performance ceiling of a retrieval evaluated at a different spatial scale. The 0.05∘ grid, explicit separation of rain occurrence and amount (Song et al., 2026), and high-frequency cloud-evolution sequences allow Huayu-Global to sharpen rainfall boundaries, timing and local magnitude relative to the 0.1∘ training target. Independent radar composites, additional dense gauge networks and oceanic observations would extend this evidence to spatial regimes that remain weakly sampled by the present evaluation.

The remaining limitations define the next scientific targets. Geostationary coverage is restricted to approximately 60∘S–60∘N, leaving high latitudes dependent on polar-orbiting observations. The Hadley Centre Integrated Surface Database (HadISD) (Dunn et al., 2012; Smith et al., 2011; Dunn et al., 2014; Dunn et al., 2016) and China Meteorological Administration (CMA) networks provide extensive land validation but sparse coverage over Africa, South America and the oceans. Warm rain, snow, orographic precipitation and intense convection also remain challenging because cloud-top radiances do not uniquely determine surface rainfall (Wang et al., 2021; Ma et al., 2022a; Zhu et al., 2024; Yang et al., 2026). These gaps motivate complementary observations and retrieval constraints rather than further expansion of the same satellite-only formulation.

The diagnostic patterns provide a roadmap for that expansion. Elevation-dependent degradation (Fig. 5, column b) makes terrain, wind fields and orographic constraints the first priority, while intensity-dependent RMSE growth (Fig. 5, column e) motivates loss reweighting and passive-microwave fusion for heavy rainfall. Ensemble uncertainty estimates and robustness to missing scans would make the fields more useful for risk-sensitive decisions, and polar-orbiting microwave observations could extend retrieval towards higher latitudes. Combined with the rapid progress of data-driven global weather prediction (Bi et al., 2023; Chen et al., 2023), these developments could turn cross-constellation precipitation retrieval into a continuously updated observation layer linking satellite systems, hydrological models and early-warning services. Huayu-Global provides the present foundation for that transition from delayed precipitation archives to active Earth-system prediction.

## 4 Methods

## 4.1 Preprocessing

The preprocessing stage summarized in Fig. 1 converts satellite observations from different geostationary platforms into georeferenced multispectral cloud-feature tensors and reprojects them to a common 0.05∘ grid. Channel-wise normalization and feature standardization are applied separately for ABI, SEVIRI, and AGRI to reduce cross-sensor heterogeneity while preserving sensor-specific information. Invalid pixels and low-quality retrieval regions are filled by spatial interpolation from valid neighbouring pixels during preprocessing; regions with persistent data gaps spanning the full interpolation window are flagged as missing and excluded from both training and evaluation. Training pairs are generated by collocating geostationary feature tensors with IMERG FR precipitation fields at matching timestamps; at each 30-min interval, one IMERG FR field is paired with two AGRI or SEVIRI scans or three ABI scans according to the native acquisition frequency. At inference time, sector outputs are transformed to the common grid and mosaicked with overlap-aware blending, in which adjacent sector outputs are combined by averaging across overlap zones at mosaic boundaries.

## 4.2 Model framework

Huayu-Global maps multispectral cloud observations to 30-min precipitation estimates through three independently trained sector models. The models share the same Head–GeoAB–Tail architecture, where GeoAB denotes Geographic Attention Block (Extended Data Fig. 1) (Song et al., 2025; Song et al., 2026; Song et al., 2026), but have separate parameters for ABI, SEVIRI, and AGRI streams. This design keeps the retrieval framework consistent while allowing each model to adapt to its sensor-domain distribution. The choice of a shared architecture with independent weights reflects the hypothesis that cloud-to-rainfall relationships share common physical structure across sensors—captured by the common Head–GeoAB–Tail design—but require sensor-specific parameterization of spectral response functions, viewing geometry, and scan cadence—captured by separately trained weights. The GeoAB modules combine attention mechanisms introduced by the Transformer (Vaswani et al., 2017), shifted-window attention derived from Swin Transformer and SwinIR designs (Liu et al., 2021; Liu et al., 2022; Liang et al., 2021), and long-range attention components developed in earlier geographic image-restoration and precipitation-retrieval work (Song et al., 2022; Song and Zhong, 2022; Song et al., 2025; Song et al., 2026). A single unified model would need to internally reconcile conflicting sensor characteristics, while three entirely separate architectures would forgo the modelling efficiency and cross-sensor consistency that a common framework provides. The sector-wise outputs are merged into a spatially continuous quasi-global product after reprojection and overlap blending. Six consecutive 30-min estimates are accumulated for evaluation against 3-hour station records.

## 4.3 Runtime performance

Single-machine runtime was measured on a 64-bit Windows 11 workstation equipped with an Intel Core i7-10700F CPU (8 cores and 16 threads), 48 GB of system memory, and one NVIDIA GeForce RTX 4060 Ti GPU with 8 GB of memory. For a representative single-timestamp Huayu-Global run with the required satellite files stored locally, model initialization and weight loading took 91.5 s, input-data loading took 195.0 s, and retrieval inference took 95.2 s. The complete first run therefore required 381.7 s (6.36 min). Because model loading is a one-time startup cost when the process remains active, the measured data-loading and inference stages for a subsequent timestamp total 290.2 s (4.84 min). These measurements characterize local computational performance and exclude upstream satellite-data delivery, local transfer, quality control, and downstream product dissemination. In this manuscript, “real-time” therefore means that inference uses only observations available for the target time and that local processing is short relative to the 30-min product cycle; it does not claim a measured end-to-end public-release latency. Faster storage or multi-GPU deployment could further reduce data loading time or support concurrent processing.

## 4.4 Training and optimization

The training data cover 2023 and 2024, whereas the independent station validation windows are July–December 2022 and January–June 2025. This temporal separation is used to evaluate generalization outside the training years. Huayu-Global adopts the dual-target loss introduced in the earlier Huayu framework (Song et al., 2026). The objective addresses the imbalance between non-raining and raining pixels by separating precipitation occurrence from precipitation amount. For each training sample $i$ , let $r_{i}$ be the IMERG FR precipitation target, $\hat{r}_{i}$ the regression output, $z_{i}=\mathbb{F}(r_{i}\geq 0.1~\mathrm{mm})$ the rain-occurrence label, and $\hat{\mathbf{q}}_{i}$ the predicted rain–no-rain probability vector. The training loss is

 $\mathcal{L}=\frac{1}{\max(N_{+},1)}\sum_{i=1}^{N}z_{i}\,\operatorname{SmoothL1}(\hat{r}_{i},r_{i})+\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\operatorname{CE}(\hat{\mathbf{q}}_{i},z_{i}),$ 

(1)

where $N$ is the number of pixels in a training batch, $N_{+}=\sum_{i}z_{i}$ , $\operatorname{CE}$ denotes cross-entropy loss, and the SmoothL1 term is evaluated only over positive-rainfall pixels. At inference, pixels classified as non-raining are set to zero, while pixels classified as raining retain the regression estimate. Space-time samples are shuffled during training, and model checkpoints are selected according to held-out validation performance. Because IMERG FR is the supervision target and is also one of the delayed comparison products, the product-ranking claims are solely based on independent station observations rather than on agreement with the training target.

All three sector models use AdamW optimization (Loshchilov, 2017), a batch size of 16, and 100 training epochs on four NVIDIA RTX 6000 Ada GPUs with 48 GB memory each. The implementation uses PyTorch with Python 3.12 on Linux. The three models share the same architecture and parameter count, while the learning rate and decay schedule are tuned separately for ABI, AGRI, and SEVIRI streams (Extended Data Table 1).

## 4.5 Evaluation metrics

Evaluation is performed on matched station-product records at 3-hour accumulation scale, with each record constructed from six consecutive 30-min precipitation fields. For 3-hour evaluation, hourly and sub-hourly station records are aggregated to the corresponding 3-hour windows. All products are evaluated over the same valid station-time set after applying common quality-control and missing-data filters, so differences in record count do not drive the product rankings in Table 1. Product values are extracted from each product’s native grid at the station coordinates before temporal accumulation, avoiding an artificial common-grid resampling step that could smooth or sharpen products differently. For 30-min products, six consecutive fields are summed to produce the 3-hour accumulation; for products natively delivered at coarser temporal resolution, the accumulation is computed over the number of constituent fields that fall within the 3-hour window. The two validation periods comprise 730 candidate windows: 00:00–03:00 and 12:00–15:00 UTC on each day from 1 July to 31 December 2022 and from 1 January to 30 June 2025. Candidate windows for which missing source satellite imagery prevented consistent generation of the corresponding gridded precipitation fields were excluded before station matching. For the remaining windows, a station-time sample was retained only when a quality-controlled gauge accumulation was available and could be collocated with all seven comparison products (PERSIANN-CCS, GSMaP_NOW, CMORPH, PERSIANN, and IMERG ER, LR, and FR). Missing, non-finite, or product-defined invalid precipitation values were excluded from metric calculations, whereas valid zero-precipitation values were retained. Consequently, the record count for an individual station represents the number of valid gauge–grid collocations rather than the 730 theoretical windows; among the 5,395 stations, the largest station-level count was 569. Precipitation occurrence is evaluated at the native subperiods used to build each Huayu-Global 3-hour record using a 0.1 mm threshold before contingency counts are accumulated over the validation sample; the 3-hour intensity threshold is also set to 0.1 mm for all products. Detection skill is assessed by critical success index (CSI), probability of detection (POD), false-alarm ratio (FAR), and accuracy (ACC). Let $\mathrm{TP}$ , $\mathrm{FN}$ , $\mathrm{FP}$ , and $\mathrm{TN}$ denote true positives, false negatives, false positives, and true negatives, respectively. The categorical metrics are computed as

 $\displaystyle\mathrm{CSI}$ 
 $\displaystyle=\frac{\mathrm{TP}}{\mathrm{TP}+\mathrm{FN}+\mathrm{FP}},$ 
 $\displaystyle\mathrm{POD}$ 
 $\displaystyle=\frac{\mathrm{TP}}{\mathrm{TP}+\mathrm{FN}},$ 

(2)

 $\displaystyle\mathrm{FAR}$ 
 $\displaystyle=\frac{\mathrm{FP}}{\mathrm{TP}+\mathrm{FP}},$ 
 $\displaystyle\mathrm{ACC}$ 
 $\displaystyle=\frac{\mathrm{TP}+\mathrm{TN}}{\mathrm{TP}+\mathrm{FN}+\mathrm{FP}+\mathrm{TN}}.$ 

Quantitative consistency is assessed by CC, R2, and RMSE. For product estimates $p_{i}$ and station observations $o_{i}$ , both expressed as 3-hour accumulated precipitation amounts,

 $\displaystyle\mathrm{RMSE}$ 
 $\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(p_{i}-o_{i})^{2}},$ 

(3)

 $\displaystyle R^{2}$ 
 $\displaystyle=1-\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}(p_{i}-o_{i})^{2}}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}(o_{i}-\bar{o})^{2}},$ 

 $\displaystyle\mathrm{CC}$ 
 $\displaystyle=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}(p_{i}-\bar{p})(o_{i}-\bar{o})}{\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{n}(p_{i}-\bar{p})^{2}}\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{n}(o_{i}-\bar{o})^{2}}},$ 

where $\bar{p}$ and $\bar{o}$ are the mean product estimate and mean station observation.

## 4.6 Statistics and uncertainty

Uncertainty ranges reported with the evaluation metrics are computed from 1,000 bootstrap resamples. A resampling block is defined by each station and UTC period and contains all valid 3-hour records. For each bootstrap iteration, station-time blocks are drawn with replacement and metrics are recomputed on the resampled set. The bootstrap distribution is summarized by the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles, giving a central 95% confidence interval. Compact tables report this uncertainty as $\pm$ one-half of the central 95% interval. These intervals quantify sampling uncertainty over the matched station network and do not include structural uncertainty from product algorithms, gauge undercatch, representativeness error between point gauges and grid cells, or unobserved oceanic regions.

## 4.7 Data

Huayu-Global uses three operational geostationary satellite systems to cover most low- and mid-latitude regions (60∘S–60∘N): GOES-16/18/19 Advanced Baseline Imager (ABI) for the Americas sector (Schmit et al., 2005; Schmit et al., 2017), Meteosat-9/10/11 Spinning Enhanced Visible and Infrared Imager (SEVIRI) for the Europe-Africa sector (Schmetz
et al., 2002), and FY-4B Advanced Geostationary Radiation Imager (AGRI) for the Asia-Pacific sector (Yang et al., 2017). GOES-16 and GOES-19 are treated as the same ABI stream, and Meteosat-10 and Meteosat-11 are treated as the same SEVIRI stream, with operational transitions handled through provider data records and sector footprint metadata. All source observations are transformed onto a common 0.05∘ analysis grid and harmonized in time before model inference. Detailed satellite specifications, data volumes, and provider information are given in Extended Data Tables 2 and 3.

Huayu-Global is benchmarked against seven representative satellite precipitation products spanning infrared-based, passive-microwave-informed, morphing-based, multi-sensor, real-time, and delayed post-processed retrieval families: GSMaP_NOW (Kubota et al., 2020), PERSIANN-CCS and PERSIANN (Nguyen et al., 2018), CMORPH (Joyce et al., 2004), and IMERG ER/LR/FR (Huffman et al., 2015; Tan et al., 2019; Huffman et al., 2023). This broad selection provides a comparison set for evaluating both event detection and quantitative precipitation estimation.

Independent in situ observations from the HadISD global station dataset (Dunn et al., 2012; Smith et al., 2011; Dunn et al., 2014; Dunn et al., 2016) and CMA station network provide the validation data, with 5,395 stations and 1,847,967 three-hourly records. IMERG FR fields are used as gridded training targets, whereas ground observations are reserved exclusively for independent validation and cross-product comparison. This separation reduces circularity between model supervision and final performance assessment, but the station network is unevenly distributed and does not directly evaluate open-ocean precipitation.

HadISD station observations are publicly available from the Met Office Hadley Centre (https://www.metoffice.gov.uk/hadobs/hadisd/). CMA station observations are subject to provider access restrictions and cannot be redistributed by the authors. The trained model files, normalization data, coverage masks, and demonstration satellite observations for 00:00 UTC on January 2, 2025 are openly available from the download location documented in the Huayu-Global repository (https://github.com/songzijiang/Huayu-Global). Processed training tensors and matched station-validation records are not publicly redistributed because of their volume and the licensing conditions of the source datasets.

Extended Data Table 2: Operational geostationary satellites used in Huayu-Global. The Americas sector is served by GOES-16/18/19 ABI observations, the Europe-Africa sector by Meteosat-9/10/11 SEVIRI observations, and the Asia-Pacific sector by FY-4B AGRI observations. These three systems together cover most low- and mid-latitude regions (60∘S–60∘N). GOES-16 and GOES-19 are handled as a continuous ABI stream according to provider operational metadata, with GOES-19 contributing after the GOES-East transition in 2025; Meteosat-10 and Meteosat-11 are likewise handled as a continuous SEVIRI stream. The coverage of AGRI (FY-4B) differs before and after 31 January 2024 owing to a change of the sub-satellite point (from 133∘E to 105∘E); the mosaicking procedure accounts for the corresponding sector footprints.

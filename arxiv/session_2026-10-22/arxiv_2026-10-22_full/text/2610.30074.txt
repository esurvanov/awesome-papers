Abstract.

Parallel AI agents can each produce useful local edits yet degrade a shared solution when their edits are combined. We model this coordination problem as discrete block optimization with an exactly evaluable objective and a known dependency graph. The graph certifies compatible sets of edits through its independent sets, and evaluating proposals against the same current solution yields verified gains that add exactly within any such set. A coordinator can therefore construct a safe compatible candidate while additionally evaluating interacting edits to capture favorable interactions. We prove that, using an optimal fractional coloring, the safe merger preserves a $1/\chi_{f}(G)$ fraction of the total verified local improvement, where $\chi_{f}(G)$ is the fractional chromatic number of the dependency graph. We further show that no merger restricted to selecting a subset of the same proposals can guarantee a larger fraction in general. Under conditional local-improvement guarantees and a block error-bound condition, repeated collaboration converges geometrically in the objective gap, separating the effects of task structure, agent allocation, and computation cost. The resulting rate gives a linear program for agent assignment and an explicit time–cost trade-off. We complement the analysis with experiments on solution quality and the cost of parallel coordination. Code-repair experiments show that verified merging resolves conflicts between individually useful edits that cause naive merging to fail. On larger conflict-free tasks, parallel agents reduce modeled completion time at higher input-token-equivalent cost. On three public road networks, fractional integration retains 922 units of verified graph-cut improvement across 90 paired runs. For route-search agents sharing exclusive access to vehicle routes, fractional scheduling reduces measured wall time by a median of 15% across twelve paired runs, with route-cost changes varying across runs.

multi-agent systems, generative AI agents, coordination, combinatorial optimization, resource and task allocation, distributed block optimization, fractional graph coloring

## 1. Introduction

Many tasks handled by AI agents can be decomposed into components that are worked on in parallel.
This can reduce latency and distribute work across components, but it also creates a coordination problem.
An edit that improves one component in isolation may interact with edits to other components once they are combined.
Program repair provides a concrete example: agents may modify different modules in parallel, yet their changes can still interfere through shared functionality or interfaces.
The central question is therefore not only how to generate useful local edits, but also how to decide which of them can be safely integrated into the shared solution.

For example, suppose a program must apply a discount exactly once, and either the pricing module or the checkout module can implement the required transformation.
Starting from a version that does not apply the discount, an edit to either module improves the program on its own.
If both edits are applied, however, the discount is applied twice.
Thus, individually useful edits to different modules need not be compatible.

The example exposes a broader coordination problem in parallel agent work.
A proposed edit can be evaluated in isolation against the current artifact, yet its value may change when other edits are applied alongside it.
We therefore separate proposal generation from proposal integration.
Agents work concurrently from the same artifact on distinct blocks, while a coordinator uses an evaluator and dependency information to decide which returned edits to combine.
Our goal is to determine how much of the verified local improvement can be guaranteed to be retained after integration.

We call this problem verified proposal integration.
The coordinator observes black-box block proposals, their verified singleton gains against a common base artifact, and a dependency certificate identifying subsets whose gains add exactly.
It need not know how the proposals were generated or model their joint effects beyond this certificate.
We ask how much of the verified local improvement a coordinator can always preserve using only this information.
Our main result is a tight worst-case bound for integrating fixed proposals. We extend the analysis to convergence and agent allocation under additional assumptions. Experiments examine the integration mechanism and its cost.

## Contributions.

• 

We formulate verified proposal integration for black-box agent edits and characterize its exact worst-case retention factor.
Given verified singleton gains and a dependency certificate, a coordinator can always preserve a $1/\chi_{f}(G)$ fraction of the total verified local improvement.
This guarantee is tight: in the worst case, no coordinator that forms its update by selecting a subset of the submitted proposals can guarantee a larger fraction (Sections 2–3).

• 

Under explicit assumptions on agent capability, assignment coverage, and task structure, we establish geometric convergence of repeated verified integration.
The convergence rate separates the effects of these three factors and yields a principled formulation for agent assignment and explicit time–cost trade-offs (Sections 4–5).

• 

We test proposal integration and its costs with language-model agents and use controlled examples to clarify the guarantees.
The experiments examine conflict resolution among individually useful edits, performance on held-out multi-round tasks, and the time–cost trade-off of parallel execution. A road-network diagnostic evaluates exact-factor merging on naturally occurring block interactions and compares fractional integration with fixed and adaptive ordinary coloring (Section 6).

## Related work.

Our formulation builds on ideas from parallel and distributed optimization, where multiple components are updated concurrently while accounting for their interactions.
In particular, it is closely related to parallel block coordinate descent and arbitrary sampling, which study how subsets of coordinates can be updated simultaneously (Richtárik and Takáč, 2016; Qu and Richtárik, 2016).
Within this line of work, Bradley et al. (2011) analyze interference among parallel coordinate updates using a spectral measure.
A complementary perspective appears in GenCD, which separates coordinate descent into selection, proposal, acceptance, and update stages and includes a coloring-based variant (Scherrer et al., 2012).
Our setting differs in two important ways.
First, proposals may be arbitrary discrete edits rather than gradient-based coordinate updates.
Second, the coordinator observes verified singleton gains and a dependency certificate without requiring a smooth model of their joint effects.

Distributed constraint optimization (DCOP) coordinates agents that control different variables to optimize a shared objective expressed through local functions (Modi et al., 2005; Fioretto et al., 2018; Troullinos et al., 2025).
This is closely related in spirit to our use of localized dependencies, but the coordination problem is different.
In DCOP, agents coordinate the choice of variable assignments themselves.
In our setting, agents have already generated candidate edits, and the coordinator decides which submitted proposals to combine using their verified singleton gains and a dependency certificate.
Our focus is therefore on verified integration of independently generated proposals rather than distributed search over the solution space.

Language-model agents have increasingly been applied to software development and repair.
MetaGPT and ChatDev organize software development through structured roles and multi-agent collaboration (Hong et al., 2024; Qian et al., 2024).
MAGIS and MASAI decompose repository-level software tasks across specialized agents (Tao et al., 2024; Arora et al., 2024).
RepairAgent instead focuses on autonomous program repair through tool use and iterative validation (Bouzenia et al., 2025), while a recent survey reviews the broader use of multi-agent systems across the software-development lifecycle (He et al., 2025).
These works focus primarily on how software agents generate, coordinate, and refine solutions.
We instead isolate the integration step, where independently generated edits must be reconciled into a single shared artifact.

A separate line of work studies how collaboration structures among agents should be designed or adapted.
GPTSwarm represents agent systems as optimizable computational graphs (Zhuge et al., 2024), while DyLAN dynamically selects agents and communication structures (Liu et al., 2024).
Hyperagent uses hypergraphs for the topology of multi-agent communication (Zhang et al., 2026).
These methods focus on structuring how agents interact during problem solving.
By contrast, our dependency graph represents interactions among components of the shared artifact rather than communication links among agents.
It identifies subsets of proposed edits whose verified gains add exactly and thereby supports their certified integration.

## 2. Problem Formulation

## Blocks and objective.

We represent the current shared artifact as $x=(x_{b})_{b=1}^{m}$ , where each block $x_{b}$ corresponds to one component of the solution, such as a program module.
For $b\in[m]\coloneqq\{1,\ldots,m\}$ , let $\mathcal{X}_{b}$ be the nonempty finite set of admissible values for block $b$ .
The resulting search space is $\mathcal{X}=\prod_{b=1}^{m}\mathcal{X}_{b}$ .

We evaluate an artifact using a known loss function $F:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}$ , where smaller values are better.
For example, in program repair, $F$ may count failed checks or combine penalties derived from an explicit evaluation rubric.
For $y\in\mathcal{X}_{b}$ , we write $(x_{-b},y)$ for the artifact obtained by replacing $x_{b}$ with $y$ while leaving all other blocks unchanged.
We define

(1)

 $\begin{gathered}F^{\star}\coloneqq\min_{x\in\mathcal{X}}F(x),\quad\Delta(x)\coloneqq F(x)-F^{\star},\\
D_{b}(x)\coloneqq F(x)-\min_{y\in\mathcal{X}_{b}}F(x_{-b},y).\end{gathered}$ 

Here $\Delta(x)$ is the global objective gap, while $D_{b}(x)$ is the largest improvement obtainable by modifying block $b$ alone.

## Dependency structure.

The loss need not separate across blocks, since edits to different blocks may affect the same part of the objective.
We capture these interactions through a factorization with known block dependencies.

Assumption 1 (Dependency Structure).

The objective admits a decomposition

(2)

 $F(x)=\sum_{a=1}^{M}\ell_{a}(x_{N(a)}),$ 

where $M$ is the number of objective factors and each factor $\ell_{a}$ depends only on the blocks indexed by the known set $N(a)\subseteq[m]$ .

This factorization induces a dependency graph $G$ whose vertices correspond to the blocks.
Two blocks $b$ and $c$ are connected by an edge whenever some factor depends on both.
An independent set in $G$ therefore contains blocks that never appear together in the same objective factor.
Edits to blocks in an independent set are compatible in the sense that no factor is affected by more than one of them.

We refer to the known factor scopes, or any graph that conservatively encodes them, as a dependency certificate.
Such a certificate ensures that blocks in the same independent set do not affect a common objective factor.
In program repair, it may be obtained from declared test–module scopes, interface dependencies, or conservative static analysis.
What matters is shared dependence in the objective rather than physical overlap between the edited components.
Adding extra edges to $G$ preserves the validity of the certificate but may weaken the resulting guarantee.
Omitting a true dependency, however, may incorrectly classify interacting edits as compatible.

For the pricing and checkout example from the introduction, let $x_{1},x_{2}\in\{0,1\}$ indicate whether the pricing and checkout modules apply the discount, and let $x_{3}\in\{0,1\}$ indicate whether an independent receipt defect is repaired.
Consider

(3)

 $F(x)=2(1-x_{1}-x_{2})^{2}+(1-x_{3}).$ 

The first term depends jointly on blocks $1$ and $2$ , while the second depends only on block $3$ .
The resulting dependency graph therefore contains only the edge $1$ – $2$ .
One valid coloring has color classes $\{1,3\}$ and $\{2\}$ , as shown in Figure 2.

## Assignments and verified proposals.

Suppose $n\geq 1$ agents are available, indexed by $[n]\coloneqq\{1,\ldots,n\}$ .
Given a current artifact $x$ , an assignment $\mathcal{M}\subseteq[n]\times[m]$ matches each agent to at most one block and each block to at most one agent.
Each assigned agent receives $x$ together with its designated block, works independently for a time budget $h>0$ , and returns a proposed replacement for that block.

Before combining any proposals, the evaluator tests each one separately against the same base artifact $x$ , leaving all other blocks unchanged.
A proposal is retained only if it is valid, returned within the time budget, and reduces $F$ .
Otherwise, the original block value is retained.
For each assigned pair $(i,b)\in\mathcal{M}$ , let $Y_{ib}$ denote the resulting value of block $b$ after this filtering step.
Its verified singleton gain is

(4)

 $g_{ib}\coloneqq F(x)-F(x_{-b},Y_{ib})\geq 0.$ 

We call this separate evaluation of each proposal against the common base artifact singleton verification.

## 3. Verified Proposal Integration

Singleton verification ensures that every retained proposal improves the common base when applied alone, but it does not determine how those proposals should be combined.

In the running example (3), start from $(0,0,0)$ with loss $3$ and let each agent propose setting its block to one.
The three verified singleton gains are $2$ , $2$ , and $1$ .
Applying all three proposals gives $(1,1,1)$ with loss $2$ , while applying only blocks $1$ and $3$ gives loss $0$ .
Thus, even accepting the full union whenever it improves $F$ can miss a substantially better compatible subset.

## Certified integration.

Let $\mathcal{I}(G)$ denote the collection of independent sets of $G$ .
A fractional coloring $\lambda$ assigns a nonnegative weight $\lambda_{I}$ to each independent set $I\in\mathcal{I}(G)$ so that every block is covered by independent sets with total weight at least one.
This fractional-cover view connects to classical work on fractional coloring and covers (Lovász, 1975; Alon et al., 1997); distributed algorithms for fractional coloring are studied by Bousquet et al. (2021).
The total weight of $\lambda$ is $\Lambda(\lambda)$ , and the fractional chromatic number $\chi_{f}(G)$ is the minimum such weight:

(5)

 $\displaystyle\Lambda(\lambda)$ 
 $\displaystyle\coloneqq\sum_{I\in\mathcal{I}(G)}\lambda_{I},$ 

 $\displaystyle\chi_{f}(G)$ 
 $\displaystyle\coloneqq\min_{\lambda}\Lambda(\lambda)$ 

 $\displaystyle\text{s.t.}\qquad\lambda_{I}$ 
 $\displaystyle\geq 0,\qquad I\in\mathcal{I}(G),$ 

 $\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}I\in\mathcal{I}(G)\\
b\in I\end{subarray}}\lambda_{I}$ 
 $\displaystyle\geq 1,\qquad b\in[m].$ 

To state the integration rule over repeated rounds, we index collaboration rounds by $k$ .
At the beginning of round $k$ , let $x^{k}$ denote the common base artifact, let $\mathcal{M}_{k}$ denote the assignment, and let $g_{ib}^{k}$ denote the verified singleton gain of proposal $(i,b)\in\mathcal{M}_{k}$ as defined above.

Fix any feasible fractional coloring $\lambda$ and write $\Lambda=\Lambda(\lambda)$ .
Only independent sets with $\lambda_{I}>0$ need to be considered in the merge rule.
For each such set $I$ , let $x^{k,I}$ denote the candidate obtained from $x^{k}$ by applying the retained proposals whose blocks belong to $I$ .
Because $I$ is independent, these proposals are compatible and their verified singleton gains add exactly.
We therefore score $I$ by

(6)

 $W_{I}^{k}\coloneqq\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}(i,b)\in\mathcal{M}_{k}\\
b\in I\end{subarray}}g_{ib}^{k}.$ 

The coordinator then selects a highest-scoring independent set among those with $\lambda_{I}>0$ and applies its proposals:

(7)

 $I_{k}\in\argmax_{I:\lambda_{I}>0}W_{I}^{k},\qquad x^{k,\mathrm{safe}}\coloneqq x^{k,I_{k}}.$ 

Ties in the choice of $I_{k}$ are broken by a fixed rule.
The following theorem shows that this rule guarantees a fixed fraction of the total verified singleton gain for every realized collection of agent proposals, regardless of how those proposals were generated.

Theorem 1 (Certified gain retention).

Let $\lambda$ be any feasible fractional coloring with total weight $\Lambda=\Lambda(\lambda)$ .
Under Assumption 1, for every realization of the agent proposals,

(8)

 $F(x^{k})-F(x^{k,\mathrm{safe}})\geq\frac{1}{\Lambda}\sum_{(i,b)\in\mathcal{M}_{k}}g_{ib}^{k}\geq 0.$ 

In particular, an optimal fractional coloring gives $\Lambda=\chi_{f}(G)$ and therefore retains at least a $1/\chi_{f}(G)$ fraction of the total verified singleton gain.

Proof.

For readability, suppress the round index $k$ and write $x=x^{k}$ , $\mathcal{M}=\mathcal{M}_{k}$ , $g_{ib}=g_{ib}^{k}$ , and $W_{I}=W_{I}^{k}$ .
Fix any $I\in\mathcal{I}(G)$ with $\lambda_{I}>0$ .
For each objective factor $\ell_{a}(x_{N(a)})$ in (2), where $a\in[M]$ , the independence of $I$ implies $|N(a)\cap I|\leq 1$ .
Hence each factor is affected by at most one proposal whose block lies in $I$ , so the total objective decrease is exactly the sum of the corresponding singleton gains:

(9)

 $F(x)-F(x^{I})=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}(i,b)\in\mathcal{M}\\
b\in I\end{subarray}}g_{ib}=W_{I}.$ 

Since $\sum_{I\in\mathcal{I}(G)}\lambda_{I}=\Lambda$ and $\lambda_{I}\geq 0$ ,

 $\displaystyle\Lambda\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}I\in\mathcal{I}(G)\\
\lambda_{I}>0\end{subarray}}W_{I}\geq\sum_{I\in\mathcal{I}(G)}\lambda_{I}W_{I}=\sum_{(i,b)\in\mathcal{M}}g_{ib}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}I\in\mathcal{I}(G)\\
b\in I\end{subarray}}\lambda_{I}\geq\sum_{(i,b)\in\mathcal{M}}g_{ib}.$ 

The first inequality follows because the maximum score dominates every $W_{I}$ with positive weight.
The equality exchanges the order of summation.
The final inequality uses the covering constraint
 $\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}I\in\mathcal{I}(G)\\
b\in I\end{subarray}}\lambda_{I}\geq 1$ 
and the nonnegativity $g_{ib}\geq 0$ .

By construction, $I_{k}$ attains the maximum score among the independent sets with $\lambda_{I}>0$ .
Using (9) for $I_{k}$ and dividing the preceding inequality by $\Lambda$ gives (8).
∎

Returning to the running example in (3), assigning unit weight to the two color classes gives $\Lambda=2$ .
Their scores are $2+1=3$ and $2$ , so the certified candidate selects blocks $1$ and $3$ and reaches the optimum.
The guarantee above is also tight in the worst case, as the next theorem shows.

Theorem 2 (Tightness of certified gain retention).

For any finite nonempty dependency graph $G$ , no coordinator that forms its update by selecting a subset of the submitted proposals can guarantee retaining more than a $1/\chi_{f}(G)$ fraction of the total verified singleton gain in the worst case.
More precisely, for every such $G$ , there exists an instance satisfying Assumption 1 in which the submitted proposals have total verified gain $\chi_{f}(G)$ , while every subset of them achieves gain at most one.
The bound remains tight when all singleton gains are required to be strictly positive, in which case the worst-case ratio can be made arbitrarily close to $1/\chi_{f}(G)$ .

Proof.

The dual of (5) maximizes $\sum_{b=1}^{m}w_{b}$ over $w_{b}\geq 0$ subject to $\sum_{b\in I}w_{b}\leq 1$ for every $I\in\mathcal{I}(G)$ .
Let $w$ be an optimal dual solution.
By strong duality, $\sum_{b=1}^{m}w_{b}=\chi_{f}(G)$ .

Consider binary blocks $z\in\{0,1\}^{m}$ with initial state $z=0$ , and let each block $b$ have a proposal that changes $z_{b}$ from zero to one.
For any $B>\sum_{b=1}^{m}w_{b}$ , define

(10)

 $F(z)\coloneqq C-\sum_{b=1}^{m}w_{b}z_{b}+B\sum_{\{b,c\}\in E(G)}z_{b}z_{c}.$ 

The interaction terms correspond exactly to the edges of $G$ , so this objective has dependency graph $G$ .
The singleton gain of block $b$ is $w_{b}$ , and hence the total verified singleton gain is $\chi_{f}(G)$ .

Now consider any subset $S\subseteq[m]$ of the submitted proposals.
If $S$ is independent, its gain is $\sum_{b\in S}w_{b}\leq 1$ by dual feasibility.
If $S$ is not independent, it contains at least one edge of $G$ , so at least one penalty term is activated and its gain is at most $\sum_{b=1}^{m}w_{b}-B<0$ .
Thus every subset achieves gain at most one, while the total singleton gain is $\chi_{f}(G)$ .
Therefore no subset-based coordinator can guarantee a fraction greater than $1/\chi_{f}(G)$ , matching Theorem 1.

If strictly positive singleton gains are required, replace each $w_{b}$ by $w_{b}+\varepsilon$ .
The best independent subset then gains at most $1+\varepsilon\alpha(G)$ , while the total singleton gain becomes $\chi_{f}(G)+\varepsilon m$ .
Choosing $B$ large enough to keep every non-independent subset unprofitable, the resulting ratio approaches $1/\chi_{f}(G)$ as $\varepsilon\downarrow 0$ .
∎

## Ordinary coloring as a practical alternative.

Ordinary graph coloring gives a directly usable special case.

Corollary 3 (Ordinary-color guarantee).

Let $C_{1},\ldots,C_{\kappa}$ be the color classes of any proper $\kappa$ -coloring.
Setting $\lambda_{C_{c}}=1$ for $c\in[\kappa]$ and $\lambda_{I}=0$ for all other independent sets gives a feasible fractional coloring with $\Lambda=\kappa$ .
Rule (6) then selects the color class with the largest total verified gain.
Writing the resulting candidate as $x^{k,\mathrm{col}}$ , we obtain

(11)

 $F(x^{k})-F(x^{k,\mathrm{col}})\geq\frac{1}{\kappa}\sum_{(i,b)\in\mathcal{M}_{k}}g_{ib}^{k}.$ 

Corollary 3 is obtained by restricting the fractional rule to the $\kappa$ color classes and assigning each unit weight.
For a separable loss, $G$ has no edges, so all retained proposals can be merged simultaneously and $\chi_{f}(G)=1$ .
The fractional-coloring guarantee then
retains the full sum of the verified gains.

A maximum-weight independent set may improve a round’s candidate but must be resolved as gains change.
Fractional coloring is reusable for a fixed graph, though costly to compute; a greedy coloring instead guarantees $1/\kappa$ .

## Interacting edits.

The dependency graph certifies when gains are additive, but an edge only indicates that two blocks may interact.
It does not determine whether that interaction is beneficial or harmful.
Two proposals may each repair a local defect and yield an additional improvement when applied together.
To quantify such effects, let $S\subseteq\mathcal{M}_{k}$ be a subset of retained proposals and define its interaction surplus by

(12)

 $I_{k}(S)\coloneqq F(x^{k})-F(x^{k,S})-\sum_{(i,b)\in S}g_{ib}^{k},$ 

where $x^{k,S}$ applies exactly the proposals in $S$ .
A positive surplus means that the joint gain exceeds the sum of the singleton gains, while a negative surplus means that it falls short.
If the blocks represented in $S$ form an independent set in $G$ , then $I_{k}(S)=0$ .
In the running example (3), the full union has gain $1$ while the singleton gains sum to $5$ , giving interaction surplus $-4$ .

Independent-set integration avoids such harmful interactions, but it can also miss beneficial ones.
Let $x^{k,\mathrm{all}}$ denote the candidate obtained by applying all retained proposals to the common base.
To capture favorable interactions that arise in this full union without sacrificing the certified guarantee, the optimistic rule compares it with the safe candidate:

(13)

 $x^{k+1}\in\operatorname*{arg\,min}_{z\in\{x^{k,\mathrm{safe}},x^{k,\mathrm{all}}\}}F(z).$ 

The safe candidate is chosen in the event of a tie and whenever the union is invalid.
Its objective value is already determined by additivity, so only the union requires an additional evaluation.

Proposition 4 (Optimistic integration).

Under Assumption 1 and exact, side-effect-free candidate evaluation, rule (13) satisfies

(14)

 $F(x^{k})-F(x^{k+1})=\max\{G_{\mathrm{safe}}^{k},G_{\mathrm{all}}^{k}\}\geq G_{\mathrm{safe}}^{k}\geq\frac{1}{\Lambda}\sum_{(i,b)\in\mathcal{M}_{k}}g_{ib}^{k},$ 

where $G_{\mathrm{safe}}^{k}\coloneqq F(x^{k})-F(x^{k,\mathrm{safe}})$ and $G_{\mathrm{all}}^{k}\coloneqq F(x^{k})-F(x^{k,\mathrm{all}})$ , with $G_{\mathrm{all}}^{k}=-\infty$ when the union is invalid.

Proof.

Rule (13) selects the candidate with the larger gain, so its gain is at least $G_{\mathrm{safe}}^{k}$ .
Theorem 1 gives the final inequality.
∎

Proposition 4 therefore shows that the optimistic rule preserves the same per-round guarantee as the safe rule while allowing the full union to be selected when it performs better.
This comparison is local to the current round, since the additional evaluation and the choice of the accepted artifact can affect the subsequent trajectory.

## 4. Global Convergence from Local Progress

The per-round integration guarantee depends only on the verified singleton gains and the dependency structure.
To obtain a global convergence rate, we introduce three additional ingredients: local solver quality, a block error bound, and assignment coverage.
We then combine these factors with the integration guarantee to obtain the contraction rate.

## Local solver quality.

Let $\mathcal{H}_{k}$ denote all information available before round $k$ , including the current artifact, previous observations, and any context retained by the agents.
We model an assigned agent as an inexact block solver and measure its quality relative to $D_{b}(x^{k})$ , the best improvement available by changing block $b$ alone.

Assumption 2 (Conditional local improvement).

For known lower bounds $\eta_{ib}(h)\in[0,1]$ , the following holds at every round, every history reached by the protocol, and every assignment with positive conditional probability:

(15)

 $\mathbb{E}\left[g_{ib}^{k}\,\middle|\,\mathcal{H}_{k},\mathcal{M}_{k}\right]\geq\eta_{ib}(h)D_{b}(x^{k}),\qquad(i,b)\in\mathcal{M}_{k}.$ 

The parameter $\eta_{ib}(h)$ quantifies the expected quality of agent $i$ on block $b$ within deadline $h$ relative to the best available single-block improvement.
For example, $\eta_{ib}(h)=1/2$ means that the agent recovers, in expectation, at least half of $D_{b}(x^{k})$ , rather than solving half of its assigned tasks exactly.
Because the expectation includes timeouts and imperfect edits, these outcomes are already reflected in the guarantee.
The bound is conditioned on both the history and the full assignment, so agent outputs may be arbitrarily correlated.
At the extremes, $\eta_{ib}(h)=0$ imposes no capability requirement on the agent–block pair, whereas any positive uniform lower bound represents a substantive assumption on its performance.

## Block error bound.

Local solver quality alone is not sufficient for global progress, since even an exact block solver cannot improve a state when no individual block admits an improving move.
We therefore require the available single-block improvements to capture a nonvanishing fraction of the remaining global error.

Assumption 3 (Block error bound).

There exists $\mu\in(0,1]$ such that, for every $k\geq 0$ , the iterates generated by the protocol satisfy almost surely

(16)

 $\sum_{b=1}^{m}D_{b}(x^{k})\geq\mu\Delta(x^{k}).$ 

The parameter $\mu$ measures how much of the remaining objective gap is visible through improvements to individual blocks.
For a separable loss, the blockwise improvements add exactly to the global gap, so $\mu=1$ .
More generally, a positive $\mu$ rules out suboptimal states at which every individual block is already locally optimal.

The role of this condition is illustrated by a simple two-block example.
Let $x_{1},x_{2}\in\{0,1\}$ and consider

(17)

 $F(x_{1},x_{2})\coloneqq 1+x_{1}+x_{2}-3x_{1}x_{2}.$ 

At $(0,0)$ , the loss is $1$ .
Changing either block alone increases the loss to $2$ , whereas changing both blocks yields the global minimum $0$ .
Hence $D_{1}(0,0)=D_{2}(0,0)=0$ while $\Delta(0,0)=1$ , so no positive $\mu$ can satisfy (16) at this state.
This obstruction arises from the task structure rather than the quality of the local solver.
A method restricted to individually improving block updates cannot leave $(0,0)$ , and progress requires either a joint update or acceptance of an intermediate increase in the objective.

## Assignment coverage.

Local solver quality translates into global progress only if useful agents are assigned to each block sufficiently often.
Let $\mathfrak{M}$ denote the finite set of agent–block matchings.
Fix a distribution $p=(p_{\mathcal{M}})_{\mathcal{M}\in\mathfrak{M}}$ and assume that, at every round, the matching is drawn according to

(18)

 $\Pr(\mathcal{M}_{k}=\mathcal{M}\mid\mathcal{H}_{k})=p_{\mathcal{M}},\qquad\sum_{\mathcal{M}\in\mathfrak{M}}p_{\mathcal{M}}=1.$ 

For each agent–block pair, define its marginal assignment probability by

(19)

 $q_{ib}\coloneqq\sum_{\mathcal{M}\ni(i,b)}p_{\mathcal{M}},\qquad a_{b}\coloneqq\sum_{i}q_{ib}\eta_{ib}(h),\qquad a\coloneqq\min_{b}a_{b}.$ 

The quantity $a_{b}$ combines how often block $b$ is assigned to agent $i$ with the corresponding local-solver quality $\eta_{ib}(h)$ .
Equivalently, it measures the guaranteed fraction of the best block- $b$ improvement recovered on average under the assignment distribution.
The quantity $a$ is the corresponding worst-case coverage across blocks.
Since a matching assigns at most one agent to each block and $\eta_{ib}(h)\leq 1$ , we have $0\leq a_{b}\leq 1$ and hence $0\leq a\leq 1$ .
If $a=0$ , then at least one block receives no guaranteed improvement under the assignment scheme, so these bounds cannot certify positive worst-case progress.

## Combining the factors.

The preceding quantities combine directly with the per-round integration guarantee.
Under the assignment distribution and Assumption 2, the expected verified gain is at least an $a$ fraction of the total available blockwise improvement.
Assumption 3 lower bounds this quantity by a $\mu$ fraction of the remaining objective gap, while the integration rule retains at least a $1/\Lambda(\lambda)$ fraction of the verified gain.
We therefore define

(20)

 $\theta_{\lambda}\coloneqq\frac{\mu a}{\Lambda(\lambda)}.$ 

Thus, in expectation, each round reduces the current objective gap by at least a $\theta_{\lambda}$ fraction.
For an optimal fractional coloring, $\Lambda(\lambda)=\chi_{f}(G)$ and hence $\theta_{\lambda}=\mu a/\chi_{f}(G)$ .
Using a proper $\kappa$ -coloring instead gives the corresponding factor $\mu a/\kappa$ .

Theorem 1 (Geometric convergence).

Fix either the safe-only or optimistic integration rule, a deadline $h$ , a feasible fractional coloring $\lambda$ , and the assignment distribution $p$ above.
Suppose Assumptions 1–3 hold along the resulting trajectory.
Then, for deterministic $x^{0}$ and every integer $K\geq 0$ ,

(21)

 $\mathbb{E}\left[\Delta(x^{K})\right]\leq(1-\theta_{\lambda})^{K}\Delta(x^{0}).$ 

The conditional-expectation proof, given in the supplementary material, formalizes the preceding argument and yields geometric decay of the expected objective gap with contraction factor $1-\theta_{\lambda}$ .
The guarantee is an average one across the randomness of the protocol and does not require every realized round to achieve the same fractional reduction.
Through $\theta_{\lambda}=\mu a/\Lambda(\lambda)$ , the rate separates the effects of task structure, agent quality and assignment coverage, and proposal integration.
This decomposition also provides the basis for the next section, where we choose assignments by balancing their expected progress against their time and token costs.

## 5. Work and Cost Allocation

For a fixed task, fractional coloring, and deadline, the assignment policy can improve the coverage factor $a$ by directing useful agents toward poorly covered blocks.
Optimizing this policy is therefore a separate problem over assignments.

## Assignment optimization.

Fix a deadline $h$ and a per-round expected token budget $b_{0}$ .
Let $C(\mathcal{M},h)$ denote an upper bound on the token cost of using matching $\mathcal{M}$ at deadline $h$ , including communication and verification.
Given the capability bounds $\eta_{ib}(h)$ , we choose the assignment distribution to maximize the weakest block coverage:

(22)

 $\displaystyle\max_{p,a}$ 
 $\displaystyle a$ 

 $\displaystyle\text{subject to}$ 
 $\displaystyle\sum_{\mathcal{M}}p_{\mathcal{M}}\sum_{i:(i,b)\in\mathcal{M}}\eta_{ib}(h)\geq a$ 

 $\displaystyle\forall b,$ 

 $\displaystyle\sum_{\mathcal{M}}p_{\mathcal{M}}C(\mathcal{M},h)\leq b_{0},$ 

 $\displaystyle\sum_{\mathcal{M}}p_{\mathcal{M}}=1,\qquad p_{\mathcal{M}}\geq 0.$ 

The coverage constraints ensure $a_{b}\geq a$ for every block, while the budget constraint limits the expected token cost.
For fixed $h$ and capability bounds $\eta_{ib}(h)$ , the linear program maximizes the coverage factor in the certified convergence rate over fixed assignment distributions.

## Rate–cost trade-off.

Improving the per-round convergence rate does not necessarily reduce the total resources required to reach a target error.
For example, increasing the deadline $h$ can improve the capability bounds $\eta_{ib}(h)$ and hence the coverage factor $a$ , while also increasing the duration and token cost of each round.
A similar trade-off arises with optimistic integration, where evaluating the full union adds per-round cost but may reduce the number of rounds when the union yields greater progress.

Let $d_{\lambda}(h)$ bound the duration of a safe-only round, including proposal generation, verification, communication, and other overhead, and let $\tau_{\mathrm{union}}(h)$ bound the additional time required to evaluate the full union.
For optimistic integration, define

(23)

 $\begin{gathered}d(h)\coloneqq d_{\lambda}(h)+\tau_{\mathrm{union}}(h),\\
C(\mathcal{M},h)\coloneqq C_{\lambda}(\mathcal{M},h)+C_{\mathrm{union}}(\mathcal{M},h),\end{gathered}$ 

where $C_{\lambda}(\mathcal{M},h)$ and $C_{\mathrm{union}}(\mathcal{M},h)$ are the corresponding token-cost bounds.
For an assignment distribution $p$ , the expected token cost per round is

(24)

 $\overline{C}(h)\coloneqq\sum_{\mathcal{M}}p_{\mathcal{M}}C(\mathcal{M},h).$ 

Suppose $a>0$ and let $0<\varepsilon<\Delta(x^{0})$ be a target expected error.
By Theorem 1 and $1-\theta_{\lambda}\leq e^{-\theta_{\lambda}}$ ,

 $\mathbb{E}\left[\Delta(x^{K})\right]\leq e^{-K\theta_{\lambda}}\Delta(x^{0}).$ 

A sufficient number of rounds to ensure $\mathbb{E}\left[\Delta(x^{K})\right]\leq\varepsilon$ is therefore

(25)

 $K_{\varepsilon}\coloneqq\left\lceil\frac{\Lambda(\lambda)}{\mu a}\log\frac{\Delta(x^{0})}{\varepsilon}\right\rceil.$ 

Let $T_{\varepsilon}$ denote the corresponding elapsed-time bound and $\overline{C}_{\varepsilon}$ the expected total token-cost bound.
Then

(26)

 $T_{\varepsilon}\leq d(h)K_{\varepsilon},\qquad\overline{C}_{\varepsilon}\leq\overline{C}(h)K_{\varepsilon}.$ 

These bounds make the deadline trade-off explicit.
Increasing $h$ may reduce $K_{\varepsilon}$ by improving the capability bounds and hence the coverage factor $a$ , while simultaneously increasing the per-round duration $d(h)$ and expected token cost $\overline{C}(h)$ .
Candidate deadlines can therefore be compared using the total-time bound $d(h)K_{\varepsilon}$ together with the corresponding token-cost constraint.

## 6. Experiments

We examine whether verified integration resolves conflicts among local repairs, when parallel work offsets its coordination cost, and whether fractional coloring retains more gain from the same proposals.
The four repair benchmarks use Python tasks with declared module dependencies and an objective $F$ that counts failed checks.
Agents see the specification, current code, and visible checks.
The coordinator uses visible and hidden checks to determine acceptance and stopping. A run is successful when its final artifact passes all checks.
We also examine proposal integration on public road networks and course-timetabling instances, and the scheduling of parallel route searches on vehicle-routing instances.

## Protocols and evaluation.

For the conflict and scale benchmarks, we compare safe color-class integration (COL) and optimistic integration (OPT) with four baselines.
MA applies all parallel proposals, while MAG accepts their union only when it lowers $F$ .
SGL assigns the whole program to one agent, and SEQ visits modules sequentially. Both baselines accept only improving edits.
All protocols start from the same faulty artifact.
Each call starts with a fresh context, and only edits to its assigned modules are kept.
The parallel rules share first-round proposals and subsequently work from their own accepted artifacts.
Appendix A provides further experimental details.

We report input-token-equivalent cost, which weights ordinary input, cache-write, and cache-read tokens and excludes output tokens.
For modeled completion time, each round contributes the duration of its slowest agent call, together with an assumed verification latency $t_{\mathrm{ver}}$ for each verification stage that must complete before the procedure can continue.
Checks performed in parallel within the same stage incur this latency only once.
Agent calls within a round are assumed to start simultaneously.
The accounting formula and sensitivity to $t_{\mathrm{ver}}$ are given in Appendices A–C.

## 6.7. Scheduling parallel route searches

We test a different use of fractional coloring: scheduling preemptible search by agents that hold exclusive locks on their assigned pairs of vehicle routes. On each of four public instances of the capacitated vehicle-routing problem (CVRP), five stochastic local-search agents are assigned consecutive pairs of routes, deliberately inducing a $C_{5}$ conflict graph. Nonadjacent agents can search concurrently on two CPU workers. Both schedules allocate exactly 500,000 candidate-move evaluations to every agent. A fixed ordinary coloring executes the compatible groups $(0,2)$ , $(1,3)$ , $(4)$ in three full-budget waves; a fractional cover executes $(0,2)$ , $(1,3)$ , $(2,4)$ , $(3,0)$ , $(4,1)$ in five half-budget waves. Thus $\chi(C_{5})=3$ and $\chi_{f}(C_{5})=5/2$ predict a shorter critical path under this locking and preemption model.

## 7. Conclusion

We formulate parallel agent collaboration as verified proposal integration. Evaluating proposals against a common artifact yields local gains, while the dependency graph identifies compatible sets whose gains add exactly. Fractional coloring gives the tight worst-case retention factor $1/\chi_{f}(G)$ , and evaluating the full union allows the coordinator to capture favorable interactions. Under the local-improvement and block error-bound assumptions, repeated integration converges geometrically. The rate separates task structure, agent allocation, and proposal integration, providing a basis for choosing assignments and balancing progress against time and cost.

The experiments show how compatibility shapes both the quality and cost of parallel work. Selecting compatible proposals resolves conflicts between individually useful repairs and improves retained gain over a fixed coloring on road-network objectives. Adaptive ordinary coloring matches the latter result, linking the improvement to the compatible sets considered. Parallel execution reduces modeled completion time on larger compatible repair tasks at higher input cost, while fractional scheduling shortens measured route-search time under exclusive route access. These results connect the integration of local edits and the scheduling of parallel work through their shared dependency structure.

## Task construction.

The seven tasks contain 26–35 checks and 9–49 lines of faulty editable code each, with 207 checks in total. Each task provides a specification, three faulty modules, and 13–19 lines of fixed code connecting them. Unit checks involve one module, while integration checks involve two adjacent modules. Each factor $\ell_{a}$ in (2) indicates whether one visible or hidden check fails.

In T1–T6, either of two adjacent modules can perform the required interface transformation. We verified that assigning the transformation to either module gives a solution with $F=0$ , whereas applying it in both modules produces the integration failures reported in Table 6. We also checked the initial failure counts and traced the module calls made by each check against its declared dependencies.

## Role of hidden checks.

Agents can run the visible checks during repair. The coordinator evaluates both visible and hidden checks when accepting edits and stops a successful run when all checks pass.

## Agent assignments and information.

The main comparison uses claude-haiku-4-5, with three replicates per task, 21 task–replicate units, and 261 agent calls. The replication uses claude-sonnet-5, with one replicate per task, seven units, and 74 calls. Each call starts with a fresh context and a separate copy of the current program. The instructions specify the task, the assigned module, and the available tests.

A module agent can inspect the starting program but may edit only its assigned module. Agents working in parallel receive the same starting program and cannot see one another’s edits during the round. Each agent can run the visible unit and integration checks involving its module and is told that evaluation will also use hidden checks of the same kinds. Only changes to the assigned module enter the proposal used by the coordinator. The single-agent baseline receives the same task specification, with permission to edit all three modules and run all visible checks.

## Repair schedules and stopping rules.

All protocols stop when $F=0$ or when they reach their prescribed limit. SGL allows up to four whole-program repair sessions, each starting from the most recently accepted program. SEQ allows two passes through the modules in a fixed order and skips a module when all checks involving it already pass. Both baselines accept an edit only when it reduces $F$ .

The four parallel protocols allow up to three rounds. Each round assigns one agent to every module involved in a failing check, with all agents starting from the same program. The protocols share their first-round proposals, allowing a paired comparison of their merge decisions, and each protocol is charged for the calls it uses. Later rounds generate fresh proposals from each protocol’s own accepted program.

## Merge decisions.

The parallel protocols differ in how they combine those proposals. MA applies their union directly, while MAG accepts the union only when it reduces $F$ . COL evaluates each changed module separately against the starting program, retains strictly improving proposals, and selects the color class with the largest summed gain, as in (6). OPT also evaluates the union of the retained proposals and selects it when its gain exceeds that of the color candidate (13).

## Reusing deferred proposals.

After a color-class merge, verified proposals from the other class are retained and evaluated against the updated program before the next agent round. This permits further progress without requesting the same edit again. Together with skipping modules whose checks pass, reuse makes the assignment of work depend on the current program. The pointwise merge guarantee applies when the proposals satisfy Assumption 1; the convergence rate in Theorem 1 additionally requires its fixed conditional assignment distribution and uniform local-improvement bounds. The reported costs include the savings from proposal reuse as well as the cost of verification.

## Input cost and verification charges.

We measure input-token-equivalent cost as input tokens $+\,1.25\times$ cache-write tokens $+\,0.1\times$ cache-read tokens. This measure excludes output tokens. One budget unit is the median call cost: 109,679 input-token equivalents for claude-haiku-4-5 and 75,441 for claude-sonnet-5.

To account for verification, we add $v\in\{0,1/4,1\}$ budget units for each full-objective evaluation required by a protocol. This includes the initial evaluation and evaluations of individual proposals, unions, and candidates subject to an acceptance gate. The loss of a color candidate follows from additivity (9), so the direct evaluations used only to check that identity are excluded from protocol cost.

## Modeled completion time.

We reconstruct completion time from measured agent-call durations, assuming that calls in a parallel round start together. A round therefore contributes the duration of its slowest call, followed by an assumed latency $t_{\mathrm{ver}}\in\{0,60,300\}$ seconds for each sequential verification step. Individual proposals are verified in parallel and together contribute one step; a subsequent union evaluation contributes another. The durations were recorded with up to 20 concurrent agents. Varying $v$ and $t_{\mathrm{ver}}$ shows how verification cost changes the comparison. Budget-success curves include every run, while median costs to solve are computed over successful runs.

## Experimental design and model replication.

The tasks, agent instructions, protocols, stopping limits, cost measures, and planned analyses were fixed before the first main-experiment call. After observing nearly identical first-round behavior across the claude-haiku-4-5 replicates, we added a claude-sonnet-5 replication to examine whether the conflict pattern depended on the model. The tasks, protocols, and cost accounting were unchanged.

## Appendix B Repair outcomes and coordination costs

Across the two models, both verified merge rules solve all 11 runs with diagnosed edit conflicts, while MA solves none and MAG solves one (Table 7). The following diagnostic identifies the ownership decisions that produce these differences.

## Conflict diagnosis and model dependence.

We diagnose an ownership conflict by testing the two adjacent proposals while using the reference implementation for all other modules. We classify the proposals as conflicting when each passes the interface check individually with the transformation absent from the adjacent module, but applying both proposals causes the check to fail. This diagnostic identified conflicts in 9 of the 18 claude-haiku-4-5 units on T1–T6: all replicates of T1 and T6, two of T3, and one of T5. claude-sonnet-5 produced conflicts in 2 of its 6 units, on T2 and T6. The models differed in how they assigned ownership: claude-haiku-4-5 double-applied the coupon, while claude-sonnet-5 double-escaped HTML. Across both models, every outcome difference among the parallel rules arose on a diagnosed conflict. All 108 audited color merges satisfied additivity (9) exactly.

## Resolving a repeated ownership conflict.

The first conflicted union lowers $F$ from 21 to 5 by repairing local defects despite applying the shared transformation twice.
In the next round, both owners respond to the failing integration checks by removing their copy of the transformation.
MA then alternates between both fixes and neither, yielding $21,5,5,5$ .
MAG accepts the first union but rejects the union of both removals because it does not lower $F$ .
The verified rules evaluate the removals separately and select one owner, giving the OPT trajectory $21,5,0$ .
On T3, the conflicted union leaves five failures while the color candidate leaves four.
OPT keeps a union when it improves more than the color candidate and can resolve any remaining interference in a later round.

## Resource use and matched budgets.

On these small tasks, SGL solves every unit with a median of one call. For claude-haiku-4-5, it uses about one quarter of the input-token-equivalent cost and one third of the modeled time of the verified teams at $t_{\mathrm{ver}}=0$ . SEQ solves 26 of 28 units but has the longest modeled completion time. The verified teams typically need an additional round and roughly twice as many objective evaluations as merge-all, which can offset their higher eventual success rate under a fixed budget.

At a 15-minute budget with $t_{\mathrm{ver}}=300$ seconds, the corresponding counts are 12 and zero. Reducing verification latency to 60 seconds changes the comparison: within ten minutes, COL, OPT, and MA solve 17, 13, and 12 units, respectively. claude-sonnet-5 shows the same qualitative ordering. ## First merges and the interaction surplus.

When ownership is consistent, repairs in adjacent modules can reinforce each other by making their shared integration checks pass. The first union solved all 12 conflict-free claude-haiku-4-5 units and all 5 conflict-free claude-sonnet-5 units. Merge-all and OPT therefore finished in one round, while the color candidate left a mean of 12.3 and 11.6 failing checks, respectively. COL needed a further round. On the 9 claude-haiku-4-5 units with conflicts, the first merge left a mean of 5.3 failures for merge-all, 9.1 for COL, and 5.1 for OPT.

Across both experiments, OPT evaluated 37 unions, accepted 26, and selected the color candidate 11 times. Two fallbacks occurred in the first round on T3 conflict units; the others occurred later, when the union offered no improvement over a single color class. The trajectories in Table 11 show how verified merging resolves these conflicts, while merge-all retains 5–6 failures through the round cap. MAG escaped only on T5 replicate 3: after its second-round union was rejected, fresh agents in the third round removed only one copy of the transformation.

## Sequential repair.

Conflicts can also arise when edits are applied sequentially. In the claude-sonnet-5 run on T4, the records agent added quoting in the first pass, and the csvrow agent then quoted the fields again. The acceptance gate kept the second edit because its local repairs reduced $F$ despite the double quoting. In the second pass, the records agent removed its quoting and solved the task. The two claude-haiku-4-5 failures, T2 replicate 1 and C0 replicate 1, reached the pass cap with one hidden check still failing: name trimming and total precision, respectively.

## Replicate dependence and task-level comparisons.

The seven tasks and three modules define 21 distinct (task, module) groups. Pass/fail behavior varied across replicates only for T3 timeparse and T5 slugs, which account for the reported differences in conflict incidence. This strong dependence makes the task the more informative unit of comparison. Verified merging outperforms merge-all on T1, T3, T5, and T6 and ties on the other tasks. The four wins and no losses give a two-sided sign-test $p$ -value of $2\cdot 2^{-4}=0.125$ ; treating the 9 conflict units as independent would instead give $p\approx 0.004$ . The claude-sonnet-5 replication changes which tasks produce conflicts while preserving the observed mechanism and ordering of the rules.

## Sources of the modeled time advantage.

The timing results in Figure 7 reflect a balance between the single agent’s efficiency and the duration of the slowest parallel call. The single agent spent 26.5–43.0 seconds per module, with a geometric mean of 28.6 seconds at $m=32$ , and ran tests 3–13 times per session. Parallel calls repaired only five bugs each but took a median of 97 seconds, used 16 tools, and consumed 128k input-token equivalents. Median call duration varied from 80 to 152 seconds across instances. Within a round, the slowest call took 1.45–3.5 times the median, setting the modeled agent time at 140–280 seconds.

As the number of modules grows, the single agent’s roughly linear repair time eventually exceeds this parallel-round duration. At $t_{\mathrm{ver}}=0$ , the crossover occurs between $m=4$ and $m=8$ ; at $t_{\mathrm{ver}}=60$ seconds, the speedup at $m=8$ is only 1.04. Verification also separates the parallel rules: OPT evaluates individual proposals and then their union, while MA evaluates only the union. Consequently, MA has a shorter modeled completion time when verification latency is positive.

The time saving comes with greater input cost. The single agent’s peak context averaged 73k, 94k, 115k, and 122k tokens at $m=4,8,16,32$ , compared with a median peak of 64k for parallel calls. Cache reuse keeps the single agent’s input-token-equivalent cost approximately linear over these sizes, whereas each module agent must read the task and program in a fresh context. The team therefore costs about twice as much at every tested size. Scaling beyond these instances will depend on the available concurrency and the growth of the maximum call duration.

## Appendix D Comparing ordinary and fractional coloring

## Units and proposal generation.

The preceding benchmarks have bipartite dependency graphs, for which ordinary and fractional coloring both give $\chi_{f}(G)=\chi(G)=2$ . To separate their guarantees, we use the five-cycle $C_{5}$ , where $\chi(C_{5})=3$ and $\chi_{f}(C_{5})=5/2$ . The benchmark contains twelve explicit-contract units spanning four domains and three ordinary-coloring rotations fixed before the runs. Five fresh gpt-6-luna module agents generate proposals for each unit in a single run, and both coloring rules receive the same first-round proposals.

## Coloring rules and retained gain.

Let $S$ be the sum of positive verified singleton gains. COL3 selects the best class of the fixed ordinary coloring, guaranteeing a gain of at least $S/3$ . FRAC5 evaluates the five independent pairs of $C_{5}$ , raising the guarantee to $2S/5$ . These pairs are all the maximal independent sets of $C_{5}$ , so with nonnegative gains the best pair is also a maximum-weight independent set.

On the paired proposals, FRAC5 matched or exceeded COL3 in every unit and improved on it in four of the twelve, raising the mean retained fraction from 0.5615 to 0.6167. Every additivity audit passed. Figure 3 shows these paired fractions alongside the directly evaluated full-union gains. Using the same proposals for both rules isolates the effect of the coloring choice.

## Appendix G External exact-factor road-network merger

## Road networks and selected regions.

The public SNAP roadNet-PA, roadNet-CA, and roadNet-TX networks (SNAP, 2026) represent undirected road connections among intersections and endpoints. We evaluate proposal integration in 30 held-out regions, ten per network. Each region contains five connected blocks of 32 vertices, with the interblock roads forming a $C_{5}$ dependency graph. Three initializations per region give 90 paired comparisons.

## Objective, agents, and common incumbent.

Each road edge touching a selected block is one binary cut factor; edges outside the selected vertices contribute only constants. Each of five separate agents controls one block and attempts 30,000 within-block one-for-zero label swaps, preserving balance. A proposal is evaluated against a common incumbent; a nonpositive, invalid, or failed singleton is retained as null. After a development-only diagnostic, we froze a two-stage design before inspecting held-out outcomes. Stage one applies a guarded full union from the initial labels to create the same improved incumbent for every rule. This shared step gained 8,395 cut edges over the 90 units and is charged to every method. Stage two runs five new agents from that incumbent.

## Same-pool integration rules.

Let $g_{i}\geq 0$ be the verified singleton reduction in cut edges for block $i$ , and let $S=\sum_{i}g_{i}$ . The block conflict graph has an edge precisely when a road joins the blocks, so independent sets have no shared cut factors and their gains add exactly. COL selects the best pre-fixed class among $\{0,2\}$ , $\{1,3\}$ , and $\{4\}$ . FRAC selects the best of the five nonadjacent pairs supporting the optimal $C_{5}$ fractional cover, each assigned weight $1/2$ . MA applies all nonnull edits without a guard; MAG applies their union only if it improves the objective; FOPT takes the better of the fractional candidate and full union. The best singleton rule selects the highest-gain proposal. Adaptive ordinary coloring selects a coloring containing the best independent set after observing gains. These provide two further controls. Every rule scores the same stage-two proposal pool.

## Retained gain.

Table 3 gives per-network gain sums. Across all 90 units, $S=1{,}617$ , FRAC retains 922 (57.0%) and fixed COL 799 (49.4%); the relative difference is 15.4%. The best singleton retains 629, MAG 242, and FOPT 926. FRAC exceeds MAG in 84 units, loses in three, and ties in three. Unguarded MA worsens the incumbent in 31 units. Adaptive ordinary coloring equals FRAC in every unit, as it can place the highest-gain independent pair in a proper three-coloring after observing gains.

## Measured resources and interpretation.

Both stages together used 900 process-level agent calls, 66.19 summed agent CPU-seconds, 14.15 seconds summed five-agent critical-path wall time, and 3,780 direct objective evaluations including runner audit checks. The complete local run, including graph loading and serialization, took 25.99 seconds on macOS arm64. Fixed COL scored three classes and FRAC five pairs from cached singleton gains; the latter required two extra score checks per unit but no additional agents or direct objective calls.

On these road networks, fractional integration retains more verified gain than ordinary coloring fixed before proposals, while adaptive ordinary coloring matches it. The three initializations within each region are repeated measurements of that region.

## Appendix H Exploratory fractional route-search schedule

## Scheduling question.

When local-search agents share vehicle routes, exclusive route access limits which searches can run concurrently. We compare the duration of ordinary and fractional preemptible execution schedules under these conflicts. Both schedules use five agents solving a capacitated vehicle-routing problem (CVRP), with the same search budget for every agent.

## Instances, graph, and search.

We use four public CVRPLIB/DIMACS routing inputs (CVRPLIB, 2026): Loggi-n401-k23 and Loggi-n501-k24, and ORTEC-n242-k12 and ORTEC-n323-k21. Every schedule for an input starts from the same deterministic feasible Clarke–Wright incumbent. A fixed route-proximity rule selects a five-route cycle, and agent $i$ searches two consecutive routes of that cycle. Adjacent agents share a route and cannot hold their route-pair locks concurrently; nonadjacent agents have disjoint locks. This rule deliberately induces $C_{5}$ rather than estimating a naturally occurring conflict graph. Each agent performs stateful stochastic two-route local search with a deterministic per-instance, per-repeat seed and exactly 500,000 candidate-move evaluations per schedule.

## Matched schedules and measurements.

The ordinary schedule uses the fixed color classes $\{0,2\}$ , $\{1,3\}$ , and $\{4\}$ , assigning the full 500,000 evaluations to an agent on its one visit. The fractional schedule uses the five independent pairs $\{0,2\}$ , $\{1,3\}$ , $\{2,4\}$ , $\{3,0\}$ , and $\{4,1\}$ , assigning 250,000 evaluations per visit; every agent appears twice. Both therefore run the same per-agent search budget. The two compatible agents in a wave execute concurrently; waves execute sequentially. We measure elapsed wall time for the entire schedule, summed process CPU time, final route cost, and feasibility. Three paired repeats per input use the same initial incumbent and corresponding seeds, with schedule order reversed in the middle repeat. The nominal critical-path length is $2.5$ rather than $3$ full-budget waves, a $16.7\%$ difference before overhead.

Fresh success events are independent across calls in this illustration, although Theorem 1 does not need that assumption. Uniform allocation assigns each agent to a distinct uniformly sampled block, so $q_{ib}=\nicefrac{{1}}{{6}}$ . Optimized allocation solves (22) without a binding token constraint. Both policies use the same three agents and the same deadline; in these solutions every agent is assigned in every round. Thus their per-round cost is equal under equal per-call billing at that deadline.

For a fixed allocation, an unrepaired block $b$ survives a round with probability $1-a_{b}$ . Its expected loss satisfies the exact recursion $\mathbb{E}\left[1-x_{b}^{k+1}\right]=(1-a_{b})\mathbb{E}\left[1-x_{b}^{k}\right]$ . Starting from zero and iterating gives

(28)

 $\begin{gathered}\tfrac{\mathbb{E}\left[F(x^{K})\right]}{6}=\tfrac{1}{6}\sum_{b=1}^{6}(1-a_{b})^{K},\\
\max_{b\in[6]}(1-a_{b})^{K}\overset{\eqref{eq:coverage}}{=}(1-a)^{K}.\end{gathered}$ 

The second quantity is the largest expected remaining loss when initially only one block is unrepaired. It measures exactly the worst-block criterion optimized by the linear program. For that criterion, the number of rounds needed to reach $0.01$ is $\lceil\log(0.01)/\log(1-a)\rceil$ .

## Checking the rate and merge identities.

We compare the exact mean-loss formula with 20,000 seeded independent runs at $h=2$ over 20 rounds. The simulated normalized mean loss is $0.006367$ , with standard error $0.000232$ , compared with the exact value $0.006752$ . We also verify the matching decomposition used to realize the allocation.

For a six-block path with objective $\sum_{b}(1-x_{b})+0.2\sum_{b=1}^{5}(x_{b}-x_{b+1})^{2}$ , odd and even blocks form two independent color classes. Enumerating all 64 states and all 64 subsets of proposed edits verifies the merge identity. Enumerating the matching and success outcomes at each state also verifies the expected-drift bound. Across the 4,096 state/subset pairs, the union gives a larger gain than the best color candidate in 1,369 cases and ties in 2,727. Optimistic integration satisfies the merge bound throughout.

## Interference on a complete graph.

A concrete instance of Theorem 2 illustrates adverse interactions. For $z\in\{0,1\}^{m}$ , start at $z=0$ and let each proposal set one coordinate to one under the objective

(29)

 $F(z)\coloneqq m-\sum_{b=1}^{m}z_{b}+2\sum_{b<c}z_{b}z_{c}.$ 

Each proposal gains one individually. Applying $r$ proposals gives

(30)

 $F(0)-F(z)=r-r(r-1)=r(2-r)\leq 1,$ 

where $r\in\{0,\ldots,m\}$ . Thus the best subset gains one out of a total singleton gain of $m$ , attaining the factor $1/m$ for the complete graph.
For $m=2,\ldots,8$ , applying all edits gives gain $m(2-m)$ , while selecting one color class gives gain one. Optimistic integration therefore selects the color candidate, rejecting the worsening union for $m\geq 3$ .

## J.1. Proof of Theorem 1

Theorem 1 combines three inequalities: the merge retains local gains, conditional agent-quality bounds relate those gains to exact block improvement, and the block error bound relates that improvement to global error.

Proof.

Expected local progress.
Write $\mathbb{E}_{k}[\cdot]\coloneqq\mathbb{E}[\cdot\mid\mathcal{H}_{k}]$ and $\mathbb{E}_{k,\mathcal{M}}[\cdot]\coloneqq\mathbb{E}[\cdot\mid\mathcal{H}_{k},\mathcal{M}]$ . Condition on the history before drawing the matching. Apply Proposition 4 (or Theorem 1 for the safe-only method), then condition on each matching and use Assumption 2:

(31)

 $\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{k}[F(x^{k})-F(x^{k+1})]$ 
 $\displaystyle\overset{\eqref{eq:optimistic-bound}}{\geq}\frac{1}{\Lambda}\sum_{\mathcal{M}}p_{\mathcal{M}}\sum_{(i,b)\in\mathcal{M}}\mathbb{E}_{k,\mathcal{M}}[g_{ib}^{k}]$ 

 $\displaystyle\overset{\eqref{eq:oracle}}{\geq}\frac{1}{\Lambda}\sum_{\mathcal{M}}p_{\mathcal{M}}\sum_{(i,b)\in\mathcal{M}}\eta_{ib}(h)D_{b}(x^{k})$ 

 $\displaystyle\overset{\eqref{eq:coverage}}{=}\frac{1}{\Lambda}\sum_{b}a_{b}D_{b}(x^{k})\geq\frac{a}{\Lambda}\sum_{b}D_{b}(x^{k})\overset{\eqref{eq:error},\eqref{eq:coverage}}{\geq}\theta_{\lambda}\Delta(x^{k}).$ 

The penultimate inequality uses $a_{b}\geq a$ from (19) and $D_{b}(x^{k})\geq 0$ , which follows because leaving the block unchanged is feasible in (1).

From progress to error.
Subtract the improvement from the old objective gap:

(32)

 $\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{k}[\Delta(x^{k+1})]$ 
 $\displaystyle\overset{\eqref{eq:task}}{=}\Delta(x^{k})-\mathbb{E}_{k}[F(x^{k})-F(x^{k+1})]$ 

 $\displaystyle\overset{\eqref{eq:drift}}{\leq}(1-\theta_{\lambda})\Delta(x^{k}).$ 

Write $e_{k}\coloneqq\mathbb{E}\left[\Delta(x^{k})\right]$ . Taking expectations in the conditional bound gives

(33)

 $e_{k+1}\overset{\eqref{eq:recursion}}{\leq}(1-\theta_{\lambda})e_{k}.$ 

The base case is $e_{0}=\Delta(x^{0})$ . If $e_{k}\leq(1-\theta_{\lambda})^{k}e_{0}$ , then

(34)

 $e_{k+1}\overset{\eqref{eq:mean-recursion}}{\leq}(1-\theta_{\lambda})e_{k}\leq(1-\theta_{\lambda})^{k+1}e_{0},$ 

where the second inequality is the induction hypothesis and uses $1-\theta_{\lambda}\geq 0$ . This proves (21). If $\theta_{\lambda}=0$ , the conclusion gives monotonicity only. If $\theta_{\lambda}=1$ , nonnegativity forces a zero gap after one round; the case $K=0$ means the unchanged initial gap.
∎

## S-EDL: Eliciting Self-Evidence from Sequence Likelihoods for Semantic Calibration of LLMs

Yawei Li Jiazheng Li David Rügamer Bernd Bischl Yulan He Mina Rezaei

Affiliation: LMU Munich

Affiliation: King’s College London

Affiliation: Munich Center for Machine Learning (MCML)

Abstract

Calibrating large language models (LLMs) in open-ended generation is uniquely challenging because many distinct token sequences can express the same underlying meaning. Prior calibration techniques target either fixed-set classification confidence or the binary correctness of individual strings, making them inherently ill-suited for the dynamic, multi-string nature of open-ended semantic outcomes. To bridge this gap, we introduce S-EDL, a novel evidential method that natively optimizes LLMs for semantic calibration. By eliciting evidence from the sequence likelihoods of the model itself, S-EDL constructs a differentiable Dirichlet prior over prompt-specific semantic classes. Optimizing the evidential loss under this prior yields a calibration-aware training signal over variable semantic classes, completely bypassing the need for fixed multiple-choice options. Evaluations across four open-ended benchmarks and three models demonstrate that S-EDL substantially reduces semantic calibration error while improving accuracy. By aligning generative likelihoods with semantic correctness, this work establishes a framework for deploying trustworthy LLMs in complex, open-ended applications. Code is available at: https://github.com/sandylaker/s-edl.

## 1 Introduction

Large language models (LLMs) are increasingly used for open-ended reasoning tasks, where reliable confidence estimates are important for deciding when model outputs can be trusted [28, 32, 56, 24]. Early research has primarily focused on multiple-choice question answering (MCQA), where calibration can be defined over a small set of answer labels [48, 53, 37, 25]. Open-ended generation is more subtle: many distinct token sequences can express the same answer, and the probability assigned to a surface form does not necessarily reflect confidence in its semantic content.

Prior work has studied open-ended uncertainty and calibration through several related targets. Semantic uncertainty metrics [20, 12] sample multiple generations and group equivalent responses to evaluate the predictive distribution over semantic classes. Building on this perspective, semantic calibration [33] translates this grouping concept into an evaluation metric for open-ended generation. In parallel, verbal confidence calibration methods [18, 43, 52, 30, 3, 9, 55] teach models to report a confidence score of correctness. However, this calibrates the binary correctness of a single response rather than the underlying distribution over alternative semantic answers. Thus, a gap remains: how to fine-tune open-ended LLMs under a semantic calibration objective.

Evidential deep learning (EDL) natively bridges this gap. By placing a Dirichlet prior over categorical probabilities, it explicitly models the strength of evidence for multiple competing meanings, allowing calibration losses to act on distributions over semantic meanings. Building on this, we propose S-EDL, a self-evidenced EDL method tailored for semantic calibration. Unlike conventional EDL methods that require a fixed set of answer labels [39, 7], S-EDL accommodates the dynamic nature of open-ended generation by transforming the sequence likelihoods of the model into a differentiable evidence vector. This formulation allocates greater evidential support to semantic classes backed by highly probable strings. The evidence vector then parametrizes a Dirichlet prior over the semantic outcomes, providing a calibration-aware training signal. Our contributions are three-fold:

• 

We extend EDL from fixed-choice calibration to open-ended semantic calibration, allowing LLMs to optimize their confidence over diverse semantic meanings.

• 

We introduce a novel parametrization that derives a differentiable evidence vector from the model’s sequence likelihoods. This formulation also theoretically unifies post-hoc metrics such as Semantic Entropy [20, 12] under a common evidential framework.

• 

Across three models and four open-ended benchmarks, S-EDL jointly improves average accuracy and calibration over supervised fine-tuning (SFT), reducing average calibration error by up to roughly 1/3.

## 2 Related work

From MCQA to semantic calibration. Early LLM calibration focused on MCQA, where a fixed label set enables standard classification-style methods, including Bayesian low-rank adaptation [48, 53, 37, 42] and evidential approaches [25]. Open-ended generation introduces a level mismatch: many distinct sequences can express the same answer. Semantic uncertainty metrics [20, 12, 11, 34, 36, 6] sample generations and group by meaning, and semantic calibration [33] turns this view into a calibration target for open-ended generation. Our work follows this semantic line.

Verbal and token-level calibration. A separate line of work asks models to emit a scalar correctness score [18, 43, 52, 30, 3, 9, 55], which calibrates a Bernoulli pushforward (correct vs. incorrect) of the output distribution rather than a categorical distribution over semantic outcomes. Meanwhile, SelfReflect [19] captures the categorical distribution by a verbalized string. Token-level methods such as label smoothing [15, 44], temperature scaling [41, 22], RCFT [50], and CATTO [35], regularize per-token distributions, supervising a different granularity than the semantic marginal targeted here.

Evidential deep learning. EDL [31, 39, 10, 7, 17, 5, 4, 54] places a Dirichlet prior over class probabilities through an evidence vector. Although previous work has applied EDL to calibrate LLMs in MCQA [25] and detect misbehavior in vision-language models [16], S-EDL is, to the best of our knowledge, the first instantiation of EDL for calibrating LLMs in open-ended generation.

## 3 Background

## 3.1 Semantic calibration in open-ended LLM generation

Our work focuses on calibration in open-ended generation, for which we adopt the framework of B-calibration [33]. Let $\mathcal{D}$ be a distribution over prompt-completion pairs $(x,y)\in\mathcal{V}^{*}\times\mathcal{V}^{L}$ , where $x$ is a prompt over vocabulary $\mathcal{V}^{*}$ and $y\in\mathcal{V}^{L}$ is a ground-truth completion of length $L$ . An autoregressive LLM $p_{\bm{\theta}}$ with parameters $\bm{\theta}\in\Theta$ assigns probability $p_{\bm{\theta}}(z|x)$ to each candidate response $z\in\mathcal{V}^{L}$ . Because distinct token sequences can convey the same meaning (e.g., “half” vs. “0.5”), B-calibration [33] introduces a collapsing function $B:\mathcal{V}^{*}\times\mathcal{V}^{L}\rightarrow\mathbb{N}$ mapping each prompt-response pair to a global semantic class, a raw identifier $s\in\mathbb{N}$ . We write $s:=B(x,z)$ for a generation’s class and $s^{*}:=B(x,y)$ for the ground-truth class of $(x,y)$ . The collapsing function $B$ can be implemented by a verification tool or an LLM-as-judge that clusters responses by meaning.
A semantic distribution is then defined as the categorical distribution over semantic classes:

 $\pi_{x}(s):=\textstyle\sum_{z\,:\,B(x,z)=s}p_{\bm{\theta}}(z|x),\quad s\in\mathbb{N},z\in\mathcal{V}^{L}.$ 

(1)

Further, denote the top class $s_{x}^{\text{top}}:={\arg\max}_{s}\pi_{x}(s)$ and the semantic confidence $\mathrm{conf}(x):=\pi_{x}(s_{x}^{\text{top}})$ . Semantic calibration requires that the confidence aligns with the true probability of correctness:

 $\mathbb{E}_{(x,y)\sim\mathcal{D}}\!\left[\,\mathbbm{1}\{s_{x}^{\text{top}}=s^{*}\}\,\big|\,\mathrm{conf}(x)\,\right]=\mathrm{conf}(x)\quad\text{almost surely.}$ 

That is, among all prompts for which the model reports semantic confidence $c$ , the top semantic class should indeed be correct with frequency $c$ [33].

## 3.2 Evidential deep learning

The calibration objective above asks the model to report not just which semantic class is most likely but also how confident it is in that belief. EDL [39, 31] provides a framework for the latter: rather than predicting a single categorical $\bm{p}\in\Delta^{K}$ over $K$ classes, it predicts a distribution over $\bm{p}$ , accounting for uncertainty over class probabilities instead of training only against a point estimate.

Dirichlet prior and evidence. In this subsection, $k\in[K]$ denotes a generic class index for a standard fixed- $K$ classification problem, separate from the semantic-label notation of Section 3.1. EDL places a Dirichlet prior $\text{Dir}(\bm{p};\bm{\alpha})$ on $\bm{p}\in\Delta^{K}$ , with Dirichlet parameters $\bm{\alpha}=(\alpha_{k})_{k\in[K]}\in[1,+\infty)^{K}$ built from a non-negative evidence vector $\bm{e}=(e_{k})_{k\in[K]}\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{K}$ via $\alpha_{k}=e_{k}+1$ .

Each $e_{k}$ represents the accumulated support for class $k$ . Conventional EDL employs a classification model $f_{\bm{\theta}}$ to predict the evidence directly, $\bm{e}=\text{SoftPlus}(f_{\bm{\theta}}(x))$ , and the Dirichlet mean serves as the predictive distribution: $\hat{p}_{k}=\mathbb{E}_{\bm{p}\sim\text{Dir}(\cdot;\bm{\alpha})}[p_{k}]=\frac{\alpha_{k}}{\alpha_{0}},\text{with \emph{Dirichlet strength} }\alpha_{0}=\textstyle\sum_{k=1}^{K}\alpha_{k}.$ 

EDL training objective. Given a one-hot target $\bm{q}=(q_{k})_{k\in[K]}\in\{0,1\}^{K}$ with $q_{k}=\mathbbm{1}\{k=t\}$ , Sensoy et al. [39] learn the model’s parameter $\bm{\theta}\in\Theta$ by minimizing the Bayes risk of MSE objective

 $\mathcal{L}_{\text{MSE}}(\bm{\theta})=\mathbb{E}_{\bm{p}\sim\text{Dir}(\cdot;\bm{\alpha})}\|\bm{q}-\bm{p}\|_{2}^{2}=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\left(q_{k}-\frac{\alpha_{k}}{\alpha_{0}}\right)^{2}+\sum_{k=1}^{K}\frac{\alpha_{k}(\alpha_{0}-\alpha_{k})}{\alpha_{0}^{2}(\alpha_{0}+1)},$ 

(2)

which admits a closed form. Previous work IB-EDL [25] has shown that EDL methods are very effective in calibrating LLMs in the MCQA setting, but their approach is limited to a fixed set of options such as A/B/C/D. In the following section, we move a step forward and instantiate this framework in the open-ended generation setting, deriving $\bm{e}$ from the LLM’s own generations.

## 4 Methodology

## 4.1 Motivation

Given EDL’s effectiveness for calibrating LLMs in MCQA, we now discuss the adaptations required to apply it in open-ended generation.
Instantiating the EDL framework in this setting hinges on one design choice: how to parametrize the evidence vector $\bm{e}$ . Conventional EDL learns $\bm{e}$ through a dedicated prediction head whose output dimension equals the number of classes. This recipe does not transfer to our setting for two reasons. First, the relevant semantic class inventory is prompt-dependent and not known in advance, so there is no canonical fixed output space. Second, and most fundamentally, the distribution whose calibration we ultimately care about is $p_{\bm{\theta}}(\cdot|x)$ ; routing evidence through an independent head decouples it from the very object we aim to calibrate. We therefore look for a parametrization that derives $\bm{e}$ from $p_{\bm{\theta}}(\cdot|x)$ .

A natural choice for constructing $\bm{e}$ is utilizing $\pi_{x}$ itself, which already encodes how $p_{\bm{\theta}}(\cdot|x)$ distributes mass over semantic classes. Since $\mathcal{V}^{L}$ is typically vast, Nakkiran et al. [33] estimate $\pi_{x}$ from $N$ i.i.d. Monte-Carlo samples $z^{(i)}\sim p_{\bm{\theta}}(\cdot|x)$ with semantic classes $s^{(i)}=B(x,z^{(i)})$ , which induce the prompt-specific semantic class set $\mathcal{S}_{x}:=\{s^{(i)}:i\in[N]\}\subset\mathbb{N}$ . Further details regarding the empirical construction of $\mathcal{S}_{x}$ in edge cases are in Section B.1. The plug-in estimator of $\pi_{x}$ is then

 $\hat{\pi}_{x}(s)=\tfrac{1}{N}\textstyle\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathbbm{1}[s^{(i)}=s],\quad s\in\mathcal{S}_{x}.$ 

(3)

However, this histogram is not a suitable evidence vector for EDL training. Once the samples are drawn, the class indicators are constants with respect to $\bm{\theta}$ , so an evidence vector proportional to $\hat{\pi}_{x}$ provides no differentiable path from the EDL loss back to the model parameters. In addition, the histogram treats all samples equally, regardless of their likelihood under $p_{\bm{\theta}}(\cdot\mid x)$ . This is misaligned with the evidential interpretation of $\bm{e}$ : a semantic class supported by high-likelihood generations should contribute more evidence than one represented only by low-likelihood samples.

Desiderata. These observations lead to two desiderata for parametrizing evidence $\bm{e}$ :

(a) 

The parametrization should assign more evidence to semantic classes supported by high-likelihood completions, so that likely completions contribute more to evidence.

(b) 

The parametrization should remain differentiable through the sample log-likelihoods, so that the EDL loss can update the likelihoods underlying the sampled semantic estimate.

Next, we instantiate these desiderata through a Monte-Carlo estimate of a tempered semantic distribution, and use this estimate to define a Dirichlet prior over the sampled semantic classes.

## 4.2 Formulation of self-evidence

Throughout this subsection, we fix the input prompt $x$ and drop the subscript from prompt-specific quantities (e.g., $\pi_{x}$ to $\pi$ , $\mathcal{S}_{x}$ to $\mathcal{S}$ ) to lighten notation; all such objects remain implicitly conditional on $x$ . We work directly with semantic labels $s\in\mathcal{S}$ , without introducing a separate local class index. In addition, we use the term self-evidence to refer to evidential support constructed from the LLM’s own Monte-Carlo samples. At the sample level, each generation $z^{(i)}$ receives a scalar self-evidence weight $u^{(i)}$ ; at the class level, these weights aggregate into the self-evidence vector $\bm{e}$ .

Tempered semantic distribution. For a scalar $\beta\geq 1$ , we define the tempered semantic distribution over classes $s\in\mathcal{S}$ and $z,z^{\prime}\in\mathcal{V}^{L}$ as

 $\pi_{\beta}(s):=\frac{\sum_{z\,:\,B(x,z)=s}p_{\bm{\theta}}(z|x)^{\beta}}{\sum_{z^{\prime}\in\mathcal{V}^{L}}p_{\bm{\theta}}(z^{\prime}|x)^{\beta}}.$ 

(4)

For $\beta=1$ , Eq. 4 recovers the standard semantic distribution $\pi$ in Eq. 1.
For $\beta>1$ , raising per-sequence likelihoods to a higher power shifts mass toward classes that the model supports with high-confidence sequences, formalizing desideratum (a).

Self-evidence estimator. Direct evaluation of Eq. 4 is intractable because both the classwise numerator and the global normalizer require sums over $\mathcal{V}^{L}$ . We therefore estimate $\pi_{\beta}$ from the same $N$ Monte-Carlo samples introduced in Section 4.1. Writing $\ell^{(i)}:=\log p_{\bm{\theta}}(z^{(i)}|x)$ for teacher-forcing log-likelihood for sample $i$ , we assign to each sample the self-evidence weight

 $u^{(i)}:=\exp\!\left((\beta-1)\,\ell^{(i)}\right)=p_{\bm{\theta}}(z^{(i)}|x)^{\beta-1}.$ 

(5)

The exponent is $\beta-1$ rather than $\beta$ because the samples $z^{(i)}$ are themselves drawn from $p_{\bm{\theta}}(\cdot|x)$ , so multiplying by $p_{\bm{\theta}}(z^{(i)}|x)^{\beta-1}$ recovers the $p_{\bm{\theta}}^{\beta}$ terms of $\pi_{\beta}$ in Eq. 4 (made precise in Proposition 1). Because $u^{(i)}$ is a smooth function of $\ell^{(i)}$ , gradients can pass through the sample log-likelihoods to $\bm{\theta}$ and satisfy desideratum (b).
We aggregate the weights within each semantic class and normalize:

 $U_{s}:=\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\sum_{i\in[N]\,:\,s^{(i)}=s}\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!u^{(i)},\qquad\hat{\pi}_{\beta}(s):=\frac{U_{s}}{\sum_{r\in\mathcal{S}}U_{r}}.$ 

(6)

A natural question is whether this estimator $\hat{\pi}_{\beta}$ recovers the true tempered distribution $\pi_{\beta}$ as the number of samples grows. The next result confirms this (see the proof in Section A.1).

Proposition 1.

As $N\to\infty$ , $\hat{\pi}_{\beta}(s)\xrightarrow{\text{a.s.}}\pi_{\beta}(s)$ for every $s\in\mathcal{S}$ .

Connection to Semantic Entropy and the practical choice of $\beta$ . Semantic Entropy (SE) [20, 12] also aggregates samples by their sequence probabilities $p_{\bm{\theta}}(z^{(i)}|x)$ , which under Eq. 5 corresponds to the special case $\beta=2$ . In our experiments, we adopt a small value $\beta=1.2$ (ablation in Section 5.5). The choice of $\beta$ reflects the two methods’ different purposes: SE measures the uncertainty as a post-hoc metric, while S-EDL uses $\pi_{\beta}$ as a training signal that drives gradient updates (Section 4.3).

Self-evidence vector and Dirichlet prior. We finally convert $\hat{\pi}_{\beta}$ into an evidential prior by distributing the sample budget $N$ over the semantic classes according to the estimated tempered distribution:

 $e_{s}:=N\cdot\hat{\pi}_{\beta}(s),\qquad\alpha_{s}:=e_{s}+1,\qquad s\in\mathcal{S}.$ 

(7)

Because $\hat{\pi}_{\beta}$ is a probability vector, the Dirichlet strength is determined a priori by the sample budget and the semantic class count:
 $\alpha_{0}=\sum_{s\in\mathcal{S}}\alpha_{s}=N+|\mathcal{S}|$ .
This differs from conventional EDL, where the model freely predicts the per-class evidences $e_{k}$ (equivalently, the Dirichlet parameters $\alpha_{k}=e_{k}+1$ ), so the total strength $\alpha_{0}=\sum_{k}\alpha_{k}$ is not fixed a priori. Here $\alpha_{0}=N+|\mathcal{S}|$ is pinned by construction, and all flexibility in the prior is routed through $\hat{\pi}_{\beta}$ . The resulting $\text{Dir}(\cdot;\bm{\alpha})$ is fed into the MSE loss of Eq. 2, completing the differentiable path promised by desideratum (b), as the entire computational chain $\ell^{(i)}\rightarrow u^{(i)}\rightarrow\hat{\pi}_{\beta}\rightarrow\bm{e}\rightarrow\bm{\alpha}\rightarrow\mathcal{L}_{\text{MSE}}$ is differentiable w.r.t. $\bm{\theta}$ .

In training, we optimize the $\mathcal{L}_{\text{MSE}}$ jointly with standard supervised fine-tuning (SFT). The SFT loss retains the next-token learning signal, while the evidential term provides the semantic-level calibration signal. Let $\mathcal{L}_{\text{SFT}}$ denote the next-token cross-entropy loss on a pair $(x,y)$ . Our final objective then is

 $\mathcal{L}_{\text{S-EDL}}(\bm{\theta})=\mathcal{L}_{\text{SFT}}(\bm{\theta})+\lambda_{\text{MSE}}\mathcal{L}_{\text{MSE}}(\bm{\theta}),$ 

where $\lambda_{\text{MSE}}$ is the weight for the evidential loss. The next subsection analyzes the gradient of $\mathcal{L}_{\text{MSE}}$ .

## 4.3 Gradient structure of S-EDL

Having constructed the self-evidence vector and the Dirichlet prior, we next examine the optimization signal S-EDL produces. The result below shows that the gradient with respect to per-sample log-likelihood factors into a normalized sample weight and a class-level correction term.

Theorem 1.

For each class $s\in\mathcal{S}$ , let $d_{s}:=\partial\mathcal{L}_{\text{MSE}}/\partial\hat{\pi}_{\beta}(s)$ , and let $\bar{d}:=\sum_{r\in\mathcal{S}}\hat{\pi}_{\beta}(r)\,d_{r}$ denote their weighted mean. Let $w^{(i)}:=u^{(i)}/\sum_{j=1}^{N}u^{(j)},i\in[N]$ be the normalized sample weight. Then

 $\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}_{\text{MSE}}}{\partial\ell^{(i)}}=(\beta-1)\,w^{(i)}\left(d_{s^{(i)}}-\bar{d}\right).$ 

(8)

The proof is shown in Section A.2. Theorem 1 shows that each sample’s contribution is determined by the sample weight $w^{(i)}$ and the class-level signal $d_{s^{(i)}}-\bar{d}$ .
Since $w^{(i)}\propto p_{\bm{\theta}}(z^{(i)}|x)^{\beta-1}$ , samples with larger model likelihoods receive larger gradient weights. Optimization is therefore concentrated on the “high-probability region” of $p_{\bm{\theta}}(\cdot|x)$ . We next characterize its direction on the two semantic classes most relevant to calibration: the ground-truth class and the most overconfident incorrect class.

Corollary 1.

Assume $\beta>1$ . For any sample $i\in[N]$ in the correct class ( $s^{(i)}=s^{*}$ ), $\partial\mathcal{L}_{\text{MSE}}/\partial\ell^{(i)}\leq 0$ , with strict inequality if $\hat{\pi}_{\beta}(s^{*})<1$ . Conversely, for any sample in the most overconfident incorrect class $s^{-}:={\arg\max}_{s\neq s^{*}}\hat{\pi}_{\beta}(s)$ , it holds that $\partial\mathcal{L}_{\text{MSE}}/\partial\ell^{(i)}\geq 0$ , with strict inequality if $\hat{\pi}_{\beta}(s^{*})>0$ .

The proof is detailed in Section A.3. Consequently, minimizing $\mathcal{L}_{\text{MSE}}$ steers the model in the desired direction. In any realistic training scenario where the model’s semantic distribution has not entirely collapsed ( $0<\hat{\pi}_{\beta}(s^{*})<1$ ), the objective actively promotes the sequences in ground-truth semantic class, while simultaneously penalizing those in the most overconfident incorrect one.
The next corollary shows that the per-sample gradients are balanced across the samples.

Corollary 2.

For the gradients $\partial\mathcal{L}_{\text{MSE}}/\partial\ell^{(i)}$ in Theorem 1, their sum $\sum_{i=1}^{N}\partial\mathcal{L}_{\text{MSE}}/\partial\ell^{(i)}=0$ .

See Section A.4 for the proof. S-EDL therefore conservatively reallocates log-likelihoods rather than uniformly inflating or suppressing them: combined with the sign structure above, the update shifts probability mass away from overconfident incorrect classes and toward the ground-truth class.

## 5 Experiments

## 5.1 Experimental setup

Datasets. We conduct training and evaluation on four open-ended STEM datasets: GSM8K [8], MATH [13], ChemistryQA [49], and FormulaReasoning [23], a physics QA dataset. These benchmarks share two properties that make them well suited to our study. First, final answers admit canonical numerical or symbolic forms, so the collapsing function that groups sampled responses into semantic classes (a symbolic verifier or an LLM-as-judge) incurs comparatively low evaluation noise. Second, they often require multi-step reasoning, so calibration over complete semantic outcomes is more relevant than calibration over individual tokens. We train on the training split of each dataset and evaluate on the corresponding test split, with the exception of MATH, where we follow standard practice [13, 26] and evaluate on MATH-500, a subset of the MATH test split widely used in other literature. Dataset construction is detailed in Section B.3.

Models and training. We fine-tune Qwen3-8B-Base [45], Ministral3-8B-Base [27], and MiMo-7B-Base [51] with LoRA adapters [14] of rank $r_{\text{LoRA}}=16$ and $\alpha_{\text{LoRA}}=32$ . For S-EDL, $\beta=1.2$ and MSE loss weight $\lambda_{\text{MSE}}=5.0$ are held fixed across all models and datasets. In addition, the number of generations per prompt during training $N_{\text{train}}$ is set as large as GPU memory permits. On GSM8K, where outputs are comparatively short, we use $N_{\text{train}}=32$ for all models. On the remaining three datasets, the memory ceiling reduces to $N_{\text{train}}=8$ for Qwen3-8B-Base and $N_{\text{train}}=12$ for Ministral3-8B-Base and MiMo-7B-Base. Complete optimization hyperparameters are in Section B.4.

Evaluation protocol. We use $\texttt{top\_p}=0.9$ for all models, with temperature $0.8$ for Qwen3-8B-Base, $0.6$ for Ministral3-8B-Base, and $0.7$ for MiMo-7B-Base. The per-model temperature is chosen on a held-out validation split to approximately match the entropy of the respective model, and is fixed across fine-tuning methods.
Following Nakkiran et al. [33], we report two semantic-level metrics computed from $N_{\text{eval}}=50$ generations per question: Semantic Accuracy (S-Acc.), the accuracy of majority voting over semantically clustered responses, and Smooth ECE (smECE), a kernel-smoothed calibration error metric. As a single-sequence-level metric, we additionally report Pass@1 accuracy.

Baselines. Besides standard SFT, we compare S-EDL against baselines spanning three categories: (i) token-level calibration, including Label Smoothing (LS) [15] and RCFT [50], which regularize token-wise output distributions; (ii) Bayesian ensembling, represented by BLoB [48]; and (iii) sequence-level correctness optimization, represented by Dr.GRPO [29] with a binary $0/1$ correctness reward. We include Dr.GRPO as a control that isolates the effect of sequence-level supervision on calibration: under the binary reward, it maximizes the probability of the correct semantic class without an explicit calibration term, which lets us assess whether operating at the sequence level alone accounts for the gains of S-EDL or whether the Brier-style calibration objective like Eq. 2 is required. The details about the baselines are outlined in Section B.5.

As shown in Table 1, S-EDL attains the lowest average smECE across all three models, improving over the strongest token-level baseline by 2.3 points on Qwen3-8B-Base (10.5 vs. LS’s 12.8), 2.3 points on Ministral3-8B-Base (8.6 vs. RCFT’s 10.9), and 2.5 points on MiMo-7B-Base (9.4 vs. LS’s 11.9).
Dr.GRPO also outperforms every token-level baseline in average smECE on Qwen3-8B-Base and MiMo-7B-Base, but its 11.4 on Ministral3-8B-Base does not improve over RCFT’s 10.9, suggesting that operating at the sequence level alone is not sufficient and that the choice of training signal also matters.

The underperformance of token-level methods may reflect a level mismatch between supervision and evaluation: LS and RCFT calibrate per-token conditional distributions, whereas sequence-level objectives act on complete responses; S-EDL further applies its loss to a sampled estimate of the semantic distribution, matching the granularity of the semantic metrics.
This mismatch may be amplified in chain-of-thought generation, where a small subset of decisive tokens (e.g., a branching choice indicated by “alternatively”) largely determines the semantic outcome [47], while most tokens are routine continuations; per-token regularization applies to both groups, potentially diluting the signal on the tokens that actually matter.

Furthermore, we observe that several baselines improve over SFT on one metric at the cost of the other.
LS provides a representative example: on MiMo-7B-Base, it lowers average Pass@1 from $56.2$ to $53.5$ while improving smECE only marginally, from $12.1$ to $11.9$ .
Averaged across the four datasets, S-EDL improves over SFT on all three metrics for every model, e.g., with $+9.6$ Pass@1, $+5.7$ S-Acc, and $-6.0$ smECE on Qwen3-8B-Base. Fig. 6 in Section C.2 visualizes the averaged S-Acc $\times$ smECE plane. S-EDL consistently lies on the Pareto frontier of the plane.
The structure of the evidential loss may help explain this behavior. Eq. 2 is a Brier-style proper scoring rule, which penalizes both classification error and miscalibration simultaneously. Dr.GRPO with a binary $0/1$ reward, maximizes the probability of the correct semantic class without an explicit calibration term, and may improve accuracy at the cost of miscalibration.

## 5.3 Cross-domain evaluation

To test the transferability of each method, we train on MATH and evaluate on three other cross-domain datasets as shown in Table 2. This setting tests not only whether accuracy transfers, but also whether semantic confidence remains aligned with correctness.
The grouping into sequence-level (Dr.GRPO, S-EDL) and token-level/Bayesian (LS, RCFT, BLoB) baselines from Section 5.2 separates more sharply in average Pass@1 in cross-domain evaluation than in-domain.
The average Pass@1 gap between the better-performing sequence-level method and the strongest token-level/Bayesian baseline widens from $5.6/9.5/10.8$ points in-domain to $13.8/16.5/13.6$ points cross-domain on Qwen3-8B-Base, Ministral3-8B-Base, and MiMo-7B-Base, respectively.

A consistent diagnostic appears against the SFT reference: on Qwen3-8B-Base and MiMo-7B-Base, none of LS, RCFT, or BLoB improves average cross-domain smECE over plain SFT.
One possible interpretation is that per-token regularization and Bayesian ensembling shape conditional distributions tied to the lexical statistics of the training distribution, so the resulting effect on the implied semantic distribution need not be preserved when input statistics change.
In contrast, objectives defined over complete responses are less tied to token-level statistics and better aligned with the semantic nature.

The two sequence-level methods themselves, however, do not behave identically under shift.
In cross-domain evaluation, the average Pass@1 of S-EDL exceeds that of Dr.GRPO by $7.3/6.3/3.3$ points across the three models.
Its average cross-domain smECE is lower by $4.7/3.0/0.1$ points.
While Dr.GRPO is effective at optimizing correctness, its binary reward does not explicitly regularize the semantic confidence.
The performance gap between the two methods suggests that coupling sequence-level training with an explicit evidential calibration objective could contribute to more generalizable improvements in accuracy and calibration.

## 5.4 Token probability profiles of generated solutions

Figure 1: Violin plots of per-token probability profiles on MATH-500 generated answers. We record the probabilities of the sampled tokens. Solid and dotted black ticks mark the mean and median, respectively. Logarithm is applied to the violin widths to improve readability.

To investigate the source of calibration improvements in S-EDL, we analyze the sampled-token probability profiles of generated solutions. For each generated solution $z$ and decoded position $t$ , we record $p_{\bm{\theta}}(z_{t}\mid x,z_{<t})$ and pool these values across all token positions $t$ and samples $z$ .

Fig. 1 and Fig. 7 (in Section C.3) illustrate three distinct mechanisms for altering token confidence on MATH-500. In Fig. 1, LS globally suppresses token probability, yielding the lowest mean $p(\text{sampled token})$ values of 0.413, 0.507, and 0.411 across the three models. Conversely, Dr.GRPO systematically sharpens distributions, reaching high-end means of 0.904, 0.954, and 0.939.

Unlike LS and Dr.GRPO, S-EDL preserves the SFT token probability profile. Across all twelve model-dataset pairs, the absolute difference between the S-EDL mean and the SFT mean remains below 0.06, and the sign of this minor difference varies across settings. Furthermore, S-EDL maintains a pooled token profile statistically indistinguishable from those of RCFT and BLoB, with means differing by at most 0.06, yet achieves average smECE values at least 2 points lower than all three. The calibration gain therefore cannot be explained by a coarse shift in token concentration.

This empirical behavior directly corroborates the semantic-level reallocation described by Corollary 2. S-EDL achieves semantic calibration by operating on the semantic-class marginal rather than uniformly on token distributions, routing probability mass between semantic outcomes without imposing a fixed direction of change on token probabilities. Pooled statistics cannot rule out position- or class-specific shifts, but the absence of global drift is informative: it isolates the calibration gain to a structural property of evidential loss rather than a side effect of token-level regularization.

## 5.5 Ablation study

Fig. 2 shows how S-EDL responds to $\beta$ and $\lambda_{\text{MSE}}$ . Across the four values of $\lambda_{\text{MSE}}$ spanning more than an order of magnitude, all three metrics improve over the SFT reference. As $\lambda_{\text{MSE}}$ grows, Pass@1 rises and smECE decreases mildly, at the cost of a small drop in S-Acc. The behavior of $\beta$ aligns with the gradient analysis in Theorem 1. Since the per-sample gradient of $\mathcal{L}_{\text{MSE}}$ is scaled by $\beta-1$ , the extreme setting $\beta=1.01$ attenuates the calibration signal by two orders of magnitude, and we include it as a stress test. For the remaining values $\beta\in\{1.1,1.2,1.5\}$ , S-EDL improves over SFT on all three metrics, with $\beta=1.2$ providing the best balance; $\beta=1.5$ overweights the highest-likelihood samples and yields slightly worse calibration. We therefore adopt $\beta=1.2$ and $\lambda_{\text{MSE}}=5.0$ throughout all experiments. See Section C.4 for $\beta$ and $\lambda_{\text{MSE}}$ ablations in other settings.

In Fig. 3, $N_{\text{train}}$ varies from 4 to 32. Even at $N_{\text{train}}=4$ , S-EDL already improves over SFT on all three metrics. Increasing $N_{\text{train}}$ yields monotonic gains, with Pass@1 and S-Acc rising and smECE decreasing, consistent with Proposition 1.

## 5.6 Accelerating semantic clustering by a find-first algorithm

The number of sampled sequences $N_{\text{train}}$ also affects training speed, since computing the EDL loss requires clustering these sequences into semantic classes.
To simplify the notation in the following analysis, we will use $N$ instead of $N_{\text{train}}$ . A naive approach, which compares every pair of sequences, requires $O(N^{2})$ oracle calls. By contrast, Dr.GRPO uses the verifier or LLM judge to score each sequence individually, requiring only $O(N)$ calls.
To reduce this gap, we introduce a find-first clustering algorithm, detailed in Algorithm 1 in Section B.2.
Instead of exhaustive pairwise checks, the algorithm maintains a growing list of cluster representatives and compares each new sequence against them until it finds an equivalent one. If no match is found, it then creates a new cluster.

As illustrated in Fig. 4, clustering $N=50$ sampled sequences with the naive pairwise approach requires $N(N-1)/2=1225$ verifier calls per prompt.
Across the evaluated models, find-first clustering reduces the average to approximately 65 calls on GSM8K and 165 calls on MATH, corresponding to roughly $1.3N$ and $3.3N$ calls, respectively.
This cuts the computational burden to a small fraction of the $O(N^{2})$ worst case, bringing the practical cost much closer to $O(N)$ .

For general tasks that rely on an LLM judge, each sequence can instead be matched against all current cluster representatives in a single classification call.
This implementation requires $O(N)$ judge calls per question prompt, matching the $O(N)$ calls of Dr.GRPO.
Decision models such as Jev [46] could be explored to further accelerate this classification step.
Across both verifier-based and judge-based settings, these implementations substantially narrows the gap in oracle calls between S-EDL and other sequence-level methods such as Dr.GRPO.

## 6 Conclusion

We presented S-EDL, a method that constructs a Dirichlet prior over prompt-specific semantic classes from a model’s own sequence likelihoods, yielding a differentiable evidential objective for fine-tuning LLMs toward semantic calibration. Our gradient analysis shows that this objective conservatively reallocates probability mass across semantic outcomes, raising the likelihoods of sequences in the ground-truth class while suppressing those of the most overconfident incorrect alternative. Empirically, this reallocation produces simultaneous gains in accuracy and calibration. More broadly, our results suggest that grounding calibration objectives in a model’s own generative distribution offers a principled path toward trustworthy open-ended LLMs.

Limitations. While our find-first algorithm mentioned above substantially accelerates semantic grouping, the associated sampling and clustering steps entail a moderate training overhead compared to standard SFT. Additionally, for general tasks that necessitate an LLM judge, this requirement imposes unavoidable memory overhead and potential noise in clustering outcomes. To streamline this process, future work could bypass external verifiers or LLMs entirely by leveraging the hidden representations of generated sequences to estimate semantic dispersion, conceptually similar to methods like EigenScore [6]. Finally, we have not yet tested S-EDL on long-context tasks or domains where semantic equivalence is intrinsically difficult to define, such as code generation. Adapting S-EDL to these challenging scenarios remains an important direction for future exploration.

## Appendix A Proofs

## A.1 Proof of Proposition 1

Proof of Proposition 1.

Apply the Strong Law of Large Numbers separately to the numerator and denominator of Eq. 6. The numerator $\tfrac{1}{N}U_{s}=\tfrac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}u^{(i)}\,\mathbbm{1}\{s^{(i)}=s\}$ is a sample average of i.i.d. terms under $z^{(i)}\sim p_{\bm{\theta}}(\cdot|x)$ , so almost surely

 $\displaystyle\lim_{N\to\infty}\tfrac{1}{N}U_{s}$ 
 $\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{z\sim p_{\bm{\theta}}(\cdot|x)}\!\left[p_{\bm{\theta}}(z|x)^{\beta-1}\,\mathbbm{1}\{B(x,z)=s\}\right]$ 

(9)

 $\displaystyle=\sum_{z\in\mathcal{V}^{L}}p_{\bm{\theta}}(z|x)\cdot p_{\bm{\theta}}(z|x)^{\beta-1}\,\mathbbm{1}\{B(x,z)=s\}$ 

(10)

 $\displaystyle=\sum_{z\,:\,B(x,z)=s}p_{\bm{\theta}}(z|x)^{\beta}.$ 

(11)

An identical argument applied to $\tfrac{1}{N}\sum_{r\in\mathcal{S}}U_{r}$ yields the partition function $\sum_{z^{\prime}\in\mathcal{V}^{L}}p_{\bm{\theta}}(z^{\prime}|x)^{\beta}$ . The continuous mapping theorem then identifies the ratio of the two limits with $\pi_{\beta}(s)$ in Eq. 4.
∎

## A.2 Proof of Theorem 1

Proof of Theorem 1.

The forward pass composes four maps:

 $\ell^{(i)}\,\xrightarrow{\exp((\beta-1)\,\cdot)}\,u^{(i)}\,\xrightarrow{\text{class sum}}\,U_{s}\,\xrightarrow{\text{normalize}}\,\hat{\pi}_{\beta}(s)\,\xrightarrow{\text{EDL loss}}\,\mathcal{L}_{\text{MSE}}.$ 

We establish $d_{s}=\partial\mathcal{L}_{\text{MSE}}/\partial\hat{\pi}_{\beta}(s)$ first, then backpropagate along the remaining three links.

Step 1: derivative w.r.t. $\hat{\pi}_{\beta}(s)$ .
Since $\alpha_{s}=N\hat{\pi}_{\beta}(s)+1$ , we have $\partial\alpha_{s}/\partial\hat{\pi}_{\beta}(s)=N$ , and the Dirichlet strength $\alpha_{0}=N+|\mathcal{S}|$ is constant in $\hat{\pi}_{\beta}$ .
Let $q_{s}:=\mathbbm{1}\{s=s^{*}\}$ be the binary indicator of the ground truth class.
Differentiating the closed-form MSE in Eq. 2 gives

 $\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}_{\text{MSE}}}{\partial\hat{\pi}_{\beta}(s)}$ 
 $\displaystyle=\underbrace{\frac{\partial\alpha_{s}}{\partial\hat{\pi}_{\beta}(s)}}_{=N}\cdot\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}_{\text{MSE}}}{\partial\alpha_{s}}$ 

(12)

 $\displaystyle=N\left[2\left(\frac{\alpha_{s}}{\alpha_{0}}-q_{s}\right)\frac{1}{\alpha_{0}}+\frac{\alpha_{0}-2\alpha_{s}}{\alpha_{0}^{2}(\alpha_{0}+1)}\right]$ 

 $\displaystyle=\frac{N}{\alpha_{0}^{2}(\alpha_{0}+1)}\left[2(\alpha_{s}-q_{s}\alpha_{0})(\alpha_{0}+1)+\alpha_{0}-2\alpha_{s}\right]$ 

 $\displaystyle=\frac{N}{\alpha_{0}(\alpha_{0}+1)}\left[2\alpha_{s}-2q_{s}(\alpha_{0}+1)+1\right].$ 

Substituting $\alpha_{0}=N+|\mathcal{S}|$ yields

 $d_{s}=\frac{N\left[\,2\alpha_{s}-2(N+|\mathcal{S}|+1)\,q_{s}+1\,\right]}{(N+|\mathcal{S}|)(N+|\mathcal{S}|+1)},$ 

(13)

Step 2: backpropagation from $\hat{\pi}_{\beta}$ to $\ell^{(i)}$ .
Let $U:=\sum_{s\in\mathcal{S}}U_{s}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}u^{(i)}$ . Then:

1. 

 $\displaystyle\frac{\partial\hat{\pi}_{\beta}(s)}{\partial U_{r}}=\frac{1}{U}\left(\delta_{sr}-\hat{\pi}_{\beta}(s)\right)$ , by direct differentiation of $U_{s}/U$ .

2. 

 $\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}_{\text{MSE}}}{\partial U_{r}}=\sum_{s\in\mathcal{S}}d_{s}\cdot\frac{1}{U}\left(\delta_{sr}-\hat{\pi}_{\beta}(s)\right)=\frac{1}{U}\left(d_{r}-\bar{d}\right)$ .

3. 

 $\partial U_{s}/\partial u^{(i)}=\mathbbm{1}\{s^{(i)}=s\}$ , hence $\partial\mathcal{L}_{\text{MSE}}/\partial u^{(i)}=(1/U)\left(d_{s^{(i)}}-\bar{d}\right)$ .

4. 

 $\partial u^{(i)}/\partial\ell^{(i)}=(\beta-1)\,u^{(i)}$ , so

 $\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}_{\text{MSE}}}{\partial\ell^{(i)}}=\frac{(\beta-1)\,u^{(i)}}{U}\left(d_{s^{(i)}}-\bar{d}\right)=(\beta-1)\,w^{(i)}\left(d_{s^{(i)}}-\bar{d}\right),$ 

which is Eq. 8.
∎

## A.3 Proof of Corollary 1

Proof of Corollary 1.

Substituting $\alpha_{s}=N\hat{\pi}_{\beta}(s)+1$ into Eq. 13 reveals that the class-level derivatives $d_{s}$ possess opposite signs for the ground-truth class and the incorrect classes. Specifically, for the ground-truth class $s^{*}$ , we have

 $d_{s^{*}}\propto 2N(\hat{\pi}_{\beta}(s^{*})-1)-2|\mathcal{S}|+1<0,$ 

and for any incorrect class $s\neq s^{*}$ , we have

 $d_{s}\propto 2N\hat{\pi}_{\beta}(s)+3>0.$ 

Consequently, $d_{s^{*}}<d_{s}$ holds for every $s\neq s^{*}$ . Furthermore, since $d_{s}$ strictly increases with respect to $\hat{\pi}_{\beta}(s)$ among the incorrect classes, the most overconfident incorrect class defined by $s^{-}:=\arg\max_{s\neq s^{*}}\hat{\pi}_{\beta}(s)$ 
attains the maximum value of $d_{s}$ among the incorrect classes, and therefore among all classes.
From Theorem 1, the gradient with respect to the sample log-likelihood is given by

 $\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}_{\text{MSE}}}{\partial\ell^{(i)}}=(\beta-1)w^{(i)}(d_{s^{(i)}}-\bar{d}),\qquad\text{where }\bar{d}=\sum_{s}\hat{\pi}_{\beta}(s)d_{s}.$ 

Given that $\beta>1$ and the normalized sample weight $w^{(i)}>0$ , the sign of the gradient is entirely determined by the difference $d_{s^{(i)}}-\bar{d}$ .
Because $\bar{d}$ is a convex combination of all class-level derivatives and $d_{s^{*}}$ represents the global minimum among them, it follows naturally that $d_{s^{*}}-\bar{d}\leq 0$ .
Therefore, for any sample belonging to the ground-truth class ( $s^{(i)}=s^{*}$ ), the gradient satisfies $\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}_{\text{MSE}}}{\partial\ell^{(i)}}\leq 0.$ 
This inequality is strict provided the semantic distribution has not fully collapsed to the ground-truth class ( $\hat{\pi}_{\beta}(s^{*})<1$ ), because the convex combination $\bar{d}$ will include positive weights from the strictly larger derivatives of the incorrect classes.

Similarly, because $d_{s^{-}}$ is the global maximum among the class-level derivatives, we have $d_{s^{-}}-\bar{d}\geq 0$ .
Thus, for any sample mapped to the most overconfident incorrect class ( $s^{(i)}=s^{-}$ ), the gradient satisfies $\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}_{\text{MSE}}}{\partial\ell^{(i)}}\geq 0$ .
This inequality becomes strict whenever $\hat{\pi}_{\beta}(s^{*})>0$ , as the convex combination $\bar{d}$ then incorporates a positive weight from the strictly smaller derivative $d_{s^{*}}<d_{s^{-}}$ . This concludes the proof.
∎

## A.4 Proof of Corollary 2

Proof of Corollary 2.

Using Theorem 1 and $\sum_{j:s^{(j)}=s}w^{(j)}=\hat{\pi}_{\beta}(s)$ , we have

 $\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}_{\text{MSE}}}{\partial\ell^{(i)}}=(\beta-1)\sum_{i=1}^{N}w^{(i)}(d_{s^{(i)}}-\bar{d})=(\beta-1)\left(\sum_{s}\hat{\pi}_{\beta}(s)d_{s}-\bar{d}\sum_{i=1}^{N}w^{(i)}\right)=0.$ 

The last equality follows from $\bar{d}=\sum_{s}\hat{\pi}_{\beta}(s)d_{s}$ and $\sum_{i=1}^{N}w^{(i)}=1$ .
∎

## A.5 Relation to GRPO-like updates

In this subsection, we show that S-EDL gradients can also be interpreted as GRPO-like updates.
Before detailing this connection, we note that the SFT term in $\mathcal{L}_{\text{S-EDL}}$ is not essential to the formulation of S-EDL.
In principle, the model can be trained using $\mathcal{L}_{\text{MSE}}$ alone with $\beta>1$ .
Its practical role is to provide dense token-level supervision early in training, when correct completions are rare and the positive signal from sampled semantic classes can be weak, as in sparse-reward learning.
Therefore, the following analysis omits the SFT term and focuses on $\mathcal{L}_{\text{MSE}}$ .

For a fixed set of sampled sequences and semantic assignments, Eq. 8 gives S-EDL a group-relative update structure similar to GRPO [40].
The key idea is to interpret the negative gradient $-\partial\mathcal{L}_{\text{MSE}}/\partial\ell^{(i)}$ as a reward signal for each sampled sequence.
Define the uncentered class reward $r_{s}:=-d_{s}$ and its weighted mean $\bar{r}:=\sum_{s\in\mathcal{S}}\hat{\pi}_{\beta}(s)r_{s}=-\bar{d}$ .
The negative gradient with respect to each sample log-likelihood then acts as an advantage coefficient:

 $A^{(i)}:=-\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}_{\text{MSE}}}{\partial\ell^{(i)}}=(\beta-1)w^{(i)}\bigl(r_{s^{(i)}}-\bar{r}\bigr),$ 

(14)

and then we have

 $-\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\mathcal{L}_{\text{MSE}}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}A^{(i)}\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\ell^{(i)}.$ 

(15)

Here, the group comparison is over the semantic classes associated with the same prompt.
The class derivative $d_{s^{(i)}}$ is centered by $\bar{d}$ , and the centered signal is modulated by $(\beta-1)w^{(i)}$ , with the sign reversed for likelihood ascent.
Original GRPO centers rewards over sampled completions using their arithmetic mean and divides by a reward standard deviation shared by the group.
S-EDL instead uses a semantic-probability-weighted mean and a sample-dependent modulation factor, so completions in the same class share the class reward but can receive different update weights.

This interpretation allows S-EDL to be incorporated into proximal policy optimization (PPO) implementations [38], with clipping and micro-batch updates as in GRPO variants.
This can improve the sample efficiency and stability of S-EDL training.

## Appendix B Implementation details

## B.1 Construction of semantic class set and handling unobserved ground-truth classes

Previous work on semantic calibration [33] establishes evaluation metrics based entirely on the sampled distribution. When adapting this purely sample-based perspective into a training objective, if the correct semantic class is absent from the generated samples, the evidential loss $\mathcal{L}_{\text{MSE}}$ can still be computed, as the label vector $\bm{q}$ then simply consists of all zero entries. Consequently, the gradient only suppresses the incorrect overconfident completions, but lacks the effect of increasing the likelihoods of the correct completions.

To remedy this and ensure the gradient direction toward the ground-truth class established in Corollary 1 remains available, S-EDL conditionally introduces $s^{*}$ into the semantic class set $\mathcal{S}_{x}$ strictly when it is absent from the generated samples. This guarantees that we do not artificially inflate the sample count or alter the distribution when the model naturally generates the correct answer. We apply this conditional handling as follows to maximize consistency across the training and evaluation pipelines.

• 

When the ground-truth class is already present in the sampled generations, we use the sampled set directly without any modifications during both training and evaluation.

• 

When the ground-truth class is unobserved during training, we explicitly include it by feeding the ground-truth sequence $y$ to the model to compute its log-likelihood and applying a penalty of $-4.6$ . This penalty scales down its probability mass to reflect that the sequence was not naturally sampled, while still preserving the gradient path for optimization.

• 

When the ground-truth class is unobserved during evaluation, we similarly include it but strictly set its sampled count to $0$ , ensuring the evaluation accurately reflects the true generative distribution of the model.

Deterministically adding the ground-truth completion $y$ means the estimator is no longer a pure Monte Carlo estimator based solely on samples from $p_{\bm{\theta}}$ . Instead, it creates a hybrid objective that mixes the generative distribution of the model with a supervised finite-candidate signal. We accept this theoretical trade-off to maintain a robust and differentiable training path.

Sequence-level training methods like Dr.GRPO require comparing $N$ sampled sequences against the ground truth answer, resulting in an $O(N)$ computational complexity per prompt. By contrast, computing the evidential loss in S-EDL requires grouping the generated sequences into semantic classes. A naive pairwise comparison between all sequences scales as $O(N^{2})$ , imposing a substantial computational bottleneck.

To address this scaling gap, we introduce a find-first semantic clustering algorithm as shown in Algorithm 1. Instead of brute-force pairwise comparison, the algorithm maintains a growing list of cluster representatives and dynamically evaluates each new sequence against them. To optimize the comparison order, we sort the existing representatives by descending string similarity to the new sequence using rapidfuzz [2]. The equivalence oracle Match is then instantiated in two ways depending on the evaluation domain.

Mathematical tasks   $\textsc{Match}(z,[r_{1},\dots,r_{K}])$ evaluates equivalence using a symbolic verifier (e.g. math-verify[21]). It sequentially checks the candidate against the sorted representatives and returns the first index $j$ where $z\equiv r_{j}$ , returning $\bot$ if no match exists. The string similarity ordering heavily biases the search toward early termination.

General tasks  For general subject tasks that rely on an LLM as a judge, Match replaces iterative pairwise checks with a single listwise classification prompt. The judge evaluates the sequence against all current cluster representatives in one forward pass, directly returning the matched index in $\{1,\dots,K\}$ or $\bot$ . The system is shown in Section C.5.1. In this context, the string similarity ordering ensures the most plausible candidate appears first in the prompt context. By grouping the comparisons into a single listwise operation, the temporal complexity of semantic clustering is reduced to exactly $O(N)$ , identically matching the scaling of Dr.GRPO.

## B.3 Dataset construction

GSM8K

We use the original training and test splits from the official release, along with the provided solutions and chain-of-thought reasoning steps.

MATH

We utilize the official training split and the associated chain-of-thought solutions. For evaluation, we employ the widely adopted MATH-500 test split.

ChemistryQA

We use the official training and test splits. To construct the training data, we prompt the GPT-OSS-120B model [1] to generate chain-of-thought solutions, retaining only those that yield the correct final answer. This filtering process results in 3062 training samples. For evaluation, we use the complete test set and assess performance directly against the provided ground-truth answers.

FormulaReasoning

We similarly use the official training and test splits. We generate chain-of-thought solutions using the GPT-OSS-120B model and filter for correctness, which yields 4096 training samples. We evaluate on the complete test set using the official ground-truth answers. During the cross-domain evaluation, we observed that models fine-tuned on the MATH dataset experience a severe accuracy degradation for all studied fine-tuning methods. This drop makes calibration performance indistinguishable across the fine-tuning methods, as most samples exhibit uniformly low semantic confidence and low accuracy across all methods. We identified that the models struggle to recall the necessary physical laws. Consequently, we manually augment the testing prompts with the relevant physical laws as hints. These hint-augmented prompts are applied exclusively during the cross-domain evaluation. Furthermore, these hint-augmented prompts are used across evaluation of all fine-tuning methods to ensure fairness.

Examples of the training prompts are provided in Section C.6.

## B.5 Hyperparameters of baselines

BLoB

During training, we set bayes_samples to 1, bayes_epsilon to 0.05, bayes_beta to 0.2, and bayes_kllr to 0.1, with bayes_kl_reweighting enabled. During evaluation, we use 5 Bayesian samples. We refer readers to the official BLoB codebase for the precise definitions of these hyperparameters.

RCFT

During training, we configure the hyperparameters by setting rcft_alpha to 0.5, rcft_calibration_scale to 0.001, rcft_num_bins to 10, and rcft_eps to 0.000001. Please refer to the official RCFT codebase for detailed explanations of these variables.

LS

During training, we apply a label smoothing coefficient of $0.1$ .

Dr.GRPO

We start the fine tuning process from the supervised checkpoint at epoch 1. To ensure a fair comparison, we set the total number of training steps for Dr.GRPO to exactly match the number of steps where the evidential loss is active during S-EDL training. ## C.6 Examples of training prompts

In the following, we provide examples of the training prompts used for fine tuning Qwen3-8B-Base. We intentionally do not enclose the chain of thought reasoning within <think> and </think> blocks. We empirically observed that the base models have not been extensively trained on data containing these special tokens. Introducing these structural tokens during LoRA fine tuning causes the model to append random text after the \boxed{} output and occasionally become trapped in repetitive generation loops.

Question: How many 4-letter words with at least one consonant can be constructed from the letters $A$, $B$, $C$, $D$, and $E$? (Note that $B$, $C$, and $D$ are consonants, any word is valid, not just English language words, and letters may be used more than once.)

Answer:<|im_end|>

<|im_start|>assistant

Reasoning: First we count the number of all 4-letter words with no restrictions on the word. Then we count the number of 4-letter words with no consonants. We then subtract to get the answer.

Each letter of a word must be one of $A$, $B$, $C$, $D$, or $E$, so the number of 4-letter words with no restrictions on the word is $5\times 5\times 5\times 5=625$. Each letter of a word with no consonant must be one of $A$ or $E$. So the number of all 4-letter words with no consonants is $2\times 2\times 2\times 2=16$. Therefore, the number of 4-letter words with at least one consonant is $625-16=609$.

•

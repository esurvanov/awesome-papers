Abstract

Online reinforcement learning (RL) has achieved significant success in decision-making tasks, but modern online RL remains highly sensitive to the quality of training experience. Understanding the contribution of individual training instances is therefore central to data-centric RL, with applications ranging from experience selection to policy interpretation. Existing data attribution methods for online RL primarily focus on local influence within a single training round, overlooking the non-local data influence across multiple training rounds. In this work, we formalize data attribution in online RL through a trajectory-level leave-one-out quantity, RL-LOO, which measures how one training sample influences a downstream target after subsequent online updates have taken place. We then derive RL-IF, a first-order estimator that propagates data influence through both optimization effects and policy-induced sampling effects, and provide theoretical guarantees on its approximation accuracy under smoothness assumptions. Our analysis further disentangles these two effects and shows both theoretically and empirically that the full RL-IF estimator can often be well approximated using the optimization effect alone. Building on this observation, we develop RINSE, a practical data filtering method for online RL. Experiments on standard control benchmarks and an RLHF-style toxicity-mitigation setting show that RINSE improves training efficiency and final performance over standard PPO and local attribution baselines. These results suggest that RL-IF provides a practical and principled framework for understanding and improving online RL.

## 1 Introduction

Online reinforcement learning (RL) has become a central paradigm for learning decision-making policies through repeated interaction with an environment, from robotic control (Todorov et al., 2012; Andrychowicz et al., 2020) to the alignment and enhancement of large language models (LLMs) (Ouyang et al., 2022; Shao et al., 2024). Despite this success, modern online RL remains highly sensitive to the quality of training experience: training often requires millions of interactions, while the quality of collected experience can vary substantially across time and trajectories, leading to well-known challenges such as sample inefficiency, high variance, and training instability (Yu, 2018; Henderson et al., 2018; Dulac-Arnold et al., 2019). Hence, understanding the contribution of individual training instances from these experiences is fundamental to data-centric studies of RL and has broad implications for applications such as data selection (Hu et al., 2026; Zhu et al., 2026), experience replay (Schaul et al., 2016), or interpretation (Hu et al., 2025).

Data attribution offers a natural lens for understanding the role of individual training instances. In supervised learning, data attribution aims to identify the training examples that most strongly drive model behavior (Koh and Liang, 2017; Deng et al., 2025), with applications in model debugging (Koh and Liang, 2017), mislabeled data detection (Pruthi et al., 2020), data valuation (Ghorbani and Zou, 2019; Jia et al., 2019), and data selection (Xia et al., 2024). Recently, Hu et al. (2025) introduce data attribution to online RL, and develop a local attribution framework for PPO. The proposed framework measures the influence of rollout records on a local policy update within a round, and shows that such local scores can identify harmful training time steps and improve sample efficiency through data filtering.

However, the existing attribution methods are not yet principled and capture only a partial notion of data influence in online RL. First, they mainly rely on heuristic influence estimation, such as gradient dot products, which are intuitive and scalable, but do not fully account for the training dynamics or correspond to a clear counterfactual notion of data value (Koh and Liang, 2017). Second, the scope of existing attribution methods is restricted to a single training round, leaving the influence of training data across rounds largely unexplored. Characterizing such inter-round influence is more challenging in online RL because the effect of a single training occurrence can propagate across future rounds through subsequent optimization updates and, in principle, through changes in the policy-induced distribution of future rollout data.

This motivates the development of a framework that directly estimates counterfactual influence beyond the local scope of individual RL training rounds. To address this, we formulate data attribution in online RL through a principled leave-one-out objective defined over multiple training rounds, and use it to study the non-local value of rollout data. Our main contributions are summarized as follows:

• 

A non-local attribution framework for online RL (Section 3.1). Prior online-RL attribution methods are local and heuristic; we formulate non-local data attribution in online RL through an RL leave-one-out objective, RL-LOO, defined over the training trajectory.

• 

A first-order estimator with optimization and sampling effects (Section 3.2). We derive RL-IF, a first-order estimator of RL-LOO, and show that online-RL data influence decomposes into optimization effect and sampling effect. This yields both a conceptual lens for understanding long-horizon influence and a computationally efficient approximation, RL-IF-opt, that is well supported by our theory and experiments.

• 

A practical data-selection algorithm with downstream gains (Section 4). Building on an efficient approximation to RL-IF, we develop RINSE, a non-local attribution-based filtering method for online RL. Across standard benchmark environments and an RLHF-style toxicity-mitigation setting, RINSE improves training efficiency and final performance relative to standard PPO and local filtering baselines.

## 2 Preliminaries

## 2.1 Background

Online RL algorithms.

We consider an online reinforcement learning (RL) process indexed by rounds $t=1,\ldots,T$ . At round $t$ , the current policy $\pi_{\theta_{t}}$ interacts with the environment to collect a rollout buffer $D_{t}$ , after which the policy is updated for $M$ gradient steps. In this work, we focus on a specific RL algorithm, proximal policy optimization (PPO) (Schulman et al., 2017). Given the state action pair $(s,a)$ , the standard clipped PPO objective maximizes

 $L^{\mathrm{clip}}(\theta):=\mathbb{E}_{(s,a)\sim D_{t}}\Big[\min\big(r_{t}(\theta)\hat{A},\mathrm{clip}(r_{t}(\theta),1-\epsilon,1+\epsilon)\hat{A}\big)\Big],\quad r_{t}(\theta):=\frac{\pi_{\theta}(a\mid s)}{\pi_{\theta_{t}}(a\mid s)}.$ 

(1)

Here, $r_{t}(\theta)$ is the policy ratio, $\hat{A}$ is an advantage estimate, $\pi_{\theta_{t}}$ is the behavior policy used to collect the rollout, and $\epsilon$ is the clipping threshold. Schulman et al. (2017) also introduced an alternative formulation of the PPO objective, in which the clipping mechanism is replaced by KL regularization. Specifically, let $\beta$ be the coefficient of the KL penalty, and the KL-regularized form of PPO maximizes

 $L^{\mathrm{KL}}(\theta):=\mathbb{E}_{(s,a)\sim D_{t}}\big[r_{t}(\theta)\hat{A}-\beta\,\mathrm{KL}\big(\pi_{\theta_{t}}(\cdot\mid s)\,\|\,\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid s)\big)\big].$ 

Let $P_{t}(\cdot\mid\theta_{t})$ denote the distribution over rollout buffers induced by $\pi_{\theta_{t}}$ , where a rollout buffer $D_{t}$ is the collection of trajectories sampled in round $t$ . Write $D_{t}^{(j)}\subseteq D_{t}$ for the $j$ -th minibatch in round $t$ , and $\theta_{t}^{(j)}$ for the parameter at the $j$ -th gradient step, with $\theta_{t}=\theta_{t}^{(0)}=\theta_{t-1}^{(M)}$ . For simplicity, we assume that the policy is updated with stochastic gradient descent, and each update takes the form:

 $\theta_{t}^{(j)}=\theta_{t}^{(j-1)}-\frac{\eta_{t}}{b}\sum_{x\in D_{t}^{(j)}}\nabla_{\theta}\ell(\theta_{t}^{(j-1)},x),\qquad D_{t}\sim P_{t}(\cdot\mid\theta_{t}),$ 

(2)

where $\eta_{t}$ is the learning rate, $b$ is the minibatch size, and $\ell$ denotes the PPO loss.

Figure 1: Two-fold data influence in RL. A training occurrence $z^{*}\in D_{k}^{(l)}$ affects learning through (i) the optimization path (blue): propagating via parameter updates to $\theta_{T}^{(M)}$ , and (ii) the sampling path (orange): altering future rollout buffers $D_{k+1},D_{k+2},\dots$ . These effects are coupled through the policy-induced data distribution.

Local data attribution in online RL.

Hu et al. (2025) propose a local data attribution framework for online RL, which measures how much a training sample contributes to a downstream target function $f$ . In this setting, the training samples are scored by gradient inner products between the per-sample gradient and gradient on an attribution target $f$ . The local attribution score is estimated as

 $I^{(k,l)}(z^{*};f):=-\eta_{k}\,\left\langle\nabla_{\theta}\ell(\theta,z^{*}),\nabla_{\theta}f(\theta)\right\rangle,$ 

(3)

A key design choice is the attribution target $f$ . Hu et al. (2025) propose to leverage a policy-gradient surrogate for downstream return, defined on a reference validation distribution. Given a reference policy $\pi^{\mathrm{ref}}$ and a reference advantage estimator $\hat{A}^{\mathrm{ref}}$ , Hu et al. (2025) define

 $f^{\mathrm{return}}(\theta):=\mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim\pi^{\mathrm{ref}},\,(s,a)\sim\tau}\big[\log\pi_{\theta}(a\mid s)\,\hat{A}^{\mathrm{ref}}(s,a)\big].$ 

(4)

The target gives the attribution score a direct behavioral interpretation: a positive score means the sample pushes the policy toward higher surrogate return, whereas a negative score suggests that removing the sample may help.

Two-fold data influence in online RL.

In online RL, the influence of a training sample is not limited to its direct effect on a parameter update. A rollout affects learning through two coupled channels, as demonstrated in Figure 1. First, it influences the checkpoint by contributing to the gradient update that produces the next parameter. This is the usual optimization effect of data: removing one sample perturbs the parameter trajectory and therefore the final checkpoint. Second, the same rollout influences future data sampling. Since subsequent rollout buffers are collected by policies obtained from earlier checkpoints, perturbing one training sample changes not only the next iterate but also the state-action distribution from which later data are drawn. This is referred to as the sampling effect: a sample influences future learning by altering the distribution of later data. Hence, in on-policy RL, data influence is inherently two-fold: it acts on both the optimization path and the sampling path. Moreover, the two effects are generally entangled rather than separable: a perturbation to one sample changes the data distribution of future rollouts, and this distributional shift in turn alters the subsequent optimization trajectory.

## 3 Measuring Data Influence in Online RL

In this section, we systematically formulate and approximate data influence in online reinforcement learning. We first introduce a leave-one-out counterfactual objective, RL-LOO, which measures the long-term effect of removing a training occurrence. Then, we derive a first-order influence estimator, RL-IF, that approximates this counterfactual without retraining by propagating perturbations through the training trajectory. The resulting recursion naturally decomposes data influence into the aforementioned optimization effect and sampling effect, and admits theoretical guarantees under suitable smoothness assumptions. Finally, we analyze both the effectiveness of RL-IF and the relative contributions of these two effects through theoretical analysis and empirical evaluation.

## 3.1 Problem Formulation

Following prior work, we study data attribution in an online PPO-style training pipeline, and extend it beyond individual RL training rounds. Let $\theta_{T}^{(M)}$ denote the final policy parameter after all rounds of training. Our goal is to systematically measure how one training sample would affect a downstream target after subsequent online updates have taken place.

We formalize this through a leave-one-out (LOO) (Koh and Liang, 2017) counterfactual specific to RL, or RL-LOO, following a similar setup in supervised learning literature (Hara et al., 2019; Wang et al., 2025). The unit of attribution is a particular occurrence of a sample in a training minibatch, written as $z^{*}\in D_{k}^{(l)}$ , which specifies both the sample identity and the minibatch in which it is used. Let $\widetilde{\theta}_{T}^{(M;-z^{*})}$ denote the final parameter obtained by removing this occurrence from the training trajectory and replaying the remaining optimization process, including the induced changes in future sampling. For a downstream target function $f$ , RL-LOO is defined as

 $\mathrm{RL}\text{-}\mathrm{LOO}^{(k,l)}(z^{*};f):=f(\widetilde{\theta}_{T}^{(M;-z^{*})})-f(\theta_{T}^{(M)}).$ 

This quantity measures the effect of removing one training occurrence on a downstream target after subsequent training. As for the attribution target, we adopt the negative of the target defined in (4), so that negative scores identify occurrences whose removal improves the surrogate objective. In the non-local setting, the reference policy is naturally chosen as the final policy $\pi_{\theta_{T}}^{(M)}$ , so that the target measures how removing one training occurrence changes the surrogate return of the final policy on its own induced distribution. $\hat{A}^{\mathrm{ref}}$ can be approximated using the corresponding critic and the same GAE procedure and hyperparameters as PPO. When exact advantages are used, we show that RL-LOO with this target serves as a local proxy for the change in final expected return in Appendix A.4.

## 3.2 Approximating Leave-One-Out in RL

We now derive a first-order estimator, RL-IF (RL InFluence), for approximating RL-LOO. At a high level, RL-LOO asks how the final policy would change if a training sample were removed from a rollout-based training trajectory. Rather than explicitly retraining the policy, RL-IF approximates this counterfactual effect through first-order perturbation analysis.

Consider the RL training process in (2). Let $z^{*}\in D_{k}^{(l)}$ be a sample removed from update $(k,l)$ , and let $\theta_{t}^{(j)}(\varepsilon)$ denote the perturbed training trajectory obtained by continuously downweighting $z^{*}$ by a scalar $\varepsilon$ . A first-order Taylor expansion gives

 $\mathrm{RL}\text{-}\mathrm{LOO}^{(k,l)}(z^{*};f)\approx\nabla_{\theta}f(\theta_{T}^{(M)})^{\top}\Delta_{T}^{(M)},\qquad\Delta_{t}^{(j)}:=\left.\frac{\partial\theta_{t}^{(j)}(\varepsilon)}{\partial\varepsilon}\right|_{\varepsilon=0},$ 

(5)

which motivates the first-order estimator

 $\mathrm{RL}\text{-}\mathrm{IF}^{(k,l)}(z^{*};f):=\nabla_{\theta}f(\theta_{T}^{(M)})^{\top}\Delta_{T}^{(M)}.$ 

The key step is to characterize the perturbation trajectory $\Delta_{t}^{(j)}$ . In online RL, perturbations propagate through two distinct channels, the optimization effect and the sampling effect, respectively. To unify the effects from both channels, we define the stage update operator

 $F_{t}^{(j)}(\vartheta,\beta):=\vartheta-\frac{\eta_{t}}{b}\sum_{x\sim S_{t}^{(j)}(\beta)}\nabla_{\theta}\ell(\vartheta,x),\quad S_{t}^{(j)}(\beta):=\begin{cases}D_{t}^{(j)},&\text{if }t=k\text{ and }j>l,\\
P_{t}(\cdot\mid\beta),&\text{if }t\geq k+1.\end{cases}$ 

(6)

where $\vartheta$ denotes the parameter being updated and $\beta$ denotes the policy parameter controlling data collection. Differentiating the update recursion with respect to $\varepsilon$ yields

 $\Delta_{t}^{(j)}=\frac{\partial F_{t}^{(j)}}{\partial\vartheta}\Delta_{t}^{(j-1)}+\frac{\partial F_{t}^{(j)}}{\partial\beta}\Delta_{t}^{(0)},$ 

where the resulting recursion naturally distinguishes the two channels: the first term associated with the current parameters $\vartheta$ captures the optimization effect, and the second term related to the sampling parameters $\beta$ captures the sampling effect. With further simplification, the resulting first-order perturbation recursion is summarized below.

Proposition 3.1: First-order perturbation recursion for RL-IF

Let $\Delta_{t}^{(j)}$ denote the perturbation induced by downweighting sample $z^{*}\in D_{k}^{(l)}$ . The perturbation is initialized as

 $\Delta_{k}^{(l)}=\frac{\eta_{k}}{b}\nabla_{\theta}\ell(\theta_{k}^{(l-1)},z^{*}),$ 

and evolves according to

 $\Delta_{t}^{(j)}=\begin{cases}A_{t}^{(j)}\Delta_{t}^{(j-1)},&\text{if }t=k,\ j>l,\\[4.0pt]
A_{t}^{(j)}\Delta_{t}^{(j-1)}+B_{t}^{(j)}\Delta_{t}^{(0)},&\text{if }t\geq k+1,\end{cases}$ 

(7)

where

 $A_{t}^{(j)}:=I-\frac{\eta_{t}}{b}H_{t}^{(j)},\qquad B_{t}^{(j)}:=-\frac{\eta_{t}}{b}\sum_{x\in D_{t}^{(j)}}\nabla_{\theta}\ell(\theta_{t}^{(j-1)},x)\nabla_{\theta}\log P_{t}(x\mid\theta_{t})^{\top}.$ 

Here $A_{t}^{(j)}$ and $B_{t}^{(j)}$ correspond to the optimization and sampling effects, respectively.

The recursion formula of RL-IF in Proposition 3.2 propagates the first-order effect of removing $z^{*}$ through both later optimization steps and future sampling decisions. The term $A_{t}^{(j)}\Delta_{t}^{(j-1)}$ captures the optimization effect, while $B_{t}^{(j)}\Delta_{t}^{(0)}$ captures the additional sampling effect caused by policy-dependent data collection. Notably, the sampling effect vanishes when the perturbation remains within the same training round. In practice, we use $\nabla\log\pi_{\theta_{t}}(a\mid s)$ to approximate $\nabla\log P_{t}((s,a)\mid\theta_{t})$ . Note that both matrix multiplications in this formula can be tackled efficiently: multiplying by $A_{t}^{(j)}$ can be computed using Hessian Vector Product (HVP), and multiplying by $B_{t}^{(j)}$ is essentially two vector products. We further provide a detailed derivation of the recursion, together with a first-order approximation guarantee for RL-IF under smoothness assumptions, in Appendix A.1 and Appendix B.1.

## 3.3 Sampling vs. Optimization Effects in RL-IF

A more detailed analysis of RL-IF can be obtained by unrolling the recursion and separating the optimization and sampling effects. In particular, the recursion in (7) admits a compact closed form.

 $\mathrm{RL}\text{-}\mathrm{IF}^{(k,l)}(z^{*};f)=\nabla_{\theta}f(\theta_{T}^{(M)})^{\top}\left(\prod_{t=T}^{k+1}C_{t}\right)\left(\prod_{j=M}^{l+1}A_{k}^{(j)}\right)\frac{\eta_{k}}{b}\nabla_{\theta}\ell(\theta_{k}^{(l-1)},z^{*}).$ 

(8)

Here, for each stage $t>k$ ,

 $A_{t}:=\prod_{j=M}^{1}A_{t}^{(j)},\qquad C_{t}:=A_{t}+\sum_{q=1}^{M}\left(\prod_{j=M}^{q+1}A_{t}^{(j)}\right)B_{t}^{(q)}.$ 

Equation (8) is the closed-form solution of the recursion. Here, the product of $A_{t}$ captures the accumulated optimization effect, while the terms involving $B_{t}^{(j)}$ capture the accumulated sampling effect. When $B_{t}^{(q)}=0$ , the expression reduces to the optimization-only case. The following theorem formalizes the gap between the optimization-only and full sampling-aware effects.

Theorem 3.1: Accumulated relative gap

Assume that $\ell(\theta;x)$ is twice differentiable, that its gradient norm is bounded by $G$ , that its Hessian norm is bounded by $\Lambda$ . Assume in addition that for each future stage $t>k$ , the sampling family $P_{t}(\cdot\mid\theta)$ is KL-smooth with score second moment bounded by $L_{\max}$ . Assume the learning rate takes the harmonic form $\eta_{t}=\frac{\eta_{0}}{M(t-k)}$ with $\eta_{0}\Lambda<1$ . Then, for any $T>k$ with $H:=T-k$ , the accumulated gap satisfies

 $\left\|\prod_{t=T}^{k+1}C_{t}-\prod_{t=T}^{k+1}A_{t}\right\|_{2}\leq(e^{\alpha}H^{\alpha}-1)\prod_{t=k+1}^{T}\|A_{t}\|_{2},$ 

(9)

where $\alpha:=\frac{G\sqrt{L_{\max}}\,\eta_{0}}{(1-\eta_{0}\Lambda)^{2}}$ .

The bound in (9) shows that the sampling effect remains a relative perturbation to the optimization effect. In particular, if $\eta_{0}\Lambda\ll 1$ , the accumulated gap over $H$ stages grows only logarithmically

 $e^{\alpha}H^{\alpha}-1\approx\alpha(1+\log H)$

Accordingly, we define the optimization-only variant RL-IF-opt as the estimator obtained by setting $B_{t}^{(j)}=0$ in the recursion, or equivalently by dropping the sampling terms in the closed-form operator $C_{t}$ . The original estimator that retains both optimization and sampling terms is denoted RL-IF-full. The gap suggests that RL-IF is often dominated by its optimization component, making RL-IF-opt a compelling simplification in practice. The proof of Theorem 3.3 is deferred to Appendix B.3.

Experimental comparison.

To further validate the theoretical findings, we evaluate attribution quality using Linear Data Modeling Scores (LDS) (Park et al., 2023). LDS measures the Spearman correlation between aggregated attribution scores and the observed effect of removing subsets of training data under retraining. We compare the two variants of RL-IF, along with TracIn (Pruthi et al., 2020), an attribution method that does not account for optimization or sampling dynamics, and a random baseline. We consider KL-regularized PPO on two consecutive training rounds, where retraining is performed with future rollouts resampled using the updated policy. The results are shown in Table 1. Both RL-IF-opt and RL-IF-full substantially outperform local attribution methods such as TracIn, while exhibiting nearly identical performance to each other. This small gap suggests that, empirically, the sampling effect contributes only marginally beyond the optimization effect. Additional setup and evaluation details are provided in Appendix D.1.

## 4 Data Selection with RL-IF

We demonstrate a direct downstream application of RL-IF in online RL: using RL-IF attribution scores to guide data selection over multiple training rounds. The goal is to identify and remove training samples that are harmful to downstream performance, thereby improving both compute efficiency and sample efficiency. We build our data selection algorithm on KL-regularized PPO, since the clipping objective in (1) is piecewise-defined and introduces non-smooth behavior at the clipping boundary, which is less compatible with the first-order approximation used in RL-IF. Our source code is available at https://github.com/sx-liu/RL-IF.

RINSE: RL-IF-based Sample Selection. The RL-IF formulation naturally leads to a data-selection algorithm, which we call RINSE (RL-INfluence-based Sample sElection), over a finite horizon window. Suppose we aim to improve PPO training for $H$ rounds, starting from round $k$ . We first run the standard PPO pipeline for $H$ subsequent rounds, obtaining policies $\pi_{\theta_{k+1}},\ldots,\pi_{\theta_{k+H}}$ and the corresponding training trajectory $\{D_{t}^{(j)}\}_{t=k}^{k+H-1}$ . This rollout trajectory provides the reference path on which long-horizon influence is evaluated.

Given this trajectory, we estimate the influence of samples in $\{D_{t}\}_{t=k}^{k+H-1}$ using RL-IF, with the final policy $\pi_{\theta_{k+H}}$ in this window as the reference policy in (4). Because the optimization-only approximation achieves comparable empirical performance at substantially lower cost, RINSE uses the optimization-only variant, RL-IF-opt, in the selection procedure. We rank samples by their attribution scores, remove those with the most negative influence based on discard ratio $\rho$ , and then retrain on the filtered datasets to obtain updated policies $\pi_{\theta_{k+1}^{\prime}},\ldots,\pi_{\theta_{k+H}^{\prime}}$ . Since this retraining stage is inherently off-policy, we use an adjusted retraining objective,

 $L^{\mathrm{KL\text{-}Retrain}}(\theta):=\mathbb{E}\big[r_{t}(\theta)\hat{A}_{t}-\beta\,\mathrm{KL}\big(\pi_{\theta_{t}^{\prime}}(\cdot\mid s_{t})\,\|\,\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid s_{t})\big)\big],$ 

which limits policy drift from the reference policies along the retraining trajectory.

Design of attribution horizon. The effectiveness of attribution-based selection depends on the attribution horizon and the choice of reference policy. Short horizons lead to overly myopic attribution that reduces to local effects, while excessively long horizons introduce severe off-policy mismatch between the selected data and later training dynamics. Moreover, the reference policy must be sufficiently strong to provide a reliable attribution target.

To balance these considerations, we adopt a window-based data selection strategy, which partitions training into consecutive windows and performs attribution within each window independently. This maintains proximity to the current training dynamics while still capturing non-local effects over multiple future updates.

We further use an adaptive horizon selection procedure. Given a maximum window size $H_{\max}$ , we progressively extend the horizon under standard training and monitor the mean episode return $r(h)$ of policy $\pi_{\theta_{k+h-1}}$ . We stop when either $h=H_{\max}$ or performance ceases to improve, i.e., $r(h-1)>r(h)$ , and select the largest valid horizon for attribution and data selection.

The complete procedure is summarized in Algorithm 1, while ablations of our design choices are presented in Appendix D.3.

Experimental setup.

We evaluate our method on six standard RL benchmarks spanning continuous control and navigation tasks from the Gym/Gymnasium ecosystem, including MuJoCo domains (Todorov et al., 2012; Brockman et al., 2016; Towers et al., 2025). We use PPO with a KL-regularized objective as the underlying training algorithm and fix the maximum horizon to $H_{\max}=5$ without tuning. We compare RINSE against three baselines: standard PPO, Random filtering, and IIF (Hu et al., 2025), a local-attribution filtering method based on TracIn scores. For fairness, Random and IIF use the same discard ratio $\rho$ as RINSE.

We report both time efficiency (TE) and sample efficiency (SE) relative to standard PPO. For each method, we measure the runtime and the amount of training data required to reach a set of target performance levels, normalized such that the PPO baseline corresponds to $100\%$ . We also report full training curves. Additional setup and metric details are provided in Appendix C.1 and Appendix D.4.

Results.

Figure 3 shows that RINSE consistently outperforms both standard PPO and IIF. Although RINSE invokes TRAINROLLOUT twice per round and incurs additional influence-computation overhead, it reduces the number of required rollouts by an average of $42.8\%$ to achieve the same performance as standard training. Consequently, RINSE remains more efficient than the local-attribution baseline on nearly all environments.

RINSE achieves the best sample efficiency and highest peak return across all six tasks, with especially large improvements on challenging continuous-control environments such as Ant and BipedalWalker. These results suggest that RL-IF-opt captures the most useful component of long-term data influence for multi-round training improvement. We further report the wall-clock breakdown in Appendix D.4.1.

## 4.1 Extension to RLHF

We also consider an RLHF-style toxicity-mitigation task. Starting from GPT-Neo 2.7B (Black et al., 2021), each training round collects prompt-response trajectories from the current policy, assigns a toxicity-based reward to each response using a pretrained classifier (Vidgen et al., 2021; Gehman et al., 2020), and then updates the policy following standard RLHF pipelines (Ouyang et al., 2022). We use the KL-regularized PPO objective described in Section 2.1. For additional efficiency, we use the Generalized Gauss–Newton matrix (GGN) (Martens, 2020; Wang et al., 2025) to approximate the Hessian term in (7), completely reducing the HVP to efficient vector product operations. Further details are in Appendix C.2.

## 5 Related Work

Training-data attribution and data valuation.

Our work is closely related to the literature on training-data attribution and data valuation (Deng et al., 2025). Classical influence functions approximate leave-one-out effects through second-order sensitivity analysis (Koh and Liang, 2017), while later methods improve scalability by tracing gradient alignment along optimization trajectories, as in TracIn (Pruthi et al., 2020), by using representer-point decompositions (Yeh et al., 2018), or by designing scalable estimators for modern large-scale models (Park et al., 2023). Recent work further studies attribution in temporally dependent or sequential training settings (Wang et al., 2025). A closely related line studies data valuation, where training examples are assigned value according to their contribution to model quality or downstream utility (Ghorbani and Zou, 2019; Jia et al., 2019; Kwon and Zou, 2022). Other work uses per-example influence signals for data cleaning, robust reweighting, and data selection (Hara et al., 2019; Xia et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2024).

Most existing attribution methods assume a fixed training distribution. In contrast, our work studies attribution in online RL, where the training distribution evolves with the policy itself. We focus specifically on non-local attribution: instead of measuring only the immediate effect of a sample on a nearby update, we study how perturbations propagate through future optimization and future data collection over an extended training trajectory.

Data-centric perspectives in RL.

A growing line of work studies RL from a data-centric perspective, asking which trajectories, transitions, or training contexts are most useful for improving policy learning. In off-policy RL, this question appears naturally in experience replay and replay prioritization, where informative transitions are replayed more frequently to improve sample efficiency (Schaul et al., 2016). Related ideas also appear in curriculum learning and curriculum design, where training tasks or experiences are structured adaptively to improve learning dynamics (Florensa et al., 2017; Jiang et al., 2021). Recent work also explores trajectory filtering, selective data reuse, and replay-buffer management for improving robustness and efficiency in RL training.

Our work is most closely related to recent attribution methods for online RL. In on-policy training, trajectories are generated by the current policy and immediately reused for optimization (Williams, 1992; Sutton et al., 1999; Schulman et al., 2015; Schulman et al., 2017). Consequently, perturbing a single training occurrence can influence not only later parameters, but also the future rollout distribution induced by the updated policy. Hu et al. (2025) take an important first step in this direction by proposing a local attribution framework for PPO that measures the influence of rollout records relative to a nearby checkpoint within a fixed rollout buffer. Our work builds directly on this line, but studies a fundamentally different target: instead of estimating the immediate local effect of a sample, we study its non-local influence on future learning outcomes over subsequent training rounds.

## 6 Conclusion and Limitations

We study data attribution in online reinforcement learning, where the training data are generated by the evolving policy rather than drawn from a fixed distribution. To capture this setting, we formulate a trajectory-level leave-one-out quantity, RL-LOO, and derive a first-order estimator, RL-IF, that propagates the effect of one training occurrence through both later optimization and future sampling. This yields a counterfactual perspective on data value in online RL that goes beyond local attribution. Empirically, the resulting optimization-only approximation, RL-IF-opt, is already effective enough to support a practical filtering method, RINSE, which improves efficiency and final performance on standard benchmarks and in an RLHF-style toxicity-mitigation setting.

Our work also has several limitations. First, the current development is specialized to PPO-style on-policy training (Schulman et al., 2017) and to a first-order approximation of the leave-one-out trajectory. Extending the framework to other online RL algorithms, including closely related trust-region methods such as TRPO (Schulman et al., 2015) and other settings with substantially different sampling or update structures, remains an important direction. Second, our derivation is based on SGD-style (Amari, 1993) updates, whereas modern deep RL and RLHF pipelines often rely on adaptive optimizers such as Adam (Kingma and Ba, 2015). Although the method works empirically in our Adam-based experiments, a derivation that explicitly accounts for adaptive preconditioning would make the estimator more principled (Deng et al., 2026). Finally, our analysis and experiments suggest that the sampling term is often smaller than the optimization term in the regimes we study, but this need not hold universally; understanding when sampling effects become dominant is an open question.

## Appendix A Additional Details on RL-IF

## A.1 Derivation of RL Leave-One-Out and RL-IF

In this section, we derive the recursion underlying RL-IF. To estimate the RL-LOO effect of a sample $z^{*}\in D_{k}^{(l)}$ , we inject a perturbation at round $k$ , step $l$ , while keeping all subsequent updates unchanged:

 $\theta_{k}^{(l)}(\varepsilon)=\theta_{k}^{(l-1)}-\frac{\eta_{k}}{b}\sum_{x\in D_{k}^{(l)}\setminus\{z^{*}\}}\nabla_{\theta}\ell(\theta_{k}^{(l-1)},x)-\frac{\eta_{k}}{b}(1-\varepsilon)\nabla_{\theta}\ell(\theta_{k}^{(l-1)},z^{*}).$ 

(10)

The injected perturbation at $(k,l)$ is obtained by differentiating (10):

 $\Delta_{k}^{(l)}:=\left.\frac{\partial\theta_{k}^{(l)}(\varepsilon)}{\partial\varepsilon}\right|_{\varepsilon=0}=\frac{\eta_{k}}{b}\nabla_{\theta}\ell(\theta_{k}^{(l-1)},z^{*}).$ 

To characterize the terminal perturbation $\Delta_{T}^{(M)}$ , we introduce a stage-update operator that makes explicit the dependence on the current iterate and the rollout distribution. For each update $(t,j)$ , define

 $F_{t}^{(j)}(\vartheta,\beta):=\vartheta-\frac{\eta_{t}}{b}\sum_{x\sim S_{t}^{(j)}(\beta)}\nabla_{\theta}\ell(\vartheta,x),\qquad S_{t}^{(j)}(\beta):=\begin{cases}D_{t}^{(j)},&\text{if }t=k\text{ and }j>l,\\
P_{t}(\cdot\mid\beta),&\text{if }t\geq k+1.\end{cases}$ 

Here $D_{t}^{(j)}$ denotes a fixed minibatch, while $P_{t}(\cdot\mid\beta)$ denotes the rollout distribution induced by $\beta$ . The perturbed trajectory follows $\theta_{t}^{(j)}(\varepsilon)=F_{t}^{(j)}\big(\theta_{t}^{(j-1)}(\varepsilon),\theta_{t}^{(0)}(\varepsilon)\big).$ Recall the definition of perturbation $\Delta_{t}^{(j)}$ in (5), and by the chain rule, we have

 $\Delta_{t}^{(j)}=\frac{\partial F_{t}^{(j)}}{\partial\vartheta}\Delta_{t}^{(j-1)}+\frac{\partial F_{t}^{(j)}}{\partial\beta}\Delta_{t}^{(0)}.$ 

(11)

Optimization effect.

Differentiating $F_{t}^{(j)}$ with respect to the current iterate $\vartheta$ gives $\frac{\partial F_{t}^{(j)}}{\partial\vartheta}=A_{t}^{(j)}=I-\frac{\eta_{t}}{b}H_{t}^{(j)},$ where $H_{t}^{(j)}:=\sum_{x\in D_{t}^{(j)}}\nabla_{\theta}^{2}\ell(\theta_{t}^{(j-1)},x)$ .

Sampling effect.

For $t=k$ and $j>l$ , the minibatch is fixed, so $S_{t}^{(j)}(\beta)=D_{t}^{(j)}$ and $\frac{\partial F_{t}^{(j)}}{\partial\beta}=0.$ Thus, by (11) we have $\Delta_{k}^{(j)}=A_{k}^{(j)}\Delta_{k}^{(j-1)}$ . For future stages $t\geq k+1$ , the sampling distribution depends on $\beta$ . Using the score-function identity,

 $\nabla_{\beta}\mathbb{E}_{x\sim P_{t}(\cdot\mid\beta)}[g(x)]=\mathbb{E}_{x\sim P_{t}(\cdot\mid\beta)}\left[g(x)\nabla_{\beta}\log P_{t}(x\mid\beta)^{\top}\right],$ 

with $g(x)=\nabla_{\theta}\ell(\vartheta,x)$ , we obtain

 $\frac{\partial F_{t}^{(j)}}{\partial\beta}=-\eta_{t}\mathbb{E}_{x\sim P_{t}(\cdot\mid\beta)}\left[\nabla_{\theta}\ell(\vartheta,x)\nabla_{\beta}\log P_{t}(x\mid\beta)^{\top}\right].$ 

Approximating the expectation with the sampled minibatch yields $\frac{\partial F_{t}^{(j)}}{\partial\beta}\approx B_{t}^{(j)}$ , where

 $B_{t}^{(j)}:=-\frac{\eta_{t}}{b}\sum_{x\in D_{t}^{(j)}}\nabla_{\theta}\ell(\theta_{t}^{(j-1)},x)\nabla_{\theta}\log P_{t}(x\mid\theta_{t}^{(0)})^{\top}.$ 

Substituting into (11), we obtain the unified recursion $\Delta_{t}^{(j)}=A_{t}^{(j)}\Delta_{t}^{(j-1)}+B_{t}^{(j)}\Delta_{t}^{(0)},$ where the second term is active only for $t\geq k+1$ .

## A.2 Efficient Computation through Adjoint Formulation

Even with the recursive formulation of RL-IF, a key challenge is efficient computation. Directly materializing $\Delta_{t}^{(j)}$ for each candidate sample is computationally prohibitive. Instead, we evaluate RL-IF by propagating an adjoint vector backward through the training trajectory. This converts the forward perturbation recursion into a single backward pass involving Hessian–vector products and score-function vector products. The resulting procedure is summarized in Algorithm 2.

The algorithm returns $\nabla_{\theta}f(\theta_{T}^{(M)})^{\top}\Delta_{T}^{(M)}$ , which approximates the RL leave-one-out effect $\mathrm{RL}\text{-}\mathrm{LOO}^{(k,l)}(z^{*};f)$ . Notably, all influence scores across the trajectory can be computed within a single backward pass.

 1:

 Training trajectory $\{\theta_{t}^{(j)},D_{t}^{(j)}\}$ , attribution target $f$ , target sample $z^{*}\in D_{k}^{(l)}$ 

 2:

 $U\leftarrow\nabla_{\theta}f(\theta_{T}^{(M)})$ 

 3:

 for $t=T$ down to $1$ do

 4:

    $U_{\mathrm{prev}}\leftarrow 0$ 

 5:

   for $j=M$ down to $1$ do

 6:

    if $t=k$ and $j=l$ then

 7:

      return $\left\langle U,\;\frac{\eta_{k}}{b}\nabla_{\theta}\ell(\theta_{k}^{(l-1)},z^{*})\right\rangle$ 

 8:

    end if

 9:

    // sampling contribution

 10:

     $U_{\mathrm{prev}}\leftarrow U_{\mathrm{prev}}+B_{t}^{(j)\top}U$ 

 11:

    // optimization step

 12:

     $U\leftarrow\left(I-\frac{\eta_{t}}{b}H_{t}^{(j)}\right)U$ 

 13:

   end for

 14:

    $U\leftarrow U+U_{\mathrm{prev}}$ 

 15:

 end for

We prove that Algorithm 2 returns the first-order RL-IF estimator. Recall the forward perturbation recursion in (7). For fixed-minibatch updates, the sampling term is inactive, which is equivalently captured by setting $B_{t}^{(j)}=0$ . The target scalar is $\nabla_{\theta}f(\theta_{T}^{(M)})^{\top}\Delta_{T}^{(M)}.$ We initialize

 $U_{T}^{(M)}=\nabla_{\theta}f(\theta_{T}^{(M)}).$ 

Suppose that, before processing update $(t,j)$ in the backward pass, the current adjoint vector is $U_{t}^{(j)}$ . Then, using (7), we have

 $\displaystyle\left(U_{t}^{(j)}\right)^{\top}\Delta_{t}^{(j)}$ 
 $\displaystyle=\left(U_{t}^{(j)}\right)^{\top}\left(A_{t}^{(j)}\Delta_{t}^{(j-1)}+B_{t}^{(j)}\Delta_{t}^{(0)}\right)$ 

 $\displaystyle=\left(A_{t}^{(j)\top}U_{t}^{(j)}\right)^{\top}\Delta_{t}^{(j-1)}+\left(B_{t}^{(j)\top}U_{t}^{(j)}\right)^{\top}\Delta_{t}^{(0)}.$ 

(12)

Therefore, the adjoint contribution to the previous minibatch iterate is $U_{t}^{(j-1)}=A_{t}^{(j)\top}U_{t}^{(j)}$ , which gives the optimization update:

 $U\leftarrow A_{t}^{(j)\top}U=\left(I-\frac{\eta_{t}}{b}H_{t}^{(j)}\right)U,$ 

where we use the symmetry of $H_{t}^{(j)}$ . The second term in (12) contributes to the previous-stage perturbation $\Delta_{t}^{(0)}$ rather than to $\Delta_{t}^{(j-1)}$ . Hence, during the backward sweep over stage $t$ , all such cross-stage contributions are accumulated as

 $U_{\mathrm{prev}}\leftarrow U_{\mathrm{prev}}+B_{t}^{(j)\top}U.$ 

After all updates in stage $t$ have been processed, the adjoint propagated through the optimization dynamics and the accumulated sampling contribution both act on the same previous-stage perturbation $\Delta_{t}^{(0)}$ . Thus they are combined by

 $U\leftarrow U+U_{\mathrm{prev}}.$ 

By repeated application of (12), the algorithm maintains the invariant that the current adjoint $U$ satisfies

 $\nabla_{\theta}f(\theta_{T}^{(M)})^{\top}\Delta_{T}^{(M)}=U^{\top}\Delta_{t}^{(j)}+U_{\mathrm{prev}}^{\top}\Delta_{t}^{(0)}$ 

whenever the backward pass is positioned at iterate $\theta_{t}^{(j)}$ . In particular, when the backward pass reaches the injection point $(k,l)$ , $\Delta_{k}^{(0)}=0$ , so we have $\nabla_{\theta}f(\theta_{T}^{(M)})^{\top}\Delta_{T}^{(M)}=U^{\top}\Delta_{k}^{(l)}$ . The injected perturbation is $\Delta_{k}^{(l)}=\frac{\eta_{k}}{b}\nabla_{\theta}\ell(\theta_{k}^{(l-1)},z^{*}).$ Substituting this expression gives

 $\nabla_{\theta}f(\theta_{T}^{(M)})^{\top}\Delta_{T}^{(M)}=\left\langle U,\;\frac{\eta_{k}}{b}\nabla_{\theta}\ell(\theta_{k}^{(l-1)},z^{*})\right\rangle.$ 

This is exactly the value returned by Algorithm 2. Therefore, Algorithm 2 correctly computes the first-order RL-IF estimator for the RL-LOO effect.

## A.3 Closed-Form RL-IF: Proof of Equation (8)

In this section, we show that the recursion in (7) directly leads to a compact closed form. For each stage $t>k$ , define

 $A_{t}:=\prod_{j=M}^{1}A_{t}^{(j)},\qquad C_{t}:=A_{t}+\sum_{q=1}^{M}\left(\prod_{j=M}^{q+1}A_{t}^{(j)}\right)B_{t}^{(q)}.$ 

(13)

Here, products are ordered from right to left along the forward trajectory; in particular,

 $\prod_{j=M}^{1}A_{t}^{(j)}=A_{t}^{(M)}A_{t}^{(M-1)}\cdots A_{t}^{(1)}.$ 

Then

 $\mathrm{RL}\text{-}\mathrm{IF}^{(k,l)}(z^{*};f)=\nabla_{\theta}f(\theta_{T}^{(M)})^{\top}\left(\prod_{t=T}^{k+1}C_{t}\right)\left(\prod_{j=M}^{l+1}A_{k}^{(j)}\right)\frac{\eta_{k}}{b}\nabla_{\theta}\ell(\theta_{k}^{(l-1)},z^{*}).$ 

(14)

Proof.

We prove the closed form in (14) by induction.

First, consider a future stage $t>k$ . Recall $\Delta_{t}^{(j)}=A_{t}^{(j)}\Delta_{t}^{(j-1)}+B_{t}^{(j)}\Delta_{t}^{(0)}.$ We claim that, for every $r=1,\dots,M$ ,

 $\Delta_{t}^{(r)}=\left(\prod_{j=r}^{1}A_{t}^{(j)}\right)\Delta_{t}^{(0)}+\sum_{q=1}^{r}\left(\prod_{j=r}^{q+1}A_{t}^{(j)}\right)B_{t}^{(q)}\Delta_{t}^{(0)},$ 

(15)

where an empty product is the identity. For $r=1$ , (15) gives

 $\Delta_{t}^{(1)}=A_{t}^{(1)}\Delta_{t}^{(0)}+B_{t}^{(1)}\Delta_{t}^{(0)},$ 

which is exactly (7). Assume (15) holds for some $r-1$ . Then

 $\displaystyle\Delta_{t}^{(r)}$ 
 $\displaystyle=A_{t}^{(r)}\Delta_{t}^{(r-1)}+B_{t}^{(r)}\Delta_{t}^{(0)}$ 

 $\displaystyle=A_{t}^{(r)}\left[\left(\prod_{j=r-1}^{1}A_{t}^{(j)}\right)\Delta_{t}^{(0)}+\sum_{q=1}^{r-1}\left(\prod_{j=r-1}^{q+1}A_{t}^{(j)}\right)B_{t}^{(q)}\Delta_{t}^{(0)}\right]+B_{t}^{(r)}\Delta_{t}^{(0)}$ 

 $\displaystyle=\left(\prod_{j=r}^{1}A_{t}^{(j)}\right)\Delta_{t}^{(0)}+\sum_{q=1}^{r-1}\left(\prod_{j=r}^{q+1}A_{t}^{(j)}\right)B_{t}^{(q)}\Delta_{t}^{(0)}+B_{t}^{(r)}\Delta_{t}^{(0)}$ 

 $\displaystyle=\left(\prod_{j=r}^{1}A_{t}^{(j)}\right)\Delta_{t}^{(0)}+\sum_{q=1}^{r}\left(\prod_{j=r}^{q+1}A_{t}^{(j)}\right)B_{t}^{(q)}\Delta_{t}^{(0)}.$ 

Thus the claim holds for $r$ , and therefore for all $r=1,\dots,M$ . Setting $r=M$ gives

 $\Delta_{t}^{(M)}=\left(\prod_{j=M}^{1}A_{t}^{(j)}\right)\Delta_{t}^{(0)}+\sum_{q=1}^{M}\left(\prod_{j=M}^{q+1}A_{t}^{(j)}\right)B_{t}^{(q)}\Delta_{t}^{(0)}.$ 

Factoring out $\Delta_{t}^{(0)}$ , we obtain

 $\Delta_{t}^{(M)}=\left[\prod_{j=M}^{1}A_{t}^{(j)}+\sum_{q=1}^{M}\left(\prod_{j=M}^{q+1}A_{t}^{(j)}\right)B_{t}^{(q)}\right]\Delta_{t}^{(0)}=C_{t}\Delta_{t}^{(0)}.$ 

(16)

Next, for the injection stage $k$ , the perturbation after $(k,l)$ is $\Delta_{k}^{(l)}=\frac{\eta_{k}}{b}\nabla_{\theta}\ell(\theta_{k}^{(l-1)},z^{*})$ . For $j=l+1,\dots,M$ , the minibatches are fixed, so $\Delta_{k}^{(j)}=A_{k}^{(j)}\Delta_{k}^{(j-1)}$ . By another direct induction over $j$ , this gives

 $\Delta_{k}^{(M)}=\left(\prod_{j=M}^{l+1}A_{k}^{(j)}\right)\Delta_{k}^{(l)}.$ 

(17)

Finally, applying (16) inductively over stages $t=k+1,\dots,T$ yields

 $\Delta_{T}^{(M)}=\left(\prod_{t=T}^{k+1}C_{t}\right)\Delta_{k}^{(M)}.$ 

Substituting (17) and the expression for $\Delta_{k}^{(l)}$ gives

 $\Delta_{T}^{(M)}=\left(\prod_{t=T}^{k+1}C_{t}\right)\left(\prod_{j=M}^{l+1}A_{k}^{(j)}\right)\frac{\eta_{k}}{b}\nabla_{\theta}\ell(\theta_{k}^{(l-1)},z^{*}).$ 

Multiplying both sides by $\nabla_{\theta}f(\theta_{T}^{(M)})^{\top}$ proves

 $\mathrm{RL}\text{-}\mathrm{IF}^{(k,l)}(z^{*};f)=\nabla_{\theta}f(\theta_{T}^{(M)})^{\top}\left(\prod_{t=T}^{k+1}C_{t}\right)\left(\prod_{j=M}^{l+1}A_{k}^{(j)}\right)\frac{\eta_{k}}{b}\nabla_{\theta}\ell(\theta_{k}^{(l-1)},z^{*}).$ 

This completes the proof.

## A.4 Construction and Justification of the Reference Target

For an attribution window starting at round $k$ , we use its terminal checkpoint $\bar{\theta}:=\theta_{k+H}$ as the reference policy. We collect an on-policy rollout $D_{k+H}$ from $\pi_{\bar{\theta}}$ and compute the reference advantages using the critic associated with this checkpoint and the same generalized advantage estimation (GAE) procedure and hyperparameters as PPO. In particular, writing $V^{\mathrm{ref}}$ for the reference critic, the estimates take the form

 $\displaystyle\delta_{t}^{\mathrm{ref}}$ 
 $\displaystyle=r_{t}+\gamma V^{\mathrm{ref}}(s_{t+1})-V^{\mathrm{ref}}(s_{t}),$ 

(18)

 $\displaystyle\hat{A}_{t}^{\mathrm{ref}}$ 
 $\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{L_{t}-1}(\gamma\lambda)^{j}\delta_{t+j}^{\mathrm{ref}},$ 

(19)

where $L_{t}$ is the number of available steps used in the estimate, with the same termination masking and truncation bootstrapping as in PPO. The reference rollout, critic, and advantage estimates are held fixed during attribution. This construction does not involve tuning over candidate reference advantages.

The terminal checkpoint is used because RL-LOO evaluates the effect of removing a training occurrence on the policy obtained at the end of the window. The following result explains the connection between the reference target and the expected return of that policy.

Ideal reference target.

For the formal statement, let $0\leq\gamma<1$ and define the normalized discounted return and discounted state occupancy by

 $\displaystyle J(\theta)$ 
 $\displaystyle:=(1-\gamma)\mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim\pi_{\theta}}\left[\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\gamma^{t}r_{t}\right],$ 

(20)

 $\displaystyle d^{\pi_{\theta}}(s)$ 
 $\displaystyle:=(1-\gamma)\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\gamma^{t}\Pr_{\pi_{\theta}}(s_{t}=s).$ 

(21)

The initial-state distribution is fixed. Let $A^{\pi_{\bar{\theta}}}$ denote the exact advantage for the usual discounted return. Following the negative-target convention in the main text, define

 $f_{\mathrm{ref}}(\phi):=-\mathbb{E}_{\begin{subarray}{c}s\sim d^{\pi_{\bar{\theta}}}\\
a\sim\pi_{\bar{\theta}}(\cdot\mid s)\end{subarray}}\left[\log\pi_{\phi}(a\mid s)A^{\pi_{\bar{\theta}}}(s,a)\right].$ 

(22)

The reference distribution and advantages in this expression are fixed as $\phi$ varies. This normalization makes the return-gradient identity explicit; the corresponding statement for unnormalized discounted return differs by the constant factor $1-\gamma$ .

Proposition A.1: Reference target and terminal return

Assume the policy-gradient identity holds and that $f_{\mathrm{ref}}$ and $J$ are twice continuously differentiable in a neighborhood of $\bar{\theta}$ . Let $\bar{\theta}_{-z}$ be the terminal parameter after deleting occurrence $z$ , and set $\Delta_{z}:=\bar{\theta}_{-z}-\bar{\theta}$ . For sufficiently small $\Delta_{z}$ ,

 $\mathrm{RL\text{-}LOO}(z;f_{\mathrm{ref}})=J(\bar{\theta})-J(\bar{\theta}_{-z})+O(\|\Delta_{z}\|_{2}^{2}).$ 

(23)

Proof.

Since the reference distribution and advantages are fixed, the policy-gradient theorem gives

 $\displaystyle\left.\nabla_{\phi}f_{\mathrm{ref}}(\phi)\right|_{\phi=\bar{\theta}}$ 
 $\displaystyle=-\mathbb{E}_{\begin{subarray}{c}s\sim d^{\pi_{\bar{\theta}}}\\
a\sim\pi_{\bar{\theta}}(\cdot\mid s)\end{subarray}}\left[\nabla_{\theta}\log\pi_{\bar{\theta}}(a\mid s)A^{\pi_{\bar{\theta}}}(s,a)\right]$ 

(24)

 $\displaystyle=-\nabla J(\bar{\theta}).$ 

(25)

Taylor expansion at $\bar{\theta}$ therefore yields

 $\displaystyle f_{\mathrm{ref}}(\bar{\theta}_{-z})-f_{\mathrm{ref}}(\bar{\theta})$ 
 $\displaystyle=-\nabla J(\bar{\theta})^{\top}\Delta_{z}+O(\|\Delta_{z}\|_{2}^{2}),$ 

(26)

 $\displaystyle J(\bar{\theta}_{-z})-J(\bar{\theta})$ 
 $\displaystyle=\nabla J(\bar{\theta})^{\top}\Delta_{z}+O(\|\Delta_{z}\|_{2}^{2}).$ 

(27)

Combining the two expansions proves (23).

Thus, with this negative target, a negative RL-LOO value locally corresponds to an increase in terminal return after deletion. RL-IF estimates this counterfactual through the first-order parameter perturbation. In practice, we replace the population target and exact advantages with the empirical reference rollout and GAE estimates, introducing sampling and advantage-estimation error in addition to the first-order approximation. Sensitivity to the reference construction is evaluated in Appendix A.4.1.

## B.1 Uniform Approximation Error of First-Order RL-IF

Theorem B.1: Uniform approximation error of first-order RL-IF

Assume that $\ell(\theta;x)$ is twice differentiable, that its gradient norm is bounded by $G$ , that its Hessian norm is bounded by $\Lambda$ , and that its Hessian is $L_{H}$ -Lipschitz continuous in $\theta$ . Assume also that the rollout distribution $P_{t}(x\mid\theta)$ has score norm bounded by $S$ and score Hessian norm bounded by $L_{P}$ . Furthermore, assume that the learning rate satisfies $M\overline{\Lambda}\eta_{t}\leq\frac{1}{T-k}$ for every $k\leq t\leq T$ . Then for the first-order RL-IF estimator $\Delta_{T}^{(M)}$ , the approximation satisfies

 $\bigl\|\widetilde{\theta}_{T}^{(M)}-\theta_{T}^{(M)}-\Delta_{T}^{(M)}\bigr\|_{2}\leq\frac{C_{\text{smooth}}}{M^{2}b^{2}},$ 

where $C_{\text{smooth}}=\frac{cG^{2}(L_{H}+\overline{\Lambda}S+GL_{P})}{\overline{\Lambda}^{3}}$ and $\overline{\Lambda}=\Lambda+GS$ are smoothness constants.

Proof.

We analyze the population update map and we do not consider minibatch noise in this theorem. We define the true counterfactual perturbation as

 $\delta_{t}^{(j)}:=\widetilde{\theta}_{t}^{(j)}-\theta_{t}^{(j)}.$ 

The first-order RL-IF perturbation $\Delta_{t}^{(j)}$ is defined by (5) and satisfies the recursion in Proposition 3.2. At the injection point $(k,l)$ , the two coincide exactly:

 $\delta_{k}^{(l)}=\Delta_{k}^{(l)}=\frac{\eta_{k}}{b}\nabla_{\theta}\ell(\theta_{k}^{(l-1)},z^{*}).$ 

Our goal is to bound the discrepancy

 $e_{t}^{(j)}:=\delta_{t}^{(j)}-\Delta_{t}^{(j)}$ 

at the terminal point $(T,M)$ . Recall the stage update map $F_{t}^{(j)}(\vartheta,\beta)$ defined in (6). By construction, the post-intervention trajectory is generated by

 $\theta_{t}^{(j)}(\varepsilon)=F_{t}^{(j)}\!\left(\theta_{t}^{(j-1)}(\varepsilon),\theta_{t}^{(0)}(\varepsilon)\right).$ 

Since $\ell(\theta;x)$ has an $L_{H}$ -Lipschitz Hessian, and the rollout distribution $P_{t}(x\mid\theta)$ has score norm bounded by $S$ and score Hessian norm bounded by $L_{P}$ , the map $F_{t}^{(j)}(\vartheta,\beta)$ is jointly smooth in $(\vartheta,\beta)$ . We next prove a bound for the remainder:

 $\displaystyle\Bigl\|$ 
 $\displaystyle F_{t}^{(j)}(\vartheta+u,\beta+v)-F_{t}^{(j)}(\vartheta,\beta)-\partial_{\vartheta}F_{t}^{(j)}(\vartheta,\beta)u-\partial_{\beta}F_{t}^{(j)}(\vartheta,\beta)v\Bigr\|_{2}\leq\frac{\eta_{t}K}{2}(\|u\|_{2}+\|v\|_{2})^{2},$ 

(28)

where $K:=L_{H}+\overline{\Lambda}S+GL_{P},\overline{\Lambda}:=\Lambda+GS.$ We denote $m_{t}^{(j)}(\vartheta,\beta)=\mathbb{E}_{x\sim P_{t}(\cdot\mid\beta)}[\nabla_{\theta}\ell(\vartheta,x)]$ , for a future round $t>k$ , write $m=m_{t}^{(j)}$ and define

 $R_{m}(u,v):=m(\vartheta+u,\beta+v)-m(\vartheta,\beta)-\partial_{\vartheta}m(\vartheta,\beta)u-\partial_{\beta}m(\vartheta,\beta)v.$ 

We decompose this remainder as

 $\displaystyle R_{m}(u,v)$ 
 $\displaystyle=\underbrace{m(\vartheta+u,\beta+v)-m(\vartheta,\beta+v)-\partial_{\vartheta}m(\vartheta,\beta+v)u}_{(\mathrm{I})}$ 

 $\displaystyle+\underbrace{\bigl(\partial_{\vartheta}m(\vartheta,\beta+v)-\partial_{\vartheta}m(\vartheta,\beta)\bigr)u}_{(\mathrm{II})}$ 

 $\displaystyle+\underbrace{m(\vartheta,\beta+v)-m(\vartheta,\beta)-\partial_{\beta}m(\vartheta,\beta)v}_{(\mathrm{III})}.$ 

Bounding (I).

For fixed $\beta+v$ ,

 $\partial_{\vartheta}m(\vartheta,\beta+v)=\mathbb{E}_{x\sim P_{t}(\cdot\mid\beta+v)}\bigl[\nabla_{\theta}^{2}\ell(\vartheta,x)\bigr].$ 

Since the loss Hessian is $L_{H}$ -Lipschitz, we have

 $\|(\mathrm{I})\|_{2}\leq\frac{L_{H}}{2}\|u\|_{2}^{2}.$ 

Bounding (II).

Using the score-function identity, the Hessian bound $\Lambda$ , and the score bound $S$ , we obtain

 $\bigl\|\partial_{\vartheta}m(\vartheta,\beta+v)-\partial_{\vartheta}m(\vartheta,\beta)\bigr\|_{2}\leq\Lambda S\|v\|_{2}.$ 

Consequently,

 $\|(\mathrm{II})\|_{2}\leq\Lambda S\|u\|_{2}\|v\|_{2}.$ 

Bounding (III).

The sampling derivative is

 $\partial_{\beta}m(\vartheta,\beta)=\mathbb{E}_{x\sim P_{t}(\cdot\mid\beta)}\left[\nabla_{\theta}\ell(\vartheta,x)\nabla_{\beta}\log P_{t}(x\mid\beta)^{\top}\right].$ 

Differentiating once more in $\beta$ produces a score-Hessian term and a score outer-product term. The gradient bound $G$ , score bound $S$ , and score-Hessian bound $L_{P}$ therefore imply

 $\|(\mathrm{III})\|_{2}\leq\frac{G(L_{P}+S^{2})}{2}\|v\|_{2}^{2}.$ 

Combining the three bounds yields

 $\displaystyle\|R_{m}(u,v)\|_{2}$ 
 $\displaystyle\leq\frac{L_{H}}{2}\|u\|_{2}^{2}+\Lambda S\|u\|_{2}\|v\|_{2}+\frac{G(L_{P}+S^{2})}{2}\|v\|_{2}^{2}$ 

 $\displaystyle\leq\frac{K}{2}(\|u\|_{2}+\|v\|_{2})^{2},$ 

where $K=L_{H}+\Lambda S+G(L_{P}+S^{2}).$ Since $F_{t}^{(j)}(\vartheta,\beta)=\vartheta-\eta_{t}m_{t}^{(j)}(\vartheta,\beta)$ , this proves (28) for $t>k$ . For the remaining updates of round $k$ , the map is independent of $\beta$ , and the fixed-minibatch average has an $L_{H}$ -Lipschitz Jacobian. Its remainder is bounded by $\eta_{k}L_{H}\|u\|_{2}^{2}/2$ , so (28) holds there as well. Applying (28) with $u=\delta_{t}^{(j-1)}$ and $v=\delta_{t}^{(0)}$ gives

 $\delta_{t}^{(j)}=A_{t}^{(j)}\delta_{t}^{(j-1)}+B_{t}^{(j)}\delta_{t}^{(0)}+r_{t}^{(j)},$ 

where

 $\|r_{t}^{(j)}\|_{2}\leq\frac{\eta_{t}K}{2}\left(\|\delta_{t}^{(j-1)}\|_{2}+\|\delta_{t}^{(0)}\|_{2}\right)^{2}.$ 

(29)

Subtracting the first-order recursion yields

 $e_{t}^{(j)}=A_{t}^{(j)}e_{t}^{(j-1)}+B_{t}^{(j)}e_{t}^{(0)}+r_{t}^{(j)}.$ 

(30)

This also applies to the remaining updates of round $k$ , with $B_{k}^{(j)}=0$ . We next control the perturbation size. The uniform Jacobian bounds are

 $\|\partial_{\vartheta}F_{t}^{(j)}(\vartheta,\beta)\|_{2}\leq 1+\eta_{t}\Lambda,\qquad\|\partial_{\beta}F_{t}^{(j)}(\vartheta,\beta)\|_{2}\leq\eta_{t}GS.$ 

Integrating these bounds along the line segment between the two inputs gives

 $\displaystyle\|F_{t}^{(j)}(\vartheta+u,\beta+v)-F_{t}^{(j)}(\vartheta,\beta)\|_{2}\leq(1+\eta_{t}\Lambda)\|u\|_{2}+\eta_{t}GS\|v\|_{2}.$ 

For round $k$ , the second term can be omitted. Thus both the true perturbation and its first-order approximation grow by at most a factor $q_{t}:=1+\eta_{t}\overline{\Lambda}$ per update, when measured relative to the maximum perturbation encountered so far. The learning-rate assumption implies

 $\sum_{t=k}^{T}M\eta_{t}\overline{\Lambda}\leq\frac{T-k+1}{T-k}\leq 2.$ 

Starting from the injection bound $\|\delta_{k}^{(l)}\|_{2}=\|\Delta_{k}^{(l)}\|_{2}\leq\eta_{k}G/b$ , we therefore obtain

 $\displaystyle D$ 
 $\displaystyle:=\max_{(t,j)\text{ at or after }(k,l)}\left\{\|\delta_{t}^{(j)}\|_{2},\|\Delta_{t}^{(j)}\|_{2}\right\}\leq\frac{\eta_{k}G}{b}\prod_{t=k}^{T}q_{t}^{M}\leq\frac{e^{2}\eta_{k}G}{b}\leq\frac{e^{2}G}{M\overline{\Lambda}b}.$ 

(31)

Here we used $T-k\geq 1$ in the last inequality. The sampling perturbation at the start of round $k$ is zero. Consequently, (29) gives, for every update after the injection,

 $\|r_{t}^{(j)}\|_{2}\leq R_{t},\qquad R_{t}:=2\eta_{t}KD^{2}.$ 

Define

 $E_{k}:=\max_{l\leq j\leq M}\|e_{k}^{(j)}\|_{2},\qquad E_{t}:=\max_{0\leq j\leq M}\|e_{t}^{(j)}\|_{2}\quad(t>k),$ 

and let $a_{t}:=M\eta_{t}\overline{\Lambda}$ . Taking norms in (30) and applying the triangle inequality yields

 $\|e_{t}^{(j)}\|_{2}\leq q_{t}\max\left\{\|e_{t}^{(j-1)}\|_{2},\|e_{t}^{(0)}\|_{2}\right\}+R_{t}.$ 

For $t>k$ , using $e_{t}^{(0)}=e_{t-1}^{(M)}$ and iterating within the round gives

 $\displaystyle E_{t}$ 
 $\displaystyle\leq q_{t}^{M}E_{t-1}+R_{t}\sum_{i=0}^{M-1}q_{t}^{i}$ 

 $\displaystyle\leq e^{a_{t}}\bigl(E_{t-1}+MR_{t}\bigr).$ 

In round $k$ , we instead start from $e_{k}^{(l)}=0$ . The remaining $M-l$ updates satisfy

 $E_{k}\leq R_{k}\sum_{i=0}^{M-l-1}q_{k}^{i}\leq Me^{a_{k}}R_{k},$ 

where the sum is empty when $l=M$ . In particular, the error accumulated during round $k$ is included.

Unrolling the round-wise bounds therefore gives

 $\displaystyle E_{T}$ 
 $\displaystyle\leq M\sum_{t=k}^{T}R_{t}\exp\left(\sum_{s=t}^{T}a_{s}\right)$ 

 $\displaystyle\leq 2e^{2}MKD^{2}\sum_{t=k}^{T}\eta_{t}.$ 

Finally,

 $\sum_{t=k}^{T}\eta_{t}\leq\frac{T-k+1}{(T-k)M\overline{\Lambda}}\leq\frac{2}{M\overline{\Lambda}}.$ 

Combining this with (31), we conclude

 $\displaystyle\bigl\|\widetilde{\theta}_{T}^{(M)}-\theta_{T}^{(M)}-\Delta_{T}^{(M)}\bigr\|_{2}$ 
 $\displaystyle=\|e_{T}^{(M)}\|_{2}$ 

 $\displaystyle\leq E_{T}$ 

 $\displaystyle\leq\frac{4e^{2}KD^{2}}{\overline{\Lambda}}$ 

 $\displaystyle\leq\frac{4e^{6}G^{2}\bigl(L_{H}+\overline{\Lambda}S+GL_{P}\bigr)}{M^{2}b^{2}\overline{\Lambda}^{\,3}}.$ 

This proves the result with the universal constant $c=4e^{6}$ .
∎

## B.2 Relative Bound between Stage-Wise Operators

Theorem B.2: Nonconvex relative gap between sampling-aware and optimization-only operators

Under the smoothness and bounded-gradient assumptions in Theorem B.1, assume in addition that, for each future stage $t>k$ , the sampling family $P_{t}(\cdot\mid\theta)$ is KL-smooth with score second moment bounded by $L_{t}$ , and that $\eta_{t}\Lambda<1$ .
Then, for every future stage $t>k$ ,

 $\|C_{t}-A_{t}\|_{2}\leq\rho_{t}\|A_{t}\|_{2},\qquad\rho_{t}:=\frac{G\sqrt{L_{t}}\,\frac{(1+\eta_{t}\Lambda)^{M}-1}{\Lambda}}{(1-\eta_{t}\Lambda)^{M}}.$ 

(32)

Proof.

Fix a future stage $t>k$ . Recall that

 $C_{t}=A_{t}+\sum_{q=1}^{M}\left(\prod_{j=M}^{q+1}A_{t}^{(j)}\right)B_{t}^{(q)},\qquad A_{t}=\prod_{j=M}^{1}A_{t}^{(j)}.$ 

Therefore,

 $C_{t}-A_{t}=\sum_{q=1}^{M}\left(\prod_{j=M}^{q+1}A_{t}^{(j)}\right)B_{t}^{(q)}.$ 

Taking operator norms and using submultiplicativity,

 $\|C_{t}-A_{t}\|_{2}\leq\sum_{q=1}^{M}\left\|\prod_{j=M}^{q+1}A_{t}^{(j)}\right\|_{2}\|B_{t}^{(q)}\|_{2}.$ 

By smoothness,

 $\|A_{t}^{(j)}\|_{2}=\left\|I-\frac{\eta_{t}}{b}H_{t}^{(j)}\right\|_{2}\leq 1+\eta_{t}\Lambda.$ 

Thus,

 $\left\|\prod_{j=M}^{q+1}A_{t}^{(j)}\right\|_{2}\leq(1+\eta_{t}\Lambda)^{M-q}.$ 

By the bounded-gradient assumption and the KL-smoothness assumption,

 $\|B_{t}^{(q)}\|_{2}\leq\eta_{t}G\sqrt{L_{t}}.$ 

Therefore,

 $\|C_{t}-A_{t}\|_{2}\leq\eta_{t}G\sqrt{L_{t}}\sum_{q=1}^{M}(1+\eta_{t}\Lambda)^{M-q}.$ 

Using the geometric sum,

 $\sum_{q=1}^{M}(1+\eta_{t}\Lambda)^{M-q}=\frac{(1+\eta_{t}\Lambda)^{M}-1}{\eta_{t}\Lambda},$ 

we obtain

 $\|C_{t}-A_{t}\|_{2}\leq G\sqrt{L_{t}}\frac{(1+\eta_{t}\Lambda)^{M}-1}{\Lambda}.$ 

It remains to relate this bound to $\|A_{t}\|_{2}$ . Since $\eta_{t}\Lambda<1$ , for every vector $v$ ,

 $\|A_{t}^{(j)}v\|_{2}=\left\|v-\frac{\eta_{t}}{b}H_{t}^{(j)}v\right\|_{2}\geq\|v\|_{2}-\frac{\eta_{t}}{b}\|H_{t}^{(j)}v\|_{2}\geq(1-\eta_{t}\Lambda)\|v\|_{2}.$ 

Applying this bound through the product gives

 $\|A_{t}v\|_{2}\geq(1-\eta_{t}\Lambda)^{M}\|v\|_{2}.$ 

Hence,

 $\|A_{t}\|_{2}\geq(1-\eta_{t}\Lambda)^{M}.$ 

Combining this with the absolute bound yields

 $\|C_{t}-A_{t}\|_{2}\leq\frac{G\sqrt{L_{t}}\frac{(1+\eta_{t}\Lambda)^{M}-1}{\Lambda}}{(1-\eta_{t}\Lambda)^{M}}\|A_{t}\|_{2}.$ 

This proves (32).
∎

## B.3 Accumulated Relative Gap

Theorem B.3: Accumulated nonconvex relative gap

Under the assumptions of Theorem B.2, for any $T>k$ ,

 $\left\|\prod_{t=T}^{k+1}C_{t}-\prod_{t=T}^{k+1}A_{t}\right\|_{2}\leq\left[\prod_{t=k+1}^{T}(1+\rho_{t})-1\right]\prod_{t=k+1}^{T}\|A_{t}\|_{2}.$ 

(33)

Furthermore, if the learning rate takes the harmonic form

 $\eta_{t}=\frac{\eta_{0}}{M(t-k)},$ 

with $\eta_{0}\Lambda<1$ , and if $L_{\max}:=\sup_{k<t\leq T}L_{t}$ , then, with $H:=T-k$ ,

 $\left\|\prod_{t=T}^{k+1}C_{t}-\prod_{t=T}^{k+1}A_{t}\right\|_{2}\leq\left(e^{\alpha}H^{\alpha}-1\right)\prod_{t=k+1}^{T}\|A_{t}\|_{2},$ 

(34)

where

 $\alpha:=\frac{G\sqrt{L_{\max}}\eta_{0}}{(1-\eta_{0}\Lambda)^{2}}.$ 

Proof.

We first prove the general accumulated product bound. Define

 $P_{C}(k,T)=\prod_{t=T}^{k+1}C_{t},\qquad P_{A}(k,T)=\prod_{t=T}^{k+1}A_{t}.$ 

Using the telescoping expansion for products,

 $P_{C}(k,T)-P_{A}(k,T)=\sum_{s=k+1}^{T}\left(\prod_{t=T}^{s+1}C_{t}\right)(C_{s}-A_{s})\left(\prod_{t=s-1}^{k+1}A_{t}\right).$ 

Taking norms gives

 $\|P_{C}(k,T)-P_{A}(k,T)\|_{2}\leq\sum_{s=k+1}^{T}\left(\prod_{t=s+1}^{T}\|C_{t}\|_{2}\right)\|C_{s}-A_{s}\|_{2}\left(\prod_{t=k+1}^{s-1}\|A_{t}\|_{2}\right).$ 

By Theorem B.2,

 $\|C_{s}-A_{s}\|_{2}\leq\rho_{s}\|A_{s}\|_{2}.$ 

Moreover,

 $\|C_{t}\|_{2}\leq\|A_{t}\|_{2}+\|C_{t}-A_{t}\|_{2}\leq(1+\rho_{t})\|A_{t}\|_{2}.$ 

Substituting these inequalities gives

 $\|P_{C}(k,T)-P_{A}(k,T)\|_{2}\leq\sum_{s=k+1}^{T}\left(\prod_{t=s+1}^{T}(1+\rho_{t})\|A_{t}\|_{2}\right)\rho_{s}\|A_{s}\|_{2}\left(\prod_{t=k+1}^{s-1}\|A_{t}\|_{2}\right).$ 

Factoring out the full product of $\|A_{t}\|_{2}$ gives

 $\|P_{C}(k,T)-P_{A}(k,T)\|_{2}\leq\left[\sum_{s=k+1}^{T}\rho_{s}\prod_{t=s+1}^{T}(1+\rho_{t})\right]\prod_{t=k+1}^{T}\|A_{t}\|_{2}.$ 

Finally, using the identity

 $\sum_{s=k+1}^{T}\rho_{s}\prod_{t=s+1}^{T}(1+\rho_{t})=\prod_{t=k+1}^{T}(1+\rho_{t})-1,$ 

we obtain

 $\left\|\prod_{t=T}^{k+1}C_{t}-\prod_{t=T}^{k+1}A_{t}\right\|_{2}\leq\left[\prod_{t=k+1}^{T}(1+\rho_{t})-1\right]\prod_{t=k+1}^{T}\|A_{t}\|_{2}.$ 

We next prove the harmonic-learning-rate refinement. From Theorem B.2,

 $\rho_{t}=G\sqrt{L_{t}}\frac{(1+\eta_{t}\Lambda)^{M}-1}{\Lambda(1-\eta_{t}\Lambda)^{M}}.$ 

Let $x_{t}=\eta_{t}\Lambda$ . Since $\eta_{t}=\eta_{0}/(M(t-k))$ , we have

 $Mx_{t}=\frac{\eta_{0}\Lambda}{t-k}\leq\eta_{0}\Lambda<1.$ 

Using

 $(1+x_{t})^{M}-1\leq\frac{Mx_{t}}{1-Mx_{t}},\qquad(1-x_{t})^{-M}\leq\frac{1}{1-Mx_{t}},$ 

we get

 $\rho_{t}\leq G\sqrt{L_{t}}\frac{M\eta_{t}}{(1-M\eta_{t}\Lambda)^{2}}.$ 

Since

 $1-M\eta_{t}\Lambda=1-\frac{\eta_{0}\Lambda}{t-k}\geq 1-\eta_{0}\Lambda,$ 

we have

 $\rho_{t}\leq\frac{G\sqrt{L_{t}}M\eta_{t}}{(1-\eta_{0}\Lambda)^{2}}=\frac{G\sqrt{L_{t}}\eta_{0}}{(1-\eta_{0}\Lambda)^{2}}\frac{1}{t-k}.$ 

Using $L_{t}\leq L_{\max}$ gives

 $\rho_{t}\leq\frac{G\sqrt{L_{\max}}\eta_{0}}{(1-\eta_{0}\Lambda)^{2}}\frac{1}{t-k}=\frac{\alpha}{t-k}.$ 

Therefore,

 $\sum_{t=k+1}^{T}\rho_{t}\leq\alpha\sum_{h=1}^{H}\frac{1}{h}\leq\alpha(1+\log H).$ 

Finally,

 $\prod_{t=k+1}^{T}(1+\rho_{t})-1\leq\exp\left(\sum_{t=k+1}^{T}\rho_{t}\right)-1\leq\exp\!\big(\alpha(1+\log H)\big)-1.$ 

Substituting this into (33) proves (34).
∎

## C.2 Experimental Setup for RLHF

Base model.

We use GPT-Neo 2.7B [Black et al., 2021] (EleutherAI/gpt-neo-2.7B, MIT license) as the base language model. We apply LoRA [Hu et al., 2022] for parameter-efficient fine-tuning, with rank $r=16$ , scaling factor $\alpha=32$ , and dropout rate $0.05$ .

Training data.

We train on the Real Toxicity Prompts dataset [Gehman et al., 2020] (allenai/real-toxicity-prompts, Apache-2.0 license). We retain examples with a prompt toxicity score above $0.3$ , use the 80% training split, and shuffle with a fixed seed. For each example, we concatenate the prompt and continuation fields, tokenize, and truncate to a length sampled uniformly in $[10,15]$ tokens to form the PPO prompt. The policy then generates a 30-token continuation with temperature $1.0$ and top- $p=1.0$ .

Reward model.

The reward signal is provided by the RoBERTa-based hate-speech classifier facebook/roberta-hate-speech-dynabench-r4-target [Vidgen et al., 2021]. Following the reference implementation, we use the raw logit of the nothate class (index $0$ ) as the scalar reward: a higher logit indicates less toxic output.

Training setup.

We optimize a PPO objective with a fixed KL penalty and no probability-ratio clipping, with a learning rate of $5\times 10^{-6}$ . The per-token KL divergence from the reference policy is penalized with coefficient $\beta=0.4$ , estimated via the $k_{1}$ approximation [Schulman, 2020]. Each rollout collects a batch of $64$ samples; PPO updates use a mini-batch size of $8$ over $4$ epochs, yielding $32$ gradient steps per rollout. We use the Adam optimizer with gradient norm clipping at $1.0$ and no weight decay. For each run, we train for $300$ rollout steps. All runs use bfloat16 precision with Flash Attention 2 [Dao, 2024].

For data attribution setups, we use $H=2$ for RINSE, and $\rho=10\%$ for both IIF and RINSE for fairness.

Evaluation setup.

We evaluate on the Wiki-Toxic dataset [Wulczyn et al., 2017] (OxAISH-AL-LLM/wiki_toxic, test split), drawn from a different distribution than the training data. We select the first $200$ toxic examples (label $=1$ ) and use up to the first $2{,}000$ characters of each comment as the evaluation prompt—substantially longer than training prompts and thus more likely to elicit toxic continuations. Continuations of $30$ tokens are generated under the same sampling parameters as training. To guard against reward hacking specific to the training signal, we score continuations with a separate classifier, DaNLP/da-electra-hatespeech-detection [Pauli et al., 2021], and report the mean toxicity probability across the $200$ samples. Each method is run with three independent seeds; we report mean $\pm$ standard error.

## D.1 Further Details on Linear Data Modeling Scores and Evaluation

Directly validating attribution methods using single-sample leave-one-out (LOO) correlation can be challenging in online RL due to the extremely low signal-to-noise ratio of individual sample effects. In particular, removing a single rollout record often induces only a negligible change in the final policy behavior, while stochasticity from environment interaction introduces substantial variance across retraining runs. To obtain a more stable evaluation protocol, we additionally adopt the linear data modeling score (LDS), which evaluates whether attribution scores can linearly predict the effect of removing a subset of training data. Concretely, LDS samples random subsets of rollout records, sums the attribution scores within each subset, and compares the aggregated score against the actual performance change after retraining without that subset. This evaluation implicitly assumes approximate linear additivity of data influence across samples, and therefore measures how well attribution scores behave as a linear proxy for collective data influence.

Experimental setup.

For the LDS evaluation, we consider model training with KL-regularized PPO on two consecutive rounds. We construct 100 random subsets of training data, each containing 4096 samples. For each subset, we remove it and retrain the model with future rollout data resampled using the updated policy for 32 independent runs, and compute the average change in validation loss. All results are further averaged over 3 random seeds. We conduct the experiments on two MuJoCo benchmark environments [Todorov et al., 2012], Ant-v4 and HalfCheetah-v4.

## D.3 Ablation on Algorithmic Design

In this section, we conduct ablation studies to justify our design choices of the horizon selection $H$ in RINSE.

## D.3.1 Ablation on Adaptive Horizon

We ablate the effect of the adaptive horizon by adopting a fixed horizon instead, on Ant-v4, HalfCheetah-v4, BipedalWalker-v3, and LunarLander-v2. As shown in Figure 7, the adaptive horizon matches or exceeds the fixed variant in every case, and the gap is largest on the harder-exploration tasks: on Ant-v4 and BipedalWalker-v3 the fixed-horizon runs progress slowly and erratically, while on LunarLander-v2 the adaptive horizon reaches a higher return with lower variance. This shows that the adaptive horizon is necessary: it stabilizes training and yields consistently higher-quality data selection without per-environment tuning.

We sweep the horizon over $H\in\{1,2,4,5,8,10\}$ on Ant-v4 and HalfCheetah-v4, holding all other hyperparameters fixed. Figure 8 shows a consistent trend that emerges across both environments. ## D.4 Further Details on Time and Sample Efficiency

We provide additional details on the computation of time efficiency (TE) and sample efficiency (SE), along with extended results. As described in the main text, both metrics are defined relative to the standard PPO baseline, which is normalized to $100\%$ . Lower values therefore indicate better efficiency.

Evaluation protocol.

For each method, we track the training trajectory and record the first time step at which a set of target performance levels is reached. In the main results, we use three thresholds corresponding to $90\%$ , $95\%$ , and $100\%$ of the peak performance achieved by the standard PPO baseline. TE and SE are then computed by averaging the relative runtime and data usage required to reach these thresholds.

In addition to this multi-level evaluation, we also report a stricter variant based on a single threshold at $100\%$ performance. This provides a complementary view that focuses on the final stage of training.

For example, if the contribution is a novel architecture, describing the architecture fully might suffice, or if the contribution is a specific model and empirical evaluation, it may be necessary to either make it possible for others to replicate the model with the same dataset, or provide access to the model. In general. releasing code and data is often one good way to accomplish this, but reproducibility can also be provided via detailed instructions for how to replicate the results, access to a hosted model (e.g., in the case of a large language model), releasing of a model checkpoint, or other means that are appropriate to the research performed.

• 

While NeurIPS does not require releasing code, the conference does require all submissions to provide some reasonable avenue for reproducibility, which may depend on the nature of the contribution. For example

(a) 

If the contribution is primarily a new algorithm, the paper should make it clear how to reproduce that algorithm.

(b) 

If the contribution is primarily a new model architecture, the paper should describe the architecture clearly and fully.

(c) 

If the contribution is a new model (e.g., a large language model), then there should either be a way to access this model for reproducing the results or a way to reproduce the model (e.g., with an open-source dataset or instructions for how to construct the dataset).

(d) 

We recognize that reproducibility may be tricky in some cases, in which case authors are welcome to describe the particular way they provide for reproducibility. In the case of closed-source models, it may be that access to the model is limited in some way (e.g., to registered users), but it should be possible for other researchers to have some path to reproducing or verifying the results.

Answer: [Yes] 

Justification: We use at least 3 random seeds in all of our experiments. We report the standard errors in tables and include error bars in figures.

Guidelines:

• 

The answer [N/A] means that the paper does not include experiments.

• 

The authors should answer [Yes] if the results are accompanied by error bars, confidence intervals, or statistical significance tests, at least for the experiments that support the main claims of the paper.

• 

The factors of variability that the error bars are capturing should be clearly stated (for example, train/test split, initialization, random drawing of some parameter, or overall run with given experimental conditions).

• 

The method for calculating the error bars should be explained (closed form formula, call to a library function, bootstrap, etc.)

• 

The assumptions made should be given (e.g., Normally distributed errors).

• 

It should be clear whether the error bar is the standard deviation or the standard error of the mean.

• 

It is OK to report 1-sigma error bars, but one should state it. The authors should preferably report a 2-sigma error bar than state that they have a 96% CI, if the hypothesis of Normality of errors is not verified.

• 

For asymmetric distributions, the authors should be careful not to show in tables or figures symmetric error bars that would yield results that are out of range (e.g., negative error rates).

• 

If error bars are reported in tables or plots, the authors should explain in the text how they were calculated and reference the corresponding figures or tables in the text.

• 

Examples of negative societal impacts include potential malicious or unintended uses (e.g., disinformation, generating fake profiles, surveillance), fairness considerations (e.g., deployment of technologies that could make decisions that unfairly impact specific groups), privacy considerations, and security considerations.

• 

The conference expects that many papers will be foundational research and not tied to particular applications, let alone deployments. • 

If there are negative societal impacts, the authors could also discuss possible mitigation strategies (e.g., gated release of models, providing defenses in addition to attacks, mechanisms for monitoring misuse, mechanisms to monitor how a system learns from feedback over time, improving the efficiency and accessibility of ML).

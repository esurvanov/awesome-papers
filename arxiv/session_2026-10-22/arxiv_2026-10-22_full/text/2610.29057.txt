## Does Every Instance Count Equally?

Order Effects in LLM Multi-Instance Processing

Jingxuan Chen

Mohammad Taher Pilehvar

Jose Camacho-Collados

Abstract

Users increasingly rely on large language models (LLMs) to analyse collections of independent inputs, such as product reviews or social media posts, and return a single aggregated answer. This setting requires multi-instance processing (MIP), where
a model combines information from multiple instances into one prediction.
We
study a task in which
the model must infer a binary label for each instance,
and
identify the most frequent label among the full instance set. The correct answer should be invariant to
instance order.
By evaluating fifteen LLMs
across five datasets and seven instance order patterns, with each instance set held fixed across patterns,
we find
substantial order sensitivity.
Accuracy
can differ by about 90 percentage points between the best and worst order patterns, with
models generally more accurate when majority-label instances appear
at the start
than
when they appear
in the middle or at the end.
For subsets of models, available
reasoning traces and
gradient-based attribution reveal uneven emphasis across positions. Traces disproportionately mention early instances, while attribution scores are generally higher near the beginning and end than in the middle.
These results show that
instance
order
can systematically
bias aggregated
predictions, highlighting the need to evaluate MIP across different input arrangements.11

## 1 Introduction

LLMs are increasingly used to assist users in analysing large collections of independent inputs, such as product reviews, social media posts,
user records,
or news snippets (Sun et al., 2025; Tang et al., 2025). This scenario requires multi-instance processing (MIP): the model must process each instance and aggregate
the information
into one prediction (Bertsch et al., 2026; Chen et al., 2026).
Existing work on MIP
reports performance degradation as instance count and context length increase (Bertsch et al., 2026; Chen et al., 2026).
Instance count and context length are closely related, as increasing the number of instances also lengthens the context, creating a natural overlap between MIP and long-context settings.

However, many long-context evaluations focus on tasks where only a small subset of the input is relevant (Hsieh et al., 2024a; Lewis et al., 2020; Liu et al., 2025).
On such tasks, models are sensitive to where the relevant information appears (Liu et al., 2024), and similar order effects have been reported for in-context learning and multiple-choice questions (Cobbina and Zhou, 2025; Pezeshkpour and Hruschka, 2024).
Less is known about how the positions of differently labelled instances affect aggregation over the full set of instances.

Because these aggregated predictions often drive downstream decisions, their reliability matters in practice (Zhu et al., 2026).
When
aggregating over the full,
unordered set of instances, omitting even a small fraction of them can change
the correct answer, but reordering them cannot.
We therefore treat permutation invariance as a necessary, though not sufficient, condition for reliable aggregation.

Figure 1: An illustrative example of our MIP setting, with 20 textual instances and two pre-defined labels, arranged in four order patterns. In this example, the model correctly predicts the majority label when instances are randomly shuffled or majority-label instances are grouped at the beginning, but not when they are grouped in the middle or at the end. Note that while all instances are color-coded for informative purposes, LLMs receive instances without label information.

To test whether LLMs are permutation invariant in MIP, we build a controlled diagnostic probe.
Given a set of instances
and two
possible
labels,
the model must infer each instance’s label and identify
the majority label across instances. Figure 1 illustrates
this setting with four representative instance order patterns.
We evaluate fifteen LLMs across five classification tasks, holding each instance set fixed while varying its order across seven patterns.

Our results suggest that
the evaluated
LLMs are broadly not robust to instance order. Across models, accuracy
can differ by about 90
percentage points between the best and worst order patterns, and the effect is systematic and directional. A majority placed at the start is
generally
easier to identify than one placed
in the middle or at the end.
This advantage remains visible across different instance counts and label distributions.
For subsets of models, we further examine reasoning traces and gradient-based attribution.
We find that reasoning traces often mention only a small fraction of instances (frequently fewer than a quarter), primarily those near the beginning, while gradient-based attribution scores are lower in the middle than at the beginning and end.

## 2 Related Work

Multi-Instance Processing.

Several lines of work study LLM prompts that contain multiple independent inputs, problems, or evidence units.
One line of research
studies batch processing and multi-task inference
to process
multiple items
jointly
more efficiently
while
preserving task performance (Cheng et al., 2023; Lin et al., 2024; Kolawole et al., 2025; Son et al., 2024).
Another line
examines
information fusion, aggregation,
and
deep reasoning
in long-context benchmarks (Bai et al., 2025; Hsieh et al., 2024a; Qiu et al., 2024; Shaham et al., 2023).
The studies closest to ours measure accuracy degradation as instance count and context length increase (Bertsch et al., 2026; Chen et al., 2026).
In our case, we isolate
the effect of instance order on aggregation accuracy
in MIP.

Position Bias and Lost-in-the-Middle Effects.

A substantial body of work shows that LLMs are highly sensitive to where relevant information appears in the input. Liu et al. (2024) established the “lost-in-the-middle” effect, showing that
LLMs can struggle to access task-relevant information when it appears in the middle of a long context rather than near the beginning or end.
Subsequent work
examines
absolute position, relative spacing between relevant pieces, and multi-evidence retrieval (Gao et al., 2024; Hsieh et al., 2024b; Song et al., 2025; Tian et al., 2025; Veseli et al., 2025).
Related order effects also arise outside retrieval: few-shot in-context learning is sensitive to demonstration order, and multiple-choice reasoning is brittle to option permutations and selection bias (Cobbina and Zhou, 2025; Pezeshkpour and Hruschka, 2024; Zheng et al., 2024).
Recent work examines order sensitivity in retrieval and multimodal settings (Paruchuri et al., 2026; Zhang et al., 2026a) and explores training and architectural approaches to order robustness (Egressy and Stühmer, 2025; Chu et al., 2026). Instead, our work focuses on aggregation tasks that require considering information from every instance in the final judgement. Although the correct answer is independent of instance order, we show that the positions of instances with different labels can systematically bias the model’s prediction.

## 3 Experimental Setting

To study
whether LLMs can perform MIP robustly under instance permutations,
we use five heterogeneous classification tasks.
The goal in our setting is to find the majority label
across a fixed instance pool. Within each pool, we present the instances in different order patterns (cf. Section 3.3).

## 3.1 Individual Tasks

We design five classification tasks from existing datasets: Math
(arithmetic reasoning) (Cobbe et al., 2021), NER (named-entity recognition) (Rahimi et al., 2019),
Occurrence (target-word counting) (Barbieri et al., 2020), Sentiment (sentiment analysis) (Socher et al., 2013) and WSD (word sense disambiguation) (Loureiro et al., 2021). The tasks and datasets are selected so as to be diverse, relatively simple and solvable by current LLMs when each instance is processed individually.
We treat each instance within a given task as an independent binary classification problem.
In the MIP setting, we ask the model to identify the majority label across all instances.

## 3.3 Instance Set Construction and Ordering

Each instance set $\mathcal{X}$ is constructed from filtered instances according to: the instance count $n$ (i.e., $n=|\mathcal{X}|$ ), the majority proportion $p$ , the majority label $y_{\mathrm{maj}}$ , and the instance selection seed $s$ . Given $n$ and $p$ , we use $s$ to sample $n_{\mathrm{maj}}=p\cdot n$ instances with label $y_{\mathrm{maj}}$ and $n_{\mathrm{min}}=(1-p)\cdot n$ instances with the corresponding minority label $y_{\mathrm{min}}$ . We then hold $\mathcal{X}$ fixed and vary the order pattern $\pi$ across seven predefined choices to construct the experimental inputs.

Order patterns.

We explore seven order patterns that specifically place labelled instances across different positions in the input.
First, the random pattern acts as a baseline and shuffles the full instance set randomly using a separate order seed $s_{\mathrm{order}}$ .
Four patterns group instances into contiguous blocks by label.
Majority-first places all majority instances before the minority ones, and majority-last reverses this order.
Majority-edges splits the majority instances into two halves placed around a central minority block, and majority-middle instead places the majority block between two halves of the minority instances.
The remaining two are soft variants that interleave the labels.
They form an even-length alternating sequence of majority–minority pairs and place the surplus majority instances either at both ends of this sequence (soft-majority-edges) or in its middle (soft-majority-middle).
Figure 1 provides a visual illustration of four representative patterns (random, majority-first, majority-last and majority-middle), while
Appendix C.3 provides formal definitions of all seven patterns.

## 3.4 Evaluation Metric

For each experiment $e$ , we attempt to parse the model output $o_{e}$ 
into a predicted majority label $\hat{y}_{e}$ .
If no label can be parsed within the allowed attempts, the experiment is invalid.44
 4

 A parseable “tie” is treated as valid but incorrect. Otherwise, we compare $\hat{y}_{e}$ with the
ground-truth majority label $y_{\mathrm{maj}}^{(e)}$ and consider the experiment
correct if $\hat{y}_{e}=y_{\mathrm{maj}}^{(e)}$ .
We report valid-only accuracy ( $\mathrm{Acc}$ ) as the fraction of valid experiments that produce a correct answer within each selection seed.
Overall accuracy pools runs across tasks, majority labels and order patterns. We then report its mean and standard deviation across selection seeds.
We select the best and worst order patterns, $\pi^{+}$ and $\pi^{-}$ , using their mean accuracies. We compute their gap as
 $\Delta_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}best-worst}}=\underset{s}{\operatorname{mean}}\bigl[\mathrm{Acc}_{s}(\pi^{+})-\mathrm{Acc}_{s}(\pi^{-})\bigr]$ ,
where $\mathrm{Acc}_{s}(\pi)$ is the accuracy of pattern $\pi$ at selection seed $s$ .
Larger values indicate greater variation in accuracy across order patterns.

## 3.5 Core Setting

Our
core setting is based on $n=2,000$ instances with proportion $p=0.55$ .
Although this setting is near-balanced in percentage terms, the majority signal
is still well-defined. It corresponds to $n_{\mathrm{maj}}=1100$ and
 $n_{\mathrm{min}}=900$ , giving an absolute margin of 200 instances.
In probabilistic terms, the chance of reversing the majority under a 55/45 distribution, even with a model predicting at only 90% accuracy,55
 5

 However, since we only keep the filtered instances, we expect all LLMs to achieve nearly perfect accuracy for each individual instance after our filtering procedure.
is on the order of one in a billion (assuming independent instance-level predictions with equal accuracy across instances). We provide the calculation in Appendix C.4.

We conduct each experiment twice by swapping which binary label serves
as $y_{\mathrm{maj}}$ . For each configuration, we test all seven order patterns over three selection seeds $s\in\{0,1,2\}$ .
For the random pattern, we additionally vary the order seed three times with $s_{\mathrm{order}}\in\{0,1,2\}$ 
to obtain a more robust estimate under random orderings.

Our
core setting
uses 2,000 instances with

To investigate whether the observed order effects
generalise beyond this specific setting, we vary the instance count (100, 200, 500, 1,000 and 2,000), and the label distribution (majority/minority: 90/10, 70/30, 60/40 and 55/45).

To assess whether prompting can reduce order sensitivity,
we compare the standard prompt with three prompting variants for three models and all patterns in the core setting,
keeping the input instances and their orderings unchanged:
(i) hint prompting, which
emphasises processing all instances rather than relying on a subset;
(ii) suggested strategy prompting, which
additionally suggests processing instances in groups and summing the group-level label counts;
(iii) self-prompting,
where the model first generates its own task instructions and then uses them to solve the task.88

## 5 Analysis of Model Behaviour

To further examine these order effects, we perform two complementary analyses: (1) an analysis of reasoning traces
to investigate how many instances are mentioned by reasoning models
(Section 5.1), and (2) a model interpretability analysis to better understand the contributions of individual input instances to LLM predictions (Section 5.2).

## 5.1 Evidence from Reasoning Traces

We use reasoning traces to investigate whether models selectively engage with particular instance positions in ways that may contribute to their order sensitivity.
For this analysis, we focus on four reasoning models:1010

## 5.1.1 Manual Analysis

We manually examine all 222 reasoning traces from
the four reasoning LLMs,
each corresponding to a
valid but incorrect
model prediction
in our core setting and collectively covering all seven order patterns.
Although our prompts explicitly instruct the models to evaluate every instance before answering, their reasoning traces rarely document such exhaustive evaluation.
Instead, 72% of the examined traces primarily mention consecutive instances from the beginning, typically stopping within the first 200, with occasional references to a few instances from the middle or end of the context. These traces accompany order-dependent errors. For example, under majority-last and majority-middle, some traces mention only the minority block, while under majority-edges, others identify the transition to the minority block but never mention the second majority block.
We provide example reasoning traces in Appendix E.1.

## 5.2 Evidence from Gradient-Based Attribution

We complement the reasoning-trace analysis with an interpretability analysis on three models. Our analysis covers NER and Sentiment with Gemma 4 26B A4B, Gemma 4 31B, and Qwen3-4B-Instruct.1212
 12

 The expanded-ordering analysis uses outputs generated locally with vLLM. For the two Gemma models, we additionally replay the OpenRouter outputs used in the main experiments and obtain closely matching positional profiles; Qwen3-4B-Instruct uses vLLM throughout.

## 5.2.1 Input $\times$ Gradient

We use Input $\times$ Gradient (IxG)
as a computationally efficient method for contrastive attribution (Ancona et al., 2018; Yin and Neubig, 2022).
It estimates the importance of each input token for the model’s final prediction between the two candidate labels.
We take the magnitude and aggregate token scores within each instance, using the resulting profile as descriptive rather than causal evidence. Appendix F.1 provides the formal definition and replay details.

To mitigate the influence that the local ordering of instances may have and to derive more reliable attribution profiles in the core setting, for each of the six structured patterns (i.e. without random), we independently shuffle the selected majority- and minority-label instances under 50 order seeds $s_{\mathrm{order}}\in\{1,2,...,50\}$ and then reconstruct the pattern. Together with three instance-selection seeds, this yields 150 generations before validity filtering for each task, majority label, and pattern. All attribution analyses include only valid outputs with the two candidate labels.
We also compare performance across these orderings with the original core results.1313
 13

 Treating the original core ordering as an additional ordering, no model–task–pattern comparison differs significantly from the corresponding 50-ordering distribution at $p<0.05$ , whether invalid outputs are counted as incorrect or excluded.

As in Section 5.1.2,
we also apply the same IxG attribution method to our generalisability setting (Appendix F.3) and prompting variants for Qwen3-4B-Instruct (Appendix F.4). The positional profiles change with instance count, with differences across models. For both Gemma models, attribution near the end is generally higher at larger instance counts, whereas Qwen3-4B-Instruct shows a stronger emphasis on the beginning. Nevertheless, profiles across label distributions and the prompting variants remain broadly similar.

## 6 Conclusion

Our controlled majority-label probe identifies instance order as a concrete source of aggregation errors in multi-instance processing.
Across fifteen LLMs and five tasks, accuracy varies widely across order patterns, with
generally higher accuracy when
majority-label instances appear first. Since the task is order-independent, these results reveal clear violations of permutation invariance
despite the instance sets remaining unchanged, with the bias towards early instances suggesting broader reliability issues in MIP.

Available reasoning traces
often
mention only a fraction of the input and disproportionately reference early instances, while gradient-based attribution profiles are also non-uniform across instance positions. We also explore prompt variants that explicitly instruct models to check all instances, but these do not consistently improve permutation robustness.
Together, these findings suggest that models may rely on shortcuts in MIP, even when the task requires them to consider every instance.

Several takeaways and implications emerge from our findings.
First, evaluations of MIP should report both accuracy and variation across input orders.
Second, future work should
investigate models and workflows that process
instances systematically and aggregate verifiable intermediate decisions without sensitivity to presentation order.
Such approaches could help ensure that all required instances are considered, regardless of their positions, and give users greater confidence in the results.
Finally, future work should investigate mechanisms that enable models to recognise when they cannot fully complete a task and alert users accordingly.

## Limitations

We study LLM reliability in multi-instance processing (MIP) through
the lens of permutation invariance. This is a necessary reliability criterion
for unordered instance sets, and its violation is already sufficient to show
that a model is unreliable in this setting. However, MIP reliability has other
important dimensions that we do not fully cover, such as robustness to task
formulation, language variations (we study only English), calibration, aggregation faithfulness, and transparency of
intermediate decisions.
Future work should
therefore examine a broader set of reliability criteria and investigate the different
mechanisms underlying MIP failures.

Our goal is primarily diagnostic.
We design a controlled probe to assess whether the evaluated LLMs satisfy permutation invariance and to characterise behaviours associated with its violations.
We therefore do not provide a comprehensive method for improving
MIP reliability. Our prompt-based
experiments
show that the tested prompting approaches do not consistently resolve
the failures we observe.
More systematic solutions may be needed, such as specialised training, explicit instance-level decomposition, tool-assisted processing, deterministic or verifiable aggregation, or agentic workflows that separate instance processing from aggregation.

Finally, our behavioural analysis relies on indirect evidence from reasoning traces and attribution signals.
Some evaluated models do
not produce reasoning traces, while others do not reveal them through the API.
Even when traces are available, they may be incomplete or may not fully reflect
the model’s internal computation, and our automatic ID extraction may contain false positives. Thus, they offer observable but potentially unfaithful evidence
of how reasoning LLMs approach a task (Turpin et al., 2023; Zhang et al., 2026b).
Attribution signals are likewise descriptive rather than causal: Input
 $\times$ Gradient is a local, first-order measure tied to the chosen decision
score and the observed input.
We therefore treat these analyses as complementary evidence of positional
imbalance, rather than direct measurements of the model’s internal computation.
Developing more faithful and explainable methods for analysing MIP behaviour
remains an important direction for future work.

## Ethical Considerations

This study provides an empirical assessment of how LLMs behave in multi-instance processing scenarios, focusing specifically on whether these models can consistently handle every instance irrespective of the order in which they appear. Our work does not entail gathering new data, engaging human participants, or deploying models within real-world decision-making pipelines. Every dataset employed in our experiments is publicly accessible and open-source, and each was obtained and used in line with its original licence, terms of use, and intended scope for research.

Furthermore, we make no assertion that any specific model or provider is fundamentally unsafe or inappropriate for use. Instead, our findings point to broader limitations shared by current LLMs under particular experimental settings. We hope this research supports more responsible deployment of LLM-based systems by encouraging practitioners to consider reliability, aggregation strategies, and potential failure modes when designing real-world applications.

## B.2 Prompt Variants

## B.2.1 Hint Prompting

We use the same hint prompt for all tasks and insert it
between the end of Instructions: and the beginning of Example response format:.

Hints:

- The input may contain many instances, and the majority label cannot be reliably determined from any subset or by sampling.

- The final answer should be based on processing all provided instances, rather than a partial scan or estimation.

## B.2.2 Suggested Strategy Prompting

We use the same suggested strategy prompt for all tasks and insert it
between the end of Instructions: and the beginning of Example response format:.

Suggested Strategy:

- The input may contain a large number of instances, and you must take all of them into account — the majority cannot be reliably inferred from a subset or by sampling.

- If there are many instances, process them in batches: solve and count within each batch, then sum the per-batch counts and decide the overall majority from those totals.

## B.2.3 Self-Prompting

First Stage

## C.3 Order Pattern Formulation

Given $n$ , $p$ , $y_{\mathrm{maj}}$ and $s$ , we select $n_{\mathrm{maj}}=p\cdot n$ majority instances and $n_{\mathrm{min}}=(1-p)\cdot n$ minority instances from the filtered data.
For each label, the filtered instances are first shuffled using $s$ ; we then select the first $n_{\mathrm{maj}}$ majority-label instances $\mathcal{X}_{\mathrm{maj}}=(x^{\mathrm{maj}}_{1},x^{\mathrm{maj}}_{2},\ldots,x^{\mathrm{maj}}_{n_{\mathrm{maj}}})$ , and the first $n_{\mathrm{min}}$ minority-label instances $\mathcal{X}_{\mathrm{min}}=(x^{\mathrm{min}}_{1},x^{\mathrm{min}}_{2},\ldots,x^{\mathrm{min}}_{n_{\mathrm{min}}})$ .
The same selected instances are used for every order pattern. Below, $\oplus$ denotes list concatenation.

Figure 8 illustrates the order patterns, using 20 instances as an example.

Figure 8: A toy example with 20 instances illustrating how the order patterns arrange instances with majority (Label A) and minority (Label B) labels.

For the random pattern, we concatenate the two selected lists, $\mathcal{X}_{\mathrm{maj}}\oplus\mathcal{X}_{\mathrm{min}}$ , and shuffle the combined list using a separate order seed $s_{\mathrm{order}}$ . The other six patterns form three natural counterpart pairs: (majority-first, majority-last), (majority-edges, majority-middle), and (soft-majority-edges, soft-majority-middle).

The majority-first and majority-last patterns group instances by label, yielding $\mathcal{X}_{\mathrm{maj}}\oplus\mathcal{X}_{\mathrm{min}}$ and $\mathcal{X}_{\mathrm{min}}\oplus\mathcal{X}_{\mathrm{maj}}$ , respectively. The majority-edges pattern places minority instances between two halves of the majority list: $\mathcal{X}_{\mathrm{maj}}^{L}\oplus\mathcal{X}_{\mathrm{min}}\oplus\mathcal{X}_{\mathrm{maj}}^{R}$ , where $\mathcal{X}_{\mathrm{maj}}^{L}$ contains the first $\lceil n_{\mathrm{maj}}/2\rceil$ majority instances. Conversely, majority-middle places all majority instances between two halves of the minority list: $\mathcal{X}_{\mathrm{min}}^{L}\oplus\mathcal{X}_{\mathrm{maj}}\oplus\mathcal{X}_{\mathrm{min}}^{R}$ , where $\mathcal{X}_{\mathrm{min}}^{L}$ contains the first $\lceil n_{\mathrm{min}}/2\rceil$ minority instances.

Unlike the majority-edges and majority-middle patterns, which
group instances into contiguous blocks by label, the soft variants keep the bulk of the instances alternating and only relocate the surplus majority instances. These soft variants
test order sensitivity when the two labels are interleaved rather than separated into homogeneous blocks. The soft-majority-edges and soft-majority-middle patterns
first form an alternating list $\mathcal{A}=(x^{\mathrm{maj}}_{1},x^{\mathrm{min}}_{1},\ldots,x^{\mathrm{maj}}_{n_{\mathrm{min}}},x^{\mathrm{min}}_{n_{\mathrm{min}}})$ , with surplus majority instances $\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{maj}}=(x^{\mathrm{maj}}_{n_{\mathrm{min}}+1},\ldots,x^{\mathrm{maj}}_{n_{\mathrm{maj}}})$ . The soft-majority-edges pattern places the surplus majority instances at the two edges, $\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{maj}}^{L}\oplus\mathcal{A}\oplus\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{maj}}^{R}$ , where $\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{maj}}^{L}$ contains the first $\lceil|\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{maj}}|/2\rceil$ surplus majority instances. Similarly, the soft-majority-middle pattern instead places the surplus majority instances in the middle of $\mathcal{A}$ : $\mathcal{A}^{L}\oplus\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{maj}}\oplus\mathcal{A}^{R}$ , where $\mathcal{A}^{L}$ contains the first $2\cdot\lceil n_{\mathrm{min}}/2\rceil$ alternating instances.

## C.4 Reference Calculation
for the 2,000 Instance Set and 55/45 Label Distribution

Our core setting of 2,000 instances with a 55/45 label distribution
contains 1,100 majority instances and 900 minority instances.
This
calculation is intended only as
an illustrative
reference point. Our actual
experiments use filtered instances that are individually solvable by the filtering models. Suppose
that a downstream evaluated model makes
independent instance-level predictions with
the same
accuracy $q=0.9$ 
for every instance, regardless of its label or position, and aggregates these predictions correctly. Let $Z$ be the
number of instances predicted as the majority label. Then

 $Z=Z_{\mathrm{maj}}+Z_{\mathrm{min}},$ 

where

 $Z_{\mathrm{maj}}\sim\mathrm{Binomial}(1100,q),$ 

 $Z_{\mathrm{min}}\sim\mathrm{Binomial}(900,1-q).$ 

Here, $Z_{\mathrm{maj}}$ counts correctly predicted majority-label instances,
while $Z_{\mathrm{min}}$ counts minority-label instances incorrectly predicted
as the majority label. The
predicted majority would be reversed
only if $Z<1000$ .
The case $Z=1000$ produces a tie, which is also incorrect for this input.

Under $q=0.9$ , the expected predicted-majority count is

 $\mathrm{E}[Z]=1100\times 0.9+900\times 0.1=1080.$ 

Its standard deviation is

 $\mathrm{SD}(Z)=\sqrt{1100q(1-q)+900(1-q)q}.$ 

With $q=0.9$ , this gives

 $\mathrm{SD}(Z)=\sqrt{2000\times 0.9\times 0.1}\approx 13.4.$ 

Therefore, the tie boundary at 1000 is approximately

 $\frac{1080-1000}{13.4}\approx 6.0$ 

standard deviations below the expected predicted-majority count.

This calculation does not model the full behaviour of LLMs, whose errors may be
correlated
or vary across labels and positions,
and whose MIP failures may arise from skipping or aggregation errors.
Rather, it provides a reference point showing that
under this idealised model, the expected predicted-majority count remains well above the tie boundary for the 55/45
label distribution
with 2,000 instances. Overall instance-level accuracy alone does not guarantee the same behaviour when these assumptions do not hold.

We conduct this analysis on Gemini 3.1 Flash-Lite, Qwen3-4B-Instruct, and Qwen3.6-35B-A3B.

First, hint prompting explicitly reminds the models that the majority label must be based on all provided instances rather than a subset. Second, suggested strategy prompting mentions processing the instances in groups,1616

## Appendix F Evidence from Gradient-Based Attribution

## F.1 Input $\times$ Gradient Method

As described in Section 5.2, we obtain IxG scores by replaying each model output with teacher forcing, without changing the observed prediction. For replay $r$ , let $y_{0}$ and $y_{1}$ denote the task’s two candidate label strings. We compute the selected-minus-alternative log-probability margin at the answer span and reverse its sign when the selected label is $y_{0}$ , yielding a score $S_{r}$ consistently oriented from $y_{0}$ to $y_{1}$ . The log-probability of a label string is the sum of its token log-probabilities.1717
 17

 Replays whose parsed output is not one of the two candidate labels, including $-1$ ties, are omitted from the attribution analysis.

Let $E_{r,t}$ be the input embedding of token $t$ , and let $T_{r,i}$ contain the tokens belonging to instance $i$ . The length-normalised IxG magnitude for instance $i$ is

 $u_{r,i}=\frac{1}{|T_{r,i}|}\sum_{t\in T_{r,i}}\left|\left\langle E_{r,t},\frac{\partial S_{r}}{\partial E_{r,t}}\right\rangle\right|.$ 

Taking the absolute value before aggregation avoids cancellation between opposing token-level scores, while division by $|T_{r,i}|$ controls for instance length. The resulting value therefore measures attribution magnitude rather than the direction in which an instance supports either label.

We then normalise the scores over the $N$ instances in each replay, with $N=2{,}000$ in the core setting:

 $R_{r,i}=\frac{Nu_{r,i}}{\sum_{j=1}^{N}u_{r,j}}.$ 

We refer to $R_{r,i}$ as Share ( $\times$ uniform). A value of $1$ is the uniform reference.
Values above or below $1$ indicate more or less attribution than this reference. Only instance tokens enter the denominator, so
instruction, template, and response tokens are excluded.

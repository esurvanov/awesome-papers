Abstract

Analogical reasoning is widely considered a fundamental component of human intelligence. Few benchmarks are designed to explicitly test far analogical reasoning—reasoning between domains that are superficially dissimilar yet share deep relational or structural similarities. In this work, we introduce an analogical reasoning benchmark called Abstraction and Reasoning Corpus of ANAlogy (ARCANA). The tasks of ARCANA require performing far analogical mappings from real-world objects or physical phenomena to abstract grid-based transformations, different from the well-known ARC-AGI benchmark which primarily emphasized innate human priors.
We validate ARCANA through a human study, demonstrating that humans solve these tasks reliably. In contrast, evaluations across a range of large language models show a distinct performance gap compared to humans, revealing systematic failure modes that remain challenging for current models. To address this gap, we propose ConceptSIR, a reasoning framework based on concept slippage, introspection, and reflection. Experiments demonstrate that ConceptSIR enables standard LLMs to solve several challenging ARCANA tasks that were unsolved by direct prompting in our evaluation. Overall, our results suggest that far analogical reasoning remains an open problem for contemporary LLMs.

## 1 Introduction

Analogical reasoning has long been regarded as a central component of human intelligence and a characteristic of general intelligence more broadly [1, 2, 3, 4]. Humans routinely draw analogies between domains that are superficially dissimilar yet share deep relational structure, allowing them to generalize knowledge flexibly from limited experience. This capability for far analogy—mapping abstract relations across distant domains—plays a critical role in human learning, reasoning, and problem solving.

In recent years, substantial progress has been made in evaluating the general reasoning abilities of artificial intelligence systems, such as Humanity’s Last Exam [5] and SWE-Bench Pro [6]. However, these benchmarks are not designed to explicitly probe far analogical reasoning. Among the most challenging existing benchmarks, ARC-AGI-1&2 evaluate abstraction, reasoning, and analogy through grid-based tasks that require inferring latent rules from a small number of examples [7].
In general, ARC-AGI-1&2 primarily focus on testing near analogy based on relative surface-level feature similarity (e.g., color, shape, position, number, center, copy, and count). While instances of far analogy may arise implicitly, explicit far analogical reasoning—particularly that requiring mappings from real-world concepts or physical phenomena to abstract symbolic representations—is rare and limited in scope. This leaves a substantial gap in the systematic evaluation of fluid intelligence and far analogical reasoning.

Inspired by ARC-AGI-2 and to address this gap, we introduce ARCANA (Abstraction and Reasoning Corpus for ANAlogy), a benchmark explicitly designed to evaluate far analogical reasoning.
ARCANA inherits the form of ARC-AGI-1&2, each task of which consists of 3-5 examples and 1-2 tests.
As shown in Figure 1, unlike ARC-AGI-1&2 which largely emphasizes innate human priors (e.g., copying the 3×3 pattern to the purple grid, filling in the grids with different colors using discovered rules), ARCANA focuses on far analogical mappings from real-world objects or physical phenomena to ARC grid transformations (e.g., threading 3D rings onto a column with physical constraints).
The benchmark draws from a broad and diverse set of conceptual sources, including physical principles (e.g., buoyancy, levers, gears, melting, the Doppler effect, Kepler’s second law), natural phenomena (e.g., planets, sunset), and object-level abstractions (e.g., cats, ducks, fireworks, 3D rings, 3D sushi).

To assess the validity and difficulty of ARCANA tasks, we conduct a human study with undergraduate participants. The results show that humans reliably solve ARCANA tasks, indicating that the benchmark captures forms of reasoning that are natural for human cognition. Meanwhile, we evaluate ARCANA across a range of large language models (LLMs), the results of which reveal lower performances, suggesting that far analogical reasoning remains a challenge for current models and exposing systematic failure modes beyond simple pattern matching.

Inspired by the Copycat model of analogical reasoning proposed by [3], we propose ConceptSIR, an analogical reasoning framework based on concept slippage, introspection, and reflection. ConceptSIR is designed to approximate key aspects of the human problem-solving process when constructing and solving ARCANA tasks. Empirical results show that ConceptSIR enables large language models to solve several ARCANA tasks that are otherwise unsolved by standard prompting strategies, providing insight into the potential of our ConceptSIR.

In summary, this work makes three contributions:
(1) we introduce ARCANA, a benchmark explicitly targeting far analogical reasoning;
(2) we present a human and model evaluation that reveals a substantial performance gap; and
(3) we propose ConceptSIR, a reasoning framework that improves model performance while highlighting remaining challenges.
Together, our results suggest that far analogy remains an open problem for contemporary AI systems and that the reasoning pipeline of ConceptSIR offers a promising direction for studying analogical reasoning.

## 2 Related Work

## 2.1 Cognitive Theories of Analogical Reasoning

Although the computational nature of analogy is not yet fully understood, a number of cognitive theories have been proposed to formalize how analogical reasoning operates. Structure-Mapping Theory (SMT) characterizes analogy as a mapping from a source to a target that prioritizes the preservation of relational structure over surface features [1]. SAGE, a later extension of SMT, incorporates frequency information over alignable structures, enabling probabilistic generalization across analogical instances [8]. The Analogical Constraint Mapping Engine (ACME) is a classic computational model that formulates analogical mapping as a process of constraint satisfaction [4].

Among these theories, our ConceptSIR draws particular inspiration from Copycat [3, 2], which involves a Slipnet, a workspace and a population of perceptual agents. Although originally developed for letter-string analogy problems, Copycat has been influential as a general cognitive theory of analogical reasoning. In fact, we believe that ARC-AGI tasks are similar to 2-dim Copycat analogy tasks to some extent: Copycat letter-string analogy tasks primarily focus on letters, numbers and symbols, while ARC-AGI tasks focus on numbers and 2-dim concepts.

Concept Slippage, a core mechanism in Copycat [2, 3], describes how activating one concept can trigger related concepts and higher-level relational structures, thereby expanding the search over possible interpretations.
One task from Copycat theory is shown in Figure 2. Given an example: $a\ b\ c\to a\ b\ d$ , what will $x\ y\ z$ change to? If we assume the alphabet is not cycle, i.e. the next letter of $z$ is not $a$ but nothing, $x\ y\ z$ might change to $x\ y$ , which is obviously not a preferred answer. If we notice that $a\ b\ c$ and $x\ y\ z$ are the beginning and last three letters of the alphabet, the concept “opposite” will be activated in the Slipnet. Therefore, the concept “successor” might slip to the concept “predecessor”, resulting in the answer $w\ y\ z$ . The process of one activated concept causing another related concept is concept slippage.

Figure 2: One task from Copycat theory. In workspace, the task is given as: if $a\ b\ c$ changing to $a\ b\ d$ , then what will $x\ y\ z$ change to? The concept “opposite” activated from $a\ b\ c$ and $x\ y\ z$ in the alphabet will promote the concept “successor” to slip to the concept “predecessor”. This will finally result in the answer $w\ y\ z$ .

## 2.2 Analogical Reasoning Benchmarks and Models

A variety of cognitive tests and benchmarks have been developed to assess analogical reasoning ability, among which Raven’s Progressive Matrices (RPM) are a canonical example and are widely used to measure human fluid intelligence [9, 10]. Other benchmarks, such as the Abstraction and Reasoning Corpus (ARC) [11, 7], place a stronger emphasis on abstraction and analogical reasoning, posing persistent challenges for contemporary artificial intelligence systems. Compared to ARC-AGI-1, ARC-AGI-2 [7] introduces substantially more complex and abstract conceptual compositions, significantly increasing task difficulty. Developed from these two benchmarks, ARC-AGI-3[12] is introduced as an interactive benchmark for studying agentic intelligence from novel environments and in which agents must explore, infer goals, build internal models of environment dynamics, and plan effective action sequences without explicit instructions. Nevertheless, systematic evaluation of far analogical reasoning grounded in real-world concepts remains limited, which motivates us to propose ARCANA.

Among models for analogical reasoning, large language models (LLMs) have emerged as the dominant approach for solving ARC-AGI-1&2, owing to their extensive pretraining and strong general-purpose reasoning capabilities. Beyond LLM-based approaches, alternative methods include train-from-scratch models such as Vision Transformer (ViT)-based picture-to-picture mappings [13], as well as approaches inspired by the Minimal Description Length (MDL) principle [14].
In parallel, several methods have been proposed for analogical reasoning beyond the ARC setting, including vector-based matching approaches that leverage BART embeddings [15], as well as models that integrate encoder–decoder architectures with Structure-Mapping Theory to induce structured correspondences [16].

## 3.1 Dataset Creation

## 3.1.1 Target-concept-target Generation Loop

ARCANA tasks are grounded in diverse real-world objects, concepts, and physical phenomena, which we refer to as targets. Each target can be abstracted into a small set of higher-level concepts that govern the grid transformation. For instance, the “threaded rings" target (shown in Figure 1(c)) can be summarized by three concepts—move, shelter, and 3D—where 3D rings move and partially occlude (“shelter") the column pattern. Once such concepts are extracted from targets, they can be slipped and re-grounded to other targets (e.g., candles burning on a cake, as shown in Figure 8(a)). With this target-concept-target generation loop, we have created several different targets that nevertheless instantiate the similar underlying rules or concepts on ARC grids.

Although some tasks in ARCANA might be mapped from physical phenomena, ARCANA still holds a crucial difference from physical reasoning benchmarks. Many tasks in ARCANA are non-physical, including cat1, shoes1, etc. Even physical inspired tasks are highly abstracted away from real physics: melt1, melt2, melt4 encode conservation of colored cells and a horizontal surface rather than density, or strict fluid dynamics; buoyancy2 concerns grid relations rather than buoyant-force calculation; and even Kepler_second_law_or_area1 can be solved through a grid invariant without knowing Kepler’s law. Therefore, we address that ARCANA evaluates explicit transfer from real-world concepts to abstract grids, and physical phenomena are only one possible source of analogy; the source concept should not be confused with the capability being evaluated.

The target-concept-target generation loop can also be viewed as an explicit process of analogy. We source targets from diverse domains (e.g., water, windmills, flags, and physical laws) and classify tasks into 34 concept categories plus an “other" class. Example concepts include wave propagation, 3D cuboid motion and shadows, fluid flow, state transitions, object-induced rotation, light propagation, and game-rule dynamics. Many tasks are compositional, combining multiple concepts (e.g., motion with rotation).

## 3.1.2 Grounding Targets into Grids

Concept attention.

Bridging targets and discrete ARC grids requires concept attention: emphasizing task-relevant concepts of a target while suppressing concepts that are secondary or irrelevant to the core rule. For example, in the melt target (shown in Figure 3(b)), ice melts only upon contact with hot water (red cells); after melting, both the ice and the hot-water become cold water (blue cells). The final cold-water volume (the number of grid cells) equals the sum of the initial ice volume and hot-water volume. We therefore focus on the melting condition and this discrete conservation rule, while ignoring physical nuances such as density differences across phases and temperatures.

Concept expansion.

Beyond concept attention, we sometimes augment a target with additional concepts (e.g., time) to fully specify its dynamics and complicate the tasks. We refer to this as concept expansion. For instance, in the charge target (shown in Figure 3(c)), green cells (analogous to charge or data) propagate along directed “wires” from a source container at the red terminal to a sink container at the green terminal. At each time step, each wire transfers one cell if the sink container has remaining capacity. Introducing an explicit temporal process (and treating it as effectively continuous) is necessary to correctly solve the test. Overall, concept expansion substantially increases the difficulty and diversity of ARCANA tasks.

Figure 4: Visualization of cat1 task in animal category. The task requires recognizing topology of the true cat, and changing the color of eyes, ears and tail into red.

Generation principles

We construct the benchmark according to four principles that are intentionally simple and human-grounded:
(1) Human-familiar targets. The targets are common and recognizable to humans; given the target, most people who are familiar with it can solve the task accurately with little effort.
(2) Concept-faithful grounding. The ARC grids inherit the target’s core concepts, and the grid-target analogy is intended to be explicit.
(3) Multiple targets, one rule. A task may admit multiple plausible analogical targets, but they must agree on the same underlying transformation rule, such as claw and chopsticks.
(4) Human-aligned correctness. When multiple rules are consistent with the examples, we choose the one most natural to humans, best aligned with everyday concepts, and lowest in cognitive burden.

## 3.2 Discussion on ARCANA Tasks

ARCANA comprises 200 tasks spanning 35 distinct concepts, with each task targeting a unique analogical mapping. We have provided the categorization of ARCANA tasks in Appendix F.

All tasks are named by the abbreviated target from the designer’s perspective followed by a numeric suffix, while different tasks are guaranteed to correspond to different rules. For example, in water_transform category, melt1, melt2 and melt4 tasks correspond to the following rules respectively: (i) ice melts if it touches hot water (as shown in Fig. 8), (ii) ice melts only if sunlight shines on it, and (iii) ice melts only if there is no light-blocking glass above it. These variants test subtly different capabilities. melt3 task is under heat category, the rule of which is that ice cream melts under the sun. Unlike the other three, its solution does not involve water-conservation-style reasoning; therefore, we do not place it in the water_transform category as the others. melt2, melt3, melt4 are shown in Appendix F, Fig 29, Fig 30, Fig 31.

Interesting results have emerged on certain tasks and categories during human study and LLMs testing, which we will discuss later. In this section we would like to clarify the difficulty and characteristics of ARCANA from the tasks themselves.

In the animal category, all tasks require recognition of topological structure, e.g. cat1 task in Fig 4. Such topological recognition is naturally hard to represent in code, which would be challenging for LLMs while easy for human. There are other similar instances in pulley category and 3d-pattern category. We therefore assumed that providing the visualization of the tasks for LLMs testing rather than merely providing the matrices in text might bring about better results in such categories, and accordingly designed the experiment setup in LLMs testing. The results of LLMs testing also confirmed our assumption.

Figure 5: Visualization of Kepler_second_law_or_area1 task in other category. We have provided the answer of the test in the figure. The same contour of each matrix represents the orbit of the planet which should be regarded as an “ellipse”, and the red grid represents the Sun (star). In either input or output matrix, the green “lines” represent the line segments joining the planet and the Sun, indicating the beginning and the end positions of one clockwise rotation. The purple grids represent the area swept out by the line segments, remaining consistent from input to output. All examples are designed to possess a unique solution.

Another difficulty of ARCANA tasks lies in abstraction and analogical reasoning, which is for both humans and LLMs. Some tasks are highly abstract, inheriting such core concepts in higher levels such as number conservation (Kepler_second_law_or_area1 task, lid2 task) and number multiple relationship (Doppler1 task, cuboid_and_shadow3 task). Faithful groundings on ARC grids for such complicated targets appear to be challenging for the representational boundary of the simple input and output matrices and the complicated physical process. Take the grounding of Kepler_second_law_or_area1 task in Fig 5 as an example (Kepler’s second law: a line segment joining a planet and the Sun sweeps out equal areas during equal intervals of time). In each matrix, the same contour should be regarded as an “ellipse” orbit of the planet, and the red grid represents the Sun. In either the input or output matrix, the green “lines” represent the line segments joining the planet and the Sun, indicating the beginning position and the end position of one rotation. The end position of the input is the same as the beginning position of the output. The purple grids represent the area swept out by the line segments. The areas remain consistent from input to output, reflecting the rule of Kepler’s second law. One confusion might be raised from the representations of the “lines”: some of the green lines are irregular in examples 4 and the test, and might result in non-unique answers. When designing this task, we have guaranteed all irregular lines are definitely given in all input matrices, and the lines that should be inferred are all straight (horizontal, vertical, or diagonal). No matter how irregular the lines could be, straight lines are always preferred. Accordingly, all examples and the test are designed to possess corresponding unique answer.

Displaying the Kepler_second_law_or_area1 task here actually requires preparation of being doubted. We believe there will still be many readers considering the task as far-fetched despite the detailed description of our designing approach. Objectively speaking, the ellipses and the oblique lines are hard to represent in matrices due to the representation boundary of the ARC grids, leading to confusion and difficulty in recognizing the target and further successful solutions. In our human study, there was still one and only one out of six participants who successfully solved the task, encouraging us to display the task in this section. From designers’ perspective, we do not consider this task as the best and the most delicate task, but an experimental and a little “far-fetched” one. However, we think the task is the most abstract one in ARCANA and visualize it as an example that indicates the difficulty of ARCANA and the difference between ARCANA and ARC-AGI benchmarks.

The Kepler_second_law_or_area1 task might also be solved if the participants have noticed the conservation rule of the purple area. The process of solving the task could be regarded as the scientific discovering process of discovering Kepler’s second law. Moreover, not all tasks in ARCANA are such abstract and difficult like this, which could also be proved by the results of human study in the next section. There are also tasks requiring meticulous reasoning especially for the tasks with the concepts augmented by time (as discussed in Section 3.1.2). Through this discussion, we wish we had shed light on the difference between ARCANA and ARC-AGI benchmarks and the importance of far analogy.

## 4 Human Study on ARCANA

## 4.1 Experiment Setup

Since solving ARCANA tasks often benefits from recognizing the intended real-world targets, unfamiliarity with the targets can increase failure rates. To reduce such idiosyncratic variance and obtain a more reliable estimate, each task is randomly assigned to at least six participants.
We recruit undergraduate participants via an online platform. In addition to final grid answers, we ask participants to (i) provide a brief one-sentence description of the inferred target, preferably in analogical terms, and (ii) rate the task’s thinking and drawing difficulty on a five-level scale (very easy to very hard), enabling finer-grained analyses of human reasoning. To ensure data validity, participants must pass a tutorial of the online platform and an attention check; submissions from participants who fail the attention check are excluded. After filtering, we obtain 1659 question-level responses from 80 participants.All details are provided in Appendix B.2.

The reported human accuracy may vary across populations, because performance on far-analogy tasks partly depends on familiarity with the targets. To reduce cultural variation, we primarily selected broadly recognizable targets and concepts, although it is not enough to guarantee equal familiarity across populations, even on the participants recruited. Our undergraduate sample was chosen to provide participants with a reasonable level of familiarity with the concepts represented in ARCANA. For example, lower performance among young children or participants unfamiliar with a physical law may reflect limited source-domain knowledge rather than difficulty with analogy itself. Conversely, these participants might perform well on far analogies grounded in domains more familiar to them.

Therefore, the reported accuracy should be interpreted in the context of the sampled undergraduate population and may not directly generalize to others. Nevertheless, the results provide evidence that, when the underlying targets are familiar, humans can successfully perform the type of far analogical mapping tested by ARCANA.

## 4.2 Experiment Analysis

ARCANA is designed from human-centered priors, therefore human performance provides a natural reference point. Moreover, ARCANA tasks may admit multiple plausible targets that nonetheless instantiate the same underlying rule (Principle 3), therefore automated evaluation could be unreliable. We manually evaluate every response. All submissions are categorized into five types: CC (answer Correct, analogy Correct), CW (answer Correct, analogy Wrong), WC (answer Wrong, analogy Correct), WW (answer Wrong, analogy Wrong), and WT (answer Wrong with Typo).
WT captures minor typographical mistakes: the participant expresses the intended rule but leaves one or two erroneous cells/blocks that are typically irrelevant to the core transformation. Although typos exhibit several forms which makes systematic categorization challenging, we still provide a rough taxonomy and concrete examples in the Appendix B.5. If the mistakes affect the rule expression, we instead label the response as WC. Typos should not be considered as “tricks” to “improve” the results of human study as we were criticized before: on the one hand, we always report strict exact-match accuracy without typo handling; on the other hand, the results of Human ( $\geq 2$ ), which is the main evaluation metrics of ARC-AGI-1 and ARC-AGI-2, are not significantly influenced by typos considered.

We acknowledge that all results are evaluated manually by one author ensuring the same criteria were applied consistently. Nevertheless, this may possibly introduce subjective bias and precludes inter-rater agreement analysis. To make our evaluation more persuasive, we conservatively classify the ambiguous cases as incorrect and consistently report strict results without typo handling. Moreover, further analysis on Human ( $\geq 2$ ) and average accuracy (in Appendix C.3) shows typo judgments do not drive the core conclusions.

Following ARC-AGI-2, we report Human ( $\geq 2$ ) accuracy for ARCANA: $93.5\%$ under strict evaluation and $96\%$ under typo-tolerant evaluation, suggesting that most tasks are well-posed. However, conversely speaking, even with typo included there are still 8 out of 200 tasks successfully solved by less than 2 participants, including Kepler_second_law_or_area1 task, wind_and_balloon2 task, etc. Although filtering out these tasks is reasonable (also applied in ARC-AGI-2), whether a task meets this threshold can be affected by participant variability due to the special characteristics of far analogy. For example, if all six participants are not familiar with physics or have forgotten Kepler’s second law (which is supposed to be on the senior high school textbooks), the solving process of the task might become another process of Kepler’s scientific discovering, which requires the ability of analogy on a higher level than solving process of those who are familiar of the physical law. Fortunately, there is still one out of six participants familiar with Kepler’s second law and successfully solved the task. Also, accordingly, this could explain the relatively “low” accuracy of the Human (Average). We therefore retain all tasks to preserve benchmark coverage and diversity.

More details are provided in the Appendix B. Overall, humans achieve $68.96\%$ average accuracy on ARCANA, including $10.61\%$ typo-level matches, while the Human ( $\geq 2$ ) accuracy reaches $96\%$ , including $2.5\%$ typo-level matches. We further observe a moderate positive association between analogy correctness and answer correctness, suggesting that analogy provides a meaningful scaffold for solving ARCANA tasks. Finally, the resulting task-level accuracy and difficulty distributions consistently indicate that ARCANA is broadly solvable for humans and aligned with human reasoning, supporting the validity of the benchmark.

## 5 LLMs Testing on ARCANA

## 5.1 Experiment Setup

We have evaluated all ARCANA tasks on four models: GPT 5.2, Gemini 3 Pro, Claude Opus 4.5, and Grok 4.1 Fast.
Most former LLM methods on ARC-AGI benchmarks employed codes as the solutions, however, according to the discussion of animal category in Section 3.2, we assume that codes might be insufficient to express rules of all ARCANA tasks. Therefore in our LLMs testing, the chosen models are required to directly output the final answer grids. Under this output format, we could assume that LLMs may also produce minor typographical mistakes. Accordingly, all LLM responses are evaluated manually with the same criteria as in the human study, and results are reported under two conventions: counting typos as correct (+Typo) versus as wrong (-Typo). To further improve performance, we employ two prompting strategies: chain-of-thought and analogical prompting. Due to the substantial stochasticity in model outputs, we follow the common practice in ARC evaluations and report pass@3 (top-3). All tests are finished through OpenRouter. Temperature was set to 1. Reasoning effort was not explicitly configured in our OpenRouter requests; therefore, each model used its model-specific default reasoning configuration. In the language-only setting, grids are serialized as numerical matrices with integers representing colors. In the vision-language setting, images are supplied through the standard image interface using Base64 encoding or URLs. Models output the final grid as a numerical matrix in both settings.

Also, the typos of LLMs might be doubted about their validity as those in human study, however there are absolutely no reasons to consider typos as “tricks” to “improve” the results of LLMs (after all we are the designers!). The typos might result from the lack of sufficient tokens or the stochastic output. We aim at exploring the ability for analogy of LLMs to our best and prevent these effects, therefore we have also manually evaluated all answers from LLMs just like humans. ## 5.2 Experiment Analysis

The experimental results are shown in Table 2. Overall, LLM performance on ARCANA is below human performance in Table 2: the pass@3 upper bounds of LLMs remain lower than the pass@1 human (average) and human ( $\geq 2$ ). Nevertheless, the strongest models still solve a non-trivial subset of tasks, suggesting that current LLMs possess meaningful (albeit limited) capability for ARCANA-style problem solving.

Language vs. vision–language prompting.
Generally, vision-language (VL) prompting yields lower performance than language-only prompting for most models (with one notable exception: GPT 5.2 improves under VL when typos are counted as correct). This trend is consistent with prior findings that adding visual inputs can reduce LLM performance [17], potentially due to misalignment between the vision and language modalities. Importantly, however, these aggregate comparisons do not imply that VL is strictly worse. The Total column is higher than either prompting alone, indicating that the two prompting modalities succeed on different subsets of tasks. Category-level inspection also suggests that some concepts benefit substantially from visual grounding (e.g., Gemini on electric_clock category and moon_and_tide1 task, GPT on pendulum category). Together, these observations support our viewpoint that ARCANA is inherently visual in nature, yet not merely visual problems.

Correct analogies support LLMs solving ARCANA.
Under analogical prompting, we collect and manually evaluate model-generated targets, and find that correct analogies tend to co-occur with correct answers, suggesting that analogy can materially support ARCANA task solving. The analogy accuracy could be find in Appendix C.5. We also observe substantial differences in analogical ability across models resulting in different result accuracy. Focusing on the two strongest models (GPT 5.2 and Gemini 3 Pro), the pulley category is particularly diagnostic: Gemini achieves 100% analogy correctness while GPT achieves 0%; correspondingly, Gemini reaches 67% answer accuracy in pulley whereas GPT remains at 0%. Overall, these results suggest that analogical reasoning is a meaningful bottleneck for ARCANA performance.

Human-model gaps and category-wise performance.
A paired task-level bootstrap over the $200$ ARCANA tasks between LLM baselines and humans shows that the $95\%$ bootstrap confidence intervals of LLM-Human differences are strictly below zero under both strict and typo-tolerant evaluation (as shown in Table 3). Even the strongest baseline remains $10.13$ percentage points below humans under strict evaluation and $17.67$ percentage points below under typo-tolerant evaluation. This indicates a statistically reliable gap between current LLMs and human performance on ARCANA.
We further examine performance by task category. Overall, human accuracy is relatively stable across categories, whereas LLM performance is more uneven. Humans outperform LLMs in most categories, but there are also categories in which some models match or exceed the human average (typically those dominated by precise, fine-grained manipulations, e.g., shelter_then_move category). At the same time, several categories expose clear gaps where LLMs lag far behind humans (e.g., water_deep_concept category and animal category), indicating that substantial differences remain between current LLMs and human analogical reasoning.

## 6 ConceptSIR: A Reasoning Framework for Concept Slippage, Introspection, Reflection

Figure 7: An overview of ConceptSIR framework. Given an ARCANA task, the framework first proposes initial concepts from multi-dimensional prompts. These concepts evolve via Concept Slippage to generate a diverse set of candidates, which are then implemented as executable code under Concept Execution. The system iteratively refines solutions through Reflection, employing mechanisms such as code mutation and concept mutation, while Introspection ensures the final prediction on the test question aligns with the established rules and human preference.

Inspired by cognitive theories of analogy-making, especially Hofstadter and Mitchell’s Copycat architecture [2, 3], we propose ConceptSIR, an LLM-driven analogical reasoning framework built around Concept Slippage, Introspection, and Reflection. Following Copycat, where Workspace structures activate Slipnet concepts that may slip to related concepts and trigger Codelets, ConceptSIR treats the LLM’s knowledge as an implicit Slipnet, context-conditioned generation as concept slippage, and concept execution as Codelets. Introspection and reflection then use execution feedback to iteratively refine conceptual hypotheses and executable rules. The framework is shown in Figure 7. Additional details are provided in Appendix D.

## 6.1 Concept Proposal and Slippage

Motivated by our human study and the breadth of LLM pretraining, we introduce a concept proposal and slippage stage to elicit diverse analogical targets. We first prompt candidates along four human-prior dimensions—color, shape, dynamics, and holistic recognition—and then use a slippage prompt to expand them into a broader concept neighborhood. We assume that it is particularly useful for LLMs, whose outputs can be overly context-dependent; increasing the diversity of candidate targets helps avoid getting trapped in a “local minima” of concepts. From a Bayesian perspective, Copycat-style slippage can be viewed as combining conceptual priors with task evidence: the Slipnet encodes prior knowledge, while structures activated in the Workspace provide likelihood-like constraints on which concepts become relevant. Analogously, LLMs generate new concepts conditioned on both knowledge learned from pretraining and the given task context. Thus, ConceptSIR implements concept slippage as an additional prompting operation that leverages LLMs’ context-conditioned generation to activate a more diverse set of related concepts.

## 6.2 Concept Execution

To reliably apply an inferred rule underlying all given demonstrations to the question grid, we represent the execution step as code rather than natural language. Natural-language rules are often ambiguous or internally inconsistent, making it difficult to ensure faithful and deterministic application to grids. Using code provides an explicit, verifiable realization of a candidate rule and is broadly consistent with prior ARC solvers. Concretely, for each candidate target produced by the slippage stage, we prompt the LLM to synthesize a corresponding program as one possible instantiation of the target’s rule, which is then executed and checked against the examples.

A contradiction might lie in the fact that the rules of some ARCANA tasks are difficult to represent in code. In ConceptSIR, we applied a relatively consistent form of expression but not a general one. The form of concept execution could be extended by atomic grid operations, providing a more general concept execution.

## 6.3 Introspection

Introspection iteratively aligns the model’s analogical interpretation and its execution with human cognition (Principle 4). Some ARC tasks admit multiple rules consistent with the demonstrations, but humans typically favor one interpretation as the intended solution with the lowest cognitive burden. For example, in a clock task, both “rotate the pointer on the right dial by $90^{\circ}$ ” and “rotate the hour hand by $90^{\circ}$ ” may fit all examples, yet the latter is more natural because “hour hand" composes more directly with the concept of a “clock".
After each code update, we run the program on the examples and questions, using introspection to identify abnormal behaviors and producing targeted correction instructions; once all examples are matched, we perform a final introspection pass on the question output to filter invalid or human-misaligned solutions.

## 6.4 Reflection

Inspired by genetic algorithms [18], we perform two levels of probabilistic mutations in parallel: code mutation and concept mutation.

At the code level, mutation primarily focuses on implementation techniques and the handling of special or edge cases. We calculate the discrepancy between the code outputs and the ground truth on example inputs, identifying the code with the smallest error as the optimal candidate. We then employ LLMs to analyze the strengths and weaknesses of this optimal code. For all other code candidates, mutation is triggered probabilistically during modification. Upon code mutation, the LLM is tasked with analyzing the pros and cons of its current code and combining them with the insights from the optimal code to propose improvements, combining the strengths of both while discarding their weaknesses. When humans attempt to solve a problem, they often generate multiple candidate solutions and then iteratively refine them by retaining what works and discarding what does not. This observation naturally motivated our idea of code mutation.

At the concept level, mutation includes broader concept slippage and finer-grained concept augmentation. Concept slippage is aligned with the process in Section 6.1, with the purpose of generating more diverse results and preventing the model from becoming trapped in cognitive corners. While concept augmentation is to complement a primary concept with its associated subconcepts, thereby facilitating more effective rule implementation (e.g., adding a “pile-up" concept on top of “gravity" for bead_chain1 task). Together, these mechanisms promote both diversity and implementability of candidate solutions.

## 7 Experimental results of ConceptSIR

## 7.1 Quantitative Analysis on ConceptSIR

Based on stage-wise observations, we use Gemini 3 Pro for concept proposal, concept slippage and introspection, and GPT 5.2 for code-centric generation and refinement. The design is heuristically based on preliminary analogy experiments and our LLM results (we found that Gemini performs best in the concept proposal and slippage stages, while GPT excels in code-centric processes). We tried multiple configurations (e.g., GPT-only vs. GPT+Gemini), while acknowledging substantial room for improvement.

We conduct a focused evaluation of ConceptSIR on a deliberately challenging subset of ARCANA in order to fully explore the capability of each modules. The subset consists of $17$ tasks from $11$ categories, many of which are unsolved or only rarely solved by direct LLM testing in Section 5. These tasks were randomly sampled with a preference for tasks with low direct-LLM accuracy. Under a strict exact-match setting with one run per task and a budget of $5$ loops, ConceptSIR solves $9/17$ tasks across $7$ categories, requiring only $2.22$ loops on average for successful cases. These results suggest that the structured cycle of concept proposal, slippage, execution, and refinement can help LLMs go beyond direct prompting, both enabling solutions on difficult tasks and improving proposal efficiency in several cases.

## 7.2 Qualitative Analysis on ConceptSIR

We clarify that ConceptSIR is not a general framework due to the code execution, prediction-error-dependent mutation, and the preliminary pipeline. Nevertheless, the qualitative result analysis shows that the modules in ConceptSIR might be critical in leading LLMs to the correct answer. Furthermore, the results also shed light on the shortages of ConceptSIR or even the LLMs.

Concept slippage contributes to program generation.
We study tasks where failure is caused primarily by selecting an incorrect analogical target, e.g., refraction2 task and bead_chain1 task. In ConceptSIR, the concept proposal stage elicits multiple candidate targets, and concept slippage diversifies and refines them, enabling Gemini to recover the intended interpretations (“refraction” for refraction2 task and “beads falling into a container” for bead_chain1 task). Conditioned on these targets, GPT solves refraction2 task in two reflection steps and produces two correct programs for bead_chain1 task within three steps. In contrast, refraction2 task cannot be solved by all the LLM baselines, while bead_chain1 task is solved only once by GPT 5.2 across 12 attempts.

Introspection and reflection contribute to program modification.
In our case studies, we find that introspection and reflection play a critical role in refining candidate programs into correct and generalizable solutions. For the bead_chain1 task, the target concept of “beads falling into a container” is under-specified; reflection introduces an implicit discrete packing constraint (e.g., “bays behave like discrete parking slots”), which captures the pile-up mechanism required to implement the rule. For candle1 task, none of the LLM baselines succeeded, while human participants solved $5/6$ instances when typos are counted as correct. Even with the target concept provided, candidate programs can fit all training examples but fail on the test case, e.g., by deleting candle structures or producing inconsistent shadows. Introspection detects these failure modes and provides targeted correction instructions, while reflection revises the program without breaking consistency on the examples. In this case, reflection reveals that the candle should be “burned down” by a fixed number of cells, rather than simply shifting an orange segment downward.

LLMs perform analogy depending both on images and matrices. In our LLM baseline tests, Gemini 3 Pro have performed a correct analogy and found out the ratio on pulley3 task with VL prompt. However, in ConceptSIR experiments, when we only provided the visualization of the task, Gemini 3 Pro failed to perform a correct analogy, resulting in false code generation. After we added the matrices, Gemini 3 Pro succeeded to generate the target “pulley”, even though there still exists problems in code generation. The observations above support the assumption that LLMs perform analogy depending on both images and matrices.

LLMs tend to be trapped in a “local minima”. In a preliminary test with GPT 5, we have tested bead_chain1 task and set the budget of the loops to 15 (which is already solved by GPT 5.2 within 3 steps). In the 15 loops, GPT 5 appeared to re-modify the same locations and fail to notice the concept “pile up”. The phenomenon is the same as the observations on the CL-bench [19]: LLMs demonstrate partial compliance by correctly handling explicit and surface-level requirements, yet systematically fail when tasks demand deeper reasoning. This phenomenon might be eliminated with stronger LLMs (e.g., ConceptSIR with GPT 5.2 has already succeeded to generate the correct answers), however, we still hold the bold view that the “local minima” or the “context ignored” exist in SOTA LLMs, possibly originating from the design of ConceptSIR or even the incomplete world model of transformer itself.

## 8 Limitations

ARCANA is currently limited in scale and representation. When we constructed ARCANA, we have tried our best to distinguish each task from each other, i.e., similar tasks could be generated from existing tasks to expand the benchmark. We mainly focus on the diversity but not the quantity of ARCANA, while leaving scaling up an unsettled problem for the community. Moreover, although ARCANA covers diverse targets, the space of real-world concepts and analogical sources is effectively unbounded, making larger benchmarks difficult without community effort. We view ARCANA as a starting point for such expansion. Furthermore, ARC-style grid abstraction provides conciseness, precision, and cognitive challenge, but limits expressivity for structures such as oblique lines, circles, and ellipses (e.g., the ellipses of Kepler_second_law_or_area1 task shown in Figure 5 are not strictly faithful expressions), motivating richer representations for future far-analogy benchmarks.

We also note several limitations of ConceptSIR. Future work should evaluate it on a larger set of ARCANA tasks beyond the current challenging subset. Although code execution offers a faithful and reproducible way to apply hypothesized rules, it is not fully expressive for all ARCANA targets, especially topology-heavy ones (e.g., animal category); extending the executor to atomic grid operations with explicit where-to-apply instructions, or expanding the framework to a true agent which could judge output the answer as code or direct matrix may improve generality. Moreover, our code-mutation procedure selects the “best” candidate solely by prediction error, which is a rigid proxy for solution quality. Additional modules, such as explicit memory or human feedback, may further improve robustness and generalization. More limitations are also mentioned in Section 7.

## 9 Conclusion

We introduce ARCANA, a benchmark for evaluating far analogical reasoning, and analyze both human and LLM performance. We further propose ConceptSIR, an LLM-driven framework based on concept slippage, introspection, and reflection, showing that it can solve tasks missed by direct LLM baselines, while also leaving substantial room for improvement. We hope ARCANA and ConceptSIR help establish far analogy as an important frontier for more human-like, general reasoning.

## Appendix A Motivation of ARCANA

The grid format is following Chollet’s motivation [11]: grids provide a concise, precise, and cognitively challenging format for evaluating abstraction and reasoning, as we stated in Section 8. We have consistently acknowledged ARC-AGI as an important inspiration for ARCANA. ## B.1 Quantification details.

Excluding typo responses, we compute the Pearson coefficient $\phi$ for a $2\times 2$ contingency table of answer correctness versus analogy correctness:

 $\phi=\frac{P_{CC}-P_{CA}P_{AC}}{\sqrt{P_{CA}(1-P_{CA})P_{AC}(1-P_{AC})}},$ 

(1)

where $CA$ denotes all correct answers, $AC$ denotes all analogy-correct answers, and $P$ denotes proportions in the filtered set. In our data, $\phi=0.391$ . A chi-square test derived from $\chi^{2}(1)=n\phi^{2}$ yields $\chi^{2}(1)\approx 226.9$ with $p\ll 0.001$ . We also report an odds ratio of $OR\approx 25.6$ (95% CI $\approx[14.3,45.8]$ ) and Yule’s $Q\approx 0.93$ .

## B.3 Analysis details.

All responses are evaluated by one author using consistent criteria, and we acknowledge the possibility of subjective annotation bias. Inter-rater agreement could not be measured. An analogy description is marked correct if it captures the core transformation rule; responses that do not express the core rule, including “I do not know,” are marked incorrect. Ambiguous cases and essential errors are conservatively classified as incorrect, and we also report strict exact-match accuracy without typo handling.

Overall, humans achieve $68.96\%$ average accuracy on ARCANA, including $10.61\%$ typo-level matches, while the Human ( $\geq 2$ humans) accuracy reaches $96\%$ , including $2.5\%$ typo-level matches. The results on Human ( $\geq 2$ humans) show that typo judgments do not drive the conclusions of ARCANA’s validity. In Figure 6(a), each histogram bin aggregates tasks by their per-task accuracy (binned in $10\%$ intervals).
For subjective difficulty, participants separately rate thinking and drawing difficulty using a five-level scale (very easy, easy, medium, hard, very hard), which we map to scores $1$ - $5$ . For each task, we average the two scores to obtain a single difficulty value. Figure 6(b) plots each task as one point and overlays fitted trend lines under the two Typo conventions.

## B.4 ARCANA tasks have only one and clear ground truth.

In ARCANA, the ground truth is designed with the deterministic grid-level transformation rule for each task. In our human study, we provided participants with the ground-truth solutions after testing received no feedback suggesting that the answers were ambiguous. When some instances feel “unclear” to participants during testing , we believe this is largely driven by variability in far-analogy mapping (i.e., differences in concept familiarity and in identifying the intended target concept) rather than ambiguity in the grid-level rule itself. The observed accuracy primarily reflects participants’ capabilities.

In alignment with the construction principles of ARCANA, we will explain the ARCANA evaluation methods in detail.

"Multiple plausible analogical targets" do not imply multiple valid grid outputs.

In the paper we mainly refer to the "multiple plausible analogical targets" but not "solutions". We mean that different participants may describe the same task using different analogical targets, while still implying the same underlying grid-level transformation rule (Construction Principle 3: multiple targets, one rule). For example, in cat1 task, some participants described the patterns as "cats", while others described them as "little devils". These different targets can still yield the same correct grid transformation, and we treat both target descriptions as plausible as long as they faithfully reflect the intended rule.

For the grid answers, ARCANA is designed to have a unique ground-truth output under strict evaluation.

For every task, we aim to ensure a single intended grid-level solution. Any designer-perceived "alternative" rules that might appear plausible are excluded by adding or modifying examples that serve as counter-evidence, so that only one rule remains consistent with the examples. While a single designer cannot guarantee absolute completeness, we made a strong effort to consider plausible alternatives and add counter-evidence examples where needed. Additionally, after the human study we provided participants with the ground-truth solutions, and we received no feedback indicating that any answers were ambiguous.

When multiple rules fit the examples, we select the human-aligned one—this is different from accepting multiple outputs.

The issue of "multiple rules consistent with the examples" (Construction Principle 4: Human-aligned correctness) is closer to the cases where multiple rules might fit all the examples. In such cases, we resolve the under-specification during dataset construction by selecting the rule that is most natural to humans and best aligned with everyday concepts (i.e., lowest in cognitive burden), rather than treating multiple grid outputs as valid at evaluation time. For instance, in clock1 task, both "rotate the pointer on the right dial by $90^{\circ}"$ and "rotate the hour hand by $90^{\circ}$ " may fit all examples, yet we select the latter as more natural and concept-aligned (as noted in the appendix). This motivation also informs why ConceptSIR includes an introspection step.

We do NOT accept unexpected grid solutions as correct.

Accordingly, grid answers are scored by strict exact-match against the single ground-truth output. If we ever observe a non-matching output that seems plausibly valid, we treat it as evidence that the task is under-specified and would revise the task by adding/modifying examples and re-testing, rather than accepting multiple outputs at evaluation time. In contrast, for analogy targets, multiple descriptions are acceptable as long as they correctly reflect the task rule.

Thus, human judgment is used only for typo annotation (and ambiguous cases are counted as incorrect), not for validating alternative grid-level solutions or as a "trick" to make the accuracy results seem better. If multiple outputs appear valid, we treat it as a dataset issue to be fixed rather than an evaluation-time exception.

## C.2 LLMs results and baseline prompts

While chain-of-thought prompts might be easy to understand, analogical prompts actually include the prompt about associating the task with real-world objects or phenomena which is targets. Beyond the two high-level prompting setups reported in Table 2 (i.e., CoT (language) and CoT, ana (VL)), we additionally use two prompt variants within each setup. For each variant, we compute pass@3 and obtain a set of tasks solved by that variant. The reported score for a setup is then computed from the union of these task-level success sets (i.e., a task is counted as solved if it is solved by either prompt variant under pass@3). We also observe occasional “truncated" outputs (the matrix is cut off mid-generation) and formatting errors (e.g., mismatched brackets). Cost and token statistics include all attempts, including failures.

Because our evaluation uses a fixed number of trials (pass@3) under specific prompting setups, the reported accuracies should be viewed as an empirical upper bound under our testing budget rather than an intrinsic ceiling of each model. Differences in cost per task can also affect how aggressively one can sample, refine, or verify outputs, further entangling performance with budget. In addition, run-to-run variability is substantial; therefore, the analysis that follows is intended to surface plausible tendencies and failure modes observed in our runs, not to claim universal properties of the models.

## C.5 Differences between language and vision-language prompting

To localize modality effects, we further inspect which task categories are solved under each prompting modality for Gemini and GPT. While most categories exhibit only small differences, we identify several cases with large modality gaps. In the electric_clock category, Gemini achieves 0% accuracy under language-only prompting but 100% under vision–language prompting. Similarly, in the pendulum category, GPT improves from 0% under language-only prompting to 75% under vision–language prompting. For moon_and_tide1, Gemini attains 100% under vision–language prompting but fails under language-only prompting, and its successful response explicitly attributes the pattern to lunar-phase variation.

Conversely, we also observe categories where performance decreases after introducing vision-language prompting. These decreases do not follow a consistent pattern across Gemini and GPT, suggesting that the impact of visual inputs is model-dependent and intertwined with output stochasticity, rather than reflecting a uniform modality effect.

A closer inspection of correct analogy responses suggests systematic differences between GPT 5.2 and Gemini 3 Pro. We reiterate that LLM outputs are highly random; our observations are based on six attempts per task and should be interpreted as descriptive hypotheses rather than deterministic conclusions.

Case studies. For moon_and_tide1, Gemini explicitly identifies “the lunar cycle and its effect on tides,” whereas GPT’s best response remains at a surface-level grid description (e.g., “Yellow controls a 2-row tide shift of the blue sea”). For newton_cradle1 and newton_cradle2, Gemini readily recognizes the intended physical concept, while GPT tends to produce less grounded interpretations (e.g., “Pipe insertion with opposite-side ejection”). Despite these failures, GPT can occasionally produce high-quality analogies: for candy_crush1, it generates a correct match-3 interpretation (“swap, clear lines, gravity, cascade”). We also observe that Gemini frequently uses terms such as “system” and “mechanic,” whereas these words are absent in GPT’s analogy responses, indicating model-specific analogical styles and domain biases.

Other models. Finally, we briefly examine Grok and Claude. Given the stochasticity of LLM outputs, these explanations should be interpreted as hypotheses grounded in our observations.

## C.8 Paired bootstrap comparison between humans and LLMs.

We further compare LLM baselines with humans using a paired task-level bootstrap over the $200$ ARCANA tasks. For each task, we compare the model’s binary solve outcome with the human per-task accuracy and compute the mean paired difference. Across all four LLMs, the $95\%$ bootstrap confidence intervals of LLM–Human differences are strictly below zero under both strict and typo-tolerant evaluation; even the strongest baseline remains $10.13$ percentage points below humans under strict evaluation and $17.67$ percentage points below under typo-tolerant evaluation. This indicates a statistically reliable gap between current LLMs and human performance on ARCANA.

We compare each LLM baseline with humans using a paired task-level bootstrap over the $200$ ARCANA tasks. For task $i$ , let $m_{i}\in\{0,1\}$ denote whether the LLM solves the task, and let $h_{i}$ denote the human per-task accuracy. We compute the paired difference $\Delta=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{i}(m_{i}-h_{i})$ and estimate $95\%$ confidence intervals by resampling tasks with replacement. Negative values indicate that the LLM underperforms humans.

## Appendix D Implementation details of ConceptSIR

## D.1 Framework details

Based on stage-wise observations, we use Gemini 3 Pro for concept proposal and slippage, and GPT 5.2 for code-centric generation and refinement. The design is heuristically based on preliminary analogy experiments and our LLM results (we found that Gemini performs best in the concept proposal and slippage stages, while GPT excels in code-centric processes). We also have tried multiple configurations (e.g., GPT-only vs. GPT+Gemini), while acknowledging substantial room for improvement.

Concept proposal and slippage details.

Our initial experiments suggest that LLM reasoning is highly sensitive to prompt context, which can reduce analogical diversity and hinder ARCANA solving. Concept Slippage provides a mechanism for counteracting this rigidity: by explicitly activating multiple candidate concepts, the model is encouraged to traverse higher-level relational abstractions and recruit a broader set of associated concepts.

Given the scale of modern LLMs, it is plausible that their training data covers many everyday targets relevant to ARCANA. From a Bayesian perspective, we further hypothesize that LLM neuron activations can be viewed as a (possibly shallower) analogue of Concept Slippage in humans. We therefore augment prompts with diverse analogy candidates to better exploit the combinational creativity of LLMs and to activate a wider range of concepts in their latent knowledge base. The four proposal dimensions (color/shape/dynamics/holistic recognition) reflect common human priors, but other dimensions can be substituted depending on the problem domain.

Concept execution details.

We treat code as a commitment to an operational semantics: it forces the model to specify edge-case handling and prevents post-hoc reinterpretation that is easy in free-form text. In practice, we generate one candidate program per target and evaluate it by executing on the example inputs and comparing outputs with ground truth; discrepancies are then passed to the subsequent reflection and introspection stages.

Introspection details.

We view human preference as arising from aesthetic and cognitive priors (e.g., simplicity, symmetry, and conceptual coherence). Introspection serves as a mechanism for detecting when a program matches demonstrations for the “wrong reasons” (overly ad hoc predicates or unnatural object choices) and steering revisions toward more coherent, human-aligned abstractions.

Reflection details.

We treat reflection as a family of code-modification operators, including syntax fixes and logic-level refinements, aimed at better instantiating the analogical rule. At each reflection step, we update multiple candidate programs in parallel. For each candidate, we compute its discrepancy on the examples (between predicted and ground-truth outputs) and select the lowest-discrepancy program as the current best. The LLM is then prompted to analyze the strengths and weaknesses of this best program; when updating the remaining candidates, we trigger code mutation with a fixed probability, asking the LLM to combine useful implementation patterns from the best program with the candidate’s own partial strengths.

At the concept level, we similarly introduce mutations over analogical targets. Concept slippage proposes larger-variance alternative targets to avoid narrow local minima. Concept augmentation expands a target by attaching relevant subconcepts that may be essential to the core transformation; for example, in bead_chain1 task, gravity alone is insufficient without an additional stacking/pile-up constraint to reproduce the packed final configuration.

Our framework is more similar to an agent, and we aim to shed light on the effectiveness of our framework based on the resulting reasoning trajectories. We also seriously state that our framework leaves a lot space for improvement, for example, concept execution form could be changed from code to other more robust forms, additional explicit memory modules could be added in order to conclude the “experience" during concept proposal (more effective prompting aspects), more aligned rule with human (more human-aligned introspection), human feedback for better alignment, and so on.

Moreover, on the assumption that analogical reasoning could benefit to most of problems solving, we believe our framework could be generalized to common analogical reasoning problems, further common tasks, since former work shows analogical prompting could improve the performance of models [20]. The concept proposal could be generalized to multi-modalities, such as vision and auditory, for handling more generalized problems (e.g., associating the music heard before for the sentiments in the past scenario). Execution can be generalized to more general forms like motion sequences and natural language. All of the above assumptions are just a beginning, and we believe there remains a lot aspects for exploration.

## D.2 Theoretical grounding: Copycat inspiration for ConceptSIR.

ConceptSIR is explicitly inspired by Copycat. In Copycat theory by Douglas R. Hofstadter and Melanie Mitchell, task-specific structures in the Workspace activate concept nodes in the Slipnet; activation can "slip" probabilistically to nearby concepts, which in turn triggers Codelets that operate in the Workspace to build/modify structures and relations on the task, further updating activations in an iterative loop.

ConceptSIR follows this spirit: the role of the Slipnet is played by the LLM’s prior knowledge; concept slippage occurs implicitly within the LLM conditioned on context; and the analog of Codelets is our executable concept instantiation (code execution). Mutation provides a mechanism to iteratively refine both concept proposals and executable rules based on feedback.

From a Bayesian perspective, concept slippage is grounded in the prior knowledge and the concepts activated by the task in the Copycat theory, which is loosely analogous to the prior and the likelihood in Bayesian theory. Similarly, LLMs generate new concepts conditioned on the knowledge learned from training data and the task contexts. We believe these two mechanisms could be analogous to each other at a high level, which makes using LLMs for concept slippage a practical instantiation.

## Appendix E Unveiling the Reasoning Dynamics of ConceptSIR

In this section we will show the detailed results of ConceptSIR. The results include the output grid in visual and the response of LLMs. All tests are finished through Openrouter.

## E.1 Quantitative analysis.

In our experiments, the “small subset” refers to 17 ARCANA tasks spanning 11 categories. Among these 17 tasks, 10 were solved by none of the four LLMs (0% accuracy for GPT 5.2, Gemini 3 Pro, Grok 4.1 Fast, Claude Opus 4.5), and 2 had low accuracy for the same LLMs. For each task, we ran ConceptSIR with a budget of 5 loops and one run per task, and we counted success only under strict exact-match correctness (no typo handling).

The tasks are: bead_chain1, candle1, clock1, clock4, heat_water1, refraction2, tetris1, snake1, tip1, water_and_trench1, pulley3, cat1, Kepler_second_law_or_area1, plastic_bag1, communicating_vessels1, shoes1, and basket1.

Under this setting, ConceptSIR solved 9/17 tasks, covering 7 categories. Among the solved tasks, the average number of loops used until success was 2.22. Notably, candle1 and refraction2 are among the 10 tasks with 0% accuracy in Table 1, yet were solved by ConceptSIR under strict evaluation. Additionally, bead_chain1 and water_and_trench1 are tasks where only GPT-5.2 (among the four LLMs in Table 1) succeeded, while ConceptSIR also succeeded. The remaining 5 solved tasks are ones that were also solved by both GPT-5.2 and Gemini 3 Pro. Beyond solve/not-solve outcomes, we also observed that ConceptSIR improves proposal efficiency on several tasks. For example, in our LLM tests, GPT-5.2 solved bead_chain1 once within 12 proposals, whereas ConceptSIR solved it twice within 2 loops (9 proposals in total).We additionally provide the accuracy of direct LLMs and human on this subset in Table 7.

## E.2 Qualitative analysis.

Here we take three tasks as examples, which are refraction2, bead_chain1, candle1. Restatement: refraction2 and candle1 are not solved by all LLM baselines, and bead_chain1 is solved once in 12 attempts by only GPT 5.2. In ConceptSIR, refraction2 is solved in 2 reflection steps, candle1 is solved in 6 reflection steps. Noting that among the three codes generated by Concept execution stage, two are correct to solve task bead_chain1 in 3 reflection steps.

As for task refraction2 in baseline testing, GPT 5.2 generates the targets ambiguously, like “light bouncing and slow down". In ConceptSIR, Gemini 3 Pro generate a more accurate target as “Light-ray / ray-tracing in a room with different materials", which further contributes to the correct program and output from GPT 5.2. The reasoning trajectory is shown in Figure 25.

Figure 25: The reasoning trajectory of refraction2. Detailed concept contributes to the correct program generation and output.

Furthermore, we analyze the reasoning trajectory on bead_chain1. We note that during baseline testing, GPT 5.2 extensively uses the word “flower", e.g., “Flowers below an orange knot are replanted into the brown garden beds", “Transplanting plants into planter boxes". Noting that the targets cannot be viewed as incorrect ones, they are still different from the targets from Gemini 3 Pro (e.g., “The task is analogous to ‘beads sliding down strings’.") that are more similar to humans. In concept proposal, Gemini 3 Pro propose four different targets from color, shape, dynamics, holistic recognition, and generate three targets through concept slippage. The trajectory of concept proposal and slippage can be viewed in Figure 26.

Figure 26: The concept proposal and slippage trajectory of bead_chain1. Concept proposal and slippage contribute better targets.

Moreover, we analyze the reasoning trajectory on candle1. First, we found in step 5, GPT 5.2 generates a code that solves all of the examples, but fails on the question. The code deletes all of the candles, which is absolutely not correct. Accordingly, during the introspection, LLMs response indicates that: Missing Objects: In the "example output" generated by the code, the candles are completely missing; Missing Effects (Shadows): The code output is almost entirely yellow, meaning it considers every empty pixel to be illuminated.". Introspection effectively filter the invalid output, and finally the code is refined to generate the correct answer. The introspection could be viewed in Figure 27.

Figure 27: The introspection trajectory of candle1. Introspection filter invalid output.

During testing on ConceptSIR, we notice that the analogy accuracy of Gemini drops. We assume that during baseline testing, Gemini 3 Pro analyzes the grids in detail and notices the relations (e.g., multiple relationship) on grids, and associates it to more complicated concepts. This indicates the potential increase space for concept proposal and slippage.

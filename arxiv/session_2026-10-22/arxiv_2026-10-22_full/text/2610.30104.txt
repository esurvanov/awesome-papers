## BatchBench: Benchmarking AI Robustness to Batch Effects and Domain Shift in Biomedical Images 

– A JUMP Cell Painting Leaderboard

Abstract

Despite recent advances, current AI systems remain highly susceptible to domain shifts, particularly in biomedical imaging, where images are usually acquired under substantially different experimental conditions. Although recent domain adaptation methods have made substantial progress, significant performance gaps under domain shift persist. To measure progress in this regard, we introduce BatchBench, a leaderboard that evaluates and compares the robustness of AI systems on biomedical images from unseen experimental batches and data sources. BatchBench is built on JUMP-CP, one of the largest publicly available biomedical imaging datasets. Generated using the Cell Painting assay, JUMP-CP exhibits pronounced batch effects and domain shifts, making it a challenging testbed for evaluating robustness to distributional changes.

Keywords: biomedical imaging, domain-adaptation, test-time-adaptation, cell-painting, jump-cp, microscopy, distribution-shift

## Vulnerability of AI systems under domain shift and batch effects.

Deep learning methods often assume that training and test data are sampled from the same underlying distribution. However, in practice, biological images are acquired across experimental batches, plates, and sites, which introduces non-biological variation known as batch effects. These effects shift the distribution of the input images, such that a model that performs well on its training distribution can fail on images from a new experimental batch or laboratory. This limits
the use of AI in applications such as drug discovery, where new data
are generated continuously and under changing conditions.

## JUMP-CP and CellPainting.

High-content screening data obtained with the Cell Painting assay 1 provide a clear example of this problem. The JUMP Cell Painting dataset (JUMP-CP) 2, one of the largest public biomedical imaging datasets, was obtained from 13 data-generating centers (sources) using different microscopes and laboratory workflows. As a result, JUMP-CP exhibits substantial batch effects across multiple levels of its data hierarchy. Because every image is annotated with its source, experimental batch, and plate, JUMP-CP provides a exceptional setting in which domain shifts are both pronounced and explicitly annotated at multiple levels.

## Domain adaptation (DA)

methods address domain shift either by learning
domain-invariant representations during training or by adapting the
model to unlabeled target data at inference time, i.e. test-time adaptation (TTA) 9, 8, 3, 5, 11, 7. These methods have substantially improved robustness on natural
images. Nevertheless, their effectiveness on biomedical images from unseen experimental conditionsremains poorly characterized, and results reported on different subsets, splits, and metrics are rarely
comparable.

## The BatchBench Leaderboard.

We introduce the BatchBench leaderboard, an open benchmark for comparing AI models on the identification of chemical perturbations in Cell Painting images acquired from sources not seen during training. The leaderboard is designed to be used by several research communities:

• 

Domain adaptation and test-time adaptation. BatchBench evaluates multi-source adaptation to unseen target domains under combined covariate and label shift. Methods that adapt to unlabeled test images, as well as methods that do not perform test-time adaptation, can be submitted through a common \codeadapt()/\codepredict() interface.

• 

Biomedical imaging and foundation models. BatchBench evaluates whether pretrained image encoders and specialized architectures generalize to unseen imaging sites, an important requirement for deployment that is not captured by in-distribution evaluation.

• 

Cell Painting and JUMP-CP.
BatchBench provides a standardized source-level evaluation of image-based compound profiling on JUMP-CP using fixed splits and a private test set.

The leaderboard presents three components: (i) JUMP-CP-2k, a
curated subset of JUMP-CP covering 2,000 compounds, with nine public
sources and four sources held out as a private test set
(Section 2); (ii) a containerized
submission protocol that keeps the test data private and supports
both conventional and test-time adaptation methods
(Section 3); and (iii) six baseline entries
covering standard supervised training, domain-invariant training,
test-time adaptation, and meta-learning (Sections 5
and 6). The baseline entries show that test-time
adaptation reduces the domain gap but does not close it. For compounds
with few training images, every current entry remains close to chance
level. The leaderboard is open for submissions on
Hugging
Face.

## 2 The BatchBench leaderboard

## Problem setting.

Let $\mathcal{D}^{(1)},\ldots,\mathcal{D}^{(S)}$ denote labeled
data from $S$ training sources, where each sample consists of an
image $\boldsymbol{x}$ , its compound label $y\in\{1,\ldots,K\}$ ,
and its domain metadata $\boldsymbol{d}=(\text{source},\text{batch},\text{plate})$ . At test time, a submission receives the
unlabeled images $\{\boldsymbol{x}_{n}\}_{n=1}^{N}$ and their metadata
 $\{\boldsymbol{d}_{n}\}_{n=1}^{N}$ from sources that do not occur in the
training data. It may adapt to these images before predicting their
labels. The leaderboard thus evaluates multi-source domain
adaptation to unseen target domains. The setting covers domain
generalization (no adaptation), test-time adaptation, and in-context
adaptation as special cases. As the the domain metadata are
hierarchical, submissions can define domains at the level of
sources, experimental batches, or plates.

## Task.

Each input is a single five-channel Cell Painting image (DNA, RNA, ER,
AGP, and Mito). The task is to identify which of the $K=2{,}000$ 
compounds the imaged cells were treated with. Test images come
exclusively from held-out sources, so every test image also originates
from an unseen experimental batch and an unseen plate.

## Compound selection and sampling.

JUMP-CP comprises images from 13 sources and more than 100,000 compound
and genetic perturbations 2. We used the dataset cpg0016 2, available from the Cell Painting Gallery 10 on the Registry of Open Data on AWS (https://registry.opendata.aws/cellpainting-gallery/). To obtain a dataset that is
practical to download and train on, we selected the 2,000 most frequent
compounds, retained all wells treated with these compounds, and sampled
one image (field of view) per well uniformly at random.

## Domain metadata and controls.

Each sample is annotated with its source, experimental batch, and plate
identifiers. Submissions may use these identifiers as domain labels
during training and to group test images at inference. Images of
negative-control wells (DMSO-treated, unperturbed cells) from the
training sources are provided as a separate file. They can be used,
for example, for control-based normalization 7.

## Preprocessing.

From each original image we extract a $500\times 500$ pixel center crop.
We then apply illumination correction by clipping the top 0.01% of
pixel intensities in each channel.

## Splits.

Sources 4, 6, 9, and 13 form the private test set of the leaderboard
and are never released (Figure 1). The
remaining nine sources are public. Source 2 serves as the validation
set for model selection, and the other eight sources form the training
set.

## Evaluation subsets.

Compounds differ greatly in their number of training images and in the
strength of their phenotypes. In addition to the overall accuracy, the
leaderboard therefore reports accuracy on three subsets of
the test set:

• 

POSCON: the eight positive-control compounds, present
on every plate, usually at fixed well positions. They have many
training images and strong,
well-characterized phenotypes.

• 

TARGET: 293 compounds from the JUMP-Target
collection, a reference library chosen to cover diverse biological
targets and mechanisms of action.

• 

REST: all remaining compounds, each represented by
few images (about 12 per compound in the public split).

The three subsets correspond to increasingly difficult regimes:
abundant data with strong phenotypes (POSCON), a curated
mechanism-of-action reference set (TARGET), and a long tail
of rare classes (REST).

## Label shift.

In addition to the source shift, the class distribution differs
between the public and the test split. POSCON images make up
51% of the public split but 36% of the test set; TARGET
images increase from 25% to 55%; REST images decrease from
24% to 9% (Table 1). The leaderboard therefore
evaluates adaptation under combined covariate and label shift, a
setting in which many TTA methods are known to
degrade 6, 12.

## Research questions.

The leaderboard is designed to answer questions that are open in each
of the communities above, for example:

• 

Do domain adaptation and TTA methods developed on natural-image
benchmarks transfer to biological domain shift?

• 

At which level of the domain hierarchy (source, batch, or
plate) should adaptation be performed?

• 

Do pretrained microscopy foundation models generalize to unseen
imaging sources better than models trained from scratch?

• 

Can models identify compounds with weak phenotypes and few
training images (TARGET, REST) on unseen sources?

## 3 Submitting to the leaderboard

## Submission format.

A leaderboard submission is a public Git repository together with a
commit hash. The repository must contain the training code and a
Dockerfile from which the evaluation server builds a container image.
Submissions are therefore independent of the deep learning framework
and model architecture. The image must provide a
\codeSubmission class that implements two methods, \codeadapt() and
\codepredict(). Ready-to-run example submissions for all baseline
entries are available in our
baseline
repository and can serve as templates.

## Evaluation.

The evaluation server passes the private test images, as an array of
shape $(N,H,W,5)$ , together with a metadata list containing the
source, batch, and plate identifier of every image, to both
\codeadapt() and \codepredict(). No labels are provided.
\codeadapt() may update the model using the unlabeled test images
and their metadata, which permits test-time adaptation. Methods that
do not adapt should implement it as a no-op. \codepredict() must return one
prediction per image, in the original order.

## Metric and ranking.

Leaderboard entries are ranked by overall accuracy, i.e., the fraction
of correctly classified images in the entire test set. Because
POSCON and TARGET together contain over 90% of the
test images, this metric is dominated by these two subsets. The
leaderboard therefore additionally reports accuracy on
POSCON, TARGET, and REST separately, so
that progress on rare compounds remains visible. Scores are published
on the leaderboard once the manual evaluation finishes.

## Rules.

Submissions may not use any data from the held-out sources (4, 6, 9,
and 13) for training, pre-training, or model selection. This also
applies to pretrained models whose pre-training data include these
sources. Test-time adaptation on the unlabeled test images through
\codeadapt() is permitted.

## 4 Development datasets

To lower the entry barrier and enable fast prototyping before a
leaderboard submission, we release two smaller variants of
JUMP-CP-2k:

• 

JUMP-CP-MNIST contains only the eight
positive-control compounds. Its images are downsampled to
 $200\times 200$ pixels, and the five imaging channels (DNA, RNA, ER,
AGP, and Mito) are combined into three channels, so that standard
RGB architectures and pretrained models can be applied without
modification.

• 

JUMP-CP-2k-200 contains all 2,000 compounds of
JUMP-CP-2k at the reduced resolution of $200\times 200$ pixels, with
all five channels.

Both variants use the same source-level split as JUMP-CP-2k.
Leaderboard rankings are computed on the full-resolution private
test set.

## 5 Baseline entries

The leaderboard is initialized with six baseline entries that
represent the main strategies for handling domain shift. All use a
ResNet18 4 backbone whose first convolution takes five
input channels.

• 

No adaptation (ResNet18). A classifier trained with
standard empirical risk minimization on the training sources and
applied to the test sources unchanged.

• 

Domain-invariant training: DANN 3 and
deepCORAL 8. Both methods encourage representations
that do not depend on the domain during training, adversarially
(DANN) or by aligning second-order feature statistics (deepCORAL).
We use the source identifier as the domain label.

• 

Test-time adaptation: AdaBN 5 and
TENT 9. Both use the trained ResNet18 baseline and adapt
it only at inference. AdaBN replaces the BatchNorm statistics with
statistics computed on the test images. TENT additionally updates
the BatchNorm affine parameters by minimizing the entropy of the
predictions. For both methods, test images are grouped by plate, so
that each plate is adapted separately.

• 

Meta-learning: ARM-BN 11. and CS-ARM-BN 7 ARM-BN trains
the model episodically to classify images using BatchNorm
statistics computed from an unlabeled context batch of the same
domain. At test time, the statistics are computed in the same way
from the unlabeled test images.

Hyperparameters were tuned with Bayesian optimization, and for every
method the model with the highest accuracy on source 2 was
selected. The code of all baseline entries is available in our
GitHub repository.

## 6 Results

Table 2 reports the current leaderboard entries.

## Test-time adaptation reduces the domain gap.

Without adaptation, ResNet18 reaches an average overall accuracy of 0.175.
Re-estimating the BatchNorm statistics on each test plate (AdaBN)
raises this to 0.244. Most of this gain comes from POSCON,
where accuracy increases from 0.465 to 0.646. TENT’s additional
entropy minimization yields a higher performance in the POSCON group, but no improvement in TARGET or REST. Domain-invariant training does not improve over the
non-adapted baseline: DANN reaches 0.170.

## The domain gap remains for compounds with few images.

Performance on POSCON is substantially higher than on TARGET and REST, consistent with the much larger number of training images per compound, lower number of classes and strong phenotypes. These factors are absent for most TARGET and REST compounds, and none of the current entries compensates for them. Identifying compounds with weak phenotypes and few training images on unseen sources is therefore the main open problem posed by the leaderboard.

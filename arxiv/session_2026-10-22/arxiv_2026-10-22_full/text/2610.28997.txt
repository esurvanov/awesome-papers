Abstract

Tokenization that partitions time series into subsequences has become a foundational paradigm in modern time series modeling. However, we find that current tokenization strategies inherently introduce spectral distortions, forcing the learned representations to diverge from the true underlying patterns such as trend, periodicity, and seasonality, thereby impairing model performance. Through theoretical analysis from a frequency perspective, we derive the boundary conditions under which such distortions occur. To overcome this distortion, we propose TimeFT, a nearly distortion-free frequency-based tokenizer for time series data. TimeFT obtains tokens through frequency-domain partitioning, frequency shifting, and Nyquist sampling. The method is parameter-free, incurs negligible computational complexity, and can serve as a drop-in replacement for existing tokenizers. Our extensive experiments on diverse tasks such as forecasting, classification, and anomaly detection demonstrate that TimeFT consistently and significantly improves performance. The code has been made available at https://anonymous.4open.science/r/TimeFT-75E3/.

## 1 Introduction

Figure 1: Left: The processes of patch and phase tokenization in the time domain. Right: Spectral shifting moves the frequency spectrum, thereby preventing high frequency components from aliasing into low frequencies.

Models [22] such as PatchTST [30], PatchMLP [36] and PhaseFormer [31] have established a tokenization paradigm [11, 18] for time series data by partitioning the raw sequence into a set of non-overlapping (or overlapping) segments:

 $\begin{split}\mathbf{Z}_{\mathrm{patch}\ i}:=&X\!\left[\,\sum_{j=1}^{i-1}S_{j}\,:\,\sum_{j=1}^{i-1}S_{j}+L_{i}\,\right]\\
&i\in\{1,2,\dots,I\}\end{split}$ 

(1)

 $\begin{split}\mathbf{Z}_{\mathrm{phase}\ i}&:=\left[\,X[\;j\;]\;\text{ for }\;j=i,i+S,\dots\right]\\
&j\leq N-1,\ i\in\{0,1,2,\dots,I\}\end{split}$ 

(2)

where $\mathbf{Z}_{\mathrm{patch}\ i}$ is the patch token [30] and $\mathbf{Z}_{\mathrm{phase}\ i}$ is the phase token [31], which represent two main classes of tokenization methods. $X$ denotes the original input sequence of length $N$ , $L_{i}$ is the length of each patch, $S_{i}$ or $S$ is the stride, and $I$ is the number of tokens. As shown in Figure 1, the patch tokenizer can be seen as segmenting the sequence in the time domain, and the phase tokenizer can be regarded as downsampling in the time domain. Extensive existing work shows that the tokenization paradigm has become a fundamental component of modern time series modeling (§5). However, the frequency domain effects of tokenization remain largely unexplored.

For patch tokens, rectangular window truncation in the time domain leads to issues such as information loss and spectral leakage in the frequency domain (§A.1). For phase tokens, downsampling in the time domain leads to frequency aliasing in the frequency domain (§A.2). These distortions in tokenization cause the patterns of tokens, such as trend, periodicity, and seasonality, to diverge significantly from those of the original time series, thereby leading the model to learn erroneous patterns and degrading model performance. The fundamental reason for this phenomenon lies in distortions caused by tokenization methods and the constraints that the sequence length imposes on the frequency. Towards this, we propose TimeFT, a nearly distortion-free frequency-based tokenizer for time series data in a model-agnostic and parameter-free manner.

 $\mathbf{Z}_{\mathrm{TimeFT}}:=\operatorname{Reshape}\Big(\operatorname{Nyquist\ Sampling}_{S}\Big(\mathcal{F}_{\mathbb{R}}^{-1}\big(\operatorname{Shift}\big(\operatorname{Block}_{K}\big(\mathcal{F}_{\mathbb{R}}(X)\big)\big)\big)\Big)\Big)$ 

(3)

where $\mathbf{Z}_{\mathrm{TimeFT}}$ is the set of TimeFT tokens, $S$ is the sampling stride, and $K$ is the number of blocks. $\operatorname{Block}_{K}(\cdot)$ , $\operatorname{Shift}(\cdot)$ , $\operatorname{Nyquist\ Sampling}_{S}(\cdot)$ and $\operatorname{Reshape}(\cdot)$ denote the operations 7, 9, 12 and 13 of TimeFT, which split the time series spectrum into multiple sub-bands that satisfy the alias-free condition, shift all of them to baseband, and then apply Nyquist sampling. $\mathcal{F}_{\mathbb{R}}$ and $\mathcal{F}_{\mathbb{R}}^{-1}$ denote the real-valued fast Fourier transform (RFFT) and its inverse transform. The comparison of three tokenization methods is illustrated in Figure 2.

Figure 2: The comparison of tokenization methods across the time domain, frequency domain and reconstruction versus origin. (a) Patch tokens lead to a completely different frequency spectrum and cause the loss of all information outside the rectangular window. (b) Phase tokens cause frequency components above the Nyquist frequency to completely alias into the low frequencies, thereby exhibiting erroneous patterns. (c) Our TimeFT effectively solves the above issues and achieves almost distortion-free tokenization. The details of reconstruction methods are provided in the Appendix B.

As mentioned, TimeFT is a parameter-free method that can serve as a fundamental time series tokenization module for a wide range of models, and its time complexity is negligible (§4.3). We prove that our method solves the above data distortion issue (§A.4). As a result, TimeFT can consistently and effectively improve model performance in various tasks (§4.2). The main contributions of this work are as follows.

• 

We find that the existing time series tokenization paradigm causes distortions in the frequency domain, which leads the data to exhibit erroneous temporal patterns. We then derive the transformation relationships in the time domain and the frequency domain during tokenization and further establish the boundary conditions of such distortions.

• 

We propose TimeFT based on the above boundary conditions, which generates tokens while preserving frequency information by employing frequency-domain partitioning, spectrum shifting, and Nyquist sampling. Our method effectively solves the frequency distortion issue during tokenization.

• 

Extensive experiments demonstrate that TimeFT achieves state-of-the-art performance compared to existing tokenization methods in forecasting, classification, and anomaly detection tasks. As a plug-in module, TimeFT results in an improvement of nearly 10% in many cases, and the time cost is consistently less than 1.4% of the training time per epoch.

## 2 Preliminaries

In this section, we first define the tokenization problem. Then, from a frequency-domain perspective, we theoretically analyze the causes of the distortions shown in Figure 2 for patch and phase tokens.

Problem Definition.

Consider a multivariate time series input denoted as $\mathbf{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{V\times N}$ , where $V$ is the number of variates (channels) and $N$ is the length of the temporal sequence. The fundamental objective of our tokenization strategy is to discretize time series $\mathbf{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{V\times N}$ into a finite collection of tokens $\mathbf{Z}\in\mathbb{R}^{V\times S\times*}$ that can be learned by models, where $S$ and $*$ are the shapes of the tokens.

Patch Tokenization in Frequency Domain.

Let $x[n]$ , $n\in\{0,1,\dots,N-1\}$ , be a discrete sequence of finite length $N$ . The patch tokenization in Equation 1 can be equivalent to the operation of rectangular windowing. The subsequence of length $L$ is obtained by extracting the segment in the rectangular window and re-indexing it from zero, i.e., $y[m]=x[n_{0}+m]$ for $m=0,\dots,L-1$ . This is equivalent to convolving with a shifted Dirichlet kernel in the frequency domain.

Intuitively, it is easy to assume that patch tokens distort high frequency information [45, 15, 50] due to reduced data length, but the actual situation is much more complex. Applying a rectangular window of length $L$ to a sequence of length $N$ induces three main distortions. First, the kernel’s slowly decaying sidelobes produce spectral leakage, spreading energy from each frequency component across the entire spectrum and potentially obscuring weak signals. Second, the width of the mainlobe establishes an absolute resolution limit: components spaced closer than half this interval merge into a single broadened peak, irrespective of the original sequence length $N$ . Moreover, the windowed sequence is further truncated to length $L$ , so it discards information beyond length $L$ while inheriting identical leakage and resolution penalties.

Phase Tokenization in Frequency Domain.

The phase tokenization in Equation 2 is equivalent to the operation of downsampling, i.e., $y[k]=x[kS]$ for $k=0,1,\dots,L-1$ , where $L=\lceil N/S\rceil$ . If the original frequency spectrum contains nonzero energy for frequencies higher than the bound $B$ , the adjacent replicas will overlap when downsampling, causing the higher frequency components to fold into lower frequencies. This phenomenon is known as spectral aliasing and constitutes an irreversible distortion. To avoid aliasing, downsampling must satisfy the Nyquist sampling theorem [32].

 $2\pi\frac{B}{f_{s}}\leq\frac{\pi}{S}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad B\leq\frac{f_{s}}{2S}$ 

(4)

where $f_{s}$ represents the sampling frequency of the original discrete time sequence. The downsampled signal will be free from aliasing if and only if the original continuous-time signal is bandlimited to less than or equal to the new Nyquist frequency $f_{s}/2S$ . Sampling that satisfies this condition is referred to as Nyquist sampling. Detailed theoretical analysis and illustrations are provided in the Appendix A.

## 3 Methodology

As shown in Figure 3, our TimeFT consists of three principal stages: (1) frequency-domain decomposition based on boundary conditions that extracts different sub-bands, (2) frequency shifting translates high-frequency components into the low-frequency band, and (3) information-preserving token extraction that transforms these representations into final tokens.

## 3.1 Boundary Conditions for Distortion-Free Tokenization

Section 2 shows that for any finite discrete sequence $\mathbf{X}$ , there exists a simple method (such as Digital-to-Analog Conversion) that can reconstruct it into a continuous process $\mathcal{X}$ (an infinite sequence) over a period of time. $\mathbf{X}$ can be regarded as being obtained from $\mathcal{X}$ . When the boundary conditions are satisfied, both the sampling and reconstruction processes are invertible and lossless, and in this case $\mathbf{X}$ is equivalent to $\mathcal{X}$ . However, due to the finite length constraint on the information contained in $\mathbf{X}$ , an incorrect $\mathcal{X}$ is often reconstructed, leading to tokens of erroneous patterns. We next present the boundary conditions for this conversion.

Boundary Condition for Patch Tokenization.

Patch tokenization multiplies $x[n]$ by a rectangular window of length $L$ and extracts the non-zero segment. The necessary and sufficient condition for the token to retain all the information required for an exact reconstruction of the original signal is that the entire valid sequence of $x[n]$ is contained within the windowed interval. For a finite-duration sequence of length $N$ , this condition is satisfied if and only if the window covers all non-zero values. If $x[n]$ is periodic with period $T$ , the condition becomes $L=mT$ ( $m\in\mathbb{N}^{+}$ ), which means that any contiguous $L$ sample block then suffices for perfect periodic extension, completely eliminating information loss. However, these situations rarely occur for general time series.

Boundary Condition for Phase Tokenization.

Compared to patch tokenization, the boundary condition of phase tokenization is much simpler. Due to the Nyquist sampling theorem, the alias-free condition requires $x[n]$ to satisfy Equation 4. Therefore, for any discrete $x[n]$ , we define:

 $B_{N}=\lfloor\frac{N}{2S}\rfloor$ 

(5)

As long as the maximum frequency component of $x[n]$ is less than or equal to $B_{N}$ , it satisfies the boundary condition for phase tokenization. In this case, the original sequence can be perfectly recovered from the tokens, making the tokenization free from information loss.

## 3.2 Frequency Domain Decomposition Based on Boundary Conditions

Figure 3: The pipeline of TimeFT is implemented by (1) frequency domain decomposition, (2) spectral shifting and (3) information-preserving token extraction. Different colors and markers are used to denote the processes of operations. Based on the frequency threshold $B_{N}$ derived from downsampling without information loss, TimeFT shifts all spectra of bandwidth $B_{N}$ to the baseband, and then obtains almost distortion-free tokens by Nyquist sampling.

In order to achieve lossless tokenization in the frequency domain, we first project the input time series into the frequency domain via the real-valued fast Fourier transform applied along the temporal axis, as shown in Stage 1 in Figure 3. Let $\mathcal{F}_{\mathbb{R}}$ denote the RFFT operator. The resulting complex-valued tensor is as follows:

 $\mathbf{X}_{\mathrm{freq}}=\mathcal{F}_{\mathbb{R}}(\mathbf{X})\in\mathbb{C}^{V\times N_{f}}$ 

(6)

where $N_{f}=\lfloor N/2\rfloor+1$ is the number of frequency bins corresponding to the non-negative frequencies of a real signal of length $N$ . We adopt a channel-independent approach for different variables to avoid frequency domain mixing. For a univariate real sequence $\mathbf{x}=(x_{0},\dots,x_{N-1})$ , its $k$ -th RFFT coefficient is given by $[\mathcal{F}_{\mathbb{R}}(\mathbf{x})]_{k}=\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}x_{n}\cdot e^{-i2\pi kn/N},\ k=0,1,\dots,N_{f}-1$ . To retain multi-frequency information, we partition the frequency representation along the spectral dimension into $K$ contiguous blocks, each of size $B_{N}$ . This makes the spectral bandwidth satisfy Equation 5 and the boundary condition in §3.1. For the $k$ -th band ( $k=0,\dots,K-1$ ), we define a full extraction that retains only the frequency components within the interval $[kB_{N},kB_{N}+B_{N}-1]$ and zeros out the rest:

 $\bigl[\mathbf{X}_{\mathrm{block}}^{(k)}\bigr]_{v,f}=\begin{cases}[\mathbf{X}_{\mathrm{freq}}]_{v,f},&\text{if }\ kB_{N}\leq f<(k+1)B_{N},\\[4.0pt]
0,&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}\quad f=0,\dots,N_{f}-1$ 

(7)

 $\mathbf{X}_{\mathrm{blocks}}=\bigl[\mathbf{X}_{\mathrm{block}}^{(0)};\mathbf{X}_{\mathrm{block}}^{(1)};\dots;\mathbf{X}_{\mathrm{block}}^{(K-1)}\bigr]\in\mathbb{C}^{V\times K\times N_{f}}$ 

(8)

where $K=\lceil N_{f}/B_{N}\rceil$ denotes the total number of spectral blocks, and the last portion of $\mathbf{X}_{\mathrm{freq}}$ is filled by zero-padding. In this step, we separate the frequency components that correspond to characteristics such as trend, periodicity, and seasonality. Through this partitioning, we obtain a series of sequences in the frequency-domain that satisfy the required frequency band length under the boundary conditions, which is the first step in avoiding aliasing.

## 3.3 Spectral Shifting

However, existing effective frequency bands are located at high frequencies, causing high frequency components to irreversibly alias into low frequencies during downsampling. Therefore, we need to shift the high frequency information down to the low frequency range. As shown in Stage 2 in Figure 3, we align all bands to the baseband and multiply by a factor that compensates for the band starting frequency:

 $\bigl[\mathbf{X}_{\mathrm{shift}}\bigr]_{v,k,b}=\bigl[\mathbf{X}_{\mathrm{blocks}}\bigr]_{v,k,\,kB_{N}+b}\cdot e^{-i2\pi(kB_{N})b/N},\quad b=0,1,\dots,B_{N}-1$ 

(9)

The frequency portion of $\mathbf{X}_{\mathrm{shift}}$ from $B_{N}$ to $N_{f}$ is zero-padded. Equivalently, one may view this as applying the shifting operator $\mathcal{S}$ to each band, which is $\mathbf{X}_{\mathrm{shift}}=\mathcal{S}\bigl(\mathbf{X}_{\mathrm{blocks}}\bigr)\in\mathbb{C}^{V\times K\times N_{f}}$ . The resulting sequences $\mathbf{X}_{\mathrm{shift}}$ satisfy the Nyquist sampling condition when sampled with a step size of $S$ , ensuring that no aliasing occurs. In Equation 9, the factor is applied to align the phase reference with the time origin after shifting the frequency band. It compensates for the phase shift introduced by the translation without altering the magnitude of the complex signal in the frequency domain. Since TimeFT does not compress the temporal dimension, the phase factor in Equation 9 is ignored for models (§A.4.2). Therefore, Equations 7-9 can be simplified to the following form to avoid computing the above complex steps:

 $\mathbf{X}_{\mathrm{shift}}=\operatorname{ZeroPad}_{B_{N}\to N_{f}}\Bigl(\operatorname{Reshape}\bigl(\mathbf{X}_{\mathrm{freq}},(V,K,B_{N})\bigr)\Bigr)\in\mathbb{C}^{V\times K\times N_{f}}$ 

(10)

where the $\operatorname{ZeroPad}_{B_{N}\to N_{f}}$ operation denotes appending zeros to the end of each frequency-domain sequence of length $B_{N}$ to extend its dimension to $N_{f}$ . The $\operatorname{Reshape(\cdot)}$ operation zero-pads at the end if the dimension is insufficient. Here, the spectrum will slightly lose the imaginary part at zero frequency. In this way, we obtain a frequency-domain sequence that satisfies the Nyquist sampling theorem.

## 3.4 Information-Preserving Token Extraction

However, the tokenization extraction operation should be performed in the time domain. As shown in Stage 3 in Figure 3, to recover the corresponding time domain sequence, we apply the inverse real fast Fourier transform (IRFFT) $\mathcal{F}_{\mathbb{R}}^{-1}$ to every frequency block independently.

 $\mathbf{X}_{\mathrm{time}}=\mathcal{F}_{\mathbb{R}}^{-1}\bigl(\mathbf{X}_{\mathrm{shift}}\bigr)\in\mathbb{R}^{V\times K\times N}$ 

(11)

where $\mathbf{X}_{\mathrm{time}}$ denotes the time-domain representation of the sequences of $K$ different frequency bands of width $B_{N}$ for each variable $V$ . Then we perform dense sampling: for every possible offset $\lambda\in\{0,1,\dots,S-1\}$ , we sample the signal starting at the index $\lambda$ with step $S$ . Formally, for the $k$ -th band of the variable $v$ , let $\mathbf{x}_{v,k}=\mathbf{X}_{\mathrm{time}}[v,k,\cdot]\in\mathbb{R}^{N}$ . The dense sampling operator $\mathcal{D}_{S}$ produces a matrix of size $S\times L$ , where $L=\lceil N/S\rceil$ :

 $\bigl[\mathcal{D}_{S}(\mathbf{x}_{v,k})\bigr]_{\lambda,\ell}=\mathbf{x}_{v,k}[\lambda+\ell\cdot S],\quad\lambda=0,\dots,S-1,\;\;\ell=0,\dots,L-1$ 

(12)

Applying this operator independently to every sub-band yields the final token tensor, which is $\mathbf{X}_{\mathrm{sampling}}=\mathcal{D}_{S}\bigl(\mathbf{X}_{\mathrm{time}}\bigr)\in\mathbb{R}^{V\times K\times S\times L}$ . Thus, each variate is represented by $K\times S$ vectors of length $L$ , where $K$ indexes the frequency bands and $S$ captures different temporal phases. In our framework, before sampling, the $k$ -th band has been shifted to the baseband and truncated to the $B$ frequency bins. Due to Equation 5 the sampling stride $S$ satisfies $S\leq{N}/(2B_{N})$ . Hence, the operator $\mathcal{D}_{S}(\cdot)$ corresponds to the Nyquist sampling operation. Consequently, the dense sampling procedure preserves information and is lossless with respect to the sub-band representation. Finally, to obtain a unified token, we first permute the dimension to group the frequency and sampling dimensions, then flatten the frequency bands into the feature dimension.

 $\mathbf{Z}_{\mathrm{TimeFT}}=\operatorname{reshape}\Bigl(\operatorname{permute}\bigl(\mathbf{X}_{\mathrm{sampling}}\bigr),\,(V,S,K\cdot L)\Bigr)\in\mathbb{R}^{V\times S\times(K\cdot L)}$ 

(13)

where $\operatorname{permute}(\cdot)$ reorders the dimension from $(V,K,S,L)$ to $(V,S,K,L)$ , and the subsequent reshape collapses the last two dimensions into a single feature dimension of size $K\cdot L$ . Since models typically apply embedding to tokens, the $K\cdot L$ dimension here does not lead to the curse of dimensionality. Each step of TimeFT is designed to be approximately invertible. The simplified spectral shifting only introduces a negligible loss at the zero-frequency component, and we can almost perfectly reconstruct the original spectrum from each token (§B.3).

## 4 Experiments

In this section, we conduct a comprehensive evaluation of TimeFT, including three major time series tasks (§4.2), the computational efficiency (§4.3), the plug-in experiment (§4.4), the ablation study (§4.5) and the hyperparameter sensitivity (§4.7). Detailed experiments are presented in Appendix C, and visualizations of TimeFT are provided in Appendix D.

## 4.1 Experimental Setup

Datasets.

We evaluate TimeFT on a comprehensive set of benchmarks spanning three core time-series tasks, and we select commonly used datasets for each task. Long-term forecasting is performed on seven real-world datasets: ETTh1, ETTh2, ETTm1, ETTm2, Weather, Electricity and Traffic [42, 41, 27]. Classification uses 10 UEA datasets [1, 20, 7]: EthanolConcentration, FaceDetection, Handwriting, Heartbeat, JapaneseVowels, PEMS-SF, SelfRegulationSCP1, SelfRegulationSCP2, SpokenArabicDigits, and UWaveGestureLibrary. Anomaly detection is conducted on five widely used benchmarks [1, 47, 17]: SMD, MSL, SMAP, SWaT, and PSM.

Baselines.

We compare TimeFT with state-of-the-art tokenization methods and classic time series models. We consider EntroPE [1], TimeMosaic [13], PhaseFormer [31], PatchMLP [36] and PatchTST [30] as standard baselines. In addition, we also use TimeFilter [19], xPatch [34], iTransformer [27], TimeMixer [37], TimesNet [41], LightTS [9], DLinear [48], GPT4TS [52], ETSformer [40], FEDformer [51] as baselines. The specific baselines differ slightly between tasks, due to the choice of baselines in previous papers. Since TimeFT is a general tokenization method rather than a model and due to expensive pre-train costs, we do not use time series foundation models (TSFMs) as baselines or plug-in models. We use PatchTST as the backbone model for TimeFT in all experiments except the plug-in experiment.

## 4.3 Time Complexity Analysis

The computational complexity of the proposed TimeFT tokenization is $\mathcal{O}(VN\log N+VKN\log N+VKN)$ . Each of the $V\times K$ frequency blocks is transformed back to the time domain via an IRFFT (Equation 11), contributing the dominant term of $\mathcal{O}(VKN\log N)$ . As shown in Table 4, the one-time overhead introduced by TimeFT is negligible relative to the aggregate cost of model training and evaluation. Moreover, tokenization can be efficiently batched and parallelized, further amortizing its practical runtime. For multiple epochs, TimeFT produces identical tokens as long as the input sequence is the same, thus eliminating the need for repeated execution.

## 4.4 Plug-in Experiment

We evaluate the universality of TimeFT using it as a plug-in tokenization module for PatchTST and PhaseFormer. As shown in Table 5, integrating TimeFT consistently improves performance in all seven datasets. For PatchTST, performance in MSE and MAE improves from nearly $10\%$ in many cases. For PhaseFormer, the corresponding improvements are more than $5\%$ in many cases. These results demonstrate that TimeFT’s near-lossless tokenization is readily transferable to different architectures, delivering immediate gains without modifying the base model design.

## 4.5 Ablation Study

We conduct the ablation study to demonstrate the contribution of each part in TimeFT. Removing Stage 1 means that only a single block exists, and the subsequent operations artificially introduce aliasing. Removing Stage 2 means that the high-frequency components of each block will individually alias into the low frequencies, and removing Stage 3 is equivalent to using different frequency components directly as tokens. As shown in Table 6, removing any individual stage significantly degrades the performance, showing that every component is essential to the model.

## 4.7 Sensitivity Analysis

We further examine the impact of the tokenization stride S on model performance across various input lengths in the ETTm1 dataset. As shown in Figure 5, the results reveal a significant synergy between the stride and the input scale, where the optimal configuration changes according to the input length. For shorter input sequences such as $N=96$ , smaller strides ensure sufficient coverage of temporal details. However, as the input length increases to $N=720$ , the model demonstrates a clear preference for larger strides. Since the stride determines the Nyquist frequency, a smaller stride results in a higher Nyquist frequency and longer tokens, while also leading to fewer tokens (downsampling). This trade-off affects the model’s performance, making it necessary to choose an appropriate stride for the input. Moreover, longer input sequences generally result in better performance.

## 5 Related Work

Existing time series tokenizers can be classified into two fundamental categories: patch tokens and phase tokens. We consider only the partitioning operation here without the embedding step. Quantization methods such as "bins" [5, 46] and some frequency methods [2, 24, 33, 8] are not discussed because they are irrelevant to our topic (§A.5). According to the modeling paradigm, token-based methods fall into the following three categories.

For deep learning models, PatchTST[30] introduces a patch-based approach to time series analysis, establishing patching as a key component of time series modeling. Subsequently, PITS [23], PatchMLP [36] and xPatch [34] demonstrate that patch tokens can improve performance. Beyond static patching [21, 14, 10], some dynamic patching methods [19, 3, 26, 44, 29] have also been proposed for time series tokenization. SRSNet [43] leverages information from contextual time series to improve the forecasting performance of patch-based models. TimeMosaic [13] employs a lightweight classifier to determine the length of patches. Additionally, PhaseFormer [31] introduces time series phase tokens, which can be viewed as an approximate transpose of patch tokenization.

For pre-trained tokenizers, TOTEM [35] divides the time series into patches and maps them to a discrete embedding using a pre-trained model. EntroPE [1] employs a pre-trained entropy model to determine optimal patch boundaries. These methods still belong to the patch token class.

For pre-trained foundation models, recent state-of-the-art TSFMs [25], including Chronos-2 [4], Sundial [28], TiRex [6], Moirai [39], and TimeFM [12], have adopted patch tokenization as a preprocessing step. Tokenization has become a fundamental component of TSFMs.

## 6 Conclusion

We identify the inherent spectral distortion in the dominant time series tokenization paradigm and derive its boundary conditions for distortion-free tokenization. We propose TimeFT, an approach obtains time series tokens in a nearly distortion-free manner through frequency-domain partitioning, shifting, and Nyquist sampling. TimeFT is parameter-free, lightweight, and plug-and-play tokenizer for time series data. Extensive experiments on various tasks showcase that TimeFT outperforms existing practices and yields nearly 10% improvement as a plug-in module in many cases.

## Appendix A Frequency Domain Theoretical Analysis and Boundary Conditions

## A.1 Patch Tokenization

Let $x[n]$ , $n\in\{0,1,\dots,N-1\}$ , be a discrete time sequence of finite length $N$ . The patch tokenization in Equation 1 can be equivalent to the operation of rectangular windowing. A rectangular window $w_{L}[n;n_{0}]$ of length $L$ starting at the index $n_{0}$ is defined as $w_{L}[n;n_{0}]=1$ for $n_{0}\leq n\leq n_{0}+L-1$ and $0$ otherwise. The windowed sequence is $x_{w}[n]=x[n]\cdot w_{L}[n;n_{0}]$ , and the sliced sequence of length $L$ is obtained by extracting the segment in the window and re-indexing it from zero, i.e., $y[m]=x_{w}[n_{0}+m]$ for $m=0,\dots,L-1$ . We analyze the spectral consequences of these operations within the continuous frequency domain provided by the discrete time Fourier transform (DTFT). The DTFT of $x[n]$ is

 $X(e^{j\omega})=\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}x[n]e^{-j\omega n}=\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}x[n]e^{-j\omega n}$ 

(14)

where $\omega\in[-\pi,\pi)$ denotes the normalized angular frequency in radians. Note that $\operatorname{DTFT}$ of a sequence $x[n]$ of length $N$ is well-defined, since by treating the values at undefined $n$ as $0$ , the sequence naturally extends to an infinite-length sequence, and the infinite summation in $\operatorname{DTFT}$ reduces to a finite sum. The DTFT of the rectangular window $w_{L}[n;n_{0}]$ is derived as follows:

 $\displaystyle W_{L}(e^{j\omega};n_{0})$ 
 $\displaystyle=\sum_{n=n_{0}}^{n_{0}+L-1}e^{-j\omega n}$ 

(15)

 $\displaystyle=e^{-j\omega n_{0}}\frac{1-e^{-j\omega L}}{1-e^{-j\omega}}$ 

 $\displaystyle=e^{-j\omega n_{0}}\cdot\frac{2je^{-j\omega L/2}\sin(\omega L/2)}{2je^{-j\omega/2}\sin(\omega/2)}$ 

 $\displaystyle=e^{-j\omega n_{0}}\,e^{-j\omega(L-1)/2}\,\frac{\sin(\omega L/2)}{\sin(\omega/2)}$ 

 $\displaystyle=e^{-j\omega\left(n_{0}+\frac{L-1}{2}\right)}\cdot\frac{\sin(\omega L/2)}{\sin(\omega/2)}$ 

where the first step expresses the windowed sum as a finite geometric series. Factor out the term corresponding to the starting index by letting $n=n_{0}+k,\ k=0,1,\dots,L-1$ :

 $\sum_{k=0}^{L-1}e^{-j\omega(n_{0}+k)}=e^{-j\omega n_{0}}\sum_{k=0}^{L-1}\left(e^{-j\omega}\right)^{k}$ 

(16)

The sum on the right is a finite geometric series with the first term $1$ , the common ratio $r=e^{-j\omega}$ , and $L$ terms. Using the geometric sum formula $\sum_{k=0}^{L-1}r^{k}=\frac{1-r^{L}}{1-r}$ (for $r\neq 1$ ), we obtain

 $\sum_{k=0}^{L-1}\left(e^{-j\omega}\right)^{k}=\frac{1-\left(e^{-j\omega}\right)^{L}}{1-e^{-j\omega}}=\frac{1-e^{-j\omega L}}{1-e^{-j\omega}}$ 

(17)

Substituting this back gives the closed form

 $W_{L}(e^{j\omega};n_{0})=e^{-j\omega n_{0}}\frac{1-e^{-j\omega L}}{1-e^{-j\omega}}$ 

(18)

In Equation 15, the factor $D_{L}(\omega)\triangleq\frac{\sin(\omega L/2)}{\sin(\omega/2)}$ is the Dirichlet kernel, a periodic sinc-like function whose main lobe has a null-to-null width of $4\pi/L$ and whose highest side lobe is approximately $-13$ dB below the main peak. The complex exponential factor $e^{-j\omega(n_{0}+(L-1)/2)}$ imparts a linear phase shift, but since we are concerned with magnitude spectral characteristics, this phase term does not affect the analysis of problems. The windowed sequence $x_{w}[n]=x[n]\cdot w_{L}[n;n_{0}]$ is the pointwise product of the original sequence and the rectangular window. By the DTFT multiplication property, multiplication in the time domain corresponds to circular convolution of their DTFTs over a period of length $2\pi$ :

 $X_{w}(e^{j\omega})=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}X(e^{j\theta})\,W_{L}(e^{j(\omega-\theta)};n_{0})\,d\theta$ 

(19)

where $X_{w}(e^{j\omega})$ is the DTFT of sequence $x_{w}[n]$ . We then yield the following:

 $\displaystyle X_{w}(e^{j\omega})$ 
 $\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}X(e^{j\theta})\,e^{-j(\omega-\theta)\left(n_{0}+\frac{L-1}{2}\right)}\frac{\sin\big((\omega-\theta)L/2\big)}{\sin\big((\omega-\theta)/2\big)}\,d\theta$ 

(20)

 $\displaystyle=e^{-j\omega\left(n_{0}+\frac{L-1}{2}\right)}\cdot\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\Big[X(e^{j\theta})\,e^{j\theta\left(n_{0}+\frac{L-1}{2}\right)}\Big]\,D_{L}(\omega-\theta)\,d\theta$ 

where $D_{L}(\omega-\theta)=\frac{\sin((\omega-\theta)L/2)}{\sin((\omega-\theta)/2)}$ is the Dirichlet kernel shifted to $\theta$ . Equation 20 reveals that, up to a global linear phase shift, $X_{w}(e^{j\omega})$ is the convolution of a phase-rotated version of $X(e^{j\omega})$ with the Dirichlet kernel $D_{L}(\cdot)$ . The distortion mainly takes the following three forms: (1) spectral leakage: Slowly decaying sidelobes of $D_{L}(\cdot)$ spread the energy from each frequency component across the entire spectrum, potentially masking weaker components. (2) resolution limit: The main lobe of $D_{L}(\cdot)$ has a width of $4\pi/L$ , which limits the ability to distinguish adjacent spectral peaks. Components closer than half this interval merge into a single broadened lobe, irrespective of the original sequence length $N$ . Moreover, the windowed sequence is further truncated to length L, so it discards information beyond length L while inheriting the identical leakage and resolution penalties. Since discarding information outside the window is intuitive in the time domain, we next analyze the underlying causes of spectral leakage and resolution limit.

Spectral Leakage.

For $X_{w}(e^{j\omega})$ in Equation 20, the linear phase factor $e^{-j\omega(n_{0}+(L-1)/2)}$ does not affect the magnitude spectrum, so the magnitude distortion is entirely determined by the convolution with the Dirichlet kernel. This convolution is the origin of spectral leakage: each frequency component of the original signal is not confined to its ideal location but spreads to neighboring frequencies due to the side lobes of $D_{L}(\omega)$ , as shown in Figure 6:

Figure 6: Illustration of spectral leakage and weak-signal masking caused by rectangular window truncation. (a) Rectangular window truncation of a time-domain signal. (b) Spectral characteristics of the Dirichlet kernel corresponding to the rectangular window. (c) Original spectrum containing adjacent strong and weak signals. (d) Masking of weak signals caused by sidelobe leakage after windowing. The decibel (dB) is a logarithmic unit, so a small difference can represent a large change.

To illustrate the effect concretely with a typical signal from practical time series, consider a real sinusoid infinite-duration sequence $x[n]=\cos(\omega_{0}n)$ . Using Euler’s formula, $x[n]=\frac{1}{2}e^{j\omega_{0}n}+\frac{1}{2}e^{-j\omega_{0}n}$ , whose DTFT consists of two impulses at $\pm\omega_{0}$ . Since the DTFT of the window sequence $x_{w}[n]$ is the convolution of $X(e^{j\omega})$ with $W_{L}(e^{j\omega};n_{0})$ , linearity allows us to treat each complex exponential. Substituting the rectangular window DTFT from Equation 19 yields the following:

 $\displaystyle X_{w}(e^{j\omega})$ 
 $\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}W_{L}(e^{j(\omega-\omega_{0})};n_{0})+\frac{1}{2}W_{L}(e^{j(\omega+\omega_{0})};n_{0})$ 

(21)

 $\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}e^{-j(\omega-\omega_{0})\left(n_{0}+\frac{L-1}{2}\right)}D_{L}(\omega-\omega_{0})+\frac{1}{2}e^{-j(\omega+\omega_{0})\left(n_{0}+\frac{L-1}{2}\right)}D_{L}(\omega+\omega_{0})$ 

When $\omega_{0}$ is sufficiently far from $0$ and $\pi$ , the main lobes centered at $\pm\omega_{0}$ are well separated, and the cross terms between them become negligible. In this case, the magnitude spectrum is approximately:

 $|X_{w}(e^{j\omega})|\approx\frac{1}{2}\big|D_{L}(\omega-\omega_{0})\big|+\frac{1}{2}\big|D_{L}(\omega+\omega_{0})\big|$ 

(22)

which explicitly shows that each ideal spectral line is replaced by a broadened main lobe accompanied by oscillatory side lobes. The factor $1/2$ accounts for the splitting of the sinusoid’s energy between positive and negative frequencies. The side lobes of $|D_{L}(\cdot)|$ slowly decay, with the highest side lobe only about $13$ dB below the main peak. Consequently, a weak sinusoidal component located near a strong one may be completely masked by the side-lobe energy of the stronger component.

Resolution Limit.

To understand the resolution limit imposed by the rectangular window, consider two complex exponentials $e^{j\omega_{1}n}$ and $e^{j\omega_{2}n}$ with $\omega_{1}<\omega_{2}$ . After windowing with $w_{L}[n;n_{0}]$ , the DTFT of each component becomes a shifted Dirichlet kernel $D_{L}(\omega-\omega_{i})$ (up to a phase factor). The magnitude spectrum therefore contains two main lobes centered at $\omega_{1}$ and $\omega_{2}$ . According to the Rayleigh criterion commonly adopted in spectral analysis, two peaks are resolved when their separation is at least half the null-to-null width of the main lobe. Since the main lobe of $D_{L}(\omega)$ has a null-to-null width of $4\pi/L$ , the critical separation is $2\pi/L$ . Hence, two sinusoidal components can be distinguished in the DTFT magnitude spectrum only if

 $|\omega_{1}-\omega_{2}|\geq\frac{2\pi}{L}$ 

(23)

If the separation is smaller, the two main lobes merge into a single broadened peak, and the components become indistinguishable regardless of the continuous nature of the DTFT. This is a trade-off relationship, reducing the window length $L$ improves temporal localization but inevitably degrades frequency resolution and exacerbates spectral leakage.

First, we analyze whether the transformation from $x_{w}[n]$ to $y[n]$ introduces the resolution limit problem. The patch tokenization extracts the windowed segment and re-indexes it to start at zero, yielding $y[m]=x_{w}[n_{0}+m]$ for $m=0,1,\dots,L-1$ . We now derive its DTFT:

 $\displaystyle Y(e^{j\omega})$ 
 $\displaystyle=\sum_{m=0}^{L-1}y[m]\,e^{-j\omega m}$ 

 $\displaystyle=\sum_{n=n_{0}}^{n_{0}+L-1}x_{w}[n]\,e^{-j\omega(n-n_{0})}$ 

(24)

 $\displaystyle=e^{j\omega n_{0}}\sum_{n=n_{0}}^{n_{0}+L-1}x_{w}[n]\,e^{-j\omega n}$ 

 $\displaystyle=e^{j\omega n_{0}}\,X_{w}(e^{j\omega})$ 

(25)

Equation 25 shows that slicing multiplies the DTFT of the windowed sequence by $e^{j\omega n_{0}}$ , which corresponds to a time advance of $n_{0}$ samples. This shows that cutting merely applies a pure time advance, leaving the magnitude spectrum unchanged. Substituting the expression for $X_{w}(e^{j\omega})$ from Equation 20:

 $X_{w}(e^{j\omega})=e^{-j\omega\left(n_{0}+\frac{L-1}{2}\right)}\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}X(e^{j\theta})\,e^{j\theta\left(n_{0}+\frac{L-1}{2}\right)}D_{L}(\omega-\theta)\,d\theta$ 

(26)

We obtain

 $\displaystyle Y(e^{j\omega})$ 
 $\displaystyle=e^{j\omega n_{0}}\,e^{-j\omega\left(n_{0}+\frac{L-1}{2}\right)}\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}X(e^{j\theta})\,e^{j\theta\left(n_{0}+\frac{L-1}{2}\right)}D_{L}(\omega-\theta)\,d\theta$ 

 $\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}X(e^{j\theta})\,e^{j\theta n_{0}}\,e^{-j(\omega-\theta)\frac{L-1}{2}}D_{L}(\omega-\theta)\,d\theta$ 

(27)

From Equation 25 it immediately follows that the magnitude spectra are identical:

 $|Y(e^{j\omega})|=|X_{w}(e^{j\omega})|$ 

(28)

Thus, the step $y[m]=x_{w}[n_{0}+m]$ for $m=0,\dots,L-1$ does not alter the leak pattern or the frequency resolution; it simply realigns the temporal origin. All subsequent discussion of spectral leakage and resolution applies equally to the sliced sequence $y[m]$ that constitutes the patch token.

Figure 7: Illustration of the picket-fence effect. (a) Picket-fence effect at linear magnitude. (b) Picket-fence effect at dB magnitude.

Although DTFT $Y(e^{j\omega})$ is a continuous function of $\omega$ , any practical spectral analysis must evaluate it at a finite set of discrete frequencies. When we sample the DTFT at $M$ uniformly spaced frequencies $\omega_{k}=2\pi k/M$ , $k=0,\dots,M-1$ , we obtain a discrete representation that is subject to the picket-fence effect. When viewing a landscape through a picket fence, one can see only the portions of the scene that align with the gaps between the slats, while the rest remains hidden. In the spectral context, the continuous DTFT is the “landscape,” and the $M$ frequency samples $\omega_{k}$ are the “gaps.” If a signal component’s true frequency lies exactly on one of these sample points, its full amplitude is observed; if it falls between two points, the observed value at the nearest sample is only an attenuated version of the actual spectral peak. The severity of this attenuation depends on how far the true frequency deviates from the nearest sampling point.

To quantify this effect, consider a single complex sinusoid of frequency $\omega_{0}$ after rectangular windowing. Its continuous DTFT is a shifted Dirichlet kernel, i.e., $Y(e^{j\omega})=e^{j\phi(\omega)}D_{L}(\omega-\omega_{0})$ , where $\phi(\omega)$ is a linear phase term. The true magnitude peak occurs exactly at $\omega=\omega_{0}$ and is equal to $|D_{L}(0)|=L$ . Suppose that the frequency $\omega_{0}$ falls between two discrete frequency grid points, written as $\omega_{0}=2\pi(k_{0}+\delta)/M$ , where $k_{0}$ is the index of the nearest grid point and $0<|\delta|<0.5$ measures the fractional bin offset. The discrete spectrum is evaluated only on the fixed grid $\omega_{k}=2\pi k/M$ . Hence, the maximum amplitude that we can observe among the discrete bins is

 $|Y(e^{j\omega_{k_{0}}})|=|D_{L}(2\pi\delta/M)|=\left|\frac{\sin(\pi\delta L/M)}{\sin(\pi\delta/M)}\right|$ 

(29)

which is strictly smaller than $L$ whenever $\delta\neq 0$ . The ratio of the observed peak to the true peak,

 $\frac{|Y(e^{j\omega_{k_{0}}})|}{|D_{L}(0)|}=\frac{1}{L}\left|\frac{\sin(\pi\delta L/M)}{\sin(\pi\delta/M)}\right|$ 

(30)

This is known as the scalloping loss or picket-fence loss. For example, as shown in Figure 7, in the worst-case offset $\delta=0.5$ with $M=L$ (no zero-padding), this ratio is about $|\sin(\pi/2)/(\pi/2)|\approx 0.637$ , implying an amplitude reduction of more than $36\%$ and nearly 4 dB compared to the true peak. The effect can be mitigated by increasing $M$ , the number of frequency samples over the same interval, which is usually achieved by zero padding the time-domain sequence $y[m]$ before computing the DFT. Zero-padding appends zeros to $y[m]$ , resulting in a denser set of DTFT evaluation points $\omega_{k}$ without altering the underlying continuous spectrum $Y(e^{j\omega})$ .

## A.2 Phase Tokenization

Let $x[n]$ be a finite-length discrete-time sequence of length $N$ , and let $S\geq 2$ be an integer downsampling factor. The downsampled sequence is $y[m]=x[mS],\ m=0,1,\dots,\lceil N/S\rceil-1$ . We decompose this operation into two stages: (i) zero insertion, and (ii) time compression. To analyze the spectral consequences, it is convenient to introduce an auxiliary zero-filled sequence:

 $w[n]=\begin{cases}x[n],&n=kS,\;k\in\mathbb{Z},\\
0,&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}$ 

(31)

which is simply $x[n]$ sampled on every $S$ -th index and padded with zeros elsewhere. The relation between $w[n]$ and the final downsampled signal $y[m]$ is

 $y[m]=w[mS],\ m\in\mathbb{Z}$ 

(32)

The sequence $w[n]$ can be expressed as the product of $x[n]$ with a periodic impulse sequence

 $p[n]=\sum_{k=-\infty}^{\infty}\delta[n-kS]$ 

(33)

where $\delta[n]$ denotes the unit sample ( $\delta[0]=1$ and $\delta[n]=0$ for $n\neq 0$ ). That is,

 $w[n]=x[n]\,p[n]$ 

(34)

The DTFT of the impulse sequence $p[n]$ is obtained via the Poisson summation formula. The sequence $p[n]=\sum_{k=-\infty}^{\infty}\delta[n-kS]$ is periodic with period $S$ . Within one period $n=0,1,\dots,S-1$ , we have $p[0]=1$ and $p[n]=0$ for $n\neq 0$ . Its discrete Fourier series coefficients are therefore

 $P_{c}[k]=\frac{1}{S}\sum_{n=0}^{S-1}p[n]\,e^{-j2\pi kn/S}=\frac{1}{S},\ k=0,1,\dots,S-1$ 

(35)

Therefore,

 $p[n]=\sum_{k=0}^{S-1}P_{c}[k]\,e^{j2\pi kn/S}=\frac{1}{S}\sum_{k=0}^{S-1}e^{j2\pi kn/S}$ 

(36)

Taking the DTFT of both sides,

 $\displaystyle P(e^{j\omega})$ 
 $\displaystyle=\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}p[n]\,e^{-j\omega n}=\frac{1}{S}\sum_{k=0}^{S-1}\;\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}e^{-j(\omega-2\pi k/S)n}$ 

 $\displaystyle=\frac{2\pi}{S}\sum_{k=0}^{S-1}\sum_{r=-\infty}^{\infty}\delta\!\left(\omega-\frac{2\pi(k+rS)}{S}\right)$ 

(37)

where we have used $\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}e^{-j\alpha n}=2\pi\sum_{r=-\infty}^{\infty}\delta(\alpha-2\pi r)$ . Letting $m=k+rS$ , the double sum collapses to a single sum over all integers $m$ :

 $P(e^{j\omega})=\frac{2\pi}{S}\sum_{m=-\infty}^{\infty}\delta\!\left(\omega-\frac{2\pi m}{S}\right)$ 

(38)

Due to the $2\pi$ -periodicity of the DTFT, only $S$ distinct impulses fall within any interval of length $2\pi$ . For the principal interval $[-\pi,\pi)$ one may restrict the index set to $m=0,1,\dots,S-1$ when operating inside the convolution integral. Multiplication in the time domain corresponds to circular convolution in the frequency domain:

 $W(e^{j\omega})=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}X(e^{j\theta})\,P\bigl(e^{j(\omega-\theta)}\bigr)\,d\theta$ 

(39)

Equations 38 and 39 give

 $\displaystyle W(e^{j\omega})$ 
 $\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}X(e^{j\theta})\,\frac{2\pi}{S}\sum_{m=-\infty}^{\infty}\delta\!\left(\omega-\theta-\frac{2\pi m}{S}\right)d\theta$ 

 $\displaystyle=\frac{1}{S}\sum_{m=-\infty}^{\infty}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}X(e^{j\theta})\,\delta\!\left(\omega-\theta-\frac{2\pi m}{S}\right)d\theta$ 

(40)

For a fixed $\omega\in(-\pi,\pi]$ , the argument of the delta function is zero when $\theta=\omega-2\pi m/S$ . The integral is not zero only if this $\theta$ is within the integration limits $[-\pi,\pi)$ . Because $X(e^{j\theta})$ is $2\pi$ -periodic, exactly $S$ distinct values of $m$ contribute (conventionally $m=0,1,\dots,S-1$ after reindexing). Exploiting the sifting property of the Dirac delta, we obtain:

 $W(e^{j\omega})=\frac{1}{S}\sum_{k=0}^{S-1}X\!\left(e^{j(\omega-2\pi k/S)}\right)$ 

(41)

Equation 41 shows that the zero-filled sequence possesses a spectrum consisting of $S$ shifted replicas of the original spectrum $X(e^{j\omega})$ (each scaled by $1/S$ ), packed into the interval $[-\pi,\pi)$ . From Equation 32 we have $y[m]=w[mS]$ . Its DTFT is

 $Y(e^{j\omega})=\sum_{m=-\infty}^{\infty}y[m]\,e^{-j\omega m}=\sum_{m=-\infty}^{\infty}w[mS]\,e^{-j\omega m}$ 

(42)

Setting $n=mS$ , we see that the sum runs over all integer multiples of $S$ . Because $w[n]=0$ for $n$ not a multiple of $S$ , we may sum over all integers $n$ :

 $Y(e^{j\omega})=\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}w[n]\,e^{-j(\omega/S)n}=W\!\left(e^{j\omega/S}\right)$ 

(43)

Thus, the time-compression $S$ in the time domain expands the frequency axis by a factor $S$ ; i.e., the DTFT of $y[m]$ is the same as that of $w[n]$ but evaluated at $\omega/S$ . Substituting Equation 41 into Equation 43 yields the DTFT of the downsampled sequence:

 $Y(e^{j\omega})=\frac{1}{S}\sum_{k=0}^{S-1}X\!\left(e^{j(\omega/S-2\pi k/S)}\right)$ 

(44)

This is a fundamental description of downsampling in the frequency domain. The original spectrum $X(e^{j\omega})$ is first compressed by the factor $S$ (argument $\omega/S$ ) and then shifted by multiples of $2\pi/S$ . If $X(e^{j\omega})$ contains non-zero energy for frequencies $|\omega|\geq\pi/S$ , the shifted replicas will overlap, causing high frequency components to irreversibly fold into lower frequencies, which is spectral aliasing. To avoid aliasing, the replicas must remain distinct.

Equation 44 shows both spectral stretching and the potential for aliasing. Consider first the term corresponding to $k=0$ , namely $\frac{1}{S}X(e^{j\omega/S})$ . Here, the original spectrum $X(e^{j\omega})$ undergoes a scaling of the frequency axis by the factor $S$ . A frequency component originally located at $\omega_{0}$ in $x[n]$ will appear at $S\omega_{0}$ in $Y(e^{j\omega})$ . This expansion of the frequency axis is called spectral stretching. Consequently, a narrowband signal in $x[n]$ spreads over a proportionally wider bandwidth in the downsampled sequence, and its amplitude is reduced by the factor $S$ .

The remaining $S-1$ terms of $k=1,\dots,S-1$ , have the form $\frac{1}{S}X\bigl(e^{j(\omega/S-2\pi k/S)}\bigr)$ . These arise from the shifted replicas created during the intermediate zero-insertion step. In the spectrum of the zero-filled sequence $W(e^{j\omega})$ in Equation 41, these replicas are centred at $\omega=2\pi k/S$ . The time-compression step $y[m]=w[mS]$ corresponds to the frequency mapping $Y(e^{j\omega})=W(e^{j\omega/S})$ , which expands the frequency axis by $S$ . Hence, a replica originally centered at $2\pi k/S$ is mapped to the new center:

 $S\cdot\frac{2\pi k}{S}=2\pi k$ 

(45)

Because the DTFT is $2\pi$ -periodic, all these centers fall at integer multiples of $2\pi$ , which are exactly the boundaries of the principal interval $[-\pi,\pi)$ . Thus, after the downsampling operation, every one of the $S$ replicas is assigned to the same baseband interval.

Figure 8: Illustration of spectral aliasing in the frequency domain. When the original spectrum is not confined to $(-\pi/S,\pi/S)$ , high frequency components appear as spurious low frequency components.

Whether these replicas overlap destructively depends on the bandwidth of $X(e^{j\omega})$ . If $X(e^{j\omega})$ contains non-zero energy for $|\omega|\geq\pi/S$ , then the $k=0$ baseband replica already extends beyond $|\omega|\leq\pi$ after stretching, and the $k\neq 0$ terms bring additional copies of the spectrum that intrude into the same region. More precisely, a frequency $\omega$ in $Y(e^{j\omega})$ receives contributions from all $k$ for which $\omega/S-2\pi k/S$ falls within the support of $X(e^{j\omega})$ . As shown in Figure 8, when the original spectrum is not confined to $(-\pi/S,\pi/S)$ , there exist frequencies at which two or more replicas contribute, causing high frequency components to appear as spurious low frequency components. This irreversible superposition is spectral aliasing. Because the overlapping terms all share the same frequency in $Y(e^{j\omega})$ , the aliased components cannot be separated after downsampling, leading to permanent distortion.

To avoid aliasing entirely, the support of $X(e^{j\omega})$ must be a subset of $(-\pi/S,\pi/S)$ . In other words, the maximum normalized frequency $\omega_{\max}$ present in $x[n]$ must satisfy

 $\omega_{\max}\leq\frac{\pi}{S}$ 

(46)

When this condition is satisfied, each frequency $\omega$ in the principal interval receives a contribution from exactly one value of $k$ (the one for which $\omega/S-2\pi k/S$ lies within the support of $X$ ). The replicas are then placed side-by-side without overlapping, and the original signal can in principle be recovered from the downsampled version.

The condition can be expressed in terms of physical frequencies by relating $\omega$ to the underlying continuous-time signal from which $x[n]$ was obtained. Assume $x[n]=x_{c}(nT)$ , with the sampling period $T=1/f_{s}$ and $f_{s}$ the original sampling rate. The normalized frequency is $\omega=2\pi f/f_{s}$ . If the analog signal $x_{c}(t)$ is bandlimited, say $|f|\leq B$ , then $X(e^{j\omega})$ is non-zero only for $|\omega|\leq 2\pi B/f_{s}$ . Imposing the no-aliasing requirement Equation 46 gives

 $\frac{2\pi B}{f_{s}}\leq\frac{\pi}{S}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad B\leq\frac{f_{s}}{2S}$ 

(47)

After downsampling, the effective sampling rate becomes $f_{s}^{\prime}=f_{s}/S$ , and its associated Nyquist frequency is $f_{s}^{\prime}/2=f_{s}/(2S)$ . Therefore, alias-free downsampling is possible if and only if the original continuous-time signal is bandlimited to less than or equal to the new Nyquist frequency.

## A.3 Boundary Conditions for Distortion-Free Tokenization

The preceding analyses establish that patch tokenization and phase tokenization are governed by distinct mathematical constraints. We now formalize the precise boundary conditions to avoid irreversible distortion.

## A.3.1 Boundary Condition for Patch Tokenization

Patch tokenization, as defined in Equation 1, is mathematically equivalent to multiplying the original sequence $x[n]$ by a rectangular window of length $L$ starting at index $n_{0}$ , and then re-indexing the non-zero segment from zero. The window sequence is $x_{w}[n]=x[n]\,w_{L}[n;n_{0}]$ , and the final patch token is $y[m]=x_{w}[n_{0}+m]$ for $m=0,\dots,L-1$ .

For an exact reconstruction of the original finite length sequence $x[n]$ of length $N$ from its patch token, it is necessary and sufficient that the window covers all non-zero samples of $x[n]$ . This requires $n_{0}\leq 0$ and $n_{0}+L-1\geq N-1$ . When the window starts at $n_{0}=0$ , the condition reduces to $L\geq N$ . Under this condition, the token $y[m]=x[m]$ for $m=0,\dots,N-1$ (and zero for $m\geq N$ ), so the original $x[n]$ can be recovered losslessly by discarding the zero-padded tail.

If $x[n]$ is periodic with a fundamental period $T\in\mathbb{N}^{+}$ , i.e., $x[n+T]=x[n]$ for all integers $n$ , then the entire signal is fully determined by any contiguous block of $T$ samples. Hence, choosing the window length as an integer multiple of the period, $L=mT$ ( $m\in\mathbb{N}^{+}$ ), guarantees that the token contains exactly the information needed to reconstruct the whole periodic sequence by periodic extension, irrespective of the starting index $n_{0}$ .

Although time domain preservation is straightforward, the windowing operation inherently alters the frequency-domain representation. As derived in Section A.1, multiplication by the rectangular window convolves the true spectrum with a Dirichlet kernel, whose main-lobe width ( $4\pi/L$ ) limits frequency resolution and whose sidelobes cause spectral leakage.

For periodic signals with $L=mT$ , an important special case arises: the discrete Fourier transform (or the DTFT evaluated at the harmonic frequencies) samples the Dirichlet kernel exactly at its zero crossings between harmonics. This coherent sampling completely eliminates spectral leakage at those discrete frequency bins because the contribution from any harmonic to a different harmonic’s bin is identically zero. However, this cancellation occurs only at the sampled frequencies; the continuous DTFT still exhibits sidelobes between the bins, and the fundamental resolution limit imposed by the main-lobe width persists. For non-periodic signals or when $L\neq mT$ , the Dirichlet kernel’s nulls no longer align with the analysis frequencies, and spectral leakage becomes inevitable.

The resolution limit states that two sinusoids can be separated in the magnitude spectrum only if their frequency separation exceeds half the main-lobe width, $|\omega_{1}-\omega_{2}|\geq\frac{2\pi}{L}$ . This limit is entirely independent of whether the lossless condition in the time domain is satisfied. This follows only from the finite observation length $L$ .

In summary, the time-domain lossless condition for patch tokenization is $L\geq N$ for finite signals, or $L=mT$ for periodic signals. In the frequency domain, spectral leakage is an unavoidable consequence of the finite rectangular window, except that for coherently sampled periodic signals it is null at the DFT bins.

## A.3.2 Boundary Condition for Phase Tokenization

Phase tokenization, equivalent to downsampling the original sequence by an integer factor $S\geq 2$ , imposes a fundamentally different and more restrictive boundary condition. Compared to patch tokenization, the boundary condition of phase tokenization is much simpler. As derived from Equation 44, the alias-free condition for the discrete time signal is given by Equation 47. This constitutes the necessary and sufficient condition for distortion-free phase tokenization. If the original signal violates this bandlimit, high-frequency energy residing outside the interval $|\omega|\leq\pi/S$ will be folded into the baseband, causing irreversible distortion.

## A.4 Analysis of TimeFT Methodology

## A.4.1 Analysis of Distortion

Consider a real sequence $\mathbf{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}$ . Its RFFT $\mathbf{X}_{\mathrm{freq}}\in\mathbb{C}^{N_{f}}$ collects the non-negative frequency components, with the $f$ -th bin corresponding to the angular frequency $\omega_{f}=2\pi f/N$ , $f=0,\dots,N_{f}-1$ . The block size is chosen as $B_{N}=\bigl\lfloor N/(2S)\bigr\rfloor$ , satisfying the boundary condition 5.

The $k-th$ spectral block $\mathbf{X}_{\mathrm{block}}^{(k)}$ retains only the frequency bins $f\in[kB_{N},\,kB_{N}+B_{N}-1]$ and zeros out the remaining ones. After the shifting step, i.e., reshaping into blocks of size $B_{N}$ and zero-padding back to length $N_{f}$ , each block becomes a spectrum $\widetilde{\mathbf{X}}^{(k)}$ concentrated in the low-frequency interval $f=0,\dots,B_{N}-1$ :

 $\bigl[\widetilde{\mathbf{X}}^{(k)}\bigr]_{b}=\begin{cases}[\mathbf{X}_{\mathrm{freq}}]_{kB_{N}+b},&0\leq b\leq B_{N}-1,\\
0,&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}$ 

(48)

The inverse RFFT of $\widetilde{\mathbf{X}}^{(k)}$ yields a real-valued time-domain sequence $\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{time}}^{(k)}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}$ . By the properties of the DFT, the spectral support of $\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{time}}^{(k)}$ is confined to (normalized) frequencies

 $|\omega|\leq\frac{2\pi(B_{N}-1)}{N}<\frac{2\pi B_{N}}{N}$ 

(49)

We then obtain the strict band limit:

 $|\omega|<\frac{2\pi}{N}\cdot\frac{N}{2S}=\frac{\pi}{S}$ 

(50)

Equation 50 is exactly the alias-free condition for downsampling by a factor $S$ (§A.2). Hence, every sub-band signal $\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{time}}^{(k)}$ obeys the Nyquist criterion for the subsequent decimation step. The operator $\mathcal{D}_{S}$ defined in Equation 12 extracts the polyphase components of $\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{time}}^{(k)}$ with offsets $\lambda=0,\dots,S-1$ . Because the signal is bandlimited to $|\omega|<\pi/S$ , each decimated polyphase stream is free from spectral aliasing. Therefore, the entire information contained in the $k$ -th frequency sub-band is almost perfectly transferred to the tokens.

The simplified spectral shifting of Equation 10 moves a complex Fourier coefficient originally located at frequency bin $kB_{N}+b$ directly to position $b$ . When $b=0$ , this coefficient becomes the DC term of the shifted block. For a real signal, the DC component of the RFFT must be purely real. The IRFFT therefore discards any imaginary part at that point. Consequently, a single complex value per block loses its imaginary part, a negligible fraction of the total coefficients. As shown in the visualization in Appendix D, TimeFT tokens can be well reconstructed into the original sequence, and thus this discarding is negligible. All other frequencies ( $b\neq 0$ ) are almost perfectly preserved. Hence, TimeFT is almost distortion-free.

## A.4.2 Linear Phase Induced by Frequency Shifting

Let $x[n]$ , $n=0,\dots,N-1$ , be a real sequence and $X_{r}[f]$ , $f=0,\dots,N_{f}-1$ , its RFFT coefficients, where $N_{f}=\lfloor N/2\rfloor+1$ . We isolate the $k$ -th sub-band of width $B_{N}$ , starting at index $kB_{N}$ ( $B_{N}=\lfloor N/(2S)\rfloor$ , $S$ is the downsampling factor) $\mathcal{I}_{k}=\{\,kB_{N},\,kB_{N}+1,\dots,\,kB_{N}+B_{N}-1\,\}$ . The spectral shifting stage moves the content of $\mathcal{I}_{k}$ to the baseband $0,\dots,B_{N}-1$ . A natural implementation copies the coefficients without modification:

 $\widetilde{X}_{r}[b]=X_{r}[kB_{N}+b],\quad b=0,\dots,B_{N}-1$ 

(51)

and zeros the remaining bins. We then perform a $N$ -point inverse RFFT to obtain a real sequence $\tilde{x}^{(k)}[n]$ of length $N$ . We will show that this simple copy operation already produces the correct baseband signal, no extra phase rotation is required when the inverse transform retains the original length $N$ .

Why Direct Copy Preserves the Phase Relations.

The $N$ -point DFT of the original signal satisfies $X[\ell]=\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}x[n]\,e^{-j\frac{2\pi\ell n}{N}}$ and for real $x[n]$ we have $X_{r}[f]=X[f]$ for $0\leq f\leq N/2$ . Consider the contribution of the $k$ -th sub-band to $x[n]$ :

 $x_{\text{band},k}[n]=\frac{2}{N}\sum_{b=0}^{B_{N}-1}\operatorname{Re}\!\Bigl(X[kB_{N}+b]\,e^{j\frac{2\pi(kB_{N}+b)n}{N}}\Bigr)$ 

(52)

Now, take the DFT of the sequence $\tilde{x}^{(k)}$ obtained from the copied spectrum 51. By construction, its DFT is non-zero only at indices $b$ and $N-b$ with coefficients $X[kB_{N}+b]$ and $X^{*}[kB_{N}+b]$ , respectively. Therefore,

 $\displaystyle\tilde{x}^{(k)}[n]$ 
 $\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{b=0}^{B_{N}-1}\Bigl(X[kB_{N}+b]\,e^{j\frac{2\pi bn}{N}}+X^{*}[kB_{N}+b]\,e^{-j\frac{2\pi bn}{N}}\Bigr)$ 

 $\displaystyle=\frac{2}{N}\sum_{b=0}^{B_{N}-1}\operatorname{Re}\!\Bigl(X[kB_{N}+b]\,e^{j\frac{2\pi bn}{N}}\Bigr)$ 

(53)

Comparing Equation 53 with the demodulated version of 52, i.e., multiplying $x_{\text{band},k}[n]$ by $e^{-j\frac{2\pi(kB_{N})}{N}n}$ and taking the real part, reveals that the two expressions are identical after canceling the complex exponential factor. Hence $\tilde{x}^{(k)}[n]$ is exactly the real baseband signal obtained by demodulating the $k$ -th band to zero frequency and low-pass filtering. All sinusoids maintain the same relative phases as they had in the original band.

When Does a Phase Factor Become Necessary?

If we were to directly obtain a critically downsampled baseband signal of length $L=N/S$ without going through the $N$ -point grid, we would need to perform an inverse transform of length $L$ . In that case, the time-axis compression changes the time origin: the new sample index $m=0$ corresponds to the original time $n=0$ , but the frequency grid changes from $2\pi b/N$ to $2\pi b/L$ . To correctly align the phases of the complex exponentials, each coefficient must be multiplied by $\exp(-j\frac{2\pi(kB_{N})}{N}b)$ , compensating for the shift of the frequency center $kB_{N}$ relative to the new sampling grid. Explicitly, if we form a length- $L$ DFT as

 $Z[b]=\frac{1}{S}\,X[kB_{N}+b]\,e^{-j\frac{2\pi(kB_{N})}{N}b},\quad b=0,\dots,B_{N}-1$ 

(54)

and apply a length- $M$ inverse DFT, the resulting sequence is exactly the downsampled version of $\tilde{x}^{(k)}[n]$ without any aliasing. This is the context in which the phase rotation $\exp(-j2\pi(kB_{N})b/N)$ appears; it accounts for the different time origin of the shortened sequence.

TimeFT Avoids the Explicit Phase Factor.

In the TimeFT pipeline we deliberately stay on the original $N$ -point grid. After copying the coefficients, we apply an $N$ -point inverse RFFT and then employ the dense sampling operator $\mathcal{D}_{S}$ to extract the tokens. Because the downsampling is performed after recovering the full-length baseband signal, the phase relations are automatically correct because the phase factor is implicitly absorbed by the subsequent decimation step. Had we shortened the inverse transform to $B_{N}$ or $L$ points, the phase compensation would have been indispensable.

## A.4.3 Analysis of Multiple Tokens

A natural question that arises is whether the distortions introduced by individual tokens can be mitigated when multiple tokens are processed jointly. One might argue that since a collection of tokens together covers the entire original sequence, no information is lost in principle. However, in the case of patch tokenization, this argument does not hold when tokens are processed independently, which is the standard step in models such as PatchTST [30]. Consequently, the patterns that the model learns are inherently distorted.

Several works have proposed cross-token learning mechanisms that allow information to flow between tokens [49, 16]. These methods can partially alleviate the distortion by enabling tokens to share contextual information, but their cross-token capacity is inherently bounded, which cannot cover all tokens simultaneously and therefore cannot fully recover the missing structure. Although these methods demonstrate the existence of the problem, they cannot fundamentally solve it.

In contrast, our TimeFT tokenization solves this problem. The token can almost losslessly reconstruct the original signal. At first glance, this may appear to introduce redundant information. However, this can be approximately viewed as a phase decomposition in the frequency domain. Extensive experiments on forecasting, classification, and anomaly detection confirm that this representation consistently improves model performance rather than introducing harmful redundancy. Furthermore, based on Appendix A.2, Phase Tokens also contain only the low-frequency information of the original sequence, resulting in similar information redundancy and even worse because of frequency aliasing. However, phase tokens have also been shown to be an effective tokenization method, such as PhaseFormer [31]. Therefore, this phenomenon does not pose a risk.

## A.5 Difference between Quantization Methods and Our Main Topic

Time series quantization methods originate from attempts to process time series in a text-like fashion inspired by large language models [5]. They discretize individual time points from continuous values into a finite set of bins, leaving the input shape unchanged. This paradigm is fundamentally different from the tokenization framework that dominates current time series modeling. A large number of current TSFMs [4, 28, 12] and deep learning time series models [30, 31, 43] adopt patch or phase tokenizations, whereas quantization remains a marginal approach.

For instance, Apollo [46] views quantization as a sampling procedure and proposes to first remove high-frequency content before discretizing the continuous data points, a scheme analogous to the “bins” used in Chronos [5]. Because such methods do not involve partitioning the time series into subsequences, they fall outside the scope of our tokenization focused analysis. We therefore do not discuss them further.

Our work is the first to provide a rigorous frequency domain theoretical foundation for the distortions introduced by the prevailing tokenization paradigm, and to derive precise boundary conditions for distortion-free tokenization. Building on this novel perspective, we propose TimeFT, a general, parameter-free, and highly effective tokenizer that consistently improves performance in diverse tasks and models.

## Appendix B Reconstruction Methods

## B.1 Reconstruction from Patch Tokens

A patch token is obtained by multiplying the original sequence $x[n]$ of length $N$ by a rectangular window of length $L$ starting at $n_{0}$ , and then discarding the zero entries. Reconstruction therefore amounts to padding the token with zeros to the original length and shifting it back to its original temporal position.

 $\hat{x}[n]=\begin{cases}y[n-n_{0}],&n_{0}\leq n\leq n_{0}+L-1,\\
0,&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}$ 

(55)

Equivalently, in the frequency domain,

 $\hat{X}(e^{j\omega})=Y(e^{j\omega})\,e^{-j\omega n_{0}}=X_{w}(e^{j\omega})$ 

(56)

where $X_{w}$ is the DTFT of the windowed sequence. As shown in Appendix A.1, it inherits these distortions without any possibility of recovery, the discarded information is permanently lost.

## B.2 Reconstruction from Phase Tokens

A phase token is obtained by downsampling the original sequence $x[n]$ of length $N$ by an integer factor $S$ , which yields $y[m]=x[mS]$ for $m=0,\dots,M_{f}-1$ , where $M_{f}=\lfloor N/S\rfloor$ . Reconstruction consists in upsampling $y[m]$ back to the original length $N$ . The practical method used in our experiments is Fourier-based sinc interpolation. In terms of RFFT, the algorithm computes the $M_{f}$ -point RFFT of the real sequence $y$ ,

 $Y_{r}[k]=\sum_{m=0}^{M_{f}-1}y[m]\,e^{-j2\pi km/M_{f}},\qquad k=0,\dots,\lfloor M_{f}/2\rfloor$ 

(57)

and then zero-pads it to the length required by the original signal. The RFFT of the reconstructed sequence $\hat{x}[n]$ thus becomes

 $\hat{X}_{r}[k]=\begin{cases}Y_{r}[k],&0\leq k\leq\lfloor M_{f}/2\rfloor,\\[6.0pt]
0,&\lfloor M_{f}/2\rfloor<k\leq\lfloor N_{f}/2\rfloor.\end{cases}$ 

(58)

The time domain reconstruction is finally obtained by IRFFT, $\hat{x}=\mathcal{F}_{\mathbb{R}}^{-1}(\hat{X}_{r})$ . We denote the upsampling operator here as $\mathcal{R}(\cdot)$ . Since $\lfloor M_{f}/2\rfloor=\lfloor N/(2S)\rfloor$ , zero-padding in Equation 58 is equivalent to applying an ideal low-pass filter with cutoff frequency $\pi/S$ to the upsampled sequence. The quality of reconstruction critically depends on whether the original signal is bandlimited to $|\omega|\leq\pi/S$ . If $x[n]$ contains energy at frequencies $|\omega|>\pi/S$ , the downsampling step produces spectral aliasing that maps high-frequency components to the low-frequency region (§A.2). The upsampling process cannot distinguish between a true low-frequency component and an aliased high-frequency one, so the reconstructed signal will still contain wrong patterns.

## B.3 Reconstruction from TimeFT Tokens

The TimeFT tokenizer produces $S$ tokens per variate. Each token is a vector of length $K\cdot L$ , where $K=\lceil N_{f}/B\rceil$ is the number of frequency blocks, $B=\lfloor N/(2S)\rfloor$ is the bandwidth, and $L=\lceil N/S\rceil$ is an intermediate variable. Reconstruction proceeds by inverting the two stages of the tokenizer: (i) polyphase recomposition and (ii) inverse spectral shifting.

Polyphase Recomposition.

For a fixed variate and frequency block index $k$ , the $S$ tokens with offsets $\lambda=0,\dots,S-1$ represent the $S$ polyphase components of the bandlimited time domain sequence $\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{time}}^{(k)}$ obtained after spectral shifting and IRFFT 11. The reconstruction first upsamples every sequence back to length $N$ using the same Fourier-based sinc interpolation described in the previous subsection:

 $\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{\mathrm{time}}^{(k)}=\mathcal{R}(\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{time}}^{(k)})$ 

(59)

Equivalently, this can be expressed as the output of a $S$ -channel synthesis filter bank with ideal interpolation filters.

Inverse Spectral Shifting.

The reconstructed bandlimited signal $\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{\mathrm{time}}^{(k)}$ occupies the low-frequency region of the $k$ -th spectral block. To recover the contribution of this block to the full spectrum, we apply $N$ -point RFFT, obtaining $\hat{\mathbf{X}}_{\mathrm{block}}^{(k)}\in\mathbb{C}^{N_{f}}$ . Only the first $B$ frequency bins are retained and the remainder are set to zero.

 $\bigl[\hat{\mathbf{X}}_{\mathrm{block}}^{(k)}\bigr]_{f}=\begin{cases}[\operatorname{RFFT}(\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{\mathrm{time}}^{(k)})]_{f},&0\leq f<B,\\
0,&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}$ 

(60)

The original spectrum was partitioned into contiguous blocks of size $B$ 7). The inverse operation simply places the retained frequency bins of the $k$ -th block back into their original positions:

 $\hat{\mathbf{X}}_{\mathrm{freq}}[kB+b]=\bigl[\hat{\mathbf{X}}_{\mathrm{block}}^{(k)}\bigr]_{b},\qquad k=0,\dots,K-1,\;\;b=0,\dots,B-1$ 

(61)

All bins of $\hat{\mathbf{X}}_{\mathrm{freq}}$ that lie beyond the last block (if any) are obtained from the zero-padding that was applied during the forward decomposition. Finally, an IRFFT produces the reconstructed time domain sequence:

 $\hat{\mathbf{x}}=\mathcal{F}_{\mathbb{R}}^{-1}\bigl(\hat{\mathbf{X}}_{\mathrm{freq}}\bigr)\in\mathbb{R}^{N}$ 

(62)

Fidelity of the Reconstruction.

The only deviation from perfect reconstruction comes from the simplified spectral shifting of Equation 10, causing the DC coefficient of each block to lose its imaginary part. Apart from this single complex value per block, the entire process is algebraically equivalent to passing the signal through a bank of ideal bandpass filters followed by perfect reconstruction synthesis. Because the block bandwidth $B$ is chosen to respect the alias-free condition $B\leq N/(2S)$ , no aliasing occurs during downsampling, and polyphase decomposition captures all samples without loss. Consequently, the reconstructed signal $\hat{\mathbf{x}}$ differs from the original $\mathbf{x}$ only in the imaginary parts of the zero-frequency bins of each spectral block. This distortion is negligible in magnitude and that leaves the amplitude spectrum essentially identical to that of the original. This explains why TimeFT tokens are almost distortion-free, instead of information loss of patch tokens and aliasing artifacts from phase tokens.

## C.4 Settings for Other Experiments

Time Complexity Analysis.

In the time complexity analysis experiment, we ensure that all experimental parameters are identical. The test is conducted on the ETTm1 dataset, with an input length of 320 and a prediction length of 336.

Plu-in Experiments.

In the plug-in experiments, we replace the tokenization modules of different methods with TimeFT, leaving the other components unchanged. For each method, we carefully tune the hyperparameters both before and after the substitution to ensure strong performance.

Ablation Study.

In the ablation study, removing a Stage means removing that module from the pipeline while keeping the rest unchanged. The evaluation is conducted on the long-term forecasting task, with an input length of 320 and a prediction length of 96.

Hyperparameter Sensitive Analysis.

Generally speaking, the only hyperparameter of TimeFT is the stride $S$ , and all other parameters are mathematically derived. Moreover, as a tokenizer, TimeFT is closely related to input length $N$ . Therefore, in the hyperparameter sensitivity experiment, we compare the effects of different strides $S$ and input lengths $N$ on model performance. For each setting, we tune other hyperparameters because the optimal configuration changes accordingly.

## Appendix D Visualization of TimeFT

Since TimeFT is a tokenization method, we present visualizations comparing different classes of tokens in the time domain, frequency domain, and after equivalent reconstruction. As shown in Figures 9, 10, 11, 12, 13, 14 and 15, patch tokens lead to (b) a completely different frequency spectrum and (c) cause the loss of all information outside the rectangular window. Phase tokens cause (e) frequency components above the Nyquist frequency to completely alias into the low frequency, thereby (f) exhibiting erroneous patterns. Our TimeFT (h) effectively solves the above issues and (i) achieves almost distortion-free tokenization.

## Appendix E Limitations and Social Impacts

Limitations.

Our work carries two limitations. Owing to the substantial computational expense involved in pre-training large-scale time series foundation models, our experimental evaluation does not encompass pre-trained TSFMs. Consequently, the behavior and potential benefits of TimeFT in a pre-training and fine-tuning pipeline remain unverified, and investigating its effectiveness under TSFMs regimes is left to future work. Furthermore, although tokenization has become a prevailing paradigm in modern time series analysis, our method intrinsically assumes a tokenization-based processing flow and therefore yields no direct improvement for models that operate directly on raw sequences without any tokenization step.

Social Impacts.

As a fundamental component in time series modeling, our work can enhance the performance of a wide range of time series methods on various real-world tasks, such as forecasting, anomaly detection, and classification. It can serve as a foundational module in improving the application of time series models in fields such as healthcare, transportation, and economics.

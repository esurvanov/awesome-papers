Abstract

Harness optimization, which improves an agent’s prompts, tools, and
workflows, is an increasingly effective way to enhance agents built on frozen or closed-source LLMs. Leading approaches such as the Darwin–Gödel Machine and the Huxley–Gödel Machine (HGM) rely on recursive self-improvement and were developed mainly for coding, where the competence needed to improve the agent coincides with the competence being improved. Planning tasks, in which
an agent must produce a sequence of tool calls that jointly satisfies interacting constraints, break this alignment. There, open-ended self-modification repeatedly explores ineffective changes and consumes substantial evaluation budgets. We introduce HGM-Dual, a diagnostic-guided framework for budget-efficient harness evolution for planning agents. First, HGM-Dual represents agent evolution as lineage-aware context. This context records what each ancestor changed, which behaviors improved or regressed, and which modifications were ineffective, giving the editor explicit edit–outcome attribution instead of an unstructured archive of past agents. Second, HGM-Dual introduces error-conditioned dual-path child generation. For each selected parent, it generates a general child that preserves open-ended exploration and specialized children that target the harness components most likely responsible for the parent’s highest-ranked constraint violations. HGM-Dual outperforms the strongest baseline by up to $14.45\%$ relative improvement in case accuracy while matching HGM variants’ final performance with $2\times$ – $4\times$ fewer evaluation budget.

## 1 Introduction

Agent harness optimization Khattab et al. (2024); Yuksekgonul et al. (2024) improves large language model (LLM) agents without updating model parameters by optimizing the prompts, tools, and workflows that govern agent behavior. Recent self-improving systems Novikov et al. (2025) automate this process through evolutionary search. The Darwin–Gödel Machine (DGM) Zhang et al. (2026b) maintains an archive of agents and generates new candidates by modifying selected parents, while the Huxley–Gödel Machine (HGM) Wang et al. (2026) selects candidates based on clade-metaproductivity, their potential to produce strong descendants.

This paradigm is well suited to coding agents because the ability being improved, understanding and modifying code, also enables self-modification. Planning tasks, however, require sequences of actions that satisfy interacting constraints Zhang et al. (2026d); Xie et al. (2024), creating a mismatch that poses two challenges for existing recursive self-improvement methods.

First, effective improvement requires a structured context of the search
history. Planning benchmarks expose a relatively small and recurring set of
constraint failures, so different agents in the search tree often encounter
similar problems. If an editor observes only its current parent’s evaluation logs, it cannot determine whether a similar fix has already
been attempted elsewhere, whether it improved or degraded performance, or
which previous modifications led to the current behavior. As a result,
different branches may repeatedly rediscover the same ineffective changes.
In HGM’s shopping search, 41.8% of edits repeat an edit pattern already
present in the tree (Figure 1, left), with each repeated
attempt consuming another full planning evaluation. What is needed is not
simply access to more previous agents, but explicit edit–outcome
context: a concise record of what was changed, what behavior changed as a
result, and which changes succeeded, regressed, or proved ineffective.

Second, improvement must be targeted at the specific constraints the
agent violates. Planning success depends on the correctness of many
interdependent decisions: a single infeasible transfer, invalid coupon, or
unmet product requirement can invalidate an otherwise valid itinerary or
cart. Consequently, a broad modification that improves behavior in general may
still leave the failing case unresolved unless it corrects the particular
constraint violation responsible for failure. Without explicit guidance,
open-ended editing may instead modify an unrelated harness component or make
a diffuse change that does not address the observed error. In HGM’s shopping
search, 19.4% of edits target a constraint category that the parent never
failed (Figure 1, middle), and in 64.2% of edits the
child retains its parent’s dominant failure category
(Figure 1, right). These results suggest that efficient
self-improvement for planning requires both learning from prior edit outcomes
and directing new edits toward the specific constraint failures observed in
the current agent.

We introduce the Huxley–Gödel Machine Dual (HGM-Dual), a
diagnostic-guided framework for budget-efficient agent harness evolution. First, it represents
agentic evolution as structured contextual information. This agent-evolution context records what was changed, which
behaviors improved or regressed, and which attempted modifications were
ineffective. The editor therefore receives explicit edit–outcome attribution
instead of treating previous agent snapshots as an unstructured archive. This
context discourages repeated failures and allows subsequent edits to build
systematically on evidence collected throughout the search.

Second, HGM-Dual introduces error-conditioned dual-path child generation. For each selected parent, the framework generates complementary children: a general child, which preserves the broad and open-ended exploration of existing evolutionary methods, and specialized children, which target the harness component most likely responsible for the parent’s observed errors. The general path retains the ability to discover unexpected improvements, while the specialized path provides diagnostic-guided local refinement. Together, the dual paths balance exploration with targeted exploitation and direct the evaluation budget toward more informative regions of the harness search space.

Experiments on travel and shopping planning tasks show that HGM-Dual outperforms the strongest baseline by up to $14.45\%$ relative improvement in case accuracy while matching HGM variants’ final performance with $2\times$ – $4\times$ fewer evaluation budget. These results demonstrate that structured evolutionary context and diagnostic guidance can make harness self-improvement both more effective and substantially more resource-efficient.

## 2 Related Works

Harness Optimization through Self-Improvement.
Recent methods improve frozen LLMs by automatically modifying their agentic harnesses, including prompts, tools, skills, and workflows Khattab et al. (2024); Opsahl-Ong et al. (2024); Zhang et al. (2025); Wang et al. (2023); Chen et al. (2026); Karten et al. (2026); Lou et al. (2026); Zhang et al. (2026a). ADAS Hu et al. (2025), DGM Zhang et al. (2026b), HGM Wang et al. (2026), GEPA Agrawal et al. (2026), and HyperAgents Zhang et al. (2026c) search over agent implementations through iterative or evolutionary self-modification. Meta-Harness Lee et al. (2026) gives an agentic proposer access to the code, scores, and execution traces of prior candidates, while SkillOpt Yang et al. (2026) optimizes a natural-language skill through bounded edits and validation. Collectively, these methods demonstrate that harness optimization can substantially improve agent performance without updating model parameters.

Nevertheless, two challenges remain. First, prior edit experience is either local or excessive: DGM Zhang et al. (2026b) and HGM Wang et al. (2026) primarily generate modifications from the selected parent’s code and evaluation logs, without providing enough context on the effectiveness of all edits attempted. On the other hand, archive-based methods Hu et al. (2025); Lee et al. (2026); Lin et al. (2026); Jiang et al. (2026) may flood the evolution context with numerous previous candidates and raw execution traces. This can obscure useful edit-outcome relationships and lead to repeated ineffective modifications across the search tree. Second, although prior methods guide candidate selection or validation Yang et al. (2026); Wang et al. (2026); Zhang et al. (2026b); Zhang et al. (2026c), edit generation itself remains largely open-ended and may not target the harness component responsible for observed failures. Such weakly targeted exploration can require substantial evaluation resources. HGM-Dual addresses these limitations by maintaining a concise, structured agent-evolution context and by generating both a general child for broad exploration and an error-conditioned specialized child for targeted refinement.

## 3 HGM-Dual

## 3.1 Problem Setup

Agent Harness Optimization.
We consider a frozen language model whose behavior is controlled by an agent harness

 $\mathcal{H}=(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{T},\mathcal{W}),$ 

(1)

where $\mathcal{P}$ specifies the prompt and instructions provided to the model, $\mathcal{T}$ denotes the set of available tools, and $\mathcal{W}$ defines the agentic workflow that governs how the model and tools interact to solve a task. The parameters of the underlying language model remain fixed throughout optimization; only the components of $\mathcal{H}$ are modified.

Given a task $x$ sampled from a task distribution $\mathcal{D}$ , executing the frozen language model under harness $\mathcal{H}$ produces an output $\mathcal{H}(x)$ . We evaluate this output using a task-specific scoring function $J(x,\mathcal{H}(x))$ , which measures the quality of the harness execution on task $x$ . Agent harness optimization seeks a harness that maximizes expected performance over the task distribution:

 $\mathcal{H}^{*}=\arg\max_{\mathcal{H}}\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D}}\left[J\!\left(x,\mathcal{H}(x)\right)\right].$ 

(2)

## HGM.

HGM optimizes an agent harness through
evolutionary self-modification. Starting from an initial harness
 $\mathcal{H}_{0}$ , HGM maintains an archive $\mathcal{A}$ of previously
evaluated harnesses organized as an evolutionary tree
(Figure 2). Each node corresponds to a harness, and
each edge corresponds to a modification that produces a child from its
parent. At each iteration, HGM selects a parent harness
 $\mathcal{H}_{i}\in\mathcal{A}$ using its clade-metaproductivity
criterion, which is designed to favor candidates with the potential to
produce strong descendants. An editor then examines the selected
harness and its evaluation feedback, proposes an open-ended
modification, evaluates the resulting child, and adds it to the
archive. The editor primarily conditions on the selected harness and its current evaluation context
 $\varepsilon_{i}$ :

 $\mathcal{H}^{\prime}=\operatorname{Edit}\left(\mathcal{H}_{i},\varepsilon_{i}\right).$ 

(3)

Thus, the archive records an evolutionary history, but that history is
not explicitly represented as structured edit-outcome evidence for
the editor. Moreover, because the edit is
open-ended, it is not explicitly constrained to address the failures
observed in the selected parent.

Figure 3: Overview of HGM-Dual and one expansion round. Given a selected parent $\mathcal{H}_{i}$ , HGM-Dual constructs
an evolution context $\mathcal{Z}_{i}$ by summarizing edits and outcomes
along its ancestral path $\mathrm{Anc}(\mathcal{H}_{i})$ . The evolution
context is provided to both editing paths: a general editor proposes
an open-ended improvement, while specialized editors target the
highest-ranked errors in the parent’s error profile $\varepsilon_{i}$ .
The resulting children are evaluated under the same protocol, and only
the strongest child is added to the archive.

## HGM-Dual.

HGM-Dual retains HGM’s evolutionary search procedure but changes how each selected parent is expanded (we refer readers to Section A.4 for full integration). As illustrated in
Figure 3, it introduces two mechanisms.
First, rather than conditioning an edit only on the selected harness
 $\mathcal{H}_{i}$ and its evaluation context $\varepsilon_{i}$ ,
HGM-Dual additionally provides an evolution context $\mathcal{Z}_{i}$ 
that summarizes the edits and outcomes along its ancestral lineage:

 $\mathcal{H}^{\prime}=\operatorname{Edit}\left(\mathcal{H}_{i},\varepsilon_{i},\mathcal{Z}_{i}\right).$ 

(4)

Second, HGM-Dual augments HGM’s open-ended child generation with
error-conditioned editing. Each expansion generates both general and error-conditioned children. Thus, HGM-Dual retains HGM’s evolutionary backbone while making edit generation both history-aware and error-aware. We summarize the HGM-Dual expansion process in Algorithm 1 and describe
them in Sections 3.3
and 3.4, respectively.

## 3.3 Agent Evolution as Interpretable Context

An editor observing only the current harness and its evaluation feedback cannot reliably attribute performance changes to prior edits or identify previously failed modifications. Providing the full archive and raw traces would address this limitation but is costly and difficult to interpret. HGM-Dual therefore compresses the relevant evolutionary history into a structured context.

For each non-root harness $\mathcal{H}_{i}$ with parent $\mathcal{H}_{p_{i}}$ , we define an evolution record

 $\zeta_{i}=\left(d_{i},\,u_{i},\,\Delta\widehat{J}_{i},\,\Delta\mathbf{f}_{i},\,b_{i}\right),$ 

(5)

where $d_{i}$ summarizes the edit, $u_{i}$ records functionality added, removed, or invoked,

 $\Delta\widehat{J}_{i}=\widehat{J}(\mathcal{H}_{i})-\widehat{J}(\mathcal{H}_{p_{i}}),$ 

(6)

measures its performance impact, and

 $\Delta\mathbf{f}_{i}=\mathbf{f}_{i}-\mathbf{f}_{p_{i}}$ 

(7)

captures changes in constraint-level errors. Finally, $b_{i}$ summarizes the observed behavioral consequences. Thus, $\zeta_{i}$ links each modification to its functional, performance, and behavioral effects.

Let $\operatorname{Anc}(\mathcal{H}_{i})=(\mathcal{H}_{0},\ldots,\mathcal{H}_{p_{i}},\mathcal{H}_{i})$ denote the ordered ancestral path to $\mathcal{H}_{i}$ . We define its lineage context as

 $\mathcal{Z}_{i}=\operatorname{Summarize}\left(\left\{\zeta_{j}:\mathcal{H}_{j}\in\operatorname{Anc}(\mathcal{H}_{i})\right\}\right).$ 

(8)

This bounded summary preserves which functionality was introduced and used, which edits produced improvements or regressions, and how behavior evolved along the lineage, while consolidating redundant records.

When expanding $\mathcal{H}_{i}$ , the editor receives its source, current evaluation context $\varepsilon_{i}$ , and lineage context $\mathcal{Z}_{i}$ . This compact memory helps avoid repeating failed changes, retain complementary improvements, and identify interactions between edits that are not apparent from the current score alone.

## 3.4 Error-Conditioned Dual-Path Child Generation

The space of possible modifications to $\mathcal{P}$ , $\mathcal{T}$ , and $\mathcal{W}$ is large, and unrestricted generation frequently produces plausible but irrelevant edits. HGM-Dual therefore uses two complementary editing paths: a general path for broad exploration and an error-conditioned path for targeting verified weaknesses. To avoid specializing to noisy failure signals, the error-conditioned path is enabled only when a one-time pre-search test shows sufficient task-level agreement ( $\kappa\geq 0.4$ ) and dominant-category stability ( $\rho_{B}\geq 0.7$ ); otherwise, expansion uses only the general path.

For a selected harness $\mathcal{H}_{i}$ , let

 $\varepsilon_{i}=\left(J(\mathcal{H}_{i}),\mathbf{f}_{i},\mathcal{S}_{i}\right),\qquad\mathbf{f}_{i}=\left(f_{i,c}\right)_{c\in\mathcal{C}},$ 

(9)

denote its evaluation context, where $J(\mathcal{H}_{i})$ is its evaluation score, $\mathbf{f}_{i}$ is the vector of
evaluator-category error frequencies,
 $\mathcal{C}$ is the set of evaluator-defined error categories, and
 $\mathcal{S}_{i}$ contains representative execution traces and evaluator
feedback. The general child is produced by

 $\mathcal{H}^{\mathrm{gen}}_{i}=\operatorname{Edit}_{\mathrm{gen}}\left(\mathcal{H}_{i};\varepsilon_{i},\mathcal{Z}_{i}\right).$ 

(10)

This editor may modify any harness component and is instructed to identify a broadly useful improvement from the task specification, current behavior, and evolutionary history. It therefore preserves the open-ended character of evolutionary search and can discover changes that are not associated with a single evaluator-defined failure.

For the specialized path, HGM-Dual selects two target errors from the observed error profile of $\mathcal{H}_{i}$ . The two target error categories are selected as

 $c_{i,k}^{*}=\operatorname{TopK}_{c\in\mathcal{C}}\left(f_{i,c},\,k\right),\qquad k\in\{1,2\},$ 

(11)

where $f_{i,c}$ denotes the measured frequency of error category $c$ for $\mathcal{H}_{i}$ . Here, $c_{i,1}^{*}$ and $c_{i,2}^{*}$ denote the first- and second-highest-ranked error categories, respectively.

For each selected target error, an error-conditioned child is generated as

 $\mathcal{H}^{\mathrm{err}}_{i,k}=\operatorname{Edit}_{\mathrm{err}}\left(\mathcal{H}_{i};c_{i,k}^{*},\mathcal{S}_{i}^{c_{i,k}^{*}},\mathcal{Z}_{i}\right),\qquad k\in\{1,2\},$ 

(12)

where $\mathcal{S}_{i}^{c_{i,k}^{*}}$ contains the traces and evaluator feedback associated with target error $c_{i,k}^{*}$ . For each target, the specialized editor is asked to diagnose the corresponding failure, identify the responsible prompt, tool, or workflow behavior, and propose a focused correction while preserving capabilities that are already successful.

All three children are executed on the same optimization set and evaluated with the same reward function $\widehat{J}$ . HGM-Dual retains

 $\mathcal{H}^{+}_{i}=\arg\max_{\mathcal{H}\in\left\{\mathcal{H}^{\mathrm{gen}}_{i},\mathcal{H}^{\mathrm{err}}_{i,1},\mathcal{H}^{\mathrm{err}}_{i,2}\right\}}\widehat{J}(\mathcal{H}).$ 

(13)

The best child, its evaluation context, and edit attribution relative to $\mathcal{H}_{i}$ are added to the archive; the others are discarded. This dual-path procedure balances exploration and exploitation: the general child enables broad improvements, while specialized children target prominent failures. Retaining only the best child limits archive growth and focuses the search budget on promising harnesses.

Benchmark.
We evaluate HGM-Dual on the DeepPlanning benchmark Zhang et al. (2026d), which consists of two constrained long-horizon planning tasks: travel planning and shopping. Both require maintaining global consistency across many interdependent decisions while satisfying numerous explicit and implicit constraints. In travel planning, the agent uses nine APIs to construct minute-level itineraries of up to seven days, evaluated by 21 feasibility checks across eight dimensions such as temporal, spatial, and budget consistency. In shopping, the agent searches an average of 171 records through 15 APIs over 45 product and user attributes, while jointly selecting products and coupons subject to product, eligibility, threshold, stacking, delivery, and budget constraints. These interacting constraints make locally valid decisions insufficient: the agent must ensure that the complete itinerary or cart remains globally feasible.

Evaluation Metrics. We assess each method along two dimensions. a) task performance: For travel, we report a composite score that measures overall constraint satisfaction and case accuracy that measures the proportion of fully correct cases. For shopping, we report a match score that measures how many ground-truth products are correctly selected and case accuracy that measures exact cart-level matches. b) Compute efficiency: Following HGM, we define the evaluation budget as the number of plans generated during optimization and apply this measure consistently to HGM, HGM variants, and HGM-Dual.

Experimental Setup. The optimization process starts from an initial harness of the task agent (seed agent) with a simple multi-turn loop, a minimal task prompt, and access to the task-specific tools of the benchmark. The agent editor is an LLM agent that updates the harness components (e.g., prompt, workflow, tool, verifier) of the task agent. The objective is to obtain the best-performing task agent by optimizing the harness alone; the underlying model weights are never updated. Information (e.g., code, logs, failing traces) supplied to the editor is gathered deterministically by a fixed procedure rather than through agentic exploration. For both travel planning and shopping tasks, we optimize on $60$ samples and evaluate on $120$ samples ( $60$ unseen samples), so that generalization beyond the optimization set is measured explicitly. Unless otherwise specified, the agent editor uses GPT-5.4 (reasoning effort medium) and the task agent uses GPT-5.4-mini (reasoning effort high).

## Error Categorization.

Error categories are produced by a deterministic, rule-based categorizer, one per domain. In travel, the categories follow the benchmark’s evaluator: each of its eight commonsense dimensions (e.g., time feasibility, business hours) is a category, and violated hard constraints from the query are grouped by the entity they concern (flight, train, hotel, restaurant, attraction, or budget). Shopping considers four product level error categories (missing product, unmet feature, user-profile mismatch, not cheapest) and three cart level error categories (budget, cart not cheapest, coupon).

Baselines. We compare HGM-Dual against ADAS Hu et al. (2025), GEPA Agrawal et al. (2026), HGM Wang et al. (2026), HGM-Customized (Agentic Editor) (HGM-CA) Zhang et al. (2026c), and HGM-Customized (Fixed Context Editor) (HGM-CF). ADAS and GEPA are two LLM driven optimization process to improve agentic system performance, while HGM is a self-referential improvement algorithm in which a task agent edits its own implementation. Since our setting requires non-coding, long-horizon planning, we also consider HGM-CA (HGM-Customized (Agentic Editor)), a domain-customized variant of HGM. HGM-CA augments the original framework with domain-specific editing mechanisms designed to adapt the agent’s prompts, tools, and workflow behavior to the planning setting. In this setting, the editor has agentic access with bash and file read/write tools to navigate task agents source code and logs. Similar to HGM-Dual’s agent editor setting, we also consider HGM-CF (HGM-Customized (Fixed Context Editor)). In this setting, task agents code and logs are gathered deterministically and provided in the context of the agent editor to generate edits. HGM-CF gives HGM the same interface used by HGM-Dual, isolating the effect of agent evolution context and error-conditioned child generation. We do not compare with DGM Zhang et al. (2026b) because HGM has previously been shown to outperform DGM in both task performance and improvement-budget efficiency.

## 4.1 Evaluation

Performance Comparison to Baseline Approaches. Table 1 reports the performance comparison to baselines (HGM, HGM-CA, HGM-CF) at $4000$ evaluation budget. Vanilla HGM barely moves beyond the seed harness on either domain, gaining only $0.60$ composite points on travel and $1.21$ on shopping, and leaving shopping case accuracy unchanged at $50.83$ . This confirms that an editor designed for coding benchmarks transfers poorly to long-horizon planning, and motivates the customized variants. HGM-CF the strongest baseline on all four metrics ( $84.11$ and $86.67$ composite, $56.25$ and $60.83$ case accuracy). HGM-Dual attains the best result on every metric in both domains, improving over HGM-CF by $3.66$ and $2.56$ composite points and by $8.13$ and $5.84$ case accuracy points on travel and shopping, respectively. Notably, the margin is consistently larger in case accuracy than in composite score, indicating that HGM-Dual does not merely accumulate partial credit across checkpoints but converts a larger fraction of plans into fully constraint-satisfying solutions

Budget Efficiency. We have analyzed the budget efficiency of HGM-Dual by evaluating the performance of selected best agents at different evaluation budget steps. We have considered selecting best agents at 1k, 2k, 3k and 4k evaluations and evaluate it on all $120$ instances for both travel and shopping task (Fig. 4(a,b)). On travel (Fig. 4(a)), HGM-CF is slightly ahead at 1k ( $84.9$ vs. $83.8$ ) but then stalls. HGM-CF returns the same agent at 1k, 2k and 3k and ends at $84.1$ . HGM-Dual, in contrast, improves at every checkpoint, overtakes HGM-CF at 2k ( $85.7$ ) and reaches $87.8$ at 4k. On shopping (Fig. 4(b)), HGM-Dual makes most of its gain within the first 1k evaluations, rising from $81.9$ to $90.4$ , and stays near that level through 4k ( $89.2$ ), whereas HGM-CF improves gradually and ends at $86.7$ . The domain-customized variant HGM-CA remains below both methods across the budget range on travel and below HGM-Dual throughout on shopping. Overall, HGM-Dual converts the evaluation budget into performance far more efficiently than HGM-CF: it either keeps improving where HGM-CF plateaus (travel) or reaches HGM-CF’s final score using roughly a quarter of the budget (shopping). Note that HGM-Dual’s rejected child’s evaluation is also counted towards the total evaluation budget used.

Error Profile Analysis. To identify the source of these gains, we decompose the failures of HGM-Dual and HGM-CF’s final agent ( $4$ k budget) on the $60$ held-out task instances (Fig. 4(c,d)). Each row is an evaluator-defined error category, and each marker gives a method’s change in the fraction of tasks failing that category relative to the seed agent; negative values indicate fewer errors. The right-hand column reports the seed agent’s own failure rate. Rows are grouped into commonsense-quality and hard-constraint errors for travel and into product- and cart-level errors for shopping. Categories failed by the seed agent on fewer than $5\%$ of tasks are omitted, filled markers denote statistically significant changes, and the bottom panels report the share of tasks with any error. On travel (Fig. 4(c)), both methods eliminate most time feasibility and itinerary structure errors, the two most frequent categories. However, HGM-Dual is the only method that also removes route consistency errors (from $16.7\%$ of tasks to $1.7\%$ , versus $10.0\%$ for HGM-CF) and sandbox compliance errors (from $10.0\%$ to $2.5\%$ , versus $8.3\%$ ), and it reduces the share of tasks with any error from $73.3\%$ to $34.6\%$ , compared with $47.5\%$ for HGM-CF. On shopping (Fig. 4(d)), the gap is concentrated in the most frequent category, unmet features: HGM-Dual more than halves it (from $24.2\%$ to $10.8\%$ ) whereas HGM-CF leaves it essentially unchanged ( $20.8\%$ ). The advantage of HGM-Dual therefore stems more from resolving frequently appearing failure modes that HGM-CF leaves untouched.

## 5 Conclusion

We introduced HGM-Dual, a diagnostic-guided framework for efficient agent harness optimization. By incorporating structured agent-evolution context, HGM-Dual enables the editor to learn from prior edit outcomes and avoid redundant or ineffective modifications. Its error-conditioned dual-path generation further combines open-ended exploration with targeted refinement of error-relevant harness components. Experiments on travel and shopping planning tasks demonstrate that these complementary mechanisms outperform strong self-improving baselines while substantially reducing the required evaluation budget. Our ablations further show that evolution context provides consistent performance gains, whereas dual-path generation accelerates improvement under constrained budgets. Together, these results demonstrate that explicitly modeling evolutionary history and balancing general with error-targeted search can make agent harness evolution more effective and resource-efficient. Future work may extend the evolution context beyond individual lineages to share useful edit-outcome evidence across the entire search tree. Future work could jointly learn an evolving context and dual-path manager to adapt evidence selection, editing strategies, and resource allocation throughout the search. Finally, evaluating HGM-Dual across broader task domains and online environments would help characterize the transferability and long-term robustness of the evolved harnesses.

## A.1 Edit redundancy

## Edit-redundancy calculation for the shopping task.

We analyze every non-root edit proposal in the search-tree archives of the
shopping task, yielding $N_{\mathrm{HGM}}=67$ proposals and
 $N_{\mathrm{Dual}}=51$ proposals.

Each proposal is tagged using the following seven error categories:

1. 

Coupon correctness: selecting, validating, or applying coupons correctly;

2. 

Profile compliance: satisfying user-profile requirements such as gender or size;

3. 

Shipping constraints: satisfying destination and delivery-time requirements;

4. 

Budget compliance: respecting minimum or maximum spending constraints;

5. 

Cart completeness: including all requested items and excluding unwanted items;

6. 

Exact feature matching: satisfying explicit product attributes such as brand,
color, season, rating, or review distribution; and

7. 

Price optimality: selecting the cheapest valid products.

The tags are determined from each proposal’s optimization goal and proposed
changes using the same rules for both methods. Because an edit can address
multiple errors, the categories are not mutually exclusive. We represent
each proposal by the exact combination of its assigned categories, which we
refer to as its edit pattern. For example,

 $(\text{profile compliance},\,\text{shipping constraints},\,\text{exact feature matching},\,\text{price optimality})$ 

is one edit pattern, whereas

 $(\text{shipping constraints},\,\text{exact feature matching})$ 

is another. Two proposals are considered to have the same edit pattern only
when their complete sets of error categories are identical.

For method $m$ , let $n_{m}(s)$ be the number of proposals assigned to edit
pattern $s$ . Its share of the tree archive is

 $p_{m}(s)=100\times\frac{n_{m}(s)}{N_{m}}.$

The edit patterns are ranked independently for HGM and HGM-Dual in descending
order of $n_{m}(s)$ . The figure reports the share of repeated edit pattern above.

## A.2 Edit-targeting mismatch

We treat each non-initial candidate as an edit and compare it with the candidate from which it was derived. Each edit’s stated objective and proposed changes are mapped to functional error categories, using labels mentioned in Section A.1.

An edit is classified as having no corresponding parent error category when it targets at least one functional component for which no error was reported in its parent. The metric is the fraction of edits satisfying this condition. The dominant error category is the category associated with the largest number of failing evaluation cases.

## A.4 Full HGM-Dual

Algorithm 2 summarizes the complete procedure.
HGM-Dual keeps the search loop of HGM (Wang et al., 2026): at each
iteration, it either expands the archive by modifying a selected
parent or evaluates an existing harness on one additional task.
Both choices are made by Thompson sampling, and at the end of the search
the harness with the highest posterior success rate is returned. The only
change is in the expansion step. Instead of producing a single
open-ended modification, HGM-Dual calls Expand
(Algorithm 1), which builds the evolution
context from the parent’s lineage (Section 3.3),
generates one general and two error-conditioned children
(Section 3.4), and adds only the strongest child to the
archive.

The symbols in Algorithm 2 are as follows. For each
harness $\mathcal{H}$ , $n_{\mathrm{success}}(\mathcal{H})$ and
 $n_{\mathrm{failure}}(\mathcal{H})$ count its passed and failed
evaluation tasks, while $n^{C}_{\mathrm{success}}(\mathcal{H})$ and
 $n^{C}_{\mathrm{failure}}(\mathcal{H})$ aggregate these counts over its
clade, i.e., the subtree rooted at $\mathcal{H}$ . The clade counts
measure how productive the descendants of $\mathcal{H}$ have been and
guide parent selection. The individual counts measure the harness’s own
performance and guide evaluation and final selection. $N_{t}$ is the number
of evaluations spent so far. The archive is expanded when
 $|\mathcal{A}|\leq N_{t}^{\alpha}$ , where $\alpha\in[0,1]$ controls how
quickly the tree widens relative to the evaluation budget. $\tau$ is a
scheduler that increases over the search. It sharpens the sampled Beta
distributions over time, shifting the search from exploration toward
exploitation. $S^{C}(\mathcal{H})$ and $S(\mathcal{H})$ are the
Thompson samples drawn from the clade-level and individual Beta
posteriors, used to select the parent to expand and the harness to
evaluate, respectively. Finally, $I_{\rho}$ is the $\rho$ -percentile of
a harness’s Beta posterior. Returning the harness that maximizes it
favors candidates whose high success rate is supported by sufficient
evaluations.

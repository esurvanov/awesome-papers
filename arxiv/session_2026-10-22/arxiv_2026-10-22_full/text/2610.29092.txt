Abstract

Multilingual large language models are increasingly deployed in settings where visible reasoning traces support verification, education, and safety auditing, yet standard evaluations typically measure only final-answer agreement or holistic response similarity. As a result, they establish outcome consistency without determining whether users across languages receive comparable supporting evidence. We introduce a trace-level divergence framework that compares visible reasoning traces as structured sequences, decomposing agreement into structural similarity, order consistency, and holistic similarity, with pair-specific calibration to enable comparison across languages. Across three reasoning benchmarks, seven languages, and six frontier LLMs, the natural baseline—embedding each whole response and comparing the two by cosine—systematically overstates agreement: $52.1\%$ – $67.3\%$ of examples in the top quartile of holistic similarity are not in the top quartile of structural similarity. This dissociation is stable across sentence encoders and step segmentations, and blind bilingual annotation studies in two English-anchored language pairs show that human judgments align with structural similarity rather than with the holistic baseline. A same-language control shows that the level of divergence is language-specific: two English traces for paraphrases of the same question agree more closely than an English trace and its counterparts in other languages. Our results show that answer-level and holistic evaluations can substantially overestimate the consistency of multilingual explanatory artifacts, motivating trace-level evaluation for reliable multilingual deployment.

## 1 Introduction

Large language models (LLMs) are increasingly deployed in settings where they produce not only final answers but also visible reasoning traces intended to justify those answers. These traces are consumed by users, educators, auditors, and process-supervision systems to understand, verify, and improve model behavior (Wei et al., 2022). As multilingual LLMs become widely deployed, an implicit expectation follows: semantically equivalent questions posed in different languages should produce not only compatible answers but also broadly comparable supporting explanations. In this paper, we study the visible chain of thought emitted by the model, not its hidden internal computation. A chain of thought need not faithfully expose the model’s internal reasoning process (Turpin et al., 2023; Lanham et al., 2023; Chen et al., 2025), and we make no claim that it does. Rather, we study the explanatory artifact that downstream users, verifiers, and chain-of-thought monitors directly observe and act upon; our claims concern what that artifact contains in each language, not whether it reflects the computation behind it.

Existing multilingual evaluations largely measure outcome consistency: whether translated prompts produce the same or correct final answer (Shi et al., 2022; Xuan et al., 2025). More recent approaches compare complete responses using holistic semantic similarity, but still collapse an entire reasoning trace into a single representation. As a result, two multilingual responses may be judged equivalent even when they expose different intermediate claims, assumptions, problem decompositions, or execution order. Some of these differences may simply reflect natural linguistic variation; others may represent substantively different supporting evidence. Existing evaluations cannot distinguish between them. We therefore argue that multilingual systems should also be evaluated for evidentiary consistency: whether users receive comparable supporting evidence for the same conclusion regardless of the language they ask in.

To address this problem, we introduce a trace-level divergence framework that compares multilingual reasoning traces as structured sequences rather than as single holistic responses. Our framework aligns step-structured chains of thought using multilingual sentence embeddings and decomposes cross-lingual trace consistency into three complementary metrics: structural similarity $S$ , measuring preservation of reasoning content; order consistency $\tau$ , measuring preservation of reasoning order; and holistic similarity $H$ , a whole-response baseline. Because multilingual sentence embeddings exhibit language-pair-dependent similarity floors and ceilings, a pair-specific calibration rescales sentence similarities onto a readable scale within each language pair before aggregation.

We evaluate the proposed framework on three reasoning benchmarks, seven languages, and six frontier LLMs spanning four model families. We find that whole-answer similarity systematically overstates agreement between multilingual reasoning traces. Across all model–dataset evaluations, holistic similarity remains consistently high ( $H=0.770$ – $0.857$ ), while structural similarity is substantially lower ( $S=0.502$ – $0.629$ ), with order consistency lower still ( $\tau=0.307$ – $0.470$ ), even when both language outputs produce the same verified-correct answer. Rank-based analyses reach the same conclusion: within each model–dataset evaluation and language pair, $52.1\%$ – $67.3\%$ of examples in the top quartile of holistic similarity are not also in the top quartile of structural similarity. Finally, blind bilingual annotation studies in two English-anchored language pairs show that human judgments of reasoning-trace equivalence align with structural similarity rather than holistic similarity, indicating that whole-response embeddings systematically overestimate agreement in the explanatory artifacts multilingual users actually observe.

Further analyses locate the divergence. In a same-language control, where each question is paraphrased in English and run through the identical pipeline, two English traces agree more closely than an English trace and its counterparts in other languages in every cell tested, although the holistic–structural dissociation itself also appears within English. Within a trace, alignment is weakest at the step stating the shared answer, and divergence grows on harder items. Throughout, we read low structural or order similarity as divergence, not failure.

Our contributions are threefold. First, we formulate multilingual evaluation as the problem of measuring the consistency of visible reasoning traces, complementing traditional outcome-based evaluation. Second, we introduce a calibrated trace-level framework that decomposes agreement into content preservation, execution order, and holistic similarity. Third, through a comprehensive empirical study, we show that answer-level and holistic evaluations systematically overstate the consistency of multilingual explanatory artifacts, motivating trace-level evaluation for reliable multilingual deployment.

## 2 Problem Formulation

We study whether multilingual LLMs produce consistent visible reasoning traces when given semantically equivalent prompts in different languages. Our goal is to evaluate two complementary notions of consistency: (i) whether multilingual prompts produce the same final answer, and (ii) whether they produce comparable supporting explanations. The former captures outcome consistency; the latter captures trace consistency. This section formalizes both notions and introduces the calibration required to compare multilingual reasoning traces across language pairs.

Setup.
Let $f_{\theta}$ denote a fixed LLM and let $\mathcal{Q}=\{q_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}$ be a set of reasoning problems specified at the level of semantic intent. For each $q_{i}$ , we collect $K$ semantic-preserving translations $\mathcal{L}_{i}=\{\ell_{i,1},\ldots,\ell_{i,K}\}$ and obtain responses

 $\displaystyle y_{i,k}$ 
 $\displaystyle=f_{\theta}(\ell_{i,k}).$ 

Each response is parsed into a final answer $\alpha_{i,k}$ and a step-structured visible reasoning trace

 $\displaystyle r_{i,k}$ 
 $\displaystyle=(s_{i,k,1},\ldots,s_{i,k,t_{i,k}}),$ 

where $t_{i,k}$ denotes the number of reasoning steps. Each step is itself a sequence of sentences,

 $\displaystyle s_{i,k,t}$ 
 $\displaystyle=(u_{i,k,t,1},\ldots,u_{i,k,t,n_{i,k,t}}),$ 

where $n_{i,k,t}$ is the number of sentences in step $t$ . Let $\alpha_{i}^{\star}$ denote the reference answer.

## 2.1 Outcome and Trace Consistency

We formalize multilingual consistency at two complementary levels. Outcome consistency asks whether translated prompts produce the same final answer. Trace consistency asks whether they produce comparable visible reasoning traces. These notions are complementary: multilingual responses may reach the same answer while exposing different supporting claims, decompositions, or execution order.

1. Outcome Consistency.
Following prior multilingual evaluation work (Qi et al., 2023; Shi et al., 2022), we define

 $\displaystyle\Delta_{\text{acc}}(q_{i})$ 
 $\displaystyle=\max_{k}\mathbf{1}[\alpha_{i,k}\neq\alpha_{i}^{\star}]$ 

 $\displaystyle\quad-\min_{k}\mathbf{1}[\alpha_{i,k}\neq\alpha_{i}^{\star}]\;\in\;\{0,1\}.$ 

(1)

Here, $\Delta_{\text{acc}}=0$ iff all language variants are uniformly correct or uniformly incorrect, and $\Delta_{\text{acc}}=1$ otherwise. While this captures multilingual agreement at the level of outcomes, it provides no information about whether the accompanying visible reasoning traces remain consistent across languages.

2. Trace Divergence.
To quantify divergence between visible reasoning traces, we define

 $\displaystyle D_{\text{trace}}(q_{i})$ 
 $\displaystyle=\frac{2}{K(K-1)}\!\!\sum_{1\leq k<k^{\prime}\leq K}\!\!d(r_{i,k},r_{i,k^{\prime}}).$ 

(2)

Rather than a single scalar, we represent trace divergence using three complementary dimensions,

 $\displaystyle d(r_{a},r_{b})$ 
 $\displaystyle=\Big(1-S(r_{a},r_{b}),\;\tfrac{1-\tau(r_{a},r_{b})}{2},$ 

 $\displaystyle\qquad\quad\;1-H(r_{a},r_{b})\Big).$ 

(3)

Thus, Eq. (2) yields a three-dimensional expectation over all language pairs. The three components capture complementary aspects of multilingual trace consistency:

• 

Structural similarity $S(\cdot,\cdot)\in[0,1]$ : preservation of step-level reasoning content across traces (§ 3.3).

• 

Order consistency $\tau(\cdot,\cdot)\in[-1,1]$ : preservation of the ordering of corresponding reasoning steps (§ 3.4).

• 

Whole-answer cosine $H(\cdot,\cdot)\in[-1,1]$ : a holistic embedding-based baseline that compares complete responses without localizing where divergence occurs (formally defined in Appendix B.6).

Raw multilingual cosines carry language-pair-specific floors and ceilings, so the sentence similarities behind $S$ and $\tau$ are calibrated per language pair before aggregation (§ 3.2, Appendix A).

## 3 Method

Given two visible reasoning traces produced for semantically equivalent prompts in different languages, our framework measures whether they preserve the same step-level content and ordering. The pipeline first parses each response into steps (§ 3.1), calibrates multilingual sentence similarities for each language pair (§ 3.2), and constructs a step-pair similarity matrix through two-level greedy alignment (§ 3.3). This matrix yields the structural similarity score $S$ and the order-consistency score $\tau$ (§ 3.4). We compute the holistic baseline $H$ separately over complete responses (Appendix B.6).

## 3.1 Step Parsing

We segment each response using line-initial step headers. The parser first matches the prompted English pattern Step $n$ :, followed by its equivalents in the seven evaluated languages, a plain numbered-list pattern, and finally paragraph boundaries. The same ordered rule set is applied to every language, and parsing terminates at the final-answer line. Each non-empty step is further divided into sentences using a Unicode-aware regular expression. No LLM is used during parsing. Appendix B.1 provides the complete parsing hierarchy and validation details.

## 3.2 Per-Pair Calibration

Raw multilingual cosine similarities $c_{a,b}(u,v)=\cos(\phi(u),\phi(v))$ , for a multilingual sentence encoder $\phi$ , exhibit language-pair-specific floors and ceilings. For each language pair $(a,b)$ we therefore estimate an unrelated-sentence floor $\widehat{\mathrm{floor}}_{a,b}$ , the mean cosine between sentences drawn from traces of different questions, and a self-translation ceiling $\widehat{\mathrm{ceiling}}_{a,b}$ , a high percentile of the cosine between trace sentences and their LLM translations into the other language (estimators and constants in Appendix B.2). Each raw sentence cosine is then rescaled as

 $\displaystyle\widetilde{c}_{a,b}(u,v)$ 
 $\displaystyle=\operatorname{clip}\!\left(\frac{c_{a,b}(u,v)-\widehat{\mathrm{floor}}_{a,b}}{\widehat{\mathrm{ceiling}}_{a,b}-\widehat{\mathrm{floor}}_{a,b}},0,1\right).$ 

(4)

Calibration aligns the empirical endpoints of each language pair, so that within a pair $0$ marks the unrelated-sentence floor and $1$ the self-translation ceiling; it does not assume that intermediate calibrated values are semantically identical across pairs. Within a pair it is a fixed monotone map, so it cannot reorder instances inside a pair, and the within-pair rank comparisons that carry our results are unaffected by it (§ 5.2). Appendix B.2 gives the estimators, the motivation for the constants, and translator-independence checks.

## 3.3 Two-Level Greedy Alignment

A trace is an ordered sequence of steps, and each step may contain multiple sentences. We therefore align traces hierarchically: an inner alignment compares sentences to score each step pair, and an outer alignment aggregates step-pair scores into a trace-level structural similarity.

Inner sentence alignment.

For steps $s_{a,i}$ and $s_{b,j}$ containing $n_{a,i}$ and $n_{b,j}$ sentences, respectively, let
 $\widetilde{\mathbf{C}}\in[0,1]^{n_{a,i}\times n_{b,j}}$ 
contain the calibrated sentence similarities from
Eq. (4). We define the precision-style score $p^{\mathrm{in}}_{i,j}=\frac{1}{n_{a,i}}\sum_{m=1}^{n_{a,i}}\max_{n}\widetilde{\mathbf{C}}_{m,n}$ , the recall-style score $r^{\mathrm{in}}_{i,j}=\frac{1}{n_{b,j}}\sum_{n=1}^{n_{b,j}}\max_{m}\widetilde{\mathbf{C}}_{m,n}$ , and the step-pair similarity $\sigma_{i,j}=F_{1}(p^{\mathrm{in}}_{i,j},r^{\mathrm{in}}_{i,j})$ .
This BERTScore-style greedy alignment (Zhang et al., 2020) is high only when the sentences in each step are covered by the other.

Outer step alignment.

Collect the step-pair similarities into
 $\mathbf{A}\in[0,1]^{t_{a}\times t_{b}}$ , where
 $\mathbf{A}_{i,j}=\sigma_{i,j}$ . The structural similarity is

 $\displaystyle S(r_{a},r_{b})$ 
 $\displaystyle=F_{1}\!\left(\frac{1}{t_{a}}\sum_{i}\max_{j}\mathbf{A}_{i,j},\;\frac{1}{t_{b}}\sum_{j}\max_{i}\mathbf{A}_{i,j}\right).$ 

(5)

The bidirectional maxima permit many-to-one correspondences, allowing traces to express the same content at different step granularities. Because $S$ ignores the locations of matched steps, it measures content preservation independently of order.

## 3.4 Soft Kendall- $\tau$ Order Consistency

Structural similarity does not account for the positions of matched steps. We therefore derive an order-consistency score from the same step-pair matrix $\mathbf{A}$ .

Soft assignment.

For the directed comparison $a\rightarrow b$ , we sharpen the step similarities and normalize each source-step row:

 $\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathbf{A}}_{i,j}$ 
 $\displaystyle=\mathbf{A}_{i,j}^{\,3},$ 

(6)

 $\displaystyle P^{a\rightarrow b}_{i,j}$ 
 $\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\dfrac{\widetilde{\mathbf{A}}_{i,j}}{\sum_{j^{\prime}}\widetilde{\mathbf{A}}_{i,j^{\prime}}},&\sum_{j^{\prime}}\widetilde{\mathbf{A}}_{i,j^{\prime}}>0,\\[8.53581pt]
\dfrac{1}{t_{b}},&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}$ 

(7)

Thus, $P^{a\rightarrow b}_{i,j}$ represents the soft correspondence between source step $i$ and target step $j$ . Sharpening concentrates mass on stronger matches without imposing a hard one-to-one assignment. Appendix D.8 reports sensitivity to the sharpening choice.

Order mass.

For each ordered source-step pair $i<k$ , we accumulate probability mass according to whether their matched target positions are concordant, discordant, or tied:

 $\displaystyle M^{a\rightarrow b}_{r}$ 
 $\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\binom{t_{a}}{2}}\sum_{i<k}\sum_{(j,\ell)\in\Omega_{r}}P^{a\rightarrow b}_{i,j}P^{a\rightarrow b}_{k,\ell},$ 

 $\displaystyle\qquad r\in\{\mathrm{con},\mathrm{dis},\mathrm{tie}\},$ 

(8)

where $\Omega_{\mathrm{con}}=\{(j,\ell):j<\ell\}$ , $\Omega_{\mathrm{dis}}=\{(j,\ell):j>\ell\}$ , and $\Omega_{\mathrm{tie}}=\{(j,\ell):j=\ell\}$ . The directed order score is

 $\displaystyle\tau^{a\rightarrow b}$ 
 $\displaystyle=\frac{M^{a\rightarrow b}_{\mathrm{con}}-M^{a\rightarrow b}_{\mathrm{dis}}}{M^{a\rightarrow b}_{\mathrm{con}}+M^{a\rightarrow b}_{\mathrm{dis}}+M^{a\rightarrow b}_{\mathrm{tie}}}.$ 

(9)

Because this score is directional, we symmetrize it:

 $\displaystyle\tau(r_{a},r_{b})$ 
 $\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left(\tau^{a\rightarrow b}+\tau^{b\rightarrow a}\right),$ 

(10)

using $\mathbf{A}$ for $a\rightarrow b$ and
 $\mathbf{A}^{\top}$ for $b\rightarrow a$ . The score is undefined when either trace has fewer than two steps, when either trace is empty, or when either directed denominator is zero. We interpret $\tau$ jointly with $S$ : high $\tau$ with low $S$ indicates that the matched content is ordered consistently but structurally incomplete.

## 4 Experimental Setup

We evaluate on three multilingual reasoning benchmarks: MMLU-ProX (Xuan et al., 2025), StrategyQA (Geva et al., 2021), and SciBench (Wang et al., 2024). Each run uses seven languages (Arabic, English, Spanish, Hindi, Russian, Vietnamese, and Chinese), yielding 21 unordered language pairs per model–dataset evaluation. The benchmark suite spans distinct reasoning regimes: MMLU-ProX tests multiple-choice academic reasoning, where answer options and domain knowledge constrain the response; StrategyQA tests yes/no commonsense reasoning, where premise interpretation and implicit world knowledge are central; and SciBench tests quantitative STEM reasoning, where traces often combine formula selection, unit conversion, and numerical computation. This spread lets us distinguish dataset effects from model-specific behavior under fixed metric definitions.

We evaluate six models from four families: Claude Opus 4.6, Claude Haiku 4.5, DeepSeek-V3.2, GPT-OSS-120B, Qwen3-235B, and Qwen3-32B, sampling 500 problems per dataset–model run. Trace metrics are reported only after the answer- and trace-validity cascade; one-language-correct cases contribute only to $\Delta_{\mathrm{acc}}$ (Appendix C.1).

Metrics follow § 3, using LaBSE (Feng et al., 2022) for step scores, BGE-M3 (Chen et al., 2024) for $H$ , and soft Kendall- $\tau$ sharpening power 3. Table 1 reports problem-level $95\%$ bootstrap confidence intervals (2,000 resamples), resampling each question’s 21 language pairs jointly. Appendices C.3, C.4, D.8, and D.5 provide filtering details, audits, and ablations.

## 5.1 Correct Answers Still Hide Trace Divergence

We first test whether agreement on the final answer implies agreement in the emitted reasoning trace by restricting to language pairs where both answers are verified correct. If multilingual reasoning were invariant, such pairs would exhibit high structural similarity $S$ , high order consistency $\tau$ , and only a small gap between the holistic score $H$ and the step-level score $S$ .

However, across the evaluations in Table 1, $H$ remains consistently high ( $0.770$ – $0.857$ ), while $S$ is substantially lower ( $0.502$ – $0.629$ ) and $\tau$ lower still ( $0.307$ – $0.470$ ). The same pattern holds on MMLU-ProX: across six 500-sample evaluations (21 language pairs), $S{=}0.513$ – $0.569$ , $\tau{=}0.348$ – $0.469$ , and $H{=}0.787$ – $0.800$ .

Taken together, these results contradict the invariance hypothesis. Even when answers agree, reasoning traces diverge: responses remain globally similar as full strings (high $H$ ) while differing in step content ( $S$ ) and, more markedly, in step ordering ( $\tau$ ), particularly on MMLU-ProX.

## 5.2 Holistic Similarity Overstates Consistency

Figure 1: Rank-based holistic-to-structural mismatch across all 18 model–dataset evaluations. Each cell reports the share of pair–question instances in the top quartile of whole-answer cosine $H$ that are not also in the top quartile of structural score $S$ , with quartiles computed within the same evaluation and language pair. Appendix D.4 reports the per-dataset breakdown, including the stricter share of top- $H$ instances falling in the bottom structural quartile.

We test whether a whole-answer embedding baseline can substitute for step-level alignment by comparing $H$ against $S$ . Because $H$ uses BGE-M3 (8K context) while $S$ uses LaBSE for trace-length reasons (Appendix B.6), we treat $H{-}S$ as a diagnostic contrast and rely on a scale-free rank comparison. If a holistic embedding captures cross-lingual reasoning structure, high $H$ should coincide with high $S$ within the same evaluation and language pair.

In practice, Table 1 shows aggregate $H{-}S$ gaps of $0.152$ to $0.327$ across the 21 language-pair evaluations; we do not interpret these absolute magnitudes as calibrated distances given the encoder asymmetry. Figure 1 provides the scale-free rank comparison (computed within each (evaluation, pair, encoder) cell, hence invariant to per-pair monotone rescaling): within each evaluation and language pair, $52.1\%$ – $67.3\%$ of top-quartile $H$ examples fall outside the top quartile of $S$ , and a nontrivial fraction land in the bottom structural quartile. Appendix D.4 reports the full rank table and representative raw point-cloud panels.

This comparison is unaffected by the per-pair calibration: quartile thresholds are computed within each language pair, where calibration is a single monotone map, and recomputing the statistic on the uncalibrated scale reproduces it to four decimal places on all three Claude Opus 4.6 evaluations (Appendix B.2.4). The reported mismatch is therefore a property of the traces rather than of the calibration constants.

These results do not support replacing step-level alignment with a holistic baseline. While $H$ serves as a coarse semantic signal, it is structurally under-informative, failing to detect missing, merged, or reordered reasoning steps: a consistency check built on whole-response cosine returns a high score on precisely the pairs where step content and order have moved.

In practice, Table 2 shows that $\tau$ and $H$ are nearly uncorrelated, while $S$ has only moderate independent association with both. The same separation holds at the aggregate level: evaluations with similar $S$ can exhibit markedly different order consistency, and large $H{-}S$ gaps or top- $H$ mismatches do not align with $\tau$ . Appendix D.3 provides a complementary cross-dataset check (Claude Opus 4.6): language-pair rankings of $H$ replicate strongly across datasets ( $\rho=0.78$ – $0.98$ ), $\tau$ rankings replicate moderately ( $\rho=0.52$ – $0.76$ ), while $S$ is more task-sensitive ( $\rho=0.11$ – $0.55$ ).

These results reject the single-score hypothesis. The three metrics capture complementary aspects of reasoning: $S$ reflects step coverage, $\tau$ captures execution order, and $H$ measures only holistic semantic similarity. The cross-dataset pattern further highlights this distinction: holistic similarity is relatively stable across tasks, order behaviour is partly language-pair dependent, and structural coverage is most sensitive to how reasoning is elicited. Collapsing these axes would obscure both task- and model-specific forms of trace divergence.

## 5.4 Divergence Is Larger Across Languages Than Within One

Every comparison so far crosses a language boundary, so on its own it cannot separate divergence caused by changing language from ordinary variation in generated reasoning. As a same-language control, we rewrite each question as a meaning-preserving English paraphrase and run it through the identical pipeline as a condition $\text{en}_{\text{para}}$ that receives the same appended instruction as a translated condition, for GPT-OSS-120B and DeepSeek-V3.2 on all three benchmarks, about 100 questions each (Appendix H). Pairing within question, we compare $S(\text{en},\text{en}_{\text{para}})$ with the same question’s mean $S$ over its English-anchored cross-lingual pairs. ## 5.5 Where Divergence Appears and What It Signals

Position. Scoring each English step by its best match in the other trace (English-anchored pairs, all 18 evaluations; Appendix I.1), alignment is weakest at the end: the final step is less similar than the middle steps by $0.042$ ( $95\%$ CI $[0.038,0.046]$ ; lower in 16 of 18 evaluations), and the gap widens to $0.058$ when both traces have the same number of steps, so it is not an unmatched extra step. Every pair here reaches the same correct answer; the step stating it is the least aligned. Where traces first part depends on the task, in an order stable across thresholds: SciBench and MMLU-ProX traces often part at the setup step, StrategyQA traces mid-trace. The profile has the same shape in all six target languages, which differ only in level.

Difficulty and correctness. Divergence is larger on harder items: indexing difficulty by the number $k$ of the seven languages that answer correctly, mean $S$ over correct EN–ZH and EN–ES pairs rises from $0.526$ at $k=2$ to $0.579$ at $k=7$ ( $\rho=0.10$ , $p<10^{-25}$ ; Appendix I.2). Scoring the pairs the main analyses exclude, among answer-agreeing pairs the $225$ that share a wrong answer have lower $S$ than the $11{,}176$ that share the correct one (Cliff’s $\delta=+0.293$ , $95\%$ CI $[+0.217,+0.366]$ ), while $H$ does not separate them ( $\delta=-0.027$ , $[-0.114,+0.055]$ ). Against correct pairs of equal $k$ , however, the $S$ contrast falls to $+0.073$ ( $[-0.061,+0.209]$ ): low $S$ marks hard items, on which shared errors concentrate, rather than errors as such.

Reading a low score. Low $S$ or $\tau$ is divergence, not failure: two correct traces can legitimately differ, and the step matrix shows where. It still helps allocate review. Answer agreement cannot flag answer-agreeing pairs that share a wrong answer; ranking such pairs by $S$ within each evaluation and language pair, the least similar quarter contains $44.0\%$ of these errors, against $28.9\%$ for $H$ and $25\%$ at random (paired difference $+15.1$ points, $[+6.2,+23.9]$ ). At a $2\%$ base rate this prioritizes a fixed review budget; it is not an error detector.

## 5.6 Robustness and Human Corroboration

The analyses above read low $S$ as genuine trace-level divergence. We check that this reading does not depend on the choice of sentence encoder or on step segmentation, and that it is corroborated by human judgment. Protocols, sampling, and full statistics are in Appendices D.6, F, and G.

Encoder robustness. Recomputing $S$ and $\tau$ under two alternative sentence encoders—BGE-M3 (568M parameters), the encoder $H$ already uses, so that $H$ and $S$ share an encoder, and a distilled MiniLM (118M)—leaves the rank-based finding intact: across three encoders and three datasets, every evaluation falls between $50.1\%$ and $65.5\%$ of top-quartile- $H$ examples not being top-quartile- $S$ , closely tracking the $52.1\%$ – $67.3\%$ range reported above. Absolute calibrated levels are less stable, with the $H{-}S$ gap widening under one encoder and narrowing under the other. We therefore interpret $S$ and $\tau$ by rank within a fixed encoder rather than by absolute level, and report them that way throughout. Because the framework reads only the emitted traces and a sentence encoder, its requirements are modest: it needs no access to weights or activations—two of the six evaluated models are closed-weight—and the 118M encoder runs on CPU.

Step segmentation. Restricting each evaluation to pairs whose traces have equal step counts lowers the statistic by $1.0$ – $2.9$ points on the three Claude Opus 4.6 evaluations and leaves all 18 evaluations between $49.5\%$ and $71.0\%$ (Appendix D.6); the parser recovers steps from $99.8\%$ – $100\%$ of traces in every language.

Their role is to check that the metric responds to something human readers also see. They cover one model’s traces and two English-anchored pairs, and we do not claim the agreement extends beyond them.

## 6 Related Work

Multilingual reasoning evaluation.
Chain-of-thought prompting elicits step-by-step reasoning (Wei et al., 2022), but multilingual evaluations largely stop at outputs. Shi et al. (2022) measure multilingual CoT accuracy and Barua et al. (2025) extend this to long-form reasoning, while benchmarks such as MMLU-ProX (Xuan et al., 2025) and metrics like RankC (Qi et al., 2023) assess consistency at the level of final answers. Qi et al. (2025) instead control the language a model thinks in and measure the resulting accuracy cost, which is complementary along a distinct axis: they vary the trace language and observe correctness, whereas we hold correctness fixed and measure how far the traces diverge. None of these localizes divergence within a trace.

Trace-level reasoning evaluation.
ROSCOE (Golovneva et al., 2023) scores reasoning chains at step level in monolingual settings, and we extend that perspective to the cross-lingual case, where similarity must be calibrated per language pair. ROSCOE is not an available baseline here: it scores a single chain against a reference to judge that chain’s quality, whereas we compare two equally valid traces whose quality is held fixed by the validity cascade, and its monolingual encoders would have to be replaced by a multilingual one—reducing the comparison to the uncalibrated step cosines our calibration exists to correct (Appendix C.4). Concurrent work (Zhao et al., 2026) studies cross-lingual transfer of rationales, but without step- or order-level alignment.

Faithfulness of the visible trace.
A separate line of work asks whether an emitted chain of thought reflects the computation that produced the answer: models can rationalize a predetermined answer (Turpin et al., 2023; Lanham et al., 2023; Chen et al., 2025), and consequential computation can leave no interpretable trace in the output tokens at all (Baherwani et al., 2026). Our measurement is orthogonal to this question: we compare two emitted traces and make no claim that either exhausts internal computation. The visible trace is nonetheless what verification, education, and chain-of-thought monitoring pipelines consume (Korbak et al., 2025), so our claim concerns a monitor’s input, not its faithfulness: whatever a monitor extracts from a trace, it extracts from a different artifact in each language. This needs only the emitted text, so the framework also applies to closed-weight models.

Multilingual embeddings and alignment.
We use LaBSE (Feng et al., 2022) for sentence embeddings and BGE-M3 (Chen et al., 2024) for holistic similarity. Our alignment extends embedding-based matching (Zhang et al., 2020) to sentence- and step-level structure, and uses a soft Kendall- $\tau$ inspired by continuous relaxations of permutations (Mena et al., 2018; Grover et al., 2019). Appendix B.8 gives a detailed comparison with prior step-level metrics.

## 7 Conclusion

We show that agreement on final answers does not imply agreement in the reasoning traces a model emits across languages: holistic similarity can remain high even when step-level structure and order diverge. The consequence is at the level of the artifact a user reads: for the same correct answer, the supporting evidence a reader is given can depend on the language they asked in—steps another reader never sees, or the same steps in a different order—more than on how the question is worded within one language. It appears in every one of the $21$ language pairs we evaluate, including the typologically closest. By decomposing trace consistency into structural similarity $S$ , order consistency $\tau$ , and holistic similarity $H$ , our framework reveals complementary axes that single-score evaluations obscure and locates where traces part. The rank-based finding is stable across sentence encoders and step segmentations, and small blind annotation studies on one model’s traces in two English-anchored language pairs are consistent with it. Because the dissociation also appears between two English traces, the decomposition extends to process-level consistency beyond cross-lingual settings, including across paraphrases, prompts, and decoding strategies.

## Limitations

Visible traces, not internal computation. A chain of thought need not be a faithful record of the computation that produced the answer (Turpin et al., 2023; Lanham et al., 2023; Chen et al., 2025), and we link the divergence we measure to no internal quantity. The visible trace is what verification, education, and auditing pipelines consume, and that is the scope of our claims. Activation-level analysis would address faithfulness directly, but it would exclude closed-weight models, including two of the six we evaluate.

Ranks, not levels. What survives an encoder change is rank structure rather than absolute scale (Appendix G), and per-pair calibration aligns only the endpoints of the similarity scale. We therefore compare $S$ and $\tau$ by rank within a fixed encoder and read absolute cross-pair values as descriptive only. The one cross-pair comparison of levels we draw, the same-language control of § 5.4, is paired within question and its calibration exposure is measured, but it remains a level comparison. Ceiling estimates additionally rely on LLM translation, whose co-dependence with the evaluated model we bound in Appendix D.1.

Scope of the evidence. The human validation covers two language pairs, both including English, with $60$ verified-correct trace pairs each, all from one model; agreement is moderate, which attenuates rather than inflates the reported correlations. We do not establish that the metrics track human judgment for other models or for pairs without English. The same-language control covers two of the six models, about $100$ questions per benchmark, and one paraphrase per question, and it does not resample an identical prompt at non-zero temperature, which would isolate decoding variation; its effect is unanimous in direction but varies about threefold in size across cells. Step boundaries are verified automatically and by a manual spot check too small to bound the residual error rate (Appendix B.1.1).

Divergence and outcomes. The association between low $S$ and shared wrong answers rests on $225$ pairs from two language pairs and is largely carried by item difficulty (§ 5.5). We draw no causal conclusion from it, and we do not show that divergence impairs verification, comprehension, or safety; that would require a study with human readers.

## Ethical Considerations

Data and annotation. Questions come from public reasoning benchmarks and the traces are model-generated, so no personal or sensitive data is involved. Annotators were bilingual speakers informed of the purpose of the study, and no personal data was collected from them; Appendix F.7 reports recruitment and consent in full.

Artifact release. We will release the evaluation code under a permissive open-source license. It builds on publicly released models and benchmarks—LaBSE, BGE-M3, and paraphrase-multilingual-MiniLM-L12-v2, together with MMLU-ProX, StrategyQA, and SciBench—and our use and redistribution are consistent with their respective licenses. We redistribute no benchmark data and no annotation labels.

Intended use. The framework diagnoses whether a system reasons the same way in different languages; it does not certify reasoning quality. Scores are relative within a fixed encoder and language pair and should not be used to rank languages, to gate access to a service, or as a training target—traces can be made to agree without any improvement in the underlying reasoning. Because all pairs in the main analyses are verified-correct, a low score there marks a different route to the same answer, not a wrong one; its association with shared errors supports prioritizing human review, not detecting errors.

Fairness. Equal accuracy can mask unequal traces, so users who read a model’s explanation may receive systematically different support depending on the language they use.

## Appendix A The Necessity of Per-Language-Pair Calibration

The trace-divergence measure $D_{\text{trace}}$ relies on cross-lingual sentence similarities that must be comparable across language pairs. In practice, raw cosine similarities violate this requirement because they conflate semantic relatedness with language-pair-specific properties of the embedding space. These differences manifest as pair-dependent similarity floors and ceilings (reported in Appendix C.4), making raw cosine scores unsuitable for direct comparison. We therefore normalize sentence similarities using pair-specific calibration before constructing the trace-level metrics.

Formally, the structural and order metrics are built from the sentence-level cosine

 $\displaystyle c_{a,b}(u,u^{\prime})=\cos\!\big(\phi(u),\phi(u^{\prime})\big),$ 

 $\displaystyle u\in\mathcal{U}_{a},\;u^{\prime}\in\mathcal{U}_{b},$ 

where $\phi$ is a multilingual sentence encoder and $\mathcal{U}_{a}$ denotes the space of sentences appearing in language- $a$ reasoning traces.

Raw cosine similarity is not directly comparable across language pairs because both the expected similarity between unrelated sentences (the floor) and the attainable similarity between semantically equivalent sentences (the ceiling) vary across languages. These shifts arise from anisotropy and uneven cross-lingual alignment in multilingual embedding spaces (Feng et al., 2022; Chen et al., 2024). We define

 $\displaystyle\mathrm{floor}_{a,b}$ 
 $\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\!\left[c_{a,b}(u,u^{\prime})\mid u\perp u^{\prime}\right],$ 

 $\displaystyle\mathrm{ceiling}_{a,b}$ 
 $\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\!\left[c_{a,b}(u,T_{a\rightarrow b}(u))\right],$ 

where $u\perp u^{\prime}$ denotes semantic independence and
 $T_{a\rightarrow b}:\mathcal{U}_{a}\rightarrow\mathcal{U}_{b}$ 
is a semantics-preserving translator.

The calibrated similarity is then

 $\displaystyle\widetilde{c}_{a,b}(u,u^{\prime})=\frac{c_{a,b}(u,u^{\prime})-\mathrm{floor}_{a,b}}{\mathrm{ceiling}_{a,b}-\mathrm{floor}_{a,b}}\in[0,1],$ 

 $\displaystyle u\in\mathcal{U}_{a},\;u^{\prime}\in\mathcal{U}_{b}.$ 

After calibration, sentence similarities depend primarily on semantic relatedness rather than language-pair-specific embedding geometry, enabling meaningful comparison and aggregation across multilingual reasoning traces.

## Appendix B Supplementary Method Details

## B.1 Step-Parsing Details

Each response is segmented using the following ordered fallback hierarchy:

1. 

the prompted English line-initial pattern Step $n$ :;

2. 

line-initial equivalents of the step marker in the seven evaluated languages;

3. 

a plain line-initial numbered-list pattern; and

4. 

paragraph boundaries.

Parsing terminates when the final-answer marker is encountered. Empty blocks are discarded, and each remaining step is divided into sentences using a Unicode-aware regular expression. The same ordered rule set is applied in every language, and the parser records which tier was used for each response. No LLM or language-specific semantic parser is invoked.

We distinguish two notions of parser quality. Coverage asks whether a response yields a non-empty sequence of steps. Boundary fidelity asks whether the extracted step count agrees with the explicit step markers in the raw output. The corresponding diagnostics by language, model, and dataset, together with a manual spot check of the recovered boundaries, are reported in Appendix B.1.1.

## B.1.1 Parser Validation

The automatic marker-count comparison evaluates whether the implementation faithfully recovers the boundaries explicitly emitted by the model. It does not establish that those model-provided boundaries constitute the uniquely correct semantic segmentation of the trace. We therefore also read the parsed output manually, inspecting roughly $20$ randomly drawn traces per model–dataset cell against the corresponding raw responses. In every trace inspected, the recovered boundaries matched the segmentation a reader would impose, and we found no case of a step being merged or split against the evident structure of the trace. This audit is small: manual boundary inspection is costly, and we did not extend it to a sample large enough to bound the residual error rate. We report it as a spot check supporting the automatic diagnostics rather than as an independent measurement of boundary fidelity.

## B.2 Calibration Details and Robustness

## B.2.1 Floor and Ceiling Estimators

The calibration interval of §3.2 is estimated independently for each language pair $(a,b)$ .

Let $\mathcal{U}_{a}$ denote the sentences appearing in language- $a$ traces, and let $q(u)$ identify the source question from which sentence $u$ was extracted. We estimate the unrelated-sentence floor using pairs sampled from distinct questions:

 $\displaystyle\widehat{\mathrm{floor}}_{a,b}$ 
 $\displaystyle=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{P}_{a,b}|}\!\!\sum_{(u,u^{\prime})\in\mathcal{P}_{a,b}}\!\!c_{a,b}(u,u^{\prime}),$ 

 $\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad q(u)\neq q(u^{\prime}),$ 

(11)

where

 $\displaystyle c_{a,b}(u,u^{\prime})$ 
 $\displaystyle=\cos\!\big(\phi(u),\phi(u^{\prime})\big)$ 

and $\phi$ is a multilingual sentence encoder.

To estimate the attainable similarity between semantically equivalent sentences, we sample
 $\mathcal{V}_{a}\subseteq\mathcal{U}_{a}$ and
 $\mathcal{V}_{b}\subseteq\mathcal{U}_{b}$ from traces whose final answers are verified as correct. Let
 $T_{a\rightarrow b}$ and $T_{b\rightarrow a}$ denote an LLM translator in the two directions. We pool the resulting self-translation similarities:

 $\mathcal{C}^{\mathrm{trans}}_{a,b}=\big\{c_{a,b}(u,T_{a\rightarrow b}(u)):u\in\mathcal{V}_{a}\big\}\\
\cup\big\{c_{a,b}(T_{b\rightarrow a}(v),v):v\in\mathcal{V}_{b}\big\}.$ 

(12)

We define the empirical ceiling as

 $\displaystyle\widehat{\mathrm{ceiling}}_{a,b}$ 
 $\displaystyle=\operatorname{clip}\big(\operatorname{perc}_{90}(\mathcal{C}^{\mathrm{trans}}_{a,b}),$ 

 $\displaystyle\qquad\quad\widehat{\mathrm{floor}}_{a,b}+\epsilon_{\mathrm{cal}},\;c_{\max}\big),$ 

(13)

where $\epsilon_{\mathrm{cal}}=0.10$ and $c_{\max}=0.98$ . Each raw sentence cosine is then rescaled by Eq. (4).

## B.2.2 Motivation for the Calibration Constants

The empirical ceiling in Eq. (13) uses the $90$ th percentile of bidirectional self-translation similarities rather than the maximum. The percentile reduces sensitivity to occasional near-duplicate or unusually literal translations that would otherwise compress the calibrated dynamic range.

The minimum margin

 $\epsilon_{\mathrm{cal}}=0.10$ 

prevents a nearly degenerate calibration interval when the estimated floor and ceiling are too close. The ceiling cap

 $c_{\max}=0.98$ 

prevents isolated near-identity translations from setting an unrealistically high endpoint.

The floor and ceiling are estimated independently for each language pair. All remaining constants—the ceiling percentile, $\epsilon_{\mathrm{cal}}$ , $c_{\max}$ , and the sharpening power used in Eq. (6)—were fixed before evaluating the reported trace-level results and were held identical across all model–dataset evaluations. They were not selected against $S$ , $\tau$ , $H$ , or the top-quartile rank statistic.

## B.2.3 What Calibration Does and Does Not Establish

The floor anchors the calibrated scale at an empirical neutral level between unrelated content. The ceiling anchors it at an empirical estimate of the encoder’s attainable similarity between semantically equivalent content for the same language pair. Calibration therefore removes pair-dependent drift at these two endpoints.

This procedure does not establish that an intermediate calibrated value has exactly the same semantic interpretation across different language pairs. Translation quality and embedding geometry may vary nonlinearly between the estimated endpoints. Accordingly, pooled absolute values should be interpreted descriptively.

The paper’s central comparison is less demanding: examples are ranked within a fixed model–dataset–language-pair cell. For a fixed embedding space, any strictly monotone pair-specific rescaling preserves this ordering except where clipping creates ties. The rank-based comparison therefore does not require intermediate calibrated values to be globally semantically identical.

## B.2.4 Rank Invariance of the Headline Statistic

The rank comparison reported in §5.2 is the share of pair–question instances in the top quartile of $H$ that do not fall in the top quartile of $S$ , with both thresholds taken within a single evaluation and language pair. Within such a cell the calibration of Eq. (4) is a fixed affine rescaling followed by clipping to $[0,1]$ . It is monotone, so it preserves the ordering of instances in the cell and leaves the quantile thresholds at the same instances; the greedy alignment of §3.3 selects matches by argmax and is likewise invariant. The only way calibration could move the statistic is by clipping distinct values onto a shared endpoint and creating ties. $H$ is an uncalibrated cosine, so any movement would fall entirely on $S$ .

The residual tie effect is nil in practice. Recomputing the statistic on the uncalibrated scale—recovered by inverting the stored per-pair floor and ceiling—reproduces the calibrated value on all three Claude Opus 4.6 evaluations, with every difference below $10^{-4}$ (Table 5). All three reproduced values lie inside the $52.1\%$ – $67.3\%$ range reported in §5.2.

Pooling the quartile thresholds across the $21$ language pairs of an evaluation, rather than computing them within each pair, defines a different statistic: it folds between-pair differences in level into a comparison that is otherwise made within a pair. Under that definition the calibrated and uncalibrated values differ by $+1.0$ , $-0.4$ , and $-1.5$ percentage points on StrategyQA, SciBench, and MMLU-ProX respectively—small, and inconsistent in sign.

Calibration is therefore not what produces the reported divergence. What it provides is a readable scale: within a language pair, $0$ marks the unrelated-pair floor and $1$ the attainable self-translation ceiling, and $S$ and $H$ are placed on a common footing so that the $H{-}S$ gap is a well-defined quantity at all. Both are statements about levels, and the rank comparison the paper relies on does not depend on either.

## B.2.5 Ceiling-Percentile Sensitivity

We assess the stability of the estimated ceilings by varying the self-translation percentile while holding the sampled translation pool fixed. This analysis measures sensitivity of the endpoint estimator rather than sensitivity to a change in encoder.

## B.2.6 Translator Independence

The ceiling translator can, in some evaluations, belong to the same broad model family as the model whose traces are being analyzed. We test whether this creates a family-specific calibration artifact by re-estimating the language-pair ceilings with an independent-family translator and recomputing the metrics; per-pair values and rank correlations are reported in Appendix D.1. The experiment tests the dependence of the estimated calibration endpoints on the translation model. It does not imply that machine translation is equally reliable for every language, particularly for lower-resource languages.

## B.3 Sharpening Sensitivity

The soft assignment sharpens step-pair similarities as $\widetilde{\mathbf{A}}_{i,j}=\mathbf{A}_{i,j}^{\,p}$ with $p=3$ , concentrating each source step’s assignment mass on its strongest candidate matches while retaining uncertainty over multiple candidates. We compare this default against a power family, $p\in\{1,2,3,5,10\}$ , and a softmax-temperature family, $P_{i,j}\propto\exp(\mathbf{A}_{i,j}/T)$ with $T\in\{0.05,0.1,0.2,0.5,1.0\}$ . Because different schemes distribute probability mass differently, their absolute $\tau$ values need not coincide, so robustness is assessed through preservation of rankings across evaluation cells. Full results are reported in Appendix D.8.

## B.4 Alternative No-Tie Order Score

The main paper reports the tie-aware order score from Eq. (9). For completeness, we also define a variant that conditions only on concordant and discordant mass:

 $\displaystyle\tau^{a\rightarrow b}_{\mathrm{no\text{-}tie}}=\frac{M^{a\rightarrow b}_{\mathrm{con}}-M^{a\rightarrow b}_{\mathrm{dis}}}{M^{a\rightarrow b}_{\mathrm{con}}+M^{a\rightarrow b}_{\mathrm{dis}}}.$ 

(14)

The corresponding symmetrized score is

 $\displaystyle\tau_{\mathrm{no\text{-}tie}}(r_{a},r_{b})=\frac{1}{2}\left(\tau^{a\rightarrow b}_{\mathrm{no\text{-}tie}}+\tau^{b\rightarrow a}_{\mathrm{no\text{-}tie}}\right).$ 

(15)

The no-tie score measures relative ordering conditional on the two source steps being assigned to distinct target positions. The tie-aware score additionally penalizes concentration of multiple source steps on the same target step and is therefore used as the primary metric.

Both directed variants are undefined when their denominators are zero. A symmetrized score is reported as undefined if either direction is undefined.

## B.5 Controlled Metric Checks

We use controlled perturbations to verify that the metrics respond to their intended dimensions. Removing a reasoning step should reduce structural similarity, because one trace no longer covers all content in the other. Rewriting a step without altering its semantic content should preserve structural similarity, subject to the multilingual encoder’s robustness. Dividing one reasoning move into multiple steps, or combining adjacent moves, should be tolerated by the many-to-one correspondences in the structural score. Permuting otherwise unchanged steps should leave structural similarity relatively stable while reducing order consistency. Appendix D.7 reports $S$ , $\tau$ , and $H$ for each perturbation family. These checks establish dimension-specific metric behavior under known transformations; they complement, but do not replace, validation on naturally generated multilingual traces.

## B.6 Whole-Answer Holistic Similarity

The holistic baseline embeds each complete visible reasoning trace as a single sequence. Let $\psi$ denote the whole-response encoder. We define

 $\displaystyle H(r_{a},r_{b})=\cos\!\big(\psi(r_{a}),\psi(r_{b})\big).$ 

(16)

Unlike $S$ , the holistic baseline does not align individual steps or identify where two traces differ. Unlike $\tau$ , it does not explicitly measure preservation of step order. It serves as a comparison against the common practice of representing an entire response with a single embedding.

Why BGE-M3 for $H$ and LaBSE for $S$ .

Step-level structural similarity $S$ uses LaBSE (Feng et al., 2022) because it is the standard cross-lingual sentence encoder and individual reasoning steps comfortably fit its 256-token context. The whole-trace baseline $H$ instead uses BGE-M3 (Chen et al., 2024) because LaBSE’s 256-token limit truncates a substantial fraction of our reasoning traces (especially on SciBench and StrategyQA, where a single verified trace frequently exceeds 1,000 tokens), and in preliminary tests the resulting LaBSE-on-truncated-trace cosines were uninformative as a holistic signal. BGE-M3’s 8K-token window covers the entire trace without truncation in our evaluations, which is the property a holistic baseline is meant to provide. Adopting LaBSE on both sides would therefore not isolate the aggregation effect from $H{-}S$ ; it would replace the encoder asymmetry with a truncation artifact whose magnitude depends on dataset-specific trace-length distributions. We accordingly do not interpret the absolute magnitude of $H{-}S$ in Table 1 as a calibrated distance, and rely on the scale-free rank comparison in Figure 1 for the qualitative claim that holistic similarity overstates structural consistency.

## B.7 Interpretation of Structural and Order Scores

The two primary trace-level metrics capture complementary properties.

High $S$ , high $\tau$ .

The traces contain similar step-level content in a similar order.

High $S$ , low $\tau$ .

The traces contain similar reasoning moves but arrange them differently.

Low $S$ , high $\tau$ .

The subset of content that can be aligned appears in a consistent order, but substantial content is absent or unmatched.

Low $S$ , low $\tau$ .

The traces differ both in step-level content and in the ordering of their matched components.

Neither metric determines whether a difference is harmful. They identify and decompose observable differences in the visible explanatory artifacts, which can then be inspected by human or automated evaluators.

## B.8 Relation to Prior Step-Level Metrics

We compare against prior step-level metrics to clarify which parts of the framework are reused and which are specific to cross-lingual reasoning. The method recomposes BERTScore-style greedy matching (Zhang et al., 2020), ROSCOE-style step alignment (Golovneva et al., 2023), multilingual sentence embeddings (Feng et al., 2022), and soft ordering ideas from continuous sorting (Mena et al., 2018; Grover et al., 2019).

The main difference is that our alignment is calibrated per language pair before aggregation. Prior step metrics typically use raw within-language cosine scores, while cross-lingual traces require a pair-specific unrelated-sentence floor and self-translation ceiling so that a score for, for example, en-es has the same interpretation as a score for hi-zh.

The second difference is that we report structure and order separately. A single alignment score can hide whether two traces contain different reasoning moves or contain similar moves in a different sequence; $S$ and $\tau$ are kept separate so these two kinds of divergence remain visible.

## Appendix C Supplementary Experimental Details

This appendix collects the experimental details that support the three main results without expanding Section 5. We place the dataset, model, verification, accuracy-audit, and calibration materials next to the discussion they support.

## C.1 Datasets, Models, and Inference

All reported evaluations use the same seven languages: Arabic (ar), English (en), Spanish (es), Hindi (hi), Russian (ru), Vietnamese (vi), and Chinese (zh). This gives $\binom{7}{2}=21$ unordered language pairs for each model–dataset run. We sample 500 problems per run from each benchmark.

MMLU-ProX (Xuan et al., 2025) supplies parallel multiple-choice questions across academic and professional domains. We verify traces by extracting the final option and comparing it with the dataset answer key; ambiguous outputs are checked by an answer-consistency judge that sees the question, reference answer, and model final answer, but not any trace-similarity metric. StrategyQA (Geva et al., 2021) provides yes/no commonsense questions requiring implicit multi-step reasoning; we verify the final yes/no answer against the dataset label, with the same answer-consistency judge used for ambiguous outputs. SciBench (Wang et al., 2024) contains open-ended STEM problems with fixed numeric or symbolic answers; the verification step checks whether the model final answer is equivalent to the reference answer before the trace is used for divergence analysis.

The model suite comprises Claude Opus 4.6, Claude Haiku 4.5, DeepSeek-V3.2, GPT-OSS-120B, Qwen3-235B, and Qwen3-32B. All reported runs use greedy decoding.

Retention cascade. For each 500-problem dataset–model run, we first verify each language-specific final answer against the reference. For every language whose answer is correct on a given question, an LLM trace judge then inspects the reasoning trace and emits VALID or INVALID. If any answer-correct trace for that question is judged INVALID, the question is dropped from trace aggregation, even if other answer-correct languages would otherwise form valid pairs. The surviving pair–question instances enter $S$ , $\tau$ , and $H$ ; one-language-correct cases are reserved for $\Delta_{\mathrm{acc}}$ . Table 1 reports the retained question counts $Q$ and pair–question counts $P$ after this cascade.

Metric settings. Step-level metrics use LaBSE (Feng et al., 2022), the holistic baseline $H$ uses BGE-M3 (Chen et al., 2024), unrelated-pair floor sampling draws up to $10^{4}$ sentence pairs per language pair, the self-translation ceiling samples up to 500 sentences per direction, and the soft Kendall- $\tau$ sharpening power is 3. Appendix D.8 shows that the cross-evaluation $\bar{\tau}$ ranking is preserved with Spearman $\geq 0.85$ across other powers and a softmax-temperature baseline. Aggregate means in Table 1 use $95\%$ percentile bootstrap intervals with 2,000 problem-level resamples, jointly resampling the 21 language-pair observations for each question.

Compute. All LLM trace generation and ceiling-translation calls use hosted APIs (AWS Bedrock for Claude, DeepSeek, GPT-OSS, and Qwen endpoints); we therefore report no local GPU hours for inference. Sentence-level embedding, per-pair calibration, and $(S,\tau,H)$ computation for the full 18 model–dataset evaluations run on a single NVIDIA A100 and take roughly $25$ hours end-to-end, dominated by LaBSE and BGE-M3 encoding of the retained traces.

## D.1 Translator-Independence Probe

Methodological concern. The default ceiling estimation in §3.2 uses an LLM translator from the same family (Claude Opus 4.6) as several of the evaluated models. A strong translator whose idiolect matches the evaluated model could inflate ceilings on exactly those pairs where the model is already consistent, partially undoing the calibration. We probe this co-dependence by re-estimating the per-pair sentence ceiling with an independent translator from a different model family and recomputing the divergence metrics on the same retained pair–question instances.

Setup. The probe uses StrategyQA on Claude Opus 4.6 ( $n=451$ retained verified-correct questions over 21 language pairs). The independent translator is Mistral-Large-3-675B-Instruct. The unrelated-pair sentence floor is shared between the two analyses (the floor does not depend on the translator); only the ceiling estimation changes. All other settings (LaBSE step embeddings, BGE-M3 holistic embedding, $90$ th-percentile self-translation ceiling, $\epsilon_{\text{cal}}=0.10$ , $c_{\max}=0.98$ , sharpening power 3) are identical.

## D.5 Parser Coverage

We measure the success rate of the regex-based step parser of §3.1 on the StrategyQA Claude Opus 4.6 traces from the post-judge verified question set ( $|\text{verified}|{=}458$ questions, equal to $500$ minus the $42$ rejected-sample questions of §E.2 and seven greater than $Q{=}451$ in Table 1 because $Q$ additionally requires that at least two answer-correct languages survive the trace-judge per question). The parser is run on the trace produced for every language on each verified question regardless of whether that language is itself answer-correct, giving $458$ parser invocations per language; per-language answer accuracies on the underlying $500$ -problem inference set are reported separately in Figure 2.

Coverage—the fraction of those $458$ traces yielding at least one parsed step—is $100.0\%$ for English, Spanish, Hindi, Russian, Vietnamese, and Chinese, and $99.8\%$ for Arabic, where exactly one trace produced no steps. No language required the generic numbered-list fallback in place of the language-specific Step $n$ : / Paso $n$ : / Chinese-character header: the fallback rate is $0.0\%$ in all seven languages. Parser failure is therefore not a material source of the divergence patterns reported in Section 4. Coverage records only whether a trace parses; Appendix B.1.1 reports the marker-agreement diagnostic and the manual boundary spot check that address where the recovered boundaries fall.

## D.8 Sharpening Ablation: Power-Law vs Softmax-Temperature

The soft assignment in §3.4 sharpens the calibrated outer step matrix $\mathbf{A}$ element-wise by a fixed power before row $L_{1}$ -normalization (default exponent $p{=}3$ ). To check that this choice is not driving the reported $\tau$ values, we re-aggregate soft Kendall- $\tau$ -with-tie on the same per-question step-similarity matrices stored in our step4_divergence artifacts (so no re-encoding or re-inference is required), comparing two sharpening families: (i) power-law $\tilde{A}_{i,j}=\max(A_{i,j},0)^{p}$ for $p\in\{1,2,3,5,10\}$ and (ii) softmax-temperature $\tilde{A}_{i,j}\propto\exp(\max(A_{i,j},0)/T)$ for $T\in\{0.05,0.1,0.2,0.5,1.0\}$ . Both families collapse to the row-stochastic soft-assignment matrix $P$ used by the soft Kendall- $\tau$ computation in §3.4.

## E.2 Rejected-Sample Case Studies

The main trace-divergence results use only language pairs for which both answers are verified correct. As a qualitative diagnostic, we also evaluate examples rejected from the primary verified set with the same trace-divergence metrics. This analysis is not a primary metric, because rejected examples can conflate answer error, answer inconsistency, and reasoning divergence. It is nevertheless useful for inspecting whether failures are shared across languages or arise from language-specific interpretations.

Compared with the corresponding verified traces, rejected traces have lower structural similarity and order consistency, while whole-output similarity remains high. For Claude Opus 4.6 on StrategyQA ( $42$ rejected questions), the rejected set has $S{=}0.563$ and $\tau{=}0.341$ , compared with $S{=}0.598$ and $\tau{=}0.418$ on the verified set. On MMLU-ProX ( $65$ rejected), the rejected set similarly drops from $S{=}0.569$ to $S{=}0.525$ and from $\tau{=}0.414$ to $\tau{=}0.354$ . For DeepSeek-V3.2 on StrategyQA ( $69$ rejected), rejected examples have $S{=}0.537$ and $\tau{=}0.270$ , compared with verified values of $S{=}0.560$ and $\tau{=}0.324$ . In all three cases $H$ remains close to $0.80$ ( $0.810$ , $0.807$ , and $0.803$ respectively), so holistic similarity alone does not distinguish verified reasoning agreement from rejected-sample failure modes.

Shared wrong reasoning.

On the StrategyQA question “Is the Foreign and Commonwealth Office a European political agency?”, the dataset answer is yes, but Claude Opus 4.6 answers no in all seven languages. The traces are highly similar ( $S{=}0.756$ , $H{=}0.887$ ): each language interprets “European political agency” as an EU or pan-European institution and rejects the label because the Foreign and Commonwealth Office is a UK government department. This case shows that high cross-lingual trace similarity can reflect a shared error rather than reliable reasoning. ## Appendix F Human Validation of the Trace-Divergence Metrics

This appendix gives the protocol, sampling procedure, and full statistics behind §5.6.

## F.1 Protocol

Task. Each item presents an English source question, the final answer (both languages reached the same verified-correct answer), and two reasoning traces labeled Trace A and Trace B. Annotators judge whether the traces reach the answer by the same reasoning. Three fields are recorded per item:

• 

equivalence, the primary variable: Same / Partially different / Substantially different, coded ordinally as $2/1/0$ .

• 

diff_*, a multi-label difference taxonomy: wording, granularity, missing content, different strategy, different order.

• 

material, binary: would the difference change what a reviewer concludes when using the trace to verify the answer? A binary item yields a more stable per-stratum rate at this sample size than a Likert scale.

Blinding. Annotators never see $S$ , $\tau$ , $H$ , or any stratum assignment; scores live only in a hidden key. The assignment of English and non-English traces to Trace A and Trace B is randomized per item to remove positional bias. Every item is labeled independently by at least two annotators, with a shared calibration subset labeled by all four. Annotators do not confer during labeling.

Annotators. Four bilingual annotators per language pair, disjoint across the two studies. §F.5 reports a shared-anchor control establishing that the two groups do not differ in labeling threshold.

## F.2 Sampling

Both studies draw from Claude Opus 4.6 outputs pooled over MMLU-ProX, SciBench, and StrategyQA, restricted to pairs where both languages are answer-correct and both traces parse into at least two steps, with duplicate question text removed. The EN–ES eligible pool contains $n=1{,}178$ pairs.

Items are sampled crossed on $H$ and $S$ : both metrics are split at pool-relative terciles and items are drawn from every cell of the resulting $3\times 3$ grid, with a round-robin over datasets inside each cell. This matters for interpretation. Stratifying on $S$ alone while holding $H$ high would suppress $H$ ’s variance by construction, so a null correlation for $H$ would be partly guaranteed by the design rather than measured. Sampling crossed avoids that: both metrics span their natural ranges, and any asymmetry between them is a property of the metrics rather than of the sample.

Table 20 confirms the design achieved this. Sample standard deviations closely match the pool for both metrics in both studies, and the sample correlation between $H$ and $S$ reproduces the pool value ( $0.25$ against a pool $0.254$ for EN–ES), so the design induces no dependence between the two axes.

## F.3 Correlational Results

Table 4 in §5.6 reports the correlations themselves. This section gives the tests behind them.

Testing the difference, not two separate nulls. Reporting that $S$ correlates while $H$ does not is two independent tests, and invites the objection that the null on $H$ merely reflects low power. The claim is a difference between dependent correlations sharing a criterion variable, so we test it as one. Williams’ test on $\rho(\text{equiv},S)$ against $\rho(\text{equiv},H)$ gives $\Delta\rho=0.41$ , bootstrap $95\%$ CI $[0.12,0.68]$ , $t(57)=2.81$ , $p=0.007$ for EN–ES, and $\Delta\rho=0.437$ , $t(57)=2.80$ , $p=0.0069$ for EN–ZH. The dissociation is significant in both pairs at effectively the same strength.

Parametric form. An ordinal logistic regression of equivalence on standardized $S$ and $H$ (EN–ES) gives $\beta_{z(S)}=0.86$ (SE $0.25$ , OR $2.36$ , $p=0.0006$ ) and $\beta_{z(H)}=-0.09$ (SE $0.24$ , OR $0.91$ , $p=0.71$ ). Likelihood-ratio tests dropping each term give $\chi^{2}(1)=13.1$ , $p=0.0003$ for $S$ and $\chi^{2}(1)=0.14$ , $p=0.71$ for $H$ . A one-standard-deviation drop in $S$ multiplies the odds of a lower human equivalence rating by roughly $2.4$ .

Generalization across language pairs. Pooling both studies gives $\rho(\text{equiv},S)=0.40$ , $p<0.001$ , $N=120$ . A Fisher- $z$ homogeneity test between pairs gives $z=0.46$ , $p=0.65$ : the two language pairs do not differ. English–Spanish is the more demanding test of the two, since Spanish is typologically closer to English and its traces should diverge less.

## F.4 Comparison with an LLM-as-Judge Baseline

A natural alternative to a structural metric is to ask a strong model directly whether two traces reason the same way. We ran that baseline on the $60$ English–Chinese pairs of the initial annotation round, giving the judge (GPT-5.6) the identical rubric our annotators used, both traces, and no similarity scores.

The structural metric predicts human-judged equivalence more closely than the judge does. On these items $\rho(\text{equiv},S)=0.419$ ( $p=0.004$ ) and $\rho(\text{equiv},\tau)=0.413$ ( $p=0.005$ ), while the judge reaches $\rho=0.235$ ( $p=0.12$ , n.s.) and the holistic baseline $\rho=0.129$ (n.s.). The judge also agrees with the human consensus less than the annotators agree with one another (pairwise agreement $0.576$ against $0.633$ ), and entering it as a fifth coder halves ordinal Krippendorff’s $\alpha$ , from $0.462$ to $0.237$ : relative to the consensus it behaves as an outlier rather than as a substitute for it. Its errors are systematic rather than noisy—exact agreement with the human majority is $0.422$ but within-one agreement is $0.956$ , so it is nearly always adjacent on the ordinal scale.

The failure mode is the one the framework targets. The judge never applied the Substantially different label at any point, and its mean rating does not vary monotonically across $S$ strata. On high- $H$ / low- $S$ pairs it rates as Same or Partially different items that annotators unanimously call substantially different—for instance a multiple-choice item answered correctly in both languages where one trace eliminates every option exhaustively and the other identifies the answer directly ( $S=0.46$ , $H=0.80$ ; human Substantially different, judge Same).

We state the strength of this comparison precisely: the confidence intervals for $S$ and for the judge overlap, so the defensible claim is that $S$ is at least as faithful to human judgment as the judge and, unlike the judge, statistically significant on this sample. Beyond faithfulness, $S$ is deterministic, localizes divergence to specific steps, and separates content from order at no per-call cost.

## F.5 Supporting Analyses

Monotonicity. Mean human equivalence rises monotonically across $S$ terciles and is flat across $H$ terciles. For EN–ES the means are $0.92/1.28/1.65$ by $S$ (Jonckheere–Terpstra $p<0.001$ ) against $1.31/1.24/1.30$ by $H$ ( $p=0.72$ ). EN–ZH shows the same pattern: $1.069/1.324/1.420$ by $S$ , and $1.433/1.162/1.300$ by $H$ .

Detection framing. Treating a majority Substantially different label as the positive class, the area under the ROC curve is $0.81$ $[0.68,0.92]$ for $S$ , $0.76$ $[0.61,0.88]$ for $\tau$ , and $0.54$ $[0.36,0.71]$ for $H$ ; a DeLong test of $S$ against $H$ gives $p=0.012$ . We present this as further evidence for the dissociation rather than as a deployable screening threshold: the positive class holds $11$ items in EN–ES, and the interval is correspondingly wide.

Difference types. In EN–ES the diff_wording rate is flat across $S$ terciles ( $0.21/0.19/0.17$ ) while the content tags (diff_missing, diff_granularity, diff_order) fall by a factor of three to four from low to high $S$ . In EN–ZH the low- $S$ stratum is dominated by missing reasoning content ( $0.477$ ), granularity ( $0.295$ ), and order ( $0.205$ ), with wording both the smallest category ( $0.114$ ) and the only one declining monotonically across $S$ ( $0.114/0.071/0.034$ ). A metric tracking surface verbalization would produce the opposite profile in both studies.

Agreement and attenuation. Ordinal Krippendorff’s $\alpha$ is $0.411$ for EN–ZH and $0.58$ for EN–ES. We use Krippendorff’s $\alpha$ as the headline agreement statistic because it is the only standard option that simultaneously accommodates a small sample, a variable number of coders per item, and ordinal data; Cohen’s $\kappa$ requires exactly two fixed raters and Fleiss’ $\kappa$ a fixed rater count and nominal data. Because the human rating is itself a noisy instrument, the correlations above are attenuated rather than inflated by this unreliability. Correcting with the composite reliability of each study’s rating mean gives $0.44\rightarrow 0.49$ for EN–ES (composite reliability $0.81$ ) and $0.371\rightarrow 0.47$ for EN–ZH ( $0.61$ ). The two pairs agree more closely once measurement error is accounted for. We report the observed values as the headline throughout.

Annotator-group control.

Because the two studies use disjoint annotator groups, a difference between language pairs could in principle reflect the annotators rather than the languages. On $12$ shared English–English anchor items labeled by both groups, mean equivalence is $1.36$ for the EN–ZH group and $1.31$ for the EN–ES group (difference $0.05$ , $[-0.19,0.29]$ ), Same rates are $34\%$ and $36\%$ (Mann–Whitney $p=0.68$ ), and $\alpha$ on the anchors is $0.61$ and $0.59$ . The groups are indistinguishable on shared items, so the EN–ES result reflects the language pair rather than a group-specific labeling threshold.

Orientation control. Comparing items where the non-English trace appeared as Trace A against those where it appeared as Trace B gives mean equivalence $1.29$ against $1.28$ (Mann–Whitney $p=0.88$ ): no presentation-order effect.

Outcome variance. Overall mean equivalence is $1.29$ (sd $0.474$ ) for EN–ZH and $1.28$ (sd $0.47$ ) for EN–ES, so the difference between studies is not an artifact of differing outcome spread.

## F.6 Annotation Guide

We reproduce the instructions given to annotators. The guide opens by stating that no similarity score of any kind will be shown and that only the reasoning is to be judged.

What is being labeled. Each item gives an English source question, the final answer (both languages reached the same verified-correct answer), and two reasoning traces labeled Trace A and Trace B, one English and one non-English, with the side assignment randomized per item. Annotators read both and decide whether the traces reach the answer by the same reasoning, recording three fields per item with fixed allowed values.

Field 1: equivalence (the primary label, exactly one value).

• 

Same — the two traces use the same reasoning: same key claims, same logical path. Pure wording or translation differences still count as Same.

• 

Partially different — largely the same reasoning, but with a real difference in one part: a step present on one side is missing or merged on the other, or one sub-argument differs, while the overall path is recognizably the same.

• 

Substantially different — the traces reason differently: different strategy, different decomposition, different premises or evidence, or different logical order of the core steps. A reader could not treat one as a faithful stand-in for the other.

Field 2: diff_* (difference type; mark each that applies, skipped entirely when equivalence is Same). diff_wording: only phrasing or translation differs. diff_granularity: same content, split into more, fewer, or uneven steps. diff_missing: a reasoning step or claim present on one side is absent on the other. diff_strategy: a genuinely different solution approach or argument. diff_order: the same core steps occur in a different order. diff_other: with a free-text description.

Field 3: material (binary). Would the difference change what a human reviewer concludes when using the trace to verify the answer or understand how it was reached? If the traces are the same or differ only cosmetically, No. If a reviewer auditing one trace would reach a materially different understanding of why the answer holds than from the other, Yes.

Rules of thumb. The guide instructs annotators to translate mentally and compare logic rather than surface tokens, noting that two traces in different languages will never be word-identical and that this alone is not a difference; to disregard correctness, since both answers are already verified correct, and to judge only whether the reasoning is the same rather than whether one trace is better; to pick the more different label when torn between two and add a note; and not to discuss items with other annotators while labeling, so that judgments remain independent.

Language check. Annotators are asked to flag any trace written in the wrong language—one that should be in Spanish but is actually in English, or the reverse—with a fixed token in the notes field, then label the item as normal. This is requested particularly of the EN–ES annotators, because English and Spanish share the Latin alphabet and a reversion that an automatic script-level check cannot see is immediately obvious to a bilingual reader.

Calibration. All annotators for a study first label a shared calibration subset. After those are complete the group meets briefly to compare judgments and settle the wording of edge cases, and the remaining items are then labeled independently. Annotators returning for the EN–ZH top-up batch were instructed that the labels and their definitions were unchanged and to apply exactly the standard they had used previously, without re-reading their earlier files to match specific judgments, since the two batches are pooled in analysis.

## F.7 Annotator Recruitment and Consent

Annotators were unpaid bilingual volunteers from the authors’ academic and professional networks, based in Asia and North America, all adults with professional or native proficiency in both languages of the pair they annotated. Participation was voluntary and could be stopped at any time; annotators were told the purpose of the study and how their labels would be used, and consented on that basis. The burden was under thirty minutes per annotator for English–Chinese and under ninety minutes for English–Spanish. No personal data was collected, and none is retained in any released artifact. We did not seek ethics-board review: the study collects no personal or sensitive data, involves no deception or intervention, and asks adult volunteers only to compare model-generated text derived from public benchmarks—a minimal-risk protocol under our institutional framework.

## Appendix G Encoder Sensitivity

Methodological concern. $S$ and $\tau$ are computed from sentence embeddings, so a natural question is how much of the reported $H{-}S$ contrast is a property of reasoning traces and how much is a property of the encoders. Two distinct worries sit inside this. First, $H$ and $S$ are produced by different encoders in the main experiments (BGE-M3 and LaBSE respectively), so the gap between them could be an artifact of that asymmetry. Second, the framework might require a strong encoder to work at all, which would limit its usefulness.

Setup. We recompute $S$ and $\tau$ from scratch under two alternative step encoders, on three Claude Opus 4.6 evaluations at full retained size (StrategyQA $n=451$ , MMLU-ProX $n=431$ , SciBench $n=362$ ), across all $21$ language pairs. The per-pair floor and ceiling are re-derived for each encoder rather than reused, since calibration constants estimated for one embedding space do not transfer. The alternatives are chosen to probe the two worries separately:

• 

BGE-M3 ( $568$ M parameters, $1024$ dimensions, retrieval-contrastive objective) is the encoder $H$ already uses, so running $S$ on it removes the $H$ – $S$ encoder asymmetry entirely. This is the only sound direction for that swap: placing LaBSE on $H$ would truncate whole traces at its $256$ -token limit, whereas individual steps fit comfortably in both encoders.

• 

paraphrase-multilingual-MiniLM-L12-v2 ( $118$ M parameters, $384$ dimensions, paraphrase-distillation objective) is a four- to five-fold reduction in scale from LaBSE ( $471$ M) and a third training objective. Its $128$ -token limit is not binding here, because the encoding unit is sentences within steps rather than whole steps: median sentence length is $56$ characters in English and $28$ in Chinese, with $99$ th percentiles of $237$ and $124$ .

Validation of the reimplementation. Before comparing encoders we re-ran the identical pipeline with LaBSE, the encoder used in the main experiments, and recovered the cached values (Spearman $0.959$ on $S$ , mean $|\Delta S|=0.0044$ , top-quartile mismatch unchanged). Movement under an alternative encoder is therefore attributable to the encoder and not to the reimplementation.

Result: the rank-based finding is stable. Across all three encoders and three datasets, every evaluation falls between $50.1\%$ and $65.5\%$ of top-quartile- $H$ examples not being top-quartile- $S$ , closely tracking the $52.1\%$ – $67.3\%$ range in Table 1. This holds when $H$ and $S$ are produced by the same encoder, which disposes of the asymmetry worry, and it holds on a distilled $118$ M model that runs on CPU, which disposes of the second: the dissociation does not require a strong encoder.

Result: absolute levels are not stable. The $H{-}S$ gap moves in both directions — widening under BGE-M3 ( $0.207\rightarrow 0.225$ on average) and narrowing under MiniLM ( $0.207\rightarrow 0.125$ ), the latter because MiniLM assigns systematically higher calibrated step similarities. $H$ is identical in every column, so this movement is entirely in $S$ . We therefore treat absolute calibrated values of $S$ and $\tau$ as encoder-relative and interpret them by rank within a fixed encoder, which is how they are reported throughout the paper.

Rank preservation and trace length. Per-question $S$ rankings are substantially preserved across encoders, and preservation rises monotonically with trace length under both alternatives (BGE-M3: $0.803<0.839<0.876$ ; MiniLM: $0.696<0.842<0.913$ , ordered from the shortest-trace dataset to the longest). Short traces yield small step-similarity matrices, which are inherently more sensitive to which encoder scores them. The weakest cell in the study is StrategyQA under MiniLM at $\rho(S)=0.696$ .

Order consistency. Which of the two metrics is more encoder-sensitive is itself encoder-dependent. Under BGE-M3, $\tau$ is the less stable of the two and falls to $\rho=0.665$ on SciBench while $S$ holds at $0.876$ ; under MiniLM the ordering reverses, with $\tau$ the more uniform ( $0.795/0.826/0.820$ ) and $S$ the more variable ( $0.696/0.842/0.913$ ). We therefore state the limitation without attaching a mechanism to it: absolute $\tau$ values should be compared within an encoder rather than across encoders.

Calibration behavior. How much work the per-pair calibration does is also encoder-specific, and does not track encoder strength. The cross-language-pair drift in the unrelated-pair floor that calibration exists to correct spans $0.157$ – $0.381$ under LaBSE on StrategyQA, but only $0.008$ – $0.022$ under BGE-M3 and $0.046$ – $0.055$ under MiniLM. On a well-aligned embedding space the floor correction is nearly inert, while the ceiling frequently saturates at the cap (all $21$ SciBench ceilings pin at $0.98$ under both alternatives). The magnitude of the calibration correction is thus a property of the embedding space rather than of the encoder’s capacity.

Scope. One model family, three datasets at full retained size, three encoders. We do not test whether the pattern holds on other model families or below $118$ M parameters.

## Appendix H Same-Language Control

This appendix gives the design and full results of the same-language control summarized in §5.4.

## H.1 Design

The paraphrase condition. Every reported $S$ and $\tau$ in the main evaluation compares traces across a language boundary, so the level of divergence alone cannot distinguish an effect of changing language from ordinary variation in generated reasoning. The control adds an eighth condition, $\text{en}_{\text{para}}$ : each English question is rewritten as a meaning-preserving English paraphrase and answered under the same system prompt, decoding (greedy, temperature $0$ , no reasoning mode), parser, retention cascade, and metric settings as every other condition. $\text{en}_{\text{para}}$ receives the same appended language instruction as a translated condition, naming English, so that the only uncontrolled difference between the pairs $(\text{en},\text{en}_{\text{para}})$ and $(\text{en},\ell)$ is the language of the output. The paraphrases are produced by Claude Opus 4.6 with the prompt below, which asks for as little surface overlap with the original as the language allows: a paraphraser left to itself restates a question nearly verbatim, which would collapse the condition onto the source and measure nothing.

Models, questions, and conditions. The control runs on GPT-OSS-120B and DeepSeek-V3.2 across all three benchmarks. For each benchmark it uses a seeded random subset of $100$ questions from the main $500$ -problem sample. On MMLU-ProX and SciBench, the seven original conditions reuse the main-run responses and only $\text{en}_{\text{para}}$ is generated. On StrategyQA, all eight conditions are generated within this run, with each question translated into the six target languages by Claude Opus 4.6 before inference. Between $41$ and $68$ questions per cell retain both $(\text{en},\text{en}_{\text{para}})$ and at least one English-anchored cross-lingual pair after the retention cascade, $337$ in total.

Calibration of the same-language pair. Floors are estimated within this run for all $28$ language pairs by the procedure of Eq. (11); cross-lingual ceilings are taken from the main runs. A same-language ceiling cannot be estimated from translation, because translating a sentence into its own language is the identity map, which would peg the ceiling at $c_{\max}$ and deflate every calibrated score in the cell. The $(\text{en},\text{en}_{\text{para}})$ ceiling is therefore estimated from sentence-level paraphrases, the same-language analogue of a translation, over $50$ sentences per direction:

Same-language ceiling: system prompt

You are a careful sentence rewriter.

Rewrite the given sentence in the same
language using different wording,
preserving its meaning, technical terms,
symbols, and numbers exactly.
Change word choice and sentence structure
where the language allows it; do not
return the sentence unchanged.
Do not add, omit, explain, or reformat
anything.
Return only the rewritten sentence.

Comparison and reporting rule. For each question, $\Delta S$ is $S(\text{en},\text{en}_{\text{para}})$ minus the mean of $S(\text{en},\ell)$ over the English-anchored cross-lingual pairs available for that question. Pairing within question matters here because question identity carries far more of the variance in $S$ than language pair does. The comparison is against English-anchored pairs because those are the pairs that share the English trace with $(\text{en},\text{en}_{\text{para}})$ . The comparison set, the per-question pairing, and the reporting of the result whichever way it fell were fixed before the run, as were decision thresholds for MMLU-ProX ( $0.030$ ) and SciBench ( $0.015$ ), set from the size of the floor-induced bias measured on stored artifacts before any paraphrase was generated: an advantage for $(\text{en},\text{en}_{\text{para}})$ below the threshold would have been read as no detectable difference. ## Appendix I Where Divergence Appears and What It Signals

This appendix gives the analyses behind §5.5. Both reuse the stored traces and step-pair matrices of the main runs; neither requires new inference.

## I.1 Positional Structure

Method. For every scored pair with English on one side we orient the calibrated step-pair matrix $\mathbf{A}$ so that its rows are the English steps, and score each English step by its best match anywhere in the other trace, $\max_{j}\mathbf{A}_{i,j}$ , the quantity whose mean is the precision term of Eq. (5). Pairs with fewer than four English steps are dropped so that the first, middle, and last steps are distinct. The analysis covers all 18 evaluations: $31{,}277$ English-anchored pairs, $192{,}243$ English steps, and $5{,}630$ questions. Confidence intervals are percentile bootstrap intervals over questions ( $2{,}000$ resamples), since one question contributes six language pairs and each pair several steps. Because the best match carries no monotonicity constraint, “position” is position in the English trace: the fifth English step may match the second step of the other trace, and order is what $\tau$ measures. Every pair in this analysis reaches the same verified-correct answer in both languages.

Profile. Table 25 bins English steps by relative position into quintiles. Alignment declines toward the end of the trace in every benchmark, though by different routes: StrategyQA drops in mid-trace, SciBench rises through the middle before a sharp terminal drop, and MMLU-ProX declines gently throughout. All three end near $0.49$ .

## I.2 Difficulty and Correctness Outcomes

Scoring the unscored pairs. The main analyses score a language pair only when both languages answer correctly, so they cannot say what divergence looks like when correctness differs. The traces for the other outcomes are stored regardless of correctness, so for EN–ZH and EN–ES, across all six models and three benchmarks, we computed $S$ , $H$ , and $\tau$ for every pair with the pipeline’s own modules and each pair’s stored floor and ceiling, with no new inference and no re-calibration. On pairs scored by both routes the recomputation reproduces the stored values exactly. All groups are drawn from questions that passed the trace-validity cascade, so trace-quality filtering is uniform across groups. The $\tau$ in this analysis is the no-tie variant of Appendix B.4; it is rank-correlated with the tie-aware score at Spearman $0.988$ on a check set of $2{,}341$ of these pairs, and we report only its rank-based effect sizes.

Difficulty. Item difficulty is the obvious common cause: hard questions plausibly produce both more variable reasoning and more errors. We index difficulty by $k$ , the number of the seven languages that answer a question correctly. Among both-correct pairs, mean $S$ rises with $k$ , from $0.526$ at $k=2$ through $0.537$ , $0.536$ , $0.545$ , and $0.553$ to $0.579$ at $k=7$ (Spearman $\rho=0.099$ , $p=8\times 10^{-26}$ , $n=11{,}176$ ). The two groups also have very different difficulty profiles: $85\%$ of both-correct pairs come from questions all seven languages solve, whereas every shared-wrong-answer pair comes from a question with $k\leq 5$ ( $106$ , $59$ , $38$ , and $22$ pairs at $k=2,\dots,5$ ). We therefore standardize the contrast to the difficulty distribution of the shared-wrong-answer pairs: within each stratum we compute $\delta$ against both-correct pairs of the same $k$ and average with weights proportional to the number of shared-wrong-answer pairs, which is the comparison a design matched on difficulty makes. Matched on $k$ , the $S$ contrast falls from $+0.293$ to $+0.073$ , with an interval that includes zero, and matched on dataset and $k$ to $+0.016$ (Table 30); $H$ stays within noise of zero throughout. The adjustment is conservative: by construction a shared-wrong-answer pair has both English and the target language wrong, so $k\leq 5$ , while a both-correct pair has $k\geq 2$ , and conditioning on $k$ removes part of the very correctness signal under test. The adjusted value is a lower bound rather than an unbiased estimate of a difficulty-free effect. Even so, the unadjusted contrast cannot be read as evidence that $S$ tracks correctness beyond difficulty. What the data support is that low $S$ marks hard items, on which shared errors concentrate.

Review prioritization. Among the $11{,}401$ pairs whose two languages give the same answer, $225$ ( $1.97\%$ ) are wrong, and no answer-consistency check can surface any of them. We rank pairs within their own evaluation and language pair, so that differences in mean similarity between cells do not stand in for the ordering a reviewer would use, and review the least similar first. Table 31 reports the share of the $225$ found at a $25\%$ review budget. Intervals are percentile bootstrap over questions with the ranking recomputed inside each resample, and $S$ and $H$ are evaluated on the same resamples so that their difference has a paired interval. This is the same association as above, expressed as a quantity a reviewer can act on, not independent evidence. At a base rate under $2\%$ , precision within the reviewed quarter stays low, so the result concerns where to spend a fixed review budget and does not license a screening threshold.

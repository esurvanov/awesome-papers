Abstract

How much can we trust an agent to find the paper we need? As researchers, we search the literature from what we have read toward what we have not. We thus describe what we want in our own terms, which a paper may show only implicitly; our requirements grow as we read; and what we seek may lie in an empty region no paper yet covers. We introduce CompLit, a literature search benchmark spanning all eight arXiv domains, with one diagnostic setting for each of these needs. The strongest agents, Codex (GPT-5.6-Sol) and Claude Code (Opus 4.8), answer 86–87% of Standard queries, yet fall to 66–68% when requirements are implicit (Implicit), lose another 6 and 15pp, respectively, as they accumulate (Cumulative), and abstain on at most 53% of the queries no paper satisfies (Unmet). No agent leads in every domain, and all are less accurate on papers from before 2019 or after 2023. Tracing their search, we find that agents often reach the right paper, or the closest one, yet misjudge it. Doing well on CompLit is a step toward agents that we can trust to find the paper we need, or to tell us that none exists.

## 1 Introduction

Researchers increasingly ask agents such as OpenAI Deep Research, Codex, or Claude Code to find papers they need (OpenAI, 2025; Anthropic, 2025). The paper an agent returns shapes what they build on, what they cite, and whether they consider their ideas new
(Greenberg, 2009; Pavlovic et al., 2021).

Despite these stakes, most literature search benchmarks (Table 1) evaluate agents from the paper’s side rather than the researcher’s. They build each query from a target paper, so it reuses the paper’s terms, the whole request comes in a single query, and a matching paper always exists. Such queries are thus bound to the target paper: its wording, a single request, and an existing answer.

Researchers, however, search outward, from what they have explored toward what they have not (Kuhlthau, 1991). Because they frame requirements from where they stand, the paper they seek may state them only implicitly (Dasigi et al., 2021); as they read on, requirements accumulate (Vakkari, 2001); and as they seek what no one has explored, their need may be unmet (Asai and Choi, 2021).

In Figure 2, a medical imaging researcher cannot send patient scans outside the hospital. After reading how DeepSeek-R1-Zero learns to reason through reinforcement learning alone (Guo et al., 2025), they look for a method that does the same in-house. A matching paper often describes how it trains without explicitly stating that it does so in-house (implicit). Reading that such a paper tests only on X-ray, the researcher also asks for CT and MRI, ruling it out (cumulative). If no paper meets them all, the direction may still be open (unmet).

We introduce CompLit, a benchmark for compositional scientific literature search that evaluates agents in these situations. Alongside a Standard reference setting close to existing benchmarks, it has one diagnostic setting per need, each requiring a distinct ability. Implicit requires an agent to infer whether a paper meets a requirement from how the paper describes its work. Cumulative requires an agent to reassess its earlier answers and move beyond them as requirements accumulate over turns. Unmet requires an agent to abstain when no paper satisfies every requirement.

We compose each query from several requirements taken from survey comparison tables, and collect 870 single-turn queries and 220 conversations, all manually verified, across all eight arXiv domains. Beyond final accuracy and its breakdown by domain and publication year, CompLit traces agents’ search dynamics over tool calls or turns for each setting, pinpointing where they go wrong.

The strongest agents already reach 86–87% accuracy on Standard (Figure 1), where, as in existing benchmarks, most requirements are stated outright, all arrive at once, and a match always exists.
Even there, average accuracy ranges from 74% in physics to 94% in finance and economics, and falls from 88% on papers from 2019–23 to 75% on older and 80% on more recent ones (Table 3).
Beyond these gaps, each diagnostic setting reveals a different way they fall short.

On Implicit, agents struggle to infer whether a paper meets a requirement from indirect evidence. The gold paper is retrieved less often, and even when it is, agents often leave it unread, or read it and still choose another paper (Figure 3). In turn, accuracy drops 19 to 37pp, and even the best agent returns a wrong paper for about a third of queries.

On Cumulative, agents struggle to reassess their way to the right paper, with Claude Code (Opus 4.8) and Codex CLI (GPT-5.6-Sol) failing in different ways. Claude Code often leaves a wrong answer but keeps picking new wrong papers, still losing 15pp from the corresponding single-turn queries, while Codex picks a wrong paper far less often in the first place, yet still loses 6. As a result, the two, which score alike on single queries, end 11pp apart (Figure 4).

On Unmet, agents struggle to abstain when no paper qualifies. They open the paper closest to the request within ten tool calls in over 90% of queries, yet still present a paper as a match in 47% to 82% of queries (Figure 5). A researcher may thus mistake an open direction for one already taken.

In summary, our contributions are as follows:

• 

We introduce CompLit to evaluate scientific literature search on researchers’ implicit, cumulative, and unmet needs across eight domains.

• 

We test thirteen baselines, from corpus retrievers to agents, reporting trends by domain and publication year.

• 

We also analyze search dynamics to pinpoint where each agent fails, not only in retrieving a paper but also in verifying it.

Literature Search Benchmarks.

Early benchmarks established natural-language retrieval as a setting for scientific literature search. LitSearch (Ajith et al., 2024) and PaSa (He et al., 2025) generate queries automatically from citation contexts, each adding a smaller researcher-written set, and PaperFindingBench and LitQA2-Search (Bragg et al., 2026) draw queries from real user logs and from biology questions. Each of these benchmarks poses a request as a single need rather than a composition of constraints.

Multi-Constraint Queries.

A complementary line structures complex needs into multiple aspects or conditions. DORIS-MAE (Wang et al., 2023) decomposes scientific queries into hierarchical aspects, and PaSaMaster-Bench (Du et al., 2026) pairs each expert-written intent with a checklist of criteria across six domains. Beyond scholarly search, BrowseComp (Wei et al., 2025) combines hard-to-find factual web clues, while MultiConIR (Lu et al., 2025) scales queries from one to ten conditions. Recent deep-research benchmarks have brought this structure to the scientific literature. SAGE (Hu et al., 2026) composes queries from paper artifacts and citation overlaps, while AutoResearchBench (Xiong et al., 2026) derives Deep and Wide queries from target papers.

By contrast, we start from the researcher’s side, where a request requires an agent to infer evidence a paper leaves implicit, to reassess candidates as the requirements accumulate, and to abstain when no paper meets the need.

Implicit, Cumulative, and Unmet.

Each of our diagnostic settings has a counterpart outside scholarly search. Retrieval and reading comprehension benchmarks make relevance depend on facts a document states only implicitly (Taghavi et al., 2025; Tchuindjo et al., 2026). Conversational benchmarks reveal the need over turns and show that models anchor on early assumptions (Ali et al., 2026; Laban et al., 2026). Unanswerable questions test whether models abstain (Rajpurkar et al., 2018; Kirichenko et al., 2026). Scholarly search demands all three as a researcher explores what they have not: properties are rarely stated outright, requirements grow with each paper read, and their conjunction may match no paper. Yet no scholarly benchmark in Table 1 tests them. While Xiong et al. (2026) covers abstention, only 60 of its queries do so, all in computer science. CompLit tests each ability in a dedicated setting, and its analysis of search dynamics shows not only whether an agent fails but where in the search it does (§4.4).

## 3 The CompLit Benchmark

We propose CompLit, a benchmark for compositional scientific literature search. We define the task (§3.1), derive the constraints of each query (§3.2), build a Standard and three diagnostic settings (§3.3), and provide a corpus (§3.4).

## 3.1 Task Definition

A query $q$ expresses a research intent and a set of constraints $\mathcal{C}=\{c_{1},\dots,c_{k}\}$ that a paper the researcher has not yet found must satisfy jointly. A system searches a corpus $\mathcal{D}$ of papers through a fixed index or the open web.

A paper $p\in\mathcal{D}$ satisfies $c_{i}$ , written $p\models c_{i}$ , if its full paper, with figures, bibliographic record, or comparison with another paper supports $c_{i}$ , and $p\models\mathcal{C}$ if it satisfies every constraint. The gold answer is the target paper $p^{*}$ with $p^{*}\models\mathcal{C}$ , or $\varnothing$ if no paper in $\mathcal{D}$ satisfies $\mathcal{C}$ . A system returns a single paper or abstains; an answer is correct if it is $p^{*}$ , or an abstention when the gold is $\varnothing$ , and we report accuracy on each set.

## 3.2 Query Generation Pipeline

We use Qwen3.5-122B-A10B (Qwen Team, 2026) as the LLM and the VLM in every step of constraint derivation and query generation, with prompts in Appendix C.1.

Constraint sources.

We start with survey comparison tables that record each surveyed paper’s attributes. The survey authors consider distinguishing in their field, so their rows share a scope yet differ on those attributes.
Each constraint $c_{i}$ is a proposition about the target paper with a source $s_{i}\in\{\text{content},\text{figural},\text{metadata},$ $\text{comparative}\}$ .

From the table, content constraints are the cell values of the paper’s row, restated as propositions, and comparative constraints contrast the paper with another paper in the same table (e.g., broader imaging modalities).
From the paper itself, figural constraints are claims a vision-language model (VLM) reads from its figures, either quantitative (an ordering, a peak value) or qualitative (an architecture pattern, a trend), and metadata constraints are the year, categories, and authors in its arXiv record.

These sources test full-paper understanding, figure interpretation, and cross-paper comparison.

Constraint verification.

To ensure factuality and high quality, we verify every constraint exhaustively against the target paper.
When $s_{i}\in\{\text{content},\text{comparative}\}$ we do not know which part of the paper supports the constraint, so a grounder LLM reads the paper and records the supporting passage, and a verifier scores whether that passage appears in the paper and supports the constraint. When $s_{i}\in\{\text{figural},\text{metadata}\}$ , the evidence is already known: a VLM verifier scores each figural claim against its figure and caption, and metadata constraints are checked against the arXiv record.

If the verifier scores a constraint below an empirical threshold of 0.9, we reground or regenerate it and score it again for up to 10 rounds; any constraint that never passes is discarded. Every constraint that passes is then rechecked by the authors, so each clears both automatic and manual reviews.

Evidence levels.

We label every content, figural, and comparative constraint with an evidence level $\ell_{i}\in\{\text{explicit},\text{paraphrased},$ $\text{inferred}\}$ , which records how a system must ground it in the paper. We judge it against the paper for a content constraint, and against the figure for a figural one.

A constraint is explicit when the system can read the fact as it stands, and paraphrased when the system must match meaning in different words, or when the claim re-describes what the figure directly depicts. It is inferred when the system must derive the fact, because the text only implies it (e.g., training in-house), or because it follows only from comparing several visual elements (e.g., a ranking or a trend).

The inferred level also covers every comparative constraint, since establishing one requires reading a second paper. These labels are the basis of the Implicit setting (§3.3).

Query writing.

We select constraints to fit a research intent, so that a query carries only the properties a researcher would need. An intent is generated from one of 16 search purposes (Haddad et al., 2026), such as methodological guidance or comparative analysis. For each paper, we select up to three intents it can serve and verify each with the same LLM. The LLM then chooses the constraints that serve each intent and writes a request around it. We repeat generation and verification to ensure quality, and record the constraints it uses as $\mathcal{C}$ .

Human verification.

The authors then check every query three ways: each constraint’s recorded evidence must be in the paper and support the constraint; the assigned intent must fit the paper and claim nothing the paper does not support; and the query must identify the target.

Uniqueness check.

We then check automatically that every gold paper is the only known match. For each query, we retrieve the top-50 papers with both BM25 (Robertson and Zaragoza, 2009) and Qwen3-Embedding-8B (Zhang et al., 2025), and the LLM judges whether any of the 100 papers satisfies all constraints. We remove a query if any paper other than the target does, or if any paper at all does for an Unmet query. This removed 5.5%, 4.3%, and 1.0% of queries in Standard, Implicit, and Unmet, respectively.

## 3.3 Diagnostic Settings

The settings follow how a literature search unfolds. Alongside Standard, each setting evaluates one challenge.

Standard.

This setting, close to existing benchmarks, serves as a reference for the diagnostic settings. Queries state constraints at all evidence levels, given at once, and every query has a target. This set supports analysis across domains, publication years, and numbers of constraints. It contains 350 queries over 251 target papers.

Implicit.

This setting tests whether a system can infer that a paper meets requirements it states only implicitly. Every constraint must be inferred: $\ell_{i}=\text{inferred}$ for every $c_{i}\in\mathcal{C}$ , so queries carry no metadata constraints. No constraint can then be resolved by lexical or semantic matching. Rather, each must be reasoned out from what the paper reports or from how it compares to a named paper. It contains 220 queries over 179 papers.

Cumulative.

This setting tests whether a system reassesses its earlier answers as requirements accumulate over turns. The need is revealed over multiple turns: following Laban et al. (2026), we rebuild each Implicit query into a conversation of 3 to 5 turns. An offline scheduler (gpt-5.6-sol (OpenAI, 2026a)) partitions $\mathcal{C}$ into disjoint subsets $\mathcal{C}^{(1)},\dots,\mathcal{C}^{(T)}$ , one per turn with one to three constraints each, and writes the user utterance for each turn in advance.

At evaluation time, a gpt-5.4-mini (OpenAI, 2026b) user simulator at temperature 0 delivers each utterance, so every system faces the same conversation with the same constraints at each turn Laban et al. (2026). The system may answer at every turn and is scored on its final answer, once all of $\mathcal{C}$ has been revealed, with per-turn answers kept for analysis. This set contains 220 conversations.

Unmet.

This setting tests whether a system abstains when no paper meets every requirement.
We construct queries as in Standard and retain those answered correctly by all three reference systems, chosen for their low cost: Harness-1 (Jiang et al., 2026), DeepSeek-V4-Pro (DeepSeek-AI, 2026), and GLM-5.2 (GLM-5-Team, 2026).
We then add a false constraint $\tilde{c}$ by perturbing a verified constraint of the target that is not in $\mathcal{C}$ , such as substituting another paper’s survey table value or reversing a figure’s trend, so that no paper in $\mathcal{D}$ satisfies $\mathcal{C}\cup\{\tilde{c}\}$ and the gold is $\varnothing$ .
Since every target is thus easy to find, this setting separates abstention from retrieval. It contains 300 queries.

## 3.4 Corpus

For index-based search, we provide a corpus of 2.95M full-text arXiv papers from the S2ORC v2 snapshot (Lo et al., 2020), with survey papers removed so that corpus-based systems cannot rely on them to find the target, while keeping every target paper. Open-web systems are not restricted, but rarely open the source survey (Appendix D.2).

## 4 Experiments

We compare thirteen baselines on the four settings (§4.2), then analyze accuracy by domain and publication year on Standard (§4.3) and search dynamics on Implicit, Cumulative, and Unmet (§4.4).

## 4.1 Experimental Setup

Evaluation sets and corpus.

We use the four settings in §3.3: Standard, Implicit, Cumulative, and Unmet, with 350, 220, 220, and 300 instances. Corpus-based baselines search the 2.95M-paper collection described in §3.4.

Baselines.

We evaluate thirteen baselines in three groups by how they acquire candidate papers. (i) Corpus retrieval: BM25 (Robertson and Zaragoza, 2009), SPECTER2 (Singh et al., 2023), ReasonIR (Shao et al., 2025), and Qwen3-Embed-8B (Zhang et al., 2025) alone and with Qwen3-Reranker-8B. (ii) Corpus search agents: DCI-Agent-Lite (Li et al., 2026), which explores the raw corpus via shell, and Harness-1 (Jiang et al., 2026), which searches it with a state-externalizing harness. (iii) Web search agents: DR Tulu (Shao et al., 2026), PaSa (He et al., 2025), DeepSeek-V4-Pro (DeepSeek-AI, 2026), Kimi-K3 (Kimi Team, 2026), Claude Code (Anthropic, 2025) with Opus 4.8, and Codex CLI (OpenAI, 2026a) with GPT-5.6-Sol, which search the open web; we shorten the last four to DeepSeek, Kimi, Claude, and Codex, and refer to them as the frontier agents.

Corpus retrieval always returns a paper and cannot abstain, so we exclude it from Unmet. Cumulative requires holding a conversation across turns, which only DeepSeek, Kimi, Claude, and Codex support, so we run it on these four.

Tools in web agents.

DR Tulu and PaSa run with their released pipelines. Kimi and DeepSeek run in inspect_evals with three tools: arxiv_search with arXiv API, web_search through Serper, and read_paper, which returns a paper’s full text with figures, given a message limit of 30. Claude and Codex run in their own web search harnesses, plus arxiv_search and read_paper as MCP tools.

Metrics.

Each baseline either returns a paper or abstains, since the task is to find one paper that satisfies all constraints jointly (§3.1). Corpus retrieval answers with its top-ranked paper (i.e., Recall@1), so it is scored comparably to agents. Agents end each run with a JSON object holding a title and a rationale, and an empty title indicates abstention.

We report accuracy, where a prediction is correct if it matches the gold paper’s title, or if the baseline abstains on an Unmet query. On Cumulative we score the answer at the final turn. Kimi and DeepSeek runs that do not commit within the message limit receive a forced final answer, counted toward accuracy and marked separately in Figure 3.

## 4.2 Main Results

On Standard, the four frontier agents score high, while the rest vary widely within each group and no group is consistently ahead.

The frontier agents score 74.3% to 87.1%, with Codex and Claude one point apart. The rest spread widely: corpus retrieval ranges from 4.6% to 66.0%. Interestingly, the reranked retriever, at 66.0%, sits between the two corpus search agents and is even 23pp above PaSa. This set shows which baselines are effective within each group, but not how they can fail when constraints are inferred, cumulative, or unsatisfiable.

On Implicit, all baselines degrade, and even the strongest by about 20pp.

Codex and Claude degrade by 19 and 20pp, Kimi by 26, and DeepSeek by 37, showing that the weaker agents degrade more. The reranked retriever degrades the most, by 40pp, whereas the corpus search agents, which browse the same index, degrade by 27–30. This suggests that agents’ reasoning over papers helps establish properties that must be inferred.

On Cumulative, Codex and Claude, which score alike on a single query, end 11pp apart.

All four agents degrade relative to Implicit, by 2 to 15pp. In particular, the gap between Codex and Claude widens from 2pp to 11 as constraints are imposed over turns. This suggests that a single-query evaluation can obscure the gap between agents in handling cumulative requirements.

On Unmet, most baselines return a paper, and even the strongest fails to abstain on almost half of the queries.

Codex abstains correctly on 52.7% of queries and Claude on 26.3%, while every other baseline stays below 20%. Among them, Harness-1, DR Tulu, and PaSa, which are trained on answerable queries, abstain on 9.0%, 14.3%, and 8.7%. This suggests that verifying every constraint and abstaining if even one fails must be trained explicitly.

## 4.3 Accuracy by Domain and Year

Covering all eight arXiv domains and papers through 2026, CompLit can show researchers in each field which agent serves them well and where it may fall short. We break down the Standard accuracy of the four frontier agents by domain and publication year (Table 3).

No agent leads across all domains, and each agent struggles with different domains.

Codex leads in physics, EESS, and mathematics, and Claude in statistics and biology. Codex scores 85% to 88% in most domains but 80.4% in computer science, below Claude and Kimi. Claude scores above 90% in statistics and biology but 79.4% in physics. Kimi and DeepSeek are weakest in physics and statistics, and DeepSeek is also the lowest of the four in EESS and computer science.

All four are more accurate on papers in 2019–23 than on older or more recent ones.

Relative to their best period, the agents score 14 to 22pp lower on papers from 2018 or earlier: Codex, Claude, and Kimi are weakest on papers from 2015 or earlier, and DeepSeek on those from 2016 to 2018. On papers from 2024 onward, Codex, Claude, and DeepSeek score 8 to 15pp below their best, and Kimi 5pp below. This shows that agents are less reliable on older and very recent papers, which may be harder to retrieve or verify.

## 4.4 Analysis of Search Dynamics

## 4.4.1 Standard vs. Implicit

Figure 3 tracks, over tool calls, the share of queries in which the gold paper has appeared in search results (retrieved),11

On Implicit, agents fail more often at every step, across retrieving, reading, and answering with the gold.

In Figure 3, all three curves shift down and to the right from Standard to Implicit: at the same number of tool calls, the gold paper is retrieved, read, or answered in fewer queries. The final share of queries with the gold paper retrieved falls by 12 to 27pp, the share with it read by 16 to 34pp, and accuracy by 19 to 37pp.

The gaps between the steps also widen, both from retrieved to read and from read to answered correctly. This suggests that when every constraint must be inferred, agents find it harder both to decide which paper to read and to verify every constraint in a paper they have read.

The weaker the agent, the wider the gaps between retrieving, reading, and answering with the gold paper.

On Implicit, the gap between retrieving and reading the gold paper is 3pp for Codex and 13 to 15pp for the other three agents, and the gap between reading and answering grows from 1 point for Codex to 3, 7, and 12pp for Claude, Kimi, and DeepSeek. Also, Kimi and DeepSeek answer many of the papers they read only when forced, recovering 22 and 25pp of their accuracy. This suggests that agents have room to improve not only in retrieving the gold paper but also in reading and answering with it once retrieved.

## 4.4.2 Revision on Cumulative

Figure 4 tracks, by user turn, the share of conversations whose current answer is the gold paper (committed to gold) or another paper (on another paper), the share that has chosen another paper at some turn (ever chose another), and their difference (escaped).

Across turns, agents differ in how they revise their answers, and wrong answers accumulate for the weaker agents.

Two questions separate the agents: whether wrong answers rise faster than correct ones, and if so, whether the agent escapes them. For Codex, correct answers rise faster, leaving few wrong ones to escape. For the other three, wrong answers lead from the second turn. Claude escapes many of them, so the share of conversations currently on a non-gold paper (solid gray) remains near 40%, even though the share that has ever chosen one (dotted) continues to rise. Kimi and DeepSeek escape a few, and their current wrong share rises to 61%. These differences emerge only as requirements arrive over turns, so a single-turn evaluation cannot show whether an agent revises its earlier answers.

## 4.4.3 Abstention on Unmet

Each Unmet query has a near-miss, the original target that satisfies every constraint except the injected one. Figure 5 tracks, by tool call, whether the agent has opened the near-miss (near-miss opened) and given its final answer (gave a final verdict). That answer either names a paper, the near-miss or any other, as satisfying every constraint (hallucinated a match), or abstains (abstained correctly).

All agents open the near-miss early, but most go on to accept it as a match.

All four agents open the near-miss in over 90% of queries within the first ten tool calls. Yet only Codex abstains in about half of the queries, while the other three end with a paper, nearly always the near-miss, in 74% to 82% of queries. Since Unmet keeps only queries whose target the reference baselines already find (§3.3), the results suggest that agents fail at verification: they find the near-miss but do not abstain.

Agents open the near-miss at the same point, but decide at different ones.

Codex commits within a few calls of opening it, whether it abstains or not, while Claude and Kimi decide gradually and DeepSeek mostly in its last few calls. The later an agent decides, the less often it abstains, from 52.7% for Codex to 17.3% for DeepSeek. Stronger agents thus seem to better gauge their confidence in the near-miss (Yoon et al., 2025).

## 5 Conclusion

We introduced CompLit, a benchmark that evaluates agents in the way researchers search: outward from what they have read, with needs that are implicit, cumulative, or unmet. Even the strongest agents lose about 20 points when every constraint is implicit and fall further, each in a different way, as requirements accumulate, while most agents accept a near-miss instead of abstaining. Beyond final accuracy, CompLit traces each agent’s search step by step, revealing where these failures arise: agents often reach the right paper, or the closest one, yet misjudge it. An agent that does well here can be trusted to find the paper that meets a need, or to say that none does.

Looking ahead, we envision two directions. One is a harness for literature search, guided by where each agent fails and which papers it finds hard. The other is a world model of a field, distilled from surveys, so that an agent knows how to search a field and check a condition before the search begins. We hope CompLit serves as a foundation for future work on scientific literature search.

## Limitations

CompLit draws its constraints from comparison tables in arXiv surveys from English arXiv papers and relies on the distinctions survey authors chose to compare. Constraints and queries are generated and verified automatically, but we check every constraint and every query by hand to remove remaining errors. Finally, open-web agents search a live web, so their results may shift slightly if the experiments are rerun.

## A.2 Human Verification

The authors reviewed every paper in a web interface that shows the paper’s full text and figures next to each generated item. Review proceeds in stages, and a paper enters the benchmark only after it passes the query stage.

1. 

Constraints. For each content, comparative, and figural constraint, the reviewer checks that the recorded evidence appears in the paper and supports the constraint, and corrects its value or level. Verifier-dropped table cells are shown too, so a wrong drop can be restored.

2. 

Intents. The reviewer checks that each intent fits the paper and claims nothing the paper does not support.

3. 

Queries. The reviewer checks that the query identifies the target and that its constraint set matches the text, and edits either one.

## A.3 Cumulative and Unmet Construction

Scheduler rules.

The Cumulative scheduler turns each Implicit query into a conversation under the following rules, and code enforces every rule except the last.

• 

The opening turn states the researcher’s broad goal and carries no constraint. Its motivation stays one level more general than every constraint, and the audit caps its score when it shares two or more content words with them.

• 

Each later turn carries one to three constraints that form one thought, and every constraint appears exactly once.

• 

A conversation has at least three turns, and the last turn asks the agent to commit to one paper or to say that none qualifies.

• 

Each turn names a search purpose from the intent catalog, and the purpose may change across turns.

• 

The scheduler holds back the constraint that most directly identifies the target for a later turn, so the opening alone does not identify it. This rule is given only as an instruction.

An audit by gpt-5.6-sol rescores each turn’s motivation and asks for a rewrite below 0.8. Figure 9 summarizes the resulting conversations: most have four turns, and most later turns reveal two constraints.

Figure 9: Cumulative conversations. Turns per conversation and constraints per turn after the opening, as a share of the 220 conversations and of their 642 later turns.

User simulator.

At evaluation time, gpt-5.4-mini at temperature 0 delivers the prepared utterance of each turn. When the agent’s last message asks a question or proposes a paper, the simulator adds one sentence that answers from what the user has already said, or that states the project has no requirement on that point. It never adds a constraint, so every agent faces the same constraints at each turn.

Unmet construction.

We start from answerable queries that the reference systems answer correctly, so that a failure on Unmet reflects abstention rather than retrieval. We use only the variant without metadata. For each query, gpt-5.6-sol writes one false clause of one of two kinds.

• 

A table value. The clause asserts a false value on a survey table column that the query does not use. The true value must appear in the paper’s body but not in its title or abstract.

• 

A figure fact. The clause reverses a verified figural claim, such as an ordering or a trend, whose fact the paper’s text also states.

Every original sentence of the query is kept, and the clause adds 7 to 30 words. A judge then reads the target paper and labels the clause as refuted when the paper contradicts it, silent when the paper does not settle it, or satisfied. We drop satisfied clauses.

## Appendix B Lexical Retrieval Across Benchmarks

We compare how hard CompLit questions are to match lexically against existing literature-search benchmarks. We run one BM25 retriever over one corpus for six benchmarks and for Standard and Implicit.

Setup.

The corpus holds the titles and abstracts of all 3,182,867 arXiv papers in the arXiv metadata snapshot of September 28, 2026. Unlike the BM25 baseline of Appendix D.1, it indexes no full bodies, so that the gold papers of every benchmark are in the corpus. BM25 uses tantivy ( $k_{1}{=}1.2$ , $b{=}0.75$ ) and takes each question verbatim. Several benchmarks list many gold papers per question, which caps Recall@1 below one, so we report H@ $k$ , the share of questions with at least one gold paper in the top $k$ .

Gold papers.

We map every gold paper to an arXiv id and skip questions left with none. PaSa (He et al., 2025) lists arXiv ids for RealScholarQuery and the AutoScholarQuery test split. For LitSearch (Ajith et al., 2024), 520 of 639 gold papers have an arXiv id in Semantic Scholar, which leaves 498 of 597 questions. For PaperFindingBench (Bragg et al., 2026), we use the May 2025 test split; 392 of 803 listed papers have an arXiv id, and most semantic questions list no gold paper because they are scored by a judge, which leaves 101 of 267 questions. For AutoResearchBench (Xiong et al., 2026), we use the Wide subset. For DORIS-MAE (Wang et al., 2023), we use the BIRCO release, match 5,505 of its 5,544 candidate abstracts to arXiv by text, and count a paper as gold if its relevance score is at least 1.0. DORIS-MAE is thus scored against all of arXiv rather than its 100-paper candidate pools. We exclude LitQA2, since only one of its 199 questions cites an arXiv paper.

Results.

Among benchmarks with one or two gold papers per question, Implicit is the hardest to match at the top ranks, while Standard is the easiest (Table 6). BM25 ranks a gold paper first for 1.8% of Implicit questions, against 7.1–18.5% on PaSa, PaperFindingBench, and LitSearch. By the top 100 the gap closes, so the gold paper is often retrieved but rarely ranked first: the constraints it satisfies are inferred rather than stated in the question. Standard reaches 25.7% at H@1, the highest of all benchmarks, consistent with its constraints being stated in the question. PaperFindingBench includes 27 metadata questions on authors and years, which a title-and-abstract index cannot answer.

## Appendix C Does the Agent Harness Matter?

Claude Code and Codex CLI run in their own harnesses, while Kimi and DeepSeek run in inspect_evals. We test whether the harness, rather than the model, explains part of the gap, by running DeepSeek on Implicit inside the Claude Code and Codex CLI harnesses.

Setup.

The model, tools, and prompt are those of the inspect_evals run (Appendix D.1): DeepSeek-V4-Pro, the same arxiv_search, text-only read_paper, and Serper web_search exposed as MCP tools, and the same single user prompt. The built-in web tools of both harnesses are disabled. Each harness keeps its own system prompt, agent loop, and context management. Without a limit, DeepSeek keeps searching and rarely answers, so each harness allows 30 tool calls per query, after which further calls are blocked with an instruction to answer. In inspect_evals, a query ends after 30 messages (median 19 tool calls), and runs that have not committed receive a forced final answer.

Results.

The two harnesses score the same, and both match inspect_evals once an answer is forced (Table 7). DeepSeek reaches 41.4% in Claude Code and 40.9% in Codex CLI, against 37.3% in inspect_evals with the forced final answer. Without it, inspect_evals reaches 12.7%, so the gap comes from forcing a commitment, not from the harness. The remaining 4pp coincides with the larger tool budget of the harnesses (30 against a median of 19 calls). In the same harnesses, DeepSeek stays far below Claude Code and Codex CLI with their own models (65.9% and 67.7%, Table 2), so the harness does not explain that gap.

## Appendix D Experimental Details

## D.1 Baseline Configurations

We describe the configuration of every baseline. All runs took place between August 12 and August 24, 2026. Web agents searched the live web in that window.

Corpus retrieval.

Every retriever returns a top-100 ranking over the 2.95M-paper corpus, and we score its first paper (Recall@1). BM25 indexes titles and full bodies with tantivy ( $k_{1}{=}1.2$ , $b{=}0.75$ ). SPECTER2 embeds the title and the first 2,000 characters of the body. ReasonIR and Qwen3-Embed-8B embed up to 32,512 tokens per paper, and Qwen3-Embed-8B uses the instruction “Given a web search query, retrieve relevant passages that answer the query”. Qwen3-Rerank-8B reranks the top 100 of Qwen3-Embed-8B using each paper’s title and opening, up to 4,096 tokens.

Corpus search agents.

DCI-Agent-Lite runs gpt-5.4-nano with high thinking effort and read and bash tools over per-month files of the arXiv metadata snapshot, which hold titles, authors, abstracts, and categories, for at most 100 turns and one hour per query. Harness-1 is served with vLLM and searches the corpus with a hybrid of BM25 and Qwen3-Embed-8B, with grep, document reading, and pruning tools. It runs a single rollout with at most 50 steps and a 32,768-token context, at temperature 1.0.

Open-web agents.

DR Tulu and PaSa run their released pipelines. DR Tulu-8B uses Google search through Serper, Semantic Scholar snippet search, and a page browser summarized by Qwen3-8B, with at most 10 tool calls. PaSa’s crawler generates five search queries and then expands two citation layers, with at most 20 papers per layer. Kimi and DeepSeek run in inspect_ai as a ReAct agent with no system message, provider-default decoding, and three tools:

• 

arxiv_search queries the arXiv API and returns titles, ids, years, authors, and truncated abstracts;

• 

web_search queries Google through Serper and returns titles, URLs, and snippets;

• 

read_paper returns a paper’s full text and attaches up to four figures as images. DeepSeek reads no images.

Claude Code and Codex CLI use their built-in web search and receive arxiv_search and read_paper as MCP tools. Claude may also call WebFetch, and Codex a shell restricted to reading files. They have no turn limit and a wall-clock limit of 900 seconds per query.

## D.2 Corpus and Survey Access

Each query is built from a survey’s comparison table, so its source survey lists the target with its attributes. We remove these surveys from the corpus for index-based search (§3.4), but open-web systems can still reach them. We therefore count how often the four frontier agents open the source survey of a query, either with read_paper or by fetching its page.

Across the four settings, the frontier agents open the source survey in 3.8% of runs (164 of 4,360): 1.8% for Codex, 4.3% for Claude, 4.9% for Kimi, and 4.0% for DeepSeek. Opening it does not help. Accuracy on queries where the agent opens the source survey is lower than on the rest in 10 of the 16 agent and setting pairs, and each of the other six rests on 12 or fewer such queries. This suggests that agents turn to the survey when a query is already hard, and that it does not lead them to the target.

## E.6 Qualitative Comparison of Agents

Tables 13–20 put the four frontier agents side by side on eight queries from six domains, two for each setting. Calls counts tool calls, and Opened records whether the agent read the gold paper, or the near-miss on Unmet. Excerpts are verbatim from the agents’ answers, and we checked every wrong answer against the text of the paper it names. We chose these cases to illustrate how agents differ, among queries where the agents disagree and every wrong answer demonstrably violates a constraint; they are not a random sample, and aggregate accuracy is given in Table 2.

Standard: checking the one distinctive requirement.

In Table 13 (physics), the distinctive requirement is a dipolar propagator from opposite dislocations. Codex and DeepSeek open the gold and confirm it. Claude searches for 48 calls, passes over the gold in one search result, settles on a recent model of deformed crystals, and concedes that the paper does not use that phrase. In Table 14 (stat), Codex and Claude open the gold and check the sparsity condition, while Kimi and DeepSeek note the gold in their search results, then follow a related method and never open it.

Implicit: checking what must be inferred.

In Table 15 (q-bio), whether a candidate’s model has four parameters, is deterministic, and adds a linear final trend must be checked against its model equations.

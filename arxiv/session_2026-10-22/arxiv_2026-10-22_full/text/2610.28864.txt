Abstract.

A systematic understanding of the global structure of neural-network loss landscapes remains challenging due to their high dimensionality. Symmetries arising from network architectures and learning objectives provide a way to investigate aspects of this structure. Although a growing number of studies have drawn attention to these symmetries, a systematic characterization of them, as well as of the critical orbits they give rise to, remains incomplete. To address this gap, in this work we establish a topological symmetry index, based on the methodologies from the family of equivariant degree theories, to decode these symmetries. The frameworks apply to arbitrary network architectures and include methods applicable to degenerate critical orbits. The index detects the isotropy of the critical points and quantifies the corresponding net topological contributions. Several examples, including softplus MLPs, Transformers, and equivariant neural networks, are presented to illustrate the computations.

Keywords:
Symmetries, Topological Index, Equivariant Degree Theory, Neural Network, Loss Landscape.

## 1. Introduction

Symmetries in neural network give rise to a rich variety of phenomena in its high-dimensional loss landscapes. One line of research, developed by Arjevani and Field, studies the spurious minima and their symmetries in shallow networks, as well as their bifurcations as network width $k$ varies. For two-layer ReLU networks in a specific teacher–student setting, they observed in [3] that critical points obtained by SGD have symmetries conjugate to maximal subgroups of $S_{k}$ of intransitive type. In [6], they analytically constructed these families of critical points and expressed them as convergent power series in $k^{-1/2}.$ The corresponding Hessian spectra were further analyzed in [4]. They further extended analogous results to symmetric tensor decomposition in [1], and studied the optimization landscape of networks with polynomial activations [2]. In [5], they identified width thresholds in a shallow ReLU network at which saddles turn into local minima and rigorously established in [7] that over-parameterization (adding neurons) introduces negative Hessian eigenvalues and thereby turns local minima into saddles. Using equivariant bifurcation theory, they further showed in [8] that the local minima emerge through steady-state bifurcations along the standard representation of $S_{k}$ rather than through spontaneous symmetry breaking of global minima. Correspondingly, Liu [30] studied bifurcations from global minima in leaky ReLU networks of arbitrary width, using the negative-slope parameter as the bifurcation parameter, and obtained unified results on the bifurcating branches and their symmetries.

Beyond the structure of critical points, other work connects network symmetries to the broader geometry of loss landscapes, training dynamics, and optimization. Zhao et al. [39] identified flat low-loss valleys with continuous symmetry-induced critical orbits, and derived initialization-fixed conserved quantities that constrain gradient-flow reachability. In [37], they further studied the connectivity of minima and identified loss barriers along linear mode connectivity. Zamir et al. [36] improved the learning-to-optimize (L2O) framework by enabling the optimizer to select, along group orbits, parameters that are more favorable for subsequent descent prior to local updates. The discovery of the symmetry actions themselves is addressed in [38], which proposes a method for automatically discovering data-dependent symmetries through infinitesimal characterizations of Lie group and expressing loss invariance as differential constraints.

The use of symmetry extends further to network design, where equivariant architectures incorporate prescribed symmetries to improve data efficiency and physical consistency. Kondor and Trivedi [29] established, under appropriate assumptions, the equivalence between compact-group equivariance and generalized convolutional structure. Tensor field networks [33] combine spherical harmonics and Clebsch–Gordan coupling to construct equivariant layers for three-dimensional point clouds, while [28] use tensor products and irreducible decompositions to implement equivariant nonlinearities entirely in Fourier space. [18] developed concentric spherical networks combining intra-sphere graph convolutions with inter-sphere radial convolutions, with equivariance established for the icosahedral rotation group. The e3nn framework [25] provides a unified set of composable operations for constructing $E(3)$ -equivariant architectures. For physical dynamical systems, Wang et al. [34] designed forecasting networks incorporating different physical symmetries, and demonstrated improvements in prediction and generalization. They subsequently introduced relaxed group and steerable convolutions with symmetry regularization to accommodate imperfectly symmetric dynamics [35], and established an approximation-error lower bound for strictly equivariant models applied to non-equivariant targets.

These studies demonstrate the importance of symmetry for understanding loss landscapes and training dynamics, as well as its value for optimizer and network design. However, when it comes to the symmetric structure of the loss landscape, most of the above analyses either focus on a specific network architecture or presuppose a priori knowledge of the critical points. These considerations motivate a complementary question:

Can a unified, computable criterion certify the presence of critical orbits and characterize their symmetries
across different network architectures without explicitly constructing the
critical points?

In this work, we address this question by introducing a topological symmetry index based on the methodology of Equivariant Degree Theory. Under suitable hypotheses, the index provides criteria for the existence of critical orbits with specified symmetries within a prescribed region.

To formulate the problem precisely, let $G$ be a symmetry group inherent in a neural network and acting on its parameter space. The interplay among architectural structure, learning setting, data distribution, activation functions, and loss specification can endow the resulting loss function $\mathscr{L}$ with $G$ -invariance, i.e. $\mathscr{L}(g\cdot\theta)=\mathscr{L}(\theta),g\in G.$ 
When the group action is isometric, this invariance implies the $G$ -equivariance of its gradient:

(1)

 $\nabla\mathscr{L}(g\cdot\theta)=g\cdot\nabla\mathscr{L}(\theta),\;\;g\in G.$ 

We therefore seek the zeros of $\nabla\mathscr{L}$ , which are precisely the critical points of $\mathscr{L}$ . The equivariance allows us to obtain integer-valued topological weights associated with orbit types. We define the symmetry index

(2)

 $\mathcal{I}_{G}(\mathscr{L},\Omega)=\Big\{\big((K_{j_{1}}),n_{j_{1}}\big),\big((K_{j_{2}}),n_{j_{2}}\big),\cdots,\big((K_{j_{s}}),n_{j_{s}}\big)\Big\}$ 

by extracting the maximal orbit types among those with nonzero coefficients associated with $\nabla\mathscr{L}$ on $\Omega$ , written as

(3)

 $\mathbf{a}=\sum_{j=1}^{m}n_{j}(K_{j})\in U(G).$ 

Here, $U(G)$ is the Euler ring (see appendix A for definition and properties of Euler ring), and each integer coefficient $n_{j},\;j=1,\cdots,m$ is associated with the conjugacy class $(K_{j}),$ where $K_{j}$ is a subgroup of $G$ . A nonzero coefficient certifies the existence of a critical orbit whose isotropy type is at least $(K_{j}).$ The selected maximal orbit types are denoted by $(K_{j_{1}}),\ldots,(K_{j_{s}})$ . The underlying degree theories and computational methods are introduced in Sections 3 and 4. One is also refered to [11, 13, 12, 17, 16, 22, 24, 23, 21, 32] and the reference therein for more comprehensive details about the constructions of equivariant degrees, as well as their applications [20, 9, 14, 10, 19, 30].

Theorem 1 (Topological symmetry index of a neural-network).

Suppose that the parameter space $V$ is a finite-dimensional orthogonal representation of a compact Lie group $G$ , and that
the loss function $\mathscr{L}$ is twice continuously
differentiable and $G$ -invariant. Let $\Omega\subset V$ be an open bounded and $G$ -invariant parameter subset such that $\nabla\mathscr{L}$ is nonzero on $\partial\Omega$ . Then the symmetry index $\mathcal{I}_{G}(\mathscr{L},\Omega)$ defined in (2) is well defined. Moreover, for every

 $\bigl((K_{j_{r}}),n_{j_{r}}\bigr)\in\mathcal{I}_{G}(\mathscr{L},\Omega),\;\;r=1,\cdots,s,$ 

there exists $\theta_{*}\in\Omega$ such that

 $\nabla\mathscr{L}(\theta_{*})=0,$ 

and its symmetry is at least $(K_{j_{r}}).$ 

The same conclusion holds when $V$ is replaced by a $G$ -manifold equipped with a $G$ -invariant Riemannian metric (see Section 4).

The subsequent sections are arranged as follows. For the readers convenience, Section 2 collects the notations and definitions used throughout the paper. Given that the loss function in (1) is of gradient type, Sections 3 and 4 present two gradient-related Equivariant Degree methods, together with the algorithms to determine $(K)$ and the corresponding $n_{K}$ . More precisely, in sections 3.1-3.2, one can find construction of gradient degree based on recursive formula and the method of reducing to slice. In section 3.3, we focus on the linear isomorphism in which the ambient space $V$ is an orthogonal $G$ -representation, and the degree is constructed through the basic degree induced by irreducible decomposition of $V.$ On the other hand, Section 4 treats the general setting of a $G$ -manifold $V$ . Without an underlying linear structure, the construction proceeds instead through orbit-type stratification and an inductive summation over the resulting strata. Section 5 then applies the resulting index to several concrete examples: a softplus teacher–student model (Section 5.1), an $O(3)$ -equivariant network (Section 5.2), and a transformer architecture (Section 5.3), using the linear isomorphism method, the slice principle, and the recursive formula, respectively. Appendix B recalls the construction and computational algorithm of the Equivariant Brouwer Degree, since, as the paper unfolds, one may quickly observe that the computation of the gradient-related Equivariant Degree methods ultimately can reduce to their Brouwer counterpart. Moreover, Appendices A and C present the properties of the Euler and Burnside rings, and the definition of amalgamated groups, respectively.

## 2. Some Equivariant Terminology

We first emphasize that the degree theories considered in this work require $G$ to be a compact Lie group. Accordingly, the present analysis does not cover noncompact groups such as the positive scaling symmetries associated with ReLU activation.

To construct the symmetry index $\mathcal{I}_{G}(\mathscr{L},\Omega)$ , one must first specify the following several ingredients:

• 

The loss function $\mathscr{L}$ should be $G$ -invariant, with $G$ -equivariant gradient $\nabla\mathscr{L}$ .

• 

The parameter space $V$ and the $G$ action on $V$ must be identified.

• 

A suitable domain $\Omega\subset V$ must be specified:
 $\Omega$ is required to be an open bounded $G$ -invariant subset of $V$ . Most importantly, $\Omega$ must be chosen so that

 $\nabla\mathscr{L}(\theta)\neq 0,\;\;\theta\in\partial\Omega,$ 

a condition on which the equivariant degree theory, and hence our approach, rests.

These constitute the basic ingredients needed for the construction of
 $\mathcal{I}_{G}(\mathscr{L},\Omega)$ . Each arises naturally from the construction of equivariant degree, and its significance will become apparent as the
framework is developed below. The resulting $G$ -equivariant gradient degree is an element of the Euler ring $U(G)$ of the form

 $\nabla_{G}\text{-}\deg(\nabla\mathscr{L},\Omega)=n_{1}(K_{1})+n_{2}(K_{2})+\cdots+n_{m}(K_{m}),$ 

from which the symmetry index $\mathcal{I}_{G}(\mathscr{L},\Omega)$ is read off directly. The precise definition of the degree, the term $(K_{j})$ and $n_{j}$ , and the techniques appropriate to the different settings to derive this degree will be developed in detail in Sections 3 and 4.

An orbit whose isotropy group belongs to the conjugacy class $(K)$ is called a $(K)$ -orbit. The set $\Phi(G)$ is partially ordered by

 $(K)\leq(H)\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad gKg^{-1}\subseteq H\text{ for some }g\in G.$

Notice that $W(K)$ acts naturally on $V^{K}$ , and its action on $V_{K}$ is free. Finally, a smooth manifold equipped with a smooth $G$ -action will be called a $G$ -manifold.

We next collect several notions common to the degree constructions in the subsequent sections. For uniformity, we formulate them for a smooth $n$ -dimensional $G$ -manifold, an $n$ -dimensional orthogonal $G$ -representation constitutes a special case.

Equip the smooth $n$ -dimensional $G$ -manifold $V$ with a $G$ -invariant Riemannian metric, and choose a relatively compact invariant open subset $\Omega$ . We write $C_{G}^{2}(V,\mathbb{R})$ for the twice continuously differentiable $G$ -invariant functions on $V$ . For $\varphi\in C_{G}^{2}(V,\mathbb{R})$ , its gradient is an equivariant section of the tangent bundle:

 $\nabla\varphi:V\longrightarrow TV,\qquad\nabla\varphi(\theta)\in T_{\theta}V.$ 

Definition 2 ( $G$ -Equivariant Gradient Admissible Pair).

We call the pair $(\nabla\varphi,\Omega)$ a
 $G$ -equivariant gradient admissible pair if $\nabla\varphi(\theta)\neq 0,\;\theta\in\partial\Omega,$ i.e. $\nabla\varphi$ doesn’t vanish on the boundary of $\Omega.$ 

Denote by $\mathcal{M}_{G}^{\nabla}(V,TV)$ all the admissible pairs in $V$ . Allowing $V$ to range over finite-dimensional smooth $G$ -manifolds, we define

 $\mathcal{M}_{G}^{\nabla}:=\bigcup_{V}\mathcal{M}_{G}^{\nabla}(V,TV).$ 

Definition 3 ( $G$ -Gradient $\Omega$ -Admissible Homotopy).

We call a homotopy $\nabla\Phi:[0,1]\times V\longrightarrow TV$ $G$ -gradient $\Omega$ -admissible if, for every $t\in[0,1]$ ,

 $\nabla\Phi_{t}:=\nabla\Phi(t,\cdot):V\longrightarrow TV$ 

is a $G$ -equivariant gradient vector field and $\nabla\Phi(\theta)\neq 0,\theta\in\partial\Omega.$ 

We now introduce the two algebraic structures in which the equivariant
degrees considered below take their values.

Definition 4 (Euler ring).

The Euler ring of $G$ is the free $\mathbb{Z}$ -module

 $U(G):=\bigoplus_{(K)\in\Phi(G)}\mathbb{Z}(K),$ 

so that each $a\in U(G)$ has a unique representation

 $a=\sum_{(K)\in\Phi(G)}n_{K}(K),\qquad n_{K}\in\mathbb{Z},$ 

with only finitely many $n_{K}$ nonzero. Multiplication on $U(G)$ is defined on generators by

 $(K)*(H):=\sum_{(S)\in\Phi(G)}c_{S}(S),\qquad(K),(H)\in\Phi(G),$ 

where

(5)

 $c_{S}:=\chi_{c}\bigl((G/K\times G/H)_{S}/N(S)\bigr),$ 

and extended bilinearly to all of $U(G)$ . Here $\chi_{c}$ denotes the Euler characteristic with respect to Alexander–Spanier cohomology with compact support.

Definition 5 (Burnside ring).

The Burnside ring of $G$ is the free $\mathbb{Z}$ -module generated by the classes with finite Weyl group,

 $A(G):=\bigoplus_{(K)\in\Phi_{0}(G)}\mathbb{Z}(K),$ 

whose elements are written as

 $b=\sum_{(K)\in\Phi_{0}(G)}n_{K}(K)$ 

with $n_{K}\in\mathbb{Z}$ . Multiplication on $A(G)$ is defined by

 $(K)\cdot(H):=\sum_{(S)\in\Phi_{0}(G)}n_{S}(S),\qquad(K),(H)\in\Phi_{0}(G),$ 

where $n_{S}$ is the number of $(S)$ -orbits in $G/K\times G/H$ , and extended bilinearly to all of $A(G)$ .

Notice that $n_{S}$ can be determined recursively from

(6)

 $n_{S}=\frac{n(S,K)|W(K)|\,n(S,H)|W(H)|-\displaystyle\sum_{(\widetilde{S})>(S)}n_{\widetilde{S}}\,n(S,\widetilde{S})\,|W(\widetilde{S})|}{|W(S)|}.$ 

The two rings are related by the ring homomorphism $\pi_{0}\colon U(G)\to A(G),$ 

(7)

 $\pi_{0}((K))=\begin{cases}(K),&(K)\in\Phi_{0}(G),\\
0,&(K)\notin\Phi_{0}(G).\end{cases}$ 

As will be seen in the subsequent sections, the homomorphism $\pi_{0}$ 
provides the key reduction from the Euler-ring-valued equivariant gradient
degree to its Burnside-ring-valued counterpart, and will play an essential
role in the computation of the corresponding basic degrees. For more details regarding Burnside ring and Euler ring, including some of their properties and corresponding proofs, one is referred to Appendix A.

## 3. Equivariant Gradient Degree on Orthogonal $G$ -representations

Having established the common notations and algebraic framework, we now introduce the equivariant gradient degree, beginning with orthogonal $G$ -representations. For a complete construction of the gradient degree, together with its properties and full proofs, we refer the readers to [24, 16].

## 3.1. Methodology based on recursive construction.

Assume that $V$ is an $n$ -dimensional orthogonal representation and that $(\nabla\varphi,\Omega)$ satisfies the gradient-admissibility conditions defined in Section 2. To explain the construction of the $G$ -equivariant gradient degree and its properties, we first introduce the class of special $\Omega$ -Morse functions. These functions also provide good representatives of $\nabla\varphi$ up to $\Omega$ -admissible $G$ -gradient homotopy, in parallel with regular normal maps for the equivariant Brouwer degree in Appendix B. To define them, we begin with the normal bundle $\pi:\mathcal{N}^{(K)}\to V_{(K)}$ of an orbit-type stratum $V_{(K)},$ whose fiber over $\theta\in V_{(K)}$ is

 $\mathcal{N}_{\theta}^{(K)}=(T_{\theta}V_{(K)})^{\perp}\subset T_{\theta}V.$ 

Endowed with the inner product inherited from $V$ , $\mathcal{N}^{(K)}$ is a Euclidean $G$ -bundle over $V_{(K)}$ . For an invariant open subset $U_{0}\subset V_{(K)}$ and $\varepsilon>0$ , the normal disk bundle of radius $\varepsilon$ over $U_{0}$ is

 $\mathcal{N}(U_{0},\varepsilon)=\{y\in\mathcal{N}^{(K)}:\pi(y)\in U_{0},\ \|y\|<\varepsilon\}.$ 

On a suitable invariant neighborhood $W$ of the zero section, the exponential map embeds the normal bundle equivariantly into $V$ .

Definition 6 ( $(K)$ -Normal Set).

An invariant open set $U\subset V$ is $(K)$ -normal when it is the exponential image of a normal disk bundle over an invariant open part of $V_{(K)}$ : $U=\exp\bigl(\mathcal{N}(U_{0},\varepsilon)\bigr),$ 
where $\mathcal{N}(U_{0},\varepsilon)\subset W.$ 

Definition 7 ( $(K)$ -Normal Vector Field).

Let $\nabla\varphi:V\to TV$ be an $\Omega$ -admissible vector field. Then $\nabla\varphi$ is called ( $K)$ -normal if there exist an $(K)$ -normal subset $U=\exp\bigl(\mathcal{N}(U_{0},\varepsilon)\bigr)$ 
and a smooth invariant function $\varphi_{0}:U_{0}\to\mathbb{R}$ 
such that

a) 

 $\nabla\varphi^{-1}(0)\cap V_{(K)}\cap\Omega\subset U_{0},$ 

b) 

 $U\subset\Omega,$ 

c) 

for all $\theta\in U$ , $\varphi(\theta)=\varphi_{0}(\pi(y))+\frac{1}{2}\|y\|^{2},$ 
where $\theta=\exp(y),\quad y\in\mathcal{N}(U_{0},\varepsilon).$ 

Definition 8 ( $\Omega$ -Morse Function).

A $G$ -gradient admissible map $\nabla\varphi$ is special $\Omega$ -Morse provided that

a) 

 $\nabla\varphi|_{\Omega}$ is of class $C^{1}$ .

b) 

All its zero orbits in $\Omega$ are regular.

c) 

 $\nabla\varphi$ is $(K)$ -normal for every orbit type $(K)$ occurring in its zero set in $\Omega$ .

The existence of such representatives, together with the $\Omega$ -admissibility of the corresponding homotopy, follows from the results in [31]. We next define the $G$ -Equivariant Gradient Degree.

Definition 9 ( $G$ -Equivariant Gradient Degree).

The $G$ -equivariant gradient degree assigns to each pair $(\nabla\varphi,\Omega)\in\mathcal{M}^{\nabla}_{G}$ an element of the Euler ring $U(G)$ . We denote this degree by $\nabla_{G}\text{-}\deg(\nabla\varphi,\Omega)$ and write it as

(8)

 $\nabla_{G}\text{-deg}(\nabla\varphi,\Omega)=\sum_{(K_{j})\in\Phi(G)}n_{j}(K_{j})=n_{1}(K_{1})+\cdots+n_{m}(K_{m}),$ 

and we call it the $G$ -equivariant gradient degree of $\nabla\varphi$ 
in $\Omega$ .

Theorem 10.

The degree
 $\nabla_{G}\mbox{\rm-}\deg:\mathcal{M}_{G}^{\nabla}\to U(G)$ introduced in Definition 9 has the following properties:

( $\mathcal{G}_{1}$ ) 

Additivity.
If two disjoint invariant open subsets $\Omega_{1},\Omega_{2}\subset\Omega$ together contain all zeros in $\Omega$ , the degree splits into their contributions:

 $\nabla_{G}\text{-deg}(\nabla\varphi,\Omega)=\nabla_{G}\text{-deg}(\nabla\varphi,\Omega_{1})+\nabla_{G}\text{-deg}(\nabla\varphi,\Omega_{2}).$ 

( $\mathcal{G}_{2}$ ) 

Homotopy.
Along any $\Omega$ -admissible $G$ -gradient homotopy $\nabla\Phi_{t}:V\longrightarrow V,$ where $t\in[0,1],$ 
the degree is preserved:

 $\nabla_{G}\text{-deg}(\nabla\Phi_{t},\Omega)=\nabla_{G}\text{-deg}(\nabla\Phi_{0},\Omega),\qquad t\in[0,1].$ 

( $\mathcal{G}_{3}$ ) 

Normalization.
For a special $\Omega$ -Morse function whose critical set in $\Omega$ consists of $G(\theta_{o})$ , and set $(K)=(G_{\theta_{o}})$ , then

 $\nabla_{G}\text{-}\deg(\nabla\varphi,\Omega)=(-1)^{m^{-}(\nabla^{2}\varphi(\theta_{o}))}(K).$ 

Here $m^{-}(\nabla^{2}\varphi(\theta_{o}))$ is the dimension of the negative eigensubspace of the Hessian $\nabla^{2}\varphi(\theta_{o})$ .

( $\mathcal{G}_{4}$ ) 

Existence. If $n_{j}\neq 0$ is a coefficient of $\nabla_{G}\text{-}\deg(\nabla\varphi,\Omega)=\sum_{j=1}^{m}n_{j}(K_{j})$ , then $\nabla\varphi$ possesses a zero $\theta_{o}\in\Omega$ with $(G_{\theta_{o}})\geq(K_{j})$ .

( $\mathcal{G}_{5}$ ) 

Product. The degree of the product of two admissible gradient maps is obtained by multiplying their degrees in $U(G)$ :

 $\nabla_{G}\text{-deg}(\nabla\varphi_{1}\times\nabla\varphi_{2},\Omega_{1}\times\Omega_{2})=\nabla_{G}\text{-deg}(\nabla\varphi_{1},\Omega_{1})\star\nabla_{G}\text{-deg}(\nabla\varphi_{2},\Omega_{2}),$ 

where $\star$ denotes multiplication in $U(G)$ .

( $\mathcal{G}_{6}$ ) 

Suspension.
Let $W$ be an orthogonal $G$ -representation and let $B(W)$ 
be its unit ball. Then

 $\nabla_{G}\text{-deg}(\nabla\varphi\times\operatorname{Id}_{W},\Omega\times B(W))=\nabla_{G}\text{-deg}(\nabla\varphi,\Omega).$ 

( $\mathcal{G}_{7}$ ) 

Hopf Property.
Two admissible $G$ -gradient maps on the unit ball $B(V)$ have equal degrees precisely when they can be connected by a $B(V)$ -admissible homotopy consisting of $G$ -gradient maps.

( $\mathcal{G}_{8}$ ) 

Functoriality.
Let
 $\psi\colon G_{0}\hookrightarrow G$ be an embedding of Lie groups. Then $\psi$ 
induces a $G_{0}$ -action on $V$ under which $\nabla\varphi$ is an $\Omega$ -admissible
 $G_{0}$ -gradient map, and

 $\Psi\big[\nabla_{G}\text{-}\deg(\nabla\varphi,\Omega)\big]=\nabla_{G_{0}}\text{-}\deg(\nabla\varphi,\Omega),$ 

where $\Psi\colon U(G)\to U(G_{0})$ denotes the homomorphism of Euler rings
induced by $\psi$ .

( $\mathcal{G}_{9}$ ) 

Reduction.
The homomorphism $\pi_{0}$ from (7) maps the gradient degree to the equivariant Brouwer degree:

 $\pi_{0}\!\left[\nabla_{G}\text{-deg}(\nabla\varphi,\Omega)\right]=G\text{-deg}(\nabla\varphi,\Omega).$ 

For proofs of some of the properties stated above, we refer the reader to Section 6 of [15].

Remark 11.

It is important to note that the computation of the $G$ -equivariant gradient degree $\nabla_{G}\text{-deg}(\nabla\varphi,\Omega)$ can be reduced, via the truncation $\pi_{0}$ , to that of the equivariant Brouwer degree. Let $J_{o}:=\{\,j:\dim W(K_{j})=0\,\}.$ 
For $j\in J_{o}$ , the coefficients $n_{j}$ can be computed via the recursive formula,

(9)

 $n_{j}=\frac{\deg\bigl((\nabla\varphi)^{K_{j}},\Omega^{K_{j}}\bigr)-\sum_{i\in J_{o},\ (K_{i})>(K_{j})}n_{i}\,n(K_{j},K_{i})\,|W(K_{i})|}{|W(K_{j})|},\qquad j\in J_{o}.$ 

Here $\deg\bigl((\nabla\varphi)^{K_{j}},\Omega^{K_{j}}\bigr)$ denotes the local Brouwer degree (the non-equivariant counterpart). One is referred to [11] Chapter 2 for the details of local Brouwer degree and its computation. Consequently,

(10)

 $\nabla_{G}\text{-deg}(\nabla\varphi,\Omega)=\sum_{j\in J_{o}}n_{j}(K_{j})+\bm{\alpha},$ 

where $\bm{\alpha}=\sum_{j\notin J_{o}}n_{j}(K_{j})$ collects all terms with $\dim W(K_{j})>0$ , which are annihilated by $\pi_{0}$ .

## 3.2. Degree on the Slice.

In many applications, one needs to compute the gradient degree on a neighborhood of an isolated orbit $G(\theta_{o})$ . In this case, the computation reduces to the normal slice at $\theta_{o}$ , on which the relevant symmetry is the isotropy group $G_{\theta_{o}}$ .

Theorem 12 (Slice Principle).

Let $\theta_{o}\in V$ . We denote by $V_{o}:=T_{\theta_{o}}G(\theta_{o})$ 
the tangent space to the orbit $G(\theta_{o})$ at $\theta_{o}$ , and let $S_{o}:=V_{o}^{\perp}$ 
be the slice to $G(\theta_{o})$ at $\theta_{o}$ . For sufficiently small $\varepsilon>0$ , let

 $B_{\varepsilon}(S_{o}):=\{v\in S_{o}:\|v\|<\varepsilon\},$ 

and $\mathcal{U}:=G\bigl(\theta_{o}+B_{\varepsilon}(S_{o})\bigr)$ 
be a tubular neighborhood of $G(\theta_{o})$ , and suppose that $(\nabla\varphi,\mathcal{U})\in\mathcal{M}_{G}^{\nabla}$ . Define $\varphi_{o}:S_{o}\to\mathbb{R}$ by

 $\varphi_{o}(v):=\varphi(\theta_{o}+v).$ 

Then

 $\nabla_{G}\text{-}\deg(\nabla\varphi,\mathcal{U})=\Psi\!\left(\nabla_{G_{\theta_{o}}}\text{-}\deg\bigl(\nabla\varphi_{o},B_{\varepsilon}(S_{o}))\right),$ 

where $\Psi:U(G_{\theta_{o}})\to U(G)$ is the ring homomorphism defined on
generators $\Psi((H)_{G_{\theta_{o}}})=(H)_{G}$ .

## 3.3. Methodology Through Symmetric Linear Isomorphisms and Basic Degree

As in the case of the equivariant Brouwer degree (see Appendix B), the equivariant gradient degree admits a linearization procedure that reduces its computation to basic degrees.

Let $L:V\to V$ be a $G$ -equivariant, self-adjoint linear isomorphism. Such an operator arises, for example, as the Hessian of $\varphi\in C^{2}_{G}(V,\mathbb{R})$ at a $G$ -fixed critical point $\theta_{o}\in V^{G}$ :

 $L=D(\nabla\varphi)(\theta_{o})=\nabla^{2}\varphi(\theta_{o}),$ 

which is self-adjoint since $\varphi$ is $C^{2}$ , and $G$ -equivariant since $\nabla\varphi$ is $G$ -equivariant and $\theta_{o}$ is $G$ -fixed. Since $L$ is self-adjoint, it is the gradient of the $G$ -invariant quadratic function

 $\widetilde{\varphi}(\theta):=\tfrac{1}{2}\langle L\theta,\theta\rangle,\qquad\nabla\widetilde{\varphi}(\theta)=L\theta,$ 

so that $\nabla_{G}\text{-deg}(L,B(V))$ is well defined. Decompose $V$ into its isotypic components,

 $V=\bigoplus_{i=0}^{r}V_{i},$ 

where $V_{i}$ contains the copies of the irreducible representation $\mathcal{V}_{i}$ (see Definition 31). Write $\delta(L)$ for the spectrum, $\delta^{-}(L)$ for its negative part, and $E(\beta)$ for the eigenspace of $\beta\in\delta(L)$ . An admissible equivariant gradient homotopy deforms $L$ to $-\mathrm{Id}$ on its negative eigenspace and to $\mathrm{Id}$ on the positive one. For each negative eigenvalue, the number of copies of $\mathcal{V}_{i}$ is given by

 $\mu_{i}(\beta)=\frac{\dim(E(\beta)\cap V_{i})}{\dim\mathcal{V}_{i}}.$ 

Applying ( $\mathcal{G}_{2}$ ) to this deformation and ( $\mathcal{G}_{5}$ ) to the resulting factors yields

## 4. Equivariant Gradient Degree on $G$ -Manifolds

Although the isotypic decomposition of $V$ developed in Section 3 provides a powerful computational tool, it relies essentially on the linear structure of $V$ . When $V$ is a more general manifold, this approach is no longer applicable, and a different framework is required. This is precisely the setting addressed in this section.

## 4.1. $G$ -Equivariant Gradient Degree on Manifolds

Let $(\nabla\varphi,\Omega)$ be an admissible gradient pair on a smooth $n$ -dimensional $G$ -manifold $V$ , where $\Omega$ is invariant, open, and relatively compact. As in Table 1, $\Phi(G,\Omega)$ records the orbit types occurring in this region.

Theorem 15.

The $G$ -equivariant (manifold) gradient degree of $\nabla\varphi$ on $\Omega$ takes the form

(14)

 $\nabla_{G}^{M}\mbox{\rm-}\deg(\nabla\varphi,\Omega)=\sum_{(K_{j})\in\Phi(G,\Omega)}n_{{j}}(K_{j})=n_{{1}}(K_{1})+\dots+n_{{m}}(K_{m}),$ 

where $n_{j}=\chi_{K_{j}}^{G}(\nabla\varphi_{j},V_{(K_{j})}\cap\Omega),$ and it
is the standard Euler characteristic , which is also called the intersection number in [27]. Moreover, The resulting degree has the following properties:

(1) 

(Existence)
Whenever $n_{j}\neq 0$ in (14), a critical point $\theta_{o}\in\Omega$ exists with $(G_{\theta_{o}})\geq(K_{j})$ .

(2) 

(Additivity)
If all critical points in $\Omega$ lie in the union of two disjoint invariant open subsets, their degree contributions add:

 $\nabla^{M}_{G}\mbox{\rm-}\deg(\nabla\varphi,\Omega)=\nabla^{M}_{G}\mbox{\rm-}\deg(\nabla\varphi,\Omega_{1})+\nabla^{M}_{G}\mbox{\rm-}\deg(\nabla\varphi,\Omega_{2}).$ 

(3) 

(Homotopy)
Let $\nabla\Phi_{t}:\overline{\Omega}\to TV,\;t\in[0,1]$ , be an $\Omega$ -admissible homotopy, then
 $\nabla^{M}_{G}\mbox{\rm-}\deg(\nabla\Phi_{t},\Omega)$ 
is independent of $t\in[0,1].$ 

(4) 

(Excision)
Let $\widetilde{\Omega}\subset\Omega$ be an open $G$ -invariant subset and $(\nabla\varphi)^{-1}(0)\cap\Omega\subset\widetilde{\Omega}.$ 
Then

 $\nabla^{M}_{G}\mbox{\rm-}\deg(\nabla\varphi,\Omega)=\nabla^{M}_{G}\mbox{\rm-}\deg(\nabla\varphi,\widetilde{\Omega}).$ 

## 4.1.1. Computations of the Gradient $G$ -Equivariant Degree on $G$ -Manifolds

A general $G$ -manifold $V$ does not admit the isotypic decomposition used in Section 3. Instead, the degree is computed recursively over the orbit-type strata of $V$ , starting from a maximal orbit type. The normal bundle $\mathcal{N}^{(K)}$ , the normal disk bundles $\mathcal{N}(U_{0},\varepsilon)$ , and the notions of $(K)$ -normal sets and $(K)$ -normal vector fields from Section 3.1 extend directly to here. However, $(K)$ -normality here is imposed on one stratum at a time, in order to isolate the contribution of each orbit type. We recall the localization results needed for this procedure (see [24] for details). For a closed subgroup $K\leq G$ , let $\Omega_{(K)}=V_{(K)}\cap\Omega$ .

Proposition 16.

Choose invariant open sets $U_{0},U_{1}\subset V_{(K)}$ and $\varepsilon>0$ satisfying

 $(\nabla\varphi)^{-1}(0)\cap\Omega_{(K)}\subset U_{0}\subset\overline{U_{0}}\subset U_{1},$ 

with $\Omega_{0}=\exp\bigl(\mathcal{N}(U_{1},2\varepsilon)\bigr)\subset\Omega.$ 
Set $U=\exp(\mathcal{N}(U_{0},\varepsilon))$ , and choose a smooth invariant cutoff $\eta:V\to[0,1]$ that equals one near $U$ and has support contained in $\Omega_{0}$ . Define

 $\widetilde{\varphi}(\exp\widetilde{\theta})=\varphi(\pi(\widetilde{\theta}))+\frac{1}{2}\|\widetilde{\theta}\|^{2},$ 

and use the cutoff to obtain

 $\varphi_{0}(\theta)=\begin{cases}\eta(\theta)\widetilde{\varphi}(\theta)+(1-\eta(\theta))\varphi(\theta),&\theta\in\Omega_{0},\\
\varphi(\theta),&\theta\notin\Omega_{0}.\end{cases}$ 

The resulting gradient is $(K)$ -normal and agrees with the original field on $V_{(K)}$ and outside $\Omega_{0}$ .

Proposition 17.

Fix a maximal orbit type $(K)\in\Phi(G,\Omega)$ . An admissible gradient homotopy $\Phi$ admits a $(K)$ -normal modification $\Phi^{\prime}$ supported in an invariant open subset $\Omega_{0}\subset\Omega$ . For every $t\in[0,1]$ , this modification agrees with $\Phi$ both on $V_{(K)}$ and outside $\Omega_{0}$ :

 $\Phi^{\prime}(t,\theta)=\Phi(t,\theta)\qquad\bigl(\theta\in V_{(K)}\cup(V\setminus\Omega_{0})\bigr).$ 

Begin with a maximal orbit type $(K_{0})$ . Proposition 16 supplies a normal replacement $\nabla\varphi_{0}$ , differing from $\nabla\varphi$ only within a small invariant tubular neighborhood $\Omega_{0}$ and agreeing with it on $V_{(K_{0})}$ . Moreover,

 $(\nabla\varphi_{0})^{-1}(0)\cap\Omega\subset\Omega_{(K_{0})}\cup\Omega_{0}^{{}^{\prime}}.$ 

Place the remaining zeros in an invariant open set $\Omega^{\prime}_{0}$ whose closure avoids $V_{(K_{0})}$ . The tubular neighborhood can be chosen disjoint from this remainder. Homotopy invariance, additivity, and excision then separate the contribution of $(K_{0})$ from the residual degree, giving

(15)

 $\displaystyle\nabla_{G}^{M}\text{-}\deg(\nabla\varphi,\Omega)$ 
 $\displaystyle=\nabla_{G}^{M}\text{-}\deg(\nabla\varphi_{0},\Omega)$ 

 $\displaystyle=\nabla_{G}^{M}\text{-}\deg(\nabla\varphi_{0},\Omega_{0})+\nabla_{G}^{M}\text{-}\deg(\nabla\varphi_{0},\Omega_{0}^{{}^{\prime}})$ 

 $\displaystyle=\nabla_{G}^{M}\text{-}\deg(\nabla\varphi_{0},\Omega_{(K_{0})})+\nabla_{G}^{M}\text{-}\deg(\nabla\varphi_{0},\Omega_{0}^{{}^{\prime}})$ 

 $\displaystyle=\nabla_{G}^{M}\text{-}\deg\Big((\nabla\varphi)|_{V_{(K_{0})}},\Omega_{(K_{0})}\Big)+\nabla_{G}^{M}\text{-}\deg(\nabla\varphi_{0},\Omega_{0}^{{}^{\prime}})$ 

 $\displaystyle=n_{0}(K_{0})+\nabla_{G}^{M}\text{-}\deg(\nabla\varphi_{0},\Omega_{0}^{{}^{\prime}}).$ 

We then repeat the same construction on the remaining invariant set $\Omega_{0}^{{}^{\prime}}.$ More precisely, for the next orbit type $(K_{1}),$ we choose an invariant neighborhood $\Omega_{1}$ of $\Omega_{(K_{1})}$ inside $\Omega_{0}^{{}^{\prime}}$ and construct an $(K_{1})$ -normal replacement $\nabla\varphi_{1}$ of $\nabla\varphi_{0}.$ Notice that, by construction, the induced gradient vector field on $V_{(K_{1})}$ remains unchanged. Namely, the restricted gradients of $\varphi_{1},\varphi_{0}$ and $\varphi$ coincide on $V_{(K_{1})}.$ Applying the same homotopy, additivity, and excision arguments gives a second contribution $n_{1},$ together with a new remainder. Iterating this procedure over all orbit types in $\Phi(G,\Omega)$ , we obtain

 $\nabla_{G}^{M}\mbox{\rm-}\deg(\nabla\varphi,\Omega)=\sum_{(K_{j})\in\Phi(G,\Omega)}n_{{j}}(K_{j}),$ 

where $(K_{0}),\ldots,(K_{m})$ are ordered by successively selecting a maximal orbit type among those remaining. Here $n_{j}=\chi^{G}_{K_{j}}\bigl(\nabla\varphi_{j},\Omega_{(K_{j})}\bigr)$ denotes the standard Euler-characteristic associated with the orbit type $(K_{j}).$ 
If the critical points of $\varphi_{j}|_{V_{(K_{j})}}$ in $\Omega_{(K_{j})}$ are
isolated and nondegenerate, then this contribution is given by the Morse-index formula

 $n_{j}=\sum_{\theta_{o}\in\Omega_{(K_{j})}}(-1)^{m^{-}(\theta_{o})}.$ 

Here $m^{-}(\theta_{o})$ denotes the dimension of the negative eigenspace of the Hessian of $\varphi_{j}|_{V_{(K_{j})}}$ at $\theta_{o}$ .

## Appendix A Euler and Burnside Rings

The additive structure of the Euler ring is $U(G)=\mathbb{Z}[\Phi(G)].$ 
Thus, its elements are finite integer linear combinations of conjugacy classes in $\Phi(G)$ . More precisely, every $a\in U(G)$ has a unique expansion

 $a=\sum_{(K)\in\Phi(G)}n_{K}(K),\qquad n_{K}\in\mathbb{Z},$ 

with only finitely many nonzero coefficients. We write

 $\operatorname{coeff}^{K}(a):=n_{K}$ 

for the coefficient associated with $(K)$ . Under the Euler-ring multiplication $*$ , the class $(G)$ serves as the identity:

 $(G)*a=a\qquad\text{for every }a\in U(G).$ 

Lemma 18.

Multiplication by an invertible element $a\in U(G)$ preserves a nonzero coefficient at $(K)$ in the product $(K)*a$ . In other words,

 $\operatorname{coeff}^{K}\bigl((K)*a\bigr)\neq 0\qquad\text{for every }(K)\in\Phi(G).$ 

Proof.

The multiplication rule ensures that every class appearing with a nonzero coefficient in $(K)*a$ lies below or equals $(K)$ . We may therefore write

 $(K)*a=\sum_{(H)\leq(K)}m_{H}(H),$ 

where the sum has finite support.
Suppose that $m_{K}=0$ . Then every class contributing to this expansion satisfies $(H)<(K)$ . Multiplying once more by $a^{-1}$ can only produce classes lying below these $(H)$ , so

 $\bigl((K)*a\bigr)*a^{-1}=\sum_{(S)<(K)}c_{S}(S).$ 

However, associativity and invertibility give

 $\bigl((K)*a\bigr)*a^{-1}=(K)*(a*a^{-1})=(K)*(G)=(K).$ 

This contradicts uniqueness of the expansion in the basis $\Phi(G)$ . Hence $m_{K}\neq 0$ .
 $\square$ 

To describe the Burnside ring, consider

 $\Phi_{0}(G)=\{(K)\in\Phi(G):\dim W(K)=0\},\qquad A(G)=\mathbb{Z}[\Phi_{0}(G)].$ 

For generators belonging to $\Phi_{0}(G)$ , the Burnside product retains precisely the $\Phi_{0}(G)$ -components of their Euler-ring product:

 $(K)\cdot(H)=\sum_{(S)\in\Phi_{0}(G)}n_{S}(S).$ 

Its coefficients are

(35)

 $n_{S}=\chi\!\left((G/K\times G/H)_{S}/N(S)\right)=\left|(G/K\times G/H)_{S}/N(S)\right|,$ 

where $\chi$ denotes the ordinary Euler characteristic. Extending this multiplication bilinearly equips $A(G)$ with the ring structure called the Burnside ring of $G$ .
The coefficients in (35) can be evaluated recursively, beginning with the larger conjugacy classes:

 $n_{S}=\frac{n(S,K)|W(K)|\,n(S,H)|W(H)|-\displaystyle\sum_{(\widetilde{S})>(S)}n(S,\widetilde{S})\,n_{\widetilde{S}}|W(\widetilde{S})|}{|W(S)|}.$ 

All classes appearing in this formula belong to $\Phi_{0}(G)$ . The quantities involved are defined using the transporter

 $N_{G}(S,K):=\{g\in G:gSg^{-1}\subseteq K\}.$ 

With this convention, its left coset spaces are

 $K\backslash N_{G}(S,K)=\{Kg:g\in N_{G}(S,K)\},$ 

and

 $n(S,K)=\left|N_{G}(K)\backslash N_{G}(S,K)\right|,$ 

where $N_{G}(K)$ is the normalizer of $K$ .
The nilpotence behavior of the basis elements depends on the dimension of their Weyl groups, as recorded in the following result:

Proposition 19.

Let $\Phi_{n}(G)=\{(K)\in\Phi(G):\dim W(K)=n\}.$ For $(K)\in\Phi_{n}(G)$ , the following alternatives hold:

(1) 

If $n>0$ , the element $(K)$ is nilpotent in $U(G)$ ; that is,

 $(K)^{k}=0\quad\text{for some positive integer }k.$ 

(2) 

If $n=0$ , every positive power remains nonzero:

 $(K)^{k}\neq 0\quad\text{for all positive integers }k.$ 

Corollary 20.

Every finite integer linear combination

 $\alpha=\sum_{j=1}^{k}n_{j}(L_{j}),\qquad\dim W(L_{j})>0\quad(j=1,\ldots,k),$ 

is nilpotent in $U(G)$ . Thus, $\alpha^{n}=0$ for some positive integer $n$ .

Proof.

We proceed by induction on the number of summands. For a single summand, Proposition 19 gives an integer $q\geq 1$ such that $(L_{1})^{q}=0$ , and consequently

 $\bigl(n_{1}(L_{1})\bigr)^{q}=n_{1}^{q}(L_{1})^{q}=0.$ 

Assume the assertion holds for sums of $k$ terms, and write a sum of $k+1$ terms as

 $\beta=\alpha+n_{k+1}(L_{k+1}),\qquad\alpha=\sum_{j=1}^{k}n_{j}(L_{j}).$ 

By the induction hypothesis and Proposition 19, there exist positive integers $p,q$ satisfying

 $\alpha^{p}=0,\qquad(L_{k+1})^{q}=0.$ 

Since $U(G)$ is commutative, the binomial expansion yields

 $\beta^{m}=\sum_{\ell=0}^{m}\binom{m}{\ell}\alpha^{\ell}n_{k+1}^{\,m-\ell}(L_{k+1})^{m-\ell}.$ 

Choose $m\geq p+q$ . In each summand, either $\ell\geq p$ , so that $\alpha^{\ell}=0$ , or $\ell<p$ , in which case $m-\ell\geq q$ and $(L_{k+1})^{m-\ell}=0$ . Every term therefore vanishes, proving that $\beta^{m}=0$ .
 $\square$ 

## Appendix B $G$ -Equivariant Brouwer Degree

In this section, we introduce the Equivariant Brouwer Degree, which provides the foundation for the gradient-related degree theories presented in sections 3 and 4. Our exposition takes a computational approach and is structured around the various methods of computing the equivariant Brouwer degree. (For a more classical and comprehensive treatment of the theory, including its formal development and further theoretical details, we refer the readers to [11]). We first introduce its definition and fundamental properties from the viewpoint of its general construction, together with a simplification by passing to a subgroup/ normal slice. We then present two concrete computational approaches: one based on reduction to regular normal maps, and the other based on linear $G$ -isomorphisms and basic degrees. Before proceeding to the main discussion, we first introduce the setting as follows:

For each finite-dimensional orthogonal representation $V$ , let $\mathcal{M}^{G}(V,V)$ contain the admissible pairs $(f,\Omega)$ . Their union over all such representations is denoted by

 $\mathcal{M}^{G}=\bigcup_{V}\mathcal{M}^{G}(V,V).$ 

Definition 21 (Fundamental Domain).

Suppose the action on $V$ is free, and write $\pi:V\to V/G$ for the quotient map. A submanifold $D_{0}$ defines a fundamental domain $D=\overline{D_{0}}$ when its distinct translates are disjoint, its closure meets every orbit, and its dimension equals that of the quotient:

 $\displaystyle gD_{0}\cap hD_{0}=\varnothing\quad(g\neq h),$ 

 $\displaystyle\pi(\overline{D_{0}})=V/G,$ 

 $\displaystyle\dim D_{0}=\dim(V/G).$ 

For an invariant open region $\Omega$ , each orbit-type subset $\Omega_{(K)}$ is an invariant submanifold. At a point $\theta$ of this stratum, the ambient representation splits into tangent and normal directions:

 $V=T_{\theta}\Omega_{(K)}\oplus N_{\theta}\Omega_{(K)}.$ 

Definition 22 (Normal map).

An admissible equivariant map $f$ is normal in $\Omega$ if it agrees locally with the identity in directions normal to the orbit-type stratum at each zero. Explicitly, for every $\theta\in f^{-1}(0)\cap\Omega_{K}$ , some $\delta_{\theta}>0$ satisfies

 $f(\theta+v)=v\qquad\bigl(v\in N_{\theta}\Omega_{(K)},\ \|v\|<\delta_{\theta}\bigr).$ 

Definition 23 (Regular normal map).

A normal map is regular normal when it is $C^{1}$ and the fixed-point restriction is regular at every zero of each occurring isotropy type. Thus, for each $(K)\in\Phi(G,f^{-1}(0)\cap\Omega)$ , zero must be a regular value of $\left.f^{K}\right|_{\Omega_{K}},$ 
where $f^{K}=\left.f\right|_{V^{K}}.$ 

## B.1. Recursive-Construction Methodology (Based on Definition and Fundamental Properties)

Definition 24 (Equivariant Brouwer Degree).

The equivariant Brouwer degree $G\text{-}\deg:\mathcal{M}^{G}\longrightarrow A(G)$ associates a Burnside-ring element with every admissible pair, and the expansion is given by

(36)

 $G\text{-}\deg(f,\Omega)=\sum_{(K_{j})\in\Phi_{0}(G)}n_{j}(K_{j})=n_{1}(K_{1})+\cdots+n_{m}(K_{m}),$ 

with coefficients

(37)

 $n_{j}=\frac{\deg\bigl(f^{K_{j}},\Omega^{K_{j}}\bigr)-\displaystyle\sum_{(H)>(K_{j})}n_{H}\,n(K_{j},H)\,|W(H)|}{|W(K_{j})|},\quad j=1,\cdots,m.$ 

Here $\deg\bigl(f^{K_{j}},\Omega^{K_{j}}\bigr)$ denotes the local Brouwer degree (the non-equivariant counterpart). One is referred to [11] Chapter 2 for the details of local Brouwer degree and its computation.

We next present the properties that uniquely determine the equivariant Brouwer degree, together with several additional properties relevant to its computation.

Theorem 25.

The following three axioms determine $G\text{-}\deg:\mathcal{M}^{G}\longrightarrow A(G)$ uniquely:

( $\mathcal{P}_{1}$ ) 

Additivity.
Let $\Omega_{1},\Omega_{2}\subset\Omega$ be $G$ -invariant disjoint open subsets and $f^{-1}(0)\cap\Omega\subset\Omega_{1}\cup\Omega_{2}.$ Then

 $G\text{-}\deg(f,\Omega)=G\text{-}\deg(f,\Omega_{1})+G\text{-}\deg(f,\Omega_{2}).$ 

( $\mathcal{P}_{2}$ ) 

Homotopy.
Let $h_{t}:V\longrightarrow V$ be an $\Omega$ -admissible $G$ -equivariant homotopy. Then
 $G\text{-}\deg(h_{t},\Omega)$ is a constant on $t\in[0,1]$ .

( $\mathcal{P}_{3}$ ) 

Normalization.
Suppose that $f$ is regular normal in $\Omega$ and that its zero set
consists of a single $G$ -orbit, i.e. $f^{-1}(0)\cap\Omega=G(x_{0}).$ 
Then

 $G\text{-}\deg(f,\Omega)=n_{0}(K)$ 

where $K=G_{x_{0}}$ and $n_{0}=\operatorname{sign}\det Df^{K}(x_{0}).$ 

Proposition 26.

The axioms also yield the following consequences:

( $\mathcal{P}_{4}$ ) 

Existence.
If, for $(K_{j})\in\Phi_{0}(G)\;\;(j=1,\cdots,m)$ , the corresponding coefficient $n_{j}\neq 0,$ 
then $f$ admits a zero $\theta_{o}$ in $\Omega$ whose isotropy group $(G_{\theta_{o}})\geq(K_{j}).$ 

( $\mathcal{P}_{5}$ ) 

Unitary.
Let $\Omega\subset V$ be an open bounded $G$ -invariant neighborhood
of the origin. Then

 $G\text{-}\deg(\operatorname{Id}_{V},\Omega)=(G).$ 

( $\mathcal{P}_{6}$ ) 

Excision.
Let open set $\Omega_{0}\subset\Omega$ be $G$ -invariant and all zeros in $\Omega$ is a subset of $\Omega_{0}.$ 
Then

 $G\text{-}\deg(f,\Omega)=G\text{-}\deg(f,\Omega_{0}).$ 

( $\mathcal{P}_{7}$ ) 

Product.
For two admissible $G$ -pairs $(f_{1},\Omega_{1})$ and $(f_{2},\Omega_{2})$ , one has

 $G\text{-}\deg\bigl(f_{1}\times f_{2},\Omega_{1}\times\Omega_{2}\bigr)=G\text{-}\deg(f_{1},\Omega_{1})\cdot G\text{-}\deg(f_{2},\Omega_{2}).$ 

The product is taken in Burnside ring.

( $\mathcal{P}_{8}$ ) 

Suspension.
Let
 $U$ be an open bounded $G$ -invariant neighborhood of the
origin of the orthogonal $G$ -representation $W$ . Then from unitary and product property, one has

 $G\text{-}\deg\bigl(f\times\operatorname{Id}_{W},\Omega\times U\bigr)=G\text{-}\deg(f,\Omega).$ 

These properties provide the main computational tools. Homotopy invariance permits a simpler representative, while nonzero coefficients certify zeros with corresponding isotropy. Additivity and excision isolate local contributions; product decompositions then allow calculations in terms of basic degrees. Section B.2 explains the regular-normal construction behind the normalization axiom.

We next localize the domain near an orbit and relate the calculation to its stabilizer. Given $\theta_{o}\in V$ , let $\mathcal{U}$ be a neighborhood of $G(\theta_{o})$ such that $f$ is $\mathcal{U}$ -admissible. Our aim is to compute $G\text{-}\deg(f,\mathcal{U}).$ 
In practical applications, this computation can be reduced to a corresponding problem on a normal slice at $\theta_{o}$ , with the symmetry reduced to $G_{\theta_{o}}$ . To formulate this reduction, we first introduce the following ring homomorphism:

To distinguish the ambient groups, write $(K)_{G^{\prime}}$ and $(K)_{G}$ for the two conjugacy classes of $K\leq G^{\prime}$ . The homomorphism $\Theta:A(G^{\prime})\to A(G)$ is specified on generators by
 $\Theta:A(G^{\prime})\longrightarrow A(G)$ 
by

(38)

 $\Theta\bigl((K)_{G^{\prime}}\bigr)=\begin{cases}(K)_{G},&\text{if }|W_{G}(K)|<\infty,\\[5.69054pt]
0,&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}$ 

Theorem 27.

Let $\theta_{o}\in V$ and
 $V_{o}:=T_{\theta_{o}}G(\theta_{o})$ be
the tangent space to the orbit $G(\theta_{o})$ at $\theta_{o}$ . Define $S_{o}:=V_{o}^{\perp}$ the slice to $G(\theta_{o})$ at $\theta_{o}$ . Denote by $P_{o}:V\to S_{o}$ the orthogonal projection. Choose $\varepsilon>0$ sufficiently small that

 $\mathcal{U}:=G\bigl(\theta_{o}+B_{\varepsilon}(S_{o})\bigr)$ 

is a tubular neighborhood. Define

 $f_{o}:S_{o}\to S_{o},\qquad f_{o}(v):=P_{o}f(\theta_{o}+v),$ 

and assume that $f_{o}$ $B_{\varepsilon}(S_{o})$ admissible, then

 $G\text{-}\deg(f,\mathcal{U})=\Theta\!\left(G_{\theta_{o}}\text{-}\deg\bigl(f_{o},B_{\varepsilon}(S_{o})\bigr)\right),$ 

where $\Theta$ is the homomorphism taken from (38).

Remark 28.

The boundary condition holds for sufficiently small $\varepsilon$ if $0$ is an isolated zero of $f_{o}$ . If $\dim G_{\theta_{o}}=\dim G$ , then $S_{o}=V$ , $P_{o}=\mathrm{Id}_{V}$ , and $f_{o}(v)=f(\theta_{o}+v)$ 

## B.2. Methodology Through Regular Normal Map

To compute $G\text{-}\deg(f,\Omega),$ we begin by fixing a bounded invariant open domain $\Omega$ on which $f$ is admissible. Based on the homotopy property introduced in ( $\mathcal{P}_{2}$ ), the objective of this section is to reduce the degree computation for the original nonlinear map $f$ to that of a simpler representative $f_{o}$ . This raises the following several questions: What should such a representative be? How can one ensure the homotopy between $f$ and $f_{o}$ $\Omega$ -admissible? And why is $f_{o}$ a good representative and how can its degree be calculated? To address these questions, we need the following theorem (see [11] for details):

Theorem 29.

Given an admissible pair $(f,\Omega)$ and $\varepsilon>0$ , there exists an equivariant map $f_{o}$ , regular normal in $\Omega$ , whose uniform distance from $f$ on $\overline{\Omega}$ is less than $\varepsilon$ :

 $\sup_{\theta\in\Omega}\|f(\theta)-f_{o}(\theta)\|<\varepsilon.$ 

Therefore, by considering the linear homotopy

 $h(t,\theta)=tf_{o}(\theta)+(1-t)f(\theta),\quad t\in[0,1],\ \theta\in V,$ 

one obtains a $G$ $\Omega$ -admissible homotopy between the original map $f$ and its regular normal approximation $f_{o}$ . By homotopy property ( $\mathcal{P}_{2}$ ), $G\text{-}\deg(f,\Omega)=G\text{-}\deg(f_{o},\Omega)$ . Moreover, regular normal approximation make the zero set amenable to degree computation: Normality provides a canonical local structure near zeros of each orbit type, fundamental domains removes the redundancy arising from the $G$ -action and reduces the problem to a local Brouwer degree computation. Regularity, together with the Inverse Function Theorem, further ensures that the zeros are isolated, allowing their local contributions to be computed individually and summed using additivity property ( $\mathcal{P}_{1}$ ).

We next present the analytic formula to compute the equivariant Brouwer degree.

Proposition 30 (Analytic Formula).

Let $(f_{o},\Omega)$ be an admissible $G$ -pair, with $f_{o}$ regular normal in
 $\Omega$ . For each $(K)\in\Phi_{0}(G,\Omega)$ , the zeros of the restriction
 $f_{o}^{K}$ in $\Omega$ are isolated and can be represented by finitely many
 $W(K)$ -orbits,

 $(f_{o}^{K})^{-1}(0)\cap\Omega=W(K)(\theta_{1})\cup\cdots\cup W(K)(\theta_{n}).$ 

Then

 $G\text{-}\deg(f_{o},\Omega)=\sum_{(K)\in\Phi_{0}(G,V)}n_{K}(K),\qquad n_{K}=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\operatorname{sign}\det Df_{o}^{K}(\theta_{j}).$ 

## B.3. Methodology Through Linear Isomorphism and Reduction to Basic Degree

Notice that both approaches discussed above may be difficult to carry out explicitly. The recursive construction in B.1 requires the computation of $\deg(f^{H},\Omega^{H})$ , while the regular-normal approach in B.2 requires an explicit regular normal approximation and suitable fundamental domains. Therefore, in this section, we consider a linearization technique based on local or global linear approximation of the original nonlinear map, reducing the degree computation to that of a linear $G$ -isomorphism.

Consider $V$ as a $n$ -dimensional orthogonal $G$ -representation and let $L:V\to V$ be a $G$ -equivariant linear isomorphism.

Definition 31 ( $G$ -Isotypic Decomposition).

The finite dimensional $G$ -representation $V$ admits a $G$ -isotypic decomposition

 $V=V_{0}\oplus V_{1}\oplus\cdots\oplus V_{r},$ 

where each $V_{i}$ is modeled on the irreducible $G$ -representation $\mathcal{V}_{i},\;\;i=0,\cdots,r.$ 

Since $L$ is $G$ -equivariant, then $L(V_{i})\subseteq V_{i}.$ Now let $\delta(L)$ be the spectrum of $L.$ For $\beta\in\delta(L),$ denote by $E(\beta)$ the generalized eigenspace, then $E(\beta)$ is $G$ -invariant. Define
 $E_{i}(\beta):=E(\beta)\cap V_{i},$ 
obviously, $L(E_{i}(\beta))=E_{i}(\beta).$ We further define

 $E_{i}^{-}:=\bigoplus_{\beta\in\delta^{-}{(L)}}E_{i}(\beta),$ 

where $\delta^{-}{(L)}$ denotes negative spectrum. It follows
that $L(E_{i}^{-})=E_{i}^{-}.$ Hence the restriction
 $L|_{E_{i}^{-}}:E_{i}^{-}\to E_{i}^{-}$ 
is a well-defined $G$ -equivariant linear isomorphism. Notice that $L|_{E_{i}^{-}}$ is $G$ -equivariantly homotopic to $-\operatorname{Id}|_{E_{i}^{-}},$ by defining $\mu_{i}(\beta)=\frac{\dim E_{i}(\beta)}{\dim\mathcal{V}_{i}}$ and
 $\mu_{i}=\sum_{\beta\in\delta^{-}(L)}\mu_{i}(\beta),$ we have

 $G\text{-}\deg\left(\left.L\right|_{E_{i}^{-}},B(E_{i}^{-})\right)=\left(G\text{-}\deg\left(-\operatorname{Id},B(\mathcal{V}_{i})\right)\right)^{\mu_{i}}.$ 

This motivates the following definition of basic degree:

Definition 32 (Basic Degree).

Define the $\mathcal{V}_{i}$ -basic degree (or simply basic degree) as following

(39)

 $\deg_{\mathcal{V}_{i}}:=G\text{-}\deg(-\text{\rm Id},B(\mathcal{V}_{i})).$ 

Moreover, the basic degree takes the form

(40)

 $\deg_{\mathcal{V}_{i}}=\sum_{(K)}n_{K}(K)$ 

where

(41)

 $n_{K}=\frac{(-1)^{\dim\mathcal{V}_{i}^{K}}-\sum_{(K)<(H)}{n_{H}\,n(K,H)\,\left|W(H)\right|}}{\left|W(K)\right|}.$ 

Therefore, by homotopy ( $\mathcal{P}_{2}$ ) and product (P7) properties, one has

(42)

 $G\text{-}\deg(L,B(V))=\prod_{\beta\in\delta^{-}(L)}\prod_{i=0}^{r}\left(\deg_{\mathcal{V}_{i}}\right)^{\mu_{i}(\beta)}=\prod_{i=0}^{r}\left(\deg_{\mathcal{V}_{i}}\right)^{\mu_{i}},$ 

where the product is taken in the Burnside ring $A(G).$ 
We next list some important properties of basic degrees.

Definition 33 (Leading Coefficient).

Let $a=(G)+\sum_{j=1}^{m}n_{j}(K_{j})\in A(G),$ 
where $n_{j}\neq 0,\;(K_{j})<(G),\;j=1,\ldots,m.$ 
Among the orbit types appearing with nonzero coefficients, suppose that
 $(K_{j_{o}})$ is maximal with respect to the ordering of orbit types.
Then the coefficient $n_{j_{o}}$ associated with $(K_{j_{o}})$ is called
a leading coefficient of $a$ .

Lemma 34.

Let $n_{o}$ denote the leading coefficient associated with the orbit type
 $(K_{o})$ in the basic degree $\deg_{\mathcal{V}_{i}}$ . Then
 $\dim\mathcal{V}_{i}^{K_{o}}$ is odd, and $n_{o}$ is determined as follows:

 $n_{o}=\begin{cases}-2,&|W(K_{o})|=1,\\[5.69054pt]
-1,&|W(K_{o})|=2.\end{cases}$ 

Lemma 35.

Every basic degree $\deg_{\mathcal{V}_{i}}\in A(G)$ is involutive in Burnside ring. In particular,

 $\left(\deg_{\mathcal{V}_{i}}\right)^{2}=\deg_{\mathcal{V}_{i}}\cdot\deg_{\mathcal{V}_{i}}=(G).$ 

## Appendix C Amalgamated Groups

We describe the subgroups of $S_{k}\times S_{k}$ using a notation based on Goursat’s lemma [26]. More generally, a subgroup $\mathcal{K}\leq G_{1}\times G_{2}$ can be represented through subgroups $K\leq G_{1}$ , $H\leq G_{2}$ , and surjective homomorphisms onto a common quotient $S$ :

 $\mathcal{K}=\{(k,h)\in K\times H:\varphi(k)=\psi(h)\}.$ 

We denote this fiber-product subgroup by

 $K^{\varphi}\times_{S}^{\psi}H.$ 

This construction is referred to as an amalgamated subgroup. Set $K_{0}=\ker\varphi,$ $H_{0}=\ker\psi.$ 
Once the identification $K/K_{0}\cong H/H_{0}\cong S$ has been fixed, we abbreviate the subgroup as

 $\mathcal{K}=K^{K_{0}}\times_{S}^{H_{0}}H.$ 

The chosen quotient identification is understood whenever this abbreviated notation is used.

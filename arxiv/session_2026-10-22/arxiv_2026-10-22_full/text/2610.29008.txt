Abstract

In recent years, automated fruit classification systems have become a more critical component in precision agriculture for efficient yield estimation, quality control, and supply chain optimization. However, they still face significant challenges under varying quality, fruit maturity, illumination, texture degradation, morphological attributes, domain shift, and post-harvest handling conditions. In this study, we address the challenging issue of cross-grade banana variety classification. This is a real-world setting where models that have been trained on specific quality grades must generalize to unseen grades to handle significant domain shift. Under a rigorous Leave-One-Grade-Out (LOGO) evaluation protocol, we present a thorough comparative analysis of convolutional neural networks (CNNs), Vision Transformers (ViTs), and dual-stream hybrid architectures using a large scale dataset of 31,678 banana images with three varieties and three quality grades. Specifically, to address the drawbacks of single-paradigm frameworks, we present a dual-stream Swin ResNet hybrid framework enhanced with KL-divergence-based self-distillation to align probabilistic feature representations across the CNN and Transformer streams. Comprehensive experimental analyzes show that the presented hybrid framework with KL-divergence based self-distillation achieves better overall performance, i.e., it attains a mean accuracy of $81.25\%$ and significantly surpasses CNN and Transformer baseline models. Qualitative analysis further confirm that the hybrid framework in this study improves discriminative feature transferability, improves attention localization, and stabilizes optimization. Overall, the experimental results demonstrate how well hybrid distillation techniques can lead to domain robust agricultural systems and can be considered a promising direction for robust agricultural vision frameworks in the real world.

## 1 Introduction

Automated fruit variety classification is becoming increasingly vital in modern farming, as it supports large-scale yield estimation, quality control, and a smooth supply chain. But variations in growing regions, inconsistent cultivation practices, improper handling during harvest and distribution, inadequate disease and pest management make bananas susceptible to quality issues like bruising, peel discoloration, premature ripening, fungal lesions, splitting, shape deformities, and uneven coloration Al-Dairi et al. (2023). These imperfections significantly diminish retail value and marketability, resulting in substantial economic losses for producers and supply chain stakeholders Fernando et al. (2019). At present, the prevailing grading approach still depends heavily on manual inspection that suffers from low throughput, high labor costs and variable assessment criteria influenced by human subjectivity. Although mechanical sorting systems can enhance grading accuracy, they remain incapable of effectively assessing surface imperfections like bruising, spotting, or peel discoloration. Also, physical contact during sorting often results in secondary damage.
These challenges and bananas being one of the world’s most vital staple crops (e.g., global production exceeding 114 million tonnes annually and serving as a primary food source for over 400 million people worldwide Yasin et al. (2025); Zhang et al. (2026)) have motivated extensive research into automated classification systems using machine learning and computer vision techniques Zhang et al. (2024).

Machine learning and computer vision-based automated classification systems offer substantial benefits, such as increased processing speed, consistent quality evaluation, reduced labour costs, and enhanced traceability throughout the supply chain Zhang et al. (2024). Early techniques mainly employed hand-crafted features (e.g., color histograms, texture descriptors) combined with classical machine learning algorithms such as Support Vector Machines (SVMs) and Random Forests. For example, the method in Zhuang et al. (2019); Santos Pereira et al. (2018) extracted peel color, local texture, and local shape features from different banana regions using local binary pattern (LBP) and classifying them using (Naive Bayes) NB, Linear Discriminant Analysis (LDA), and SVM algorithms Ghazal et al. (2021). While Fu et al. (2019) presented a multi-scale method that used VM and AdaBoost algorithms with fused LBP (local binary pattern) and HOG (histogram of oriented gradients) features. Nonetheless, these methods demonstrated limited generalization capability (i.e., failing to capture the complex hierarchical representations), especifically when confronting variations in lighting conditions, camera angles, and fruit quality grades.

With recent advances in computer vision and deep learning, intelligent inspection and sorting systems have emerged as a critical solution to overcoming these limitations. The deep learning algorithms have catalyzed a paradigm shift in agricultural automation, including fruit quality assessment and variety classification. In particular, Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) have revolutionized computer vision tasks by enabling automatic feature extraction directly from raw image data Li et al. (2021). CNNs have demonstrated exceptional success across various agricultural applications, including crop disease detection, fruit ripeness assessment, and variety classification Wallelign et al. (2018); Olisah et al. (2024); Gurjar et al. (2026); Ponce et al. (2019). For instance, the study in Sheikh et al. (2025); XU2 (2026) employed deep convolutional features learned from banana images using a transfer learning framework. Specifically, a fine-tuned deep learning classifier was used to distinguish banana varieties based on shape, color, and texture cues captured in the learned feature maps. While, the work in Shuprajhaa et al. (2023) proposed a CNN-XGBoost hybrid model for classifying banana ripening stages, where a convolutional neural network extracts deep visual features such as color, texture, and shape, and XGBoost performs the classification. Moreover, the proposed system used Linear Discriminant Analysis (LDA) for dimensionality reduction, thereby enabling effective training with smaller datasets.

Despite remarkable advances in deep learning for automated classification of fruit and banana varieties, several crucial challenges remain inadequately addressed Too et al. (2019); Mehdipour et al. (2026); Sarode and Patil (2026). Most current models (e.g., CNNs, Vision Transformers (ViTs), or hybrid architectures) perform well on in-distribution data but struggle to generalize across domain shifts commonly encountered in real-world banana production and distribution as well as other agriculture settings. These shifts arise from variations in quality grades, maturity levels, lighting conditions, and camera setups, leading to substantial degradation in model performance when confronted with unseen distributions of visual or quality-grade features. CNNs, while effective at capturing local texture and shape information, often fail to model global structural relationships critical for distinguishing visually similar varieties. Conversely, ViTs capture long-range dependencies and global context but typically require large datasets and may underutilize local texture cues that remain discriminative under quality degradation. Hybrid approaches that combine the strengths of both paradigms show promise, yet their efficacy for cross-grade and cross-domain agricultural classification remains largely unexplored. Consequently, ensuring robust, reliable performance across diverse fruit quality conditions remains an open and pressing research problem in precision banana agriculture. All in all, not only do existing methods struggle to generalize when confronted with unseen quality grades (particularly in settings where inter-class differences are subtle and intra-class variability is high), but the effectiveness of hybrid architectures that combine complementary strengths for cross-grade classification remains underexplored.

As deep learning and vision-based systems are increasingly being deployed in banana orchards, packing houses, and markets, robustness to variations in grades, lighting, maturity, and surface quality is as crucial as classification accuracy. Thus, in this work, we focus on cross-grade banana variety classification, a challenging yet practically relevant task. To address the limitations of single-paradigm models, we propose a dual-stream CNN-Transformer hybrid architecture augmented with KL-divergence-based distillation. By encouraging alignment between the predictive distributions of a Swin Transformer (teacher) and a ResNet-based CNN (student), the model learns grade-invariant representations that are more robust to domain shift.
The primary novelty of this work lies not only in the proposed CNN-Transformer architecture, but also in establishing cross-grade generalisability as a distinct and practically meaningful evaluation problem for banana variety classification. This paper makes the following key contributions:

• 

We establish a Leave-One-Grade-Out (LOGO) cross-validation framework, in which each grade is completely held out during testing, providing a more realistic assessment of model robustness to unseen grades than conventional random splits.

• 

We systematically evaluate CNNs, Vision Transformers, and hybrid architectures under the LOGO protocol, thereby offering one of the first large-scale studies of banana variety classification under a rigorous cross-grade evaluation protocol.

• 

We introduce a hybrid model integrating a Swin Transformer and ResNet backbone to leverage feature fusion to improve cross-grade generalization and outperform pure CNN or Transformer baselines.

• 

We propose a self-distillation mechanism that aligns probability distributions between architectural streams, significantly improving robustness on challenging domains and accelerating convergence.

• 

Through attention-based visualizations (Grad-CAM and Score-CAM), inter-stream alignment analysis, and training dynamics studies, we offer in-depth insights into how hybrid representations and knowledge distillation jointly mitigate domain shift effects.

To the best of our knowledge, we present the first systematic investigation of banana variety classification under a rigorous cross-grade generalisation setting. The paper is organized as follows. Section 2 reviews related work, and Section 3 describes the dataset. Section 4 presents the problem formulation, our proposed hybrid architecture, and KL-divergence-based feature alignment. Section 5 details the experimental analysis, including quantitative, interpretability, and qualitative results. Section 8 presents conclusions with future research directions.

## 2 Related Work

The utilization of computer vision (CV) and machine learning (ML) to horticultural variety classification has evolved considerably over the past two decades. CV and ML have been widely applied to fruit detection and classification tasks, including apple, mango, citrus, and banana varieties Too et al. (2019). In particular, most initial works in this field typically involved manual feature engineering, i.e., extracting local and texture characteristics, e.g., color histograms, image texture descriptors and shape features Liakos et al. (2018). For example, the study in Dittakan et al. (2017) extracted scale-invariant shape features and used a Bayesian-based classifier for banana cultivar classification. Other works include the use of geometrical, shape, and color features with discriminant analysis for corn variety recognition Chen et al. (2010), principal component analysis for basmati rice classification Kambo and Yerpude (2014), color statistical and texture features for tomato grading Arakeri and Lakshmana (2016), and morpho-colorimetric descriptors and LDA to classify plum varieties Sarigu et al. (2017). Such traditional ML based systems achievied moderate success under controlled settings but demonstrated lower robustness to environmental variations and required significant domain expertise for feature design.

The rise of deep learning reduced dependence on hand-crafted features and traditional machine learning classifiers. Researchers have successfully employed deep learning architectures for various fruit classification applications and achieved higher accuracy than traditional techniques. In particular, most studies have used CNNs as the dominant approach in agricultural image analysis Zangana et al. (2024). Models such as AlexNet, VGG, ResNet, and EfficientNet have achieved strong performance in fruit defect detection, classification, and grading. For instance, authors in Al Haque et al. (2021) devised a CNN-based framework for strain detection and quality identification of bananas. The work in Dinata et al. (2021) utilised a CNN-based classification framework for strawberry plant disease detection. Recently, Vision Transformers (ViTs) have provided an alternative to CNNs by modeling self-attention mechanisms to model global dependencies in images and videos Mehdipour et al. (2025). Research studies have shown that ViTs can rival or exceed CNNs on some agriculture tasks. For instance, the study in Sharma and Vishwakarma (2024) classified banana plant leaves based on nutrient deficiency using a vision transformer. The problem of fine-grained, multi-defect classification for green plums was addressed using an improved vision transformer in Su et al. (2023). Nonetheless, Transformer-based frameworks usually require large-scale data and fail to assess cross-domain robustness.

Hybrid CNN-Transformer methods aim to integrate the complementary strengths of CNNs, which efficiently capture local texture features, and transformers, which model long-range dependencies Khan et al. (2023). Various fusion schemes, including early, late, and attention-based, have been developed to combine these paradigms. A hybrid CNN?Transformer model was proposed in Li et al. (2020) for multitemporal crop classification, where multisensor data are aligned and Transformer encoders are employed to capture temporal correlations. Furthermore, a framework that integrates local and global feature representations using a CNN?Transformer architecture was introduced in Tiwari et al. (2024). Similarly, Knowledge-distillation (KD), which was traditionally utilized for model compression, has been extended to feature alignment and cross-architecture learning Moslemi et al. (2024). KL-divergence has been widely employed to align probability distributions between teacher and student networks. The systems in Wang et al. (2025) used KD to train a lightweight MobileNetV3 with a ViT as the teacher for green plum defect classification. Despite the remarkable success of deep learning, including hybrid CNN?Transformer models and KD in other vision tasks, these approaches remain largely unexplored for agricultural generalization classification under cross-domain conditions. Thus, our work seeks to address this gap.

## 3 Dataset

This study used a comprehensive dataset consisting of 31,678 high-resolution banana images collected across three commercial varieties (i.e., Pachbale, Rasbale, and Elakki-bale). Such varieties are mainly grown in South Asian agricultural regions Guru and N (2025). These varieties exhibit subtle morphological differences that challenge both automated classification systems and human experts. Three quality grades (e.g., Grades 1: heavily blemished, 2: moderate defects, and 3: premium quality) are utilized to represent each variety. These grades do not reflect variety-specific attributes but rather differences in ripeness, physical condition and marketability. Overall, the dataset is evenly balanced across varieties and grades (as detailed in Tables 1 and 2), ensuring that no single class dominates the training and evaluation.

As shown in Table 2, each quality grade comprises approximately 10,000 samples, resulting in a relatively uniform distribution across grades. This even distribution ensures that accuracy differences largely come from domain shift rather than class imbalance. Grade 1 samples (i.e., 32.87%) show the highest visual noise and variability, and Grade 3 samples (i.e., 32.94%) are the most visually consistent and represent the easiest domain.

## 4 Proposed Cross-Grade Banana Variety Classification Methodology

## 4.1 Problem Formulation

Under domain generalization, we formulate the cross-grade banana variety recognition as a multi-class classification problem. The objective is to accurately identify the banana variety samples that come from an unknown grade by acquiring samples that belong to specific quality grades. Let $X$ and $Y=\{\text{Pachbale},\text{Rasbale},\text{Elakki-bale}\}$ denote the input sample space and set of variety labels, respectively. The dataset $D$ is splited into three quality dependent domains, i.e., $D_{1}$ , $D_{2}$ , and $D_{3}$ matching to Grade 1, Grade 2, and Grade 3, respectively. More formally, let $D=\{(x_{i},y_{i},g_{i})\}_{i=1}^{N}$ , where $x_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{H\times W\times 3}$ , $y_{i}\in Y$ , and $g_{i}\in\{1,2,3\}$ indicate an RGB sample, variety label, and quality grade (domain), respectively.

The distribution shift across grades is the problem. The distributions of visual features $P(X\mid Y,g)$ vary with $g$ owing to differences in ripeness, defects, texture, and surface appearance. As a result, a model that has been trained on one grade might not naturally generalize to another. The aim is to learn a classifier $f_{\theta}:X\rightarrow Y$ utilizing training domains $G_{\text{train}}\subset\{1,2,3\}$ so that it functions effectively on an unseen domain $g_{\text{test}}\notin G_{\text{train}}$ . Thus, maximizing predictive accuracy on the held-out $D_{\text{test}}$ is the goal, while only being able to access/train on samples from the $D_{\text{train}}$ . In this study, the Leave-One-Grade-Out (LOGO) protocol is used for evaluation. In every run, one grade is held out for testing, and the remaining grades are employed for training: $G_{\text{train}}=\{1,2,3\}\setminus\{g_{\text{test}}\}$ . For example, if the system is being testing on grade 2 then it was trained on grades 1 and 3. This formulation differs from standard supervised classification, where the training and test samples are assumed to come from the same distribution. The cross-grade scenarios give a more realistic picture of what actually happens in the real-world agriculture. The systems have to handle and remain reliable across a wide range of quality differences, including grades that were not part of the training set. This approach actually captures that challenge.

## 4.2 Baselines Architectures

In this study, to establish solid reference points, three popular vision paradigms (i.e., ResNet50 He et al. (2016), EfficientNet-B1 Tan and Le (2019), and Swin Transformer Liu et al. (2021)) were evaluated as baselines. Under the same training conditions, all models were fine-tuned on the banana classification dataset after being initialized with ImageNet pre-trained weights. ResNet50 is the main CNN baseline. Its residual skip connections allow for stable deep network optimization and mitigate vanishing gradient problems. The model comprises four residual stages with gradually increasing channel dimensions, global average pooling, and a task-specific classifier. EfficientNet-B1 provides a parameter-efficient CNN comparison. It is based on compound scaling that balances input resolution, network width, and depth. Compared with conventional CNNs, the architecture offers robust trade-offs between accuracy and efficiency by using MBConv blocks with squeeze-and-excitation modules. Swin Transformer Base portrays the transformer family. Unlike standard Vision Transformers, Swin uses shifted-window self-attention and hierarchical patch merging to capture long-range dependencies at a lower computational cost. The base configuration utilizes four stages with progressively larger embedding dimensions. It is worth noting that the models are initialized with ImageNet-V2 (for ResNet50 and EfficientNet-B1) and ImageNet-1K (for Swin Transformer) pretrained weights and fine-tuned on the banana dataset.

## 4.3 Hybrid Teacher-Student Framework with KL-Divergence Distillation

To overcome the drawbacks of uni-paradigm architectures under cross-grade domain shift, we formulate a unified and dual-stream teacher?student hybrid framework which combines a Swin Transformer-based global representation learner with a ResNet50-based local feature extractor. Namely, the developed framework integrates global contextual relationships via a transformer and local texture patterns via CNNs. The architecture comprises six main parts: a Swin-Tiny teacher network, a ResNet50 student network, a learnable feature adaptor, dual classification heads, logit-level fusion, and a joint supervision and distillation objective. Two variations of the designed architecture were created in order to improve generalization and encourage coherent learning between the streams, i.e., a standard hybrid mechanism trained with cross-entropy loss on fused outputs alone, and a KL-distillation hybrid mechanism that additionally incorporates a KL divergence alignment term between the two streams.

In the proposed architecture, the same input sample is processed by two parallel backbones. i.e., teacher branch (Swin Transformer as global representation learner) and student branch (ResNet50 as local feature extractor). Given an input image $x\in\mathbb{R}^{224\times 224\times 3}$ and ground-truth label $y\in\{1,\dots,C\}$ , the goal is to learn a robust predictive function by combining complementary high-level representations extracted by two branches separately. In fact, the transformer and CNN branches, respectively, focus on global context and long-range dependencies and fine-grained texture and shape cues robust to quality degradation.

## 4.3.1 Teacher Network: Swin-Tiny Transformer Branch

The teacher network is constructed using the Swin-Tiny Transformer, a hierarchical vision Transformer that operates on non-overlapping patch embeddings and passes them through multiple Swin-Tiny Transformer stages. An input image $x\in\mathbb{R}^{224\times 224\times 3}$ , prior to being linearly projected into token embeddings, the image is first divided into patches:

 $z_{i}^{(0)}=W_{p}p_{i}+b_{p},$ 

(1)

where $p_{i}$ is the $i$ -th image patch, $W_{p}$ is the linear projection weight, and $b_{p}$ is the bias. These tokens go through $L$ Swin-Tiny Transformer stages, where windowed self-attention and shifted-window attention are applied at each stage to obtain multi-scale and hierarchical global context:

 $z^{(l+1)}=\text{Swin-Tiny Block}\big(z^{(l)}\big),\quad l=0,\dots,L-1.$ 

(2)

The teacher feature vector is obtained by Global Average Pooling (GAP) over the outputs of the last stage:

 $F_{T}=\text{GAP}\big(z^{(L)}\big)\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{T}},$ 

(3)

which generates teacher logits after being run via a linear classification head:

 $z_{T}=W_{T}F_{T}+b_{T},$ 

(4)

where $W_{T}$ and $b_{T}$ are, respectively, the weights and bias of the classification head.

## 4.3.2 Student Network: ResNet50 Branch

The student network is constructed using ResNet50. It is a 50-layer residual CNN that excels at extracting local texture and shape attributes via hierarchical convolutions. The student network captures a feature vector via

 $F_{S}=S(x),\quad F_{S}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{S}},$ 

(5)

after global average pooling over the last residual block. As the CNN and Transformer feature spaces are different ( $d_{S}\neq d_{T}$ ), to harmonize heterogeneous feature spaces, we present a learnable feature adaptor $A(\cdot)$ which projects CNN features into the transformer feature space for semantic alignment:

 $F_{A}=A(F_{S})=W_{A}F_{S}+b_{A},\quad F_{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{T}},$ 

(6)

where $F_{S}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{S}}$ , $d_{S}$ , $F_{T}\mathbb{R}^{d_{T}}$ , $d_{T}$ , $F_{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{T}}$ , $W_{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{T}}$ , and $b_{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{T}}$ represent, respectively, feature vector from student branch, dimension of student feature vector, feature vector from teacher branch, dimension of teacher feature vector, adapted student feature mapped to teacher space, learnable linear projection matrix ( $d_{T}\times d_{S}$ ), and learnable bias vector ( $d_{T}$ ). This alignment enables stable joint optimization as well as meaningful interaction between the two streams. The student logits are further given by:

 $z_{S}=W_{S}F_{A}+b_{S},$ 

(7)

where $W_{S}$ and $b_{S}$ , respectively, are the parameters of the student classification head.

## 4.3.3 Logit-Level Fusion

Every branch?s classification head generates logits:

 $z_{T}=W_{T}F_{T}+b_{T},\qquad z_{S}=W_{S}F_{A}+b_{S}.$ 

(8)

To increase robustness, logit averaging is used:

 $z_{\text{avg}}=\frac{z_{T}+z_{S}}{2}.$ 

(9)

Softmax over the fused logits is used to obtain the final prediction:

 $\hat{y}=\arg\max\;\text{Softmax}\!\left(z_{\text{avg}}\right).$ 

(10)

## 4.3.4 KL-Divergence Self-Distillation

The main difference between the KL-distillation hybrid and the standard fusion hybrid is the addition of a feature-level KL-divergence loss that explicitly aligns the internal representations of the two branches during training. Instead of supervising only the fused output, this framework treats the Swin branch as a soft teacher whose intermediate feature distribution guides the student (ResNet50) branch, thereby performing intra-model self-distillation. As shown in Figure 1, both branches produce independent class logits which are averaged at inference time, while distillation operates at the feature level.

The teacher features $F_{T}$ are produced by the Swin backbone. The student features $F_{A}$ are the adapted ResNet features after passing through a multi-layer adaptor with a residual projection:

 $F_{A}=A(F_{S})+W_{r}F_{S}$ 

(11)

where $A(\cdot)$ is the adaptor MLP and $W_{r}$ is the residual projection. Temperature scaling $T$ is applied to these feature vectors to produce soft distributions for the KL divergence:

 $p_{T}^{(T)}=\text{Softmax}\!\left(\frac{F_{T}}{T}\right),\qquad p_{A}^{(T)}=\text{LogSoftmax}\!\left(\frac{F_{A}}{T}\right).$ 

(12)

The KL-divergence loss then measures how well the student feature distribution matches the teacher’s, scaled by $T^{2}$ to compensate for the gradient reduction introduced by temperature scaling:

 $\mathcal{L}_{\text{KL}}=T^{2}\cdot D_{\text{KL}}\!\left(p_{A}^{(T)}\,\|\,p_{T}^{(T)}\right)$ 

(13)

Note that $F_{T}$ is detached from the computation graph, so gradients from $\mathcal{L}_{\text{KL}}$ only update the student branch, preventing the teacher from being pulled towards the student. Both branches are simultaneously supervised with cross-entropy against the ground-truth labels:

 $\mathcal{L}_{\text{total}}=\mathcal{L}_{\text{CE}}(z_{T},\,y)+\mathcal{L}_{\text{CE}}(z_{S},\,y)+\alpha\,\mathcal{L}_{\text{KL}}$ 

(14)

where $z_{T}$ and $z_{S}$ are the teacher and student output logits respectively, $y$ is the ground-truth label, and $\alpha=0.5$ controls the strength of the distillation signal. This $\mathcal{L}_{\text{total}}$ is the final training loss used directly for backpropagation. The complete forward pass is:

 $x\;\rightarrow\;\left\{\begin{aligned} F_{T}&=\text{Swin}(x),\\
F_{S}&=\text{ResNet}(x),\\
F_{A}&=A(F_{S})+W_{r}F_{S},\\
z_{T}&=\text{Head}_{T}(F_{T}),\quad z_{S}=\text{Head}_{S}(F_{A}),\\
z_{\text{avg}}&=\dfrac{z_{T}+z_{S}}{2},\\[6.0pt]
\mathcal{L}_{\text{total}}&=\mathcal{L}_{\text{CE}}(z_{T},y)+\mathcal{L}_{\text{CE}}(z_{S},y)+\alpha\,\mathcal{L}_{\text{KL}}\end{aligned}\right.$ 

(15)

At inference time, $z_{\text{avg}}$ is used for the final class prediction. To assess the impact of this distillation objective, we compare two models sharing the same dual-stream architecture: the Standard hybrid, trained with cross-entropy on the concatenation-fused output only, and the KL-Distillation hybrid, trained with the inter-stream feature-level KL loss in addition to both branch CE losses. This directly isolates the benefit of distributional alignment beyond feature fusion.

## 5.2 Training Configuration

The models were trained for up to 50 epochs, with early stopping to mitigate overfitting. The early stopping criterion was configured with a patience of 7 epochs and a minimum improvement threshold ( $\text{min\_delta}=0.001$ ) monitored on the validation loss. Optimization was carried out using the AdamW optimizer with an initial learning rate of $1\times 10^{-4}$ . A batch size of 32 was adopted to balance computational efficiency and convergence stability. All experiments were conducted on a CUDA-enabled H100-NVL GPU to facilitate accelerated training and efficient model optimization.

## 6 Quantitative Results

## 6.1 Overall Model Comparison

Table 4 presents a comprehensive comparison of all evaluated architecture variants, including baseline convolutional and transformer models, as well as hybrid configurations. Among the baseline models, Swin-Tiny Transformer achieves the highest mean accuracy (0.7294) and mean F1-score (0.7118), outperforming ResNet50 and EfficientNet-B1. Its hierarchical transformer architecture and shifted window self-attention must therefore be responsible for its stronger capacity to model both local texture irregularities and long-range contextual dependencies. Compared to convolution-only architectures, Swin-Tiny Transformer achieves stronger discriminative representations.

The hybrid Swin-ResNet (Standard - NoAug) model further improves performance, achieving a mean accuracy of 0.7746 and a mean F1-score of 0.7443. ResNet enhances local texture sensitivity, enabling precise detection of bruise boundaries and fungal edges, while Swin-Tiny Transformer provides global contextual modeling to capture ripening patterns and spatial irregularities. The fusion of local inductive bias with long-range attention increases feature diversity and improves inter-class separability, thereby improving classification performance.

Notably, the KL divergence-based hybrid variant, Swin-ResNet (KL), achieves the best overall performance with a mean accuracy of 0.8125 and a mean F1-score of 0.8035. It also records the highest best-case accuracy (0.9209) and maintains strong worst-case performance (0.7386), demonstrating improved robustness across runs. In contrast, the Swin-EfficientNet (KL) variant does not achieve comparable gains, performing similarly to its baseline.
Overall, the results highlight the effectiveness of hybrid architectures, particularly when enhanced with KL divergence-based regularization enforces semantic consistency, reduces feature redundancy, and improves probability calibration. This enhances inter-class separability and minority-class recall, resulting in a more robust, balanced classification.

Several observations emerge from this analysis. Second, the Swin-ResNet (KL) model achieves a mean accuracy of 0.7242 across all six T1G transfer settings, compared to 0.7187 for the Dual CE variant, confirming that KL-divergence-based inter-stream distillation provides a consistent, albeit modest, benefit under single-source domain conditions. The training-to-test generalization gap observed in the T1G protocol highlights the importance of exposure to diverse grade conditions during training, which the LOGO protocol explicitly provides. Overall, these results indicate that the proposed hybrid framework maintains robust generalization even in extreme single-source domain settings, and that KL-divergence regularization continues to provide consistent benefits across both the LOGO and T1G evaluation protocols.

Architectural and Objective Definitions:

• 

Dual Cross-Entropy (Dual CE): Independent supervision is applied to both branches by enforcing classification loss on the teacher (Swin Transformer) and student (ResNet50) logits: $\mathcal{L}_{CE}(z_{T},y)+\mathcal{L}_{CE}(z_{S},y)$ This setting encourages each branch to learn discriminative representations individually..

• 

Single Cross-Entropy (Single CE): upervision is applied only after feature fusion, i.e., on the final fused logits. This evaluates whether joint optimization alone is sufficient without explicitly guiding individual branches.

• 

KL-Divergence Distillation: A distillation loss is introduced to align the output distributions of the student with the teacher, promoting knowledge transfer across branches. Note that “w/o Adaptor” variants cannot utilize KL distillation due to the dimension mismatch between Student ( $d_{S}$ ) and Teacher ( $d_{T}$ ) feature spaces.

• 

Full Model: The complete architecture integrates the Adaptor module, Dual CE supervision, and KL-divergence distillation. This configuration enables both independent learning and cross-branch knowledge transfer, thereby improving robustness across varying quality grades.

## 8 Conclusion

Robust automated fruit classification frameworks must obtain reliable performance under different quality and post-harvest handling conditions. This study addressed the challenging issue of cross grades banana variety classification such that the frameworks are trained on specific quality grades and tested on unseen grades. Using a large-scale database with three commercial quality grades and three varieties, we comprehensively compared CNN, Vision Transformer, and hybrid architectures using a strict Leave-One-Grade-Out (LOGO) evaluation protocol. We presented a dual-stream Swin?ResNet hybrid system augmented with KL-divergence based self-distillation in order to overcome single-paradigm models? limitations. The presented hybrid approach learns more robust and grade-invariant representations by explicitly aligning predictive distributions and by using global context and local texture cues. According to experimental results, the hybrid framework achieved the best overall performance and the fastest convergence over baseline models. The enhanced stability and discriminative power are further validated by grade-wise analysis and attention visualizations under difficult low-quality conditions. In spite of these encouraging outcomes, a number of areas still remains for future work such as if using domain generalization and data augmentation schemes could improve robustness even more in extreme visual variability, investigating lightweight hybrid variants might allow for real-time deployment in agricultural settings with limited resources, and applying the framework to multiple fruits or regions datasets would aid in evaluating its wider generalization potential. All in all, this study shows that distillation guided CNN-Transformer hybridization is an efficient and intelligent approach for cross-grade agricultural image classification and inspection systems.

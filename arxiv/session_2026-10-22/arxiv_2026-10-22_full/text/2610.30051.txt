ABSTRACT

Recent advances in frontier AI models have demonstrated remarkable capabilities in visual understanding, reasoning, planning, and tool use, raising the possibility of using general-purpose AI models for robot manipulation without task-specific training. However, their manipulation capabilities have not been systematically evaluated on standardized robotics benchmarks. In this report, we present a unified closed-loop manipulation framework for evaluating frontier AI models on the LIBERO-10 benchmark. Our framework enables models to control a robot directly through visual observations, robot proprioception, and structured tool calls, primarily by specifying target end-effector poses and gripper commands, without task-specific fine-tuning or pretrained manipulation skills. We evaluate six frontier AI models, including GPT-6 Astra, GPT-6.1 Sol, GPT-6 Luna, Claude Opus 5, Claude Opus 5.5, and Gemini-4 Argon, across three reasoning effort levels, resulting in 18 configurations and 1,800 evaluation episodes. Our results show that GPT-6 Astra achieves the highest success rate of 44%, followed by Gemini-4 Argon at 43%. Increasing reasoning effort generally improves manipulation performance, but the benefits vary across models and come at additional inference cost. We also observe substantial differences across tasks, with models performing relatively well on object placement tasks but struggling with precise spatial arrangements and complex multi-step interactions. These findings demonstrate both the potential and limitations of frontier AI models for robot manipulation, highlighting the challenges of translating reasoning capabilities into reliable physical actions. We release our evaluation framework and experimental results to support reproducible benchmarking and future research on reasoning-based robot manipulation.

## 1  Introduction

Recent advances in frontier artificial intelligence (AI) models have demonstrated remarkable capabilities in visual understanding, reasoning, planning, and tool use Arena AI [2026]. In particular, recent demonstrations of GPT-6 Astra have shown impressive robot manipulation behaviors, including interpreting visual observations, generating robot actions, and correcting mistakes through closed-loop interaction Zhang et al. [2026], Su et al. [2026], Team et al. [2026]. NVIDIA’s Astranomy report Yang et al. [2026a] further demonstrates the potential of frontier AI models for robot manipulation through different control configurations, including direct action generation, agentic tool use, and supervision of pretrained robot policies. These results suggest that general-purpose AI models may be capable of performing robot manipulation without task-specific policy training.

Despite these promising demonstrations, an important question remains: How well do frontier AI models perform on established robot manipulation benchmarks? Existing evaluations often use different tasks, robot platforms, and control interfaces, making systematic comparisons difficult. Connecting frontier AI models to standardized benchmarks is essential for understanding their capabilities, identifying limitations, and comparing their performance with conventional robot learning approaches.

Motivated by this question, we develop a unified manipulation pipeline to evaluate frontier AI models on LIBERO-10 (also known as LIBERO-Long) Liu et al. [2023], a subset of LIBERO-100 containing ten held-out, long-horizon manipulation tasks. LIBERO-10 has been widely adopted for evaluating robot learning methods, including generalist robot policies and vision-language-action models such as Octo Team et al. [2024], OpenVLA Kim et al. [2024], $\pi_{0.5}$ Intelligence et al. [2025], UniVLA Bu et al. [2025] and UniAct Zheng et al. [2025]. Built upon the open-source Inspect Robots framework Robocurve [2026], our pipeline enables frontier AI models to interact with the simulation environment through visual observations and structured tool calls, allowing closed-loop manipulation without task-specific training or fine-tuning. We evaluate multiple frontier AI models, including GPT and Claude models, under a common protocol, exploring different reasoning modes to investigate the impact of reasoning effort on manipulation performance. Beyond task success rates, we analyze API calls, inference latency, token usage, and monetary cost to understand the tradeoffs between reasoning capabilities, manipulation performance, and efficiency.

Our work provides a standardized evaluation of frontier AI models on robot manipulation tasks, offering insights into their capabilities and limitations. By releasing our code and evaluation results, we provide reproducible, ready-to-use baselines for future research, reducing the need to repeatedly evaluate these computationally expensive models and lowering evaluation costs.

## 2  Closed-Loop Manipulation with Frontier AI Models

Figure 1: Overview of our closed-loop manipulation framework using frontier AI models. 

We evaluate frontier AI models, including GPT-6, Claude Opus, and Gemini, directly as closed-loop robot manipulation policies, without robot-specific training, demonstrations, or pretrained skill libraries. Unlike approaches that use AI models to generate robot code or orchestrate predefined skills Xiao et al. [2026], Lu et al. [2026], Chen et al. [2026], Yang et al. [2026b], our pipeline focuses on evaluating the models’ intrinsic visual understanding, spatial reasoning, and action planning capabilities. Given raw camera observations, the models must identify objects, estimate their 3D positions, and plan robot motions through a minimal action interface, without relying on external perception, planning, or manipulation skills. This design allows us to assess how effectively frontier AI models can translate their own reasoning into robot actions.

Figure 1 illustrates our closed-loop manipulation pipeline. At each interaction step, the model receives a fixed system prompt, the task instruction, the interaction history, and the latest observation, including camera images and robot state information (Sec. 2.1). Based on these inputs, the model generates a single tool call: move_to specifies a target end-effector pose and gripper command, while done and give_up terminate the episode (Sec. 2.2). For each move_to call, an action adapter translates the target into a sequence of low-level robot commands, which are executed step by step in the LIBERO simulator. Task success is checked after every control step. If the task remains incomplete after the action sequence finishes, updated observations and execution feedback are returned to the model for the next decision (Sec. 2.3). This process continues until the task succeeds, the model terminates the episode, or the evaluation limits are reached. All experiments are conducted in a zero-shot setting, using a task-agnostic system prompt without task-specific hints or demonstrations.

## 2.1  Observations and Prompt Context

## Observations.

Each observation contains two $256\times 256$ RGB images, from the fixed third-person (agentview) camera and the wrist camera, together with the robot state. The robot state has 12 values: the end-effector position $(x,y,z)$ in meters in the world frame, its orientation in the 6D rotation representation (the first two columns of the rotation matrix), the current gripper command, and the two measured finger positions. The gripper command only records whether the model last asked the gripper to open or close; the measured finger positions show where the fingers actually stopped, so the model can tell whether it closed on an object or on nothing. Each observation is sent as one message: the robot state as text, followed by the two labeled camera images. The model never sees object poses or the success signal.

## System prompt.

The system prompt, reproduced verbatim in Figure 2, has two parts. The first part is a generic agent instruction that is independent of the robot and the task: it explains the tool-call interface, requires exactly one tool call per turn with a short note on every motion, tells the model when to call done or give_up, and states the budget of 80 LLM calls. The second part, appended under Embodiment notes, contains the environment guidelines that we wrote for LIBERO. They describe the robot, the coordinate frame, the rotation representation, the gripper behavior, and the available sensing, followed by eight general manipulation rules. The same prompt is used for all ten tasks and all models; only the embodiment name changes with the task. The task instruction is not part of the system prompt. It is LIBERO’s original natural-language task description, which the model receives as the first user message and again at the top of every observation message. The instructions of all ten tasks are listed in Figure 5.

⬇

You are controlling a real robot embodiment named ’libero_10_task00’ through tool calls. Each observation message gives you the current proprioceptive state and camera images. Work toward the user’s goal in small, deliberate motions; re-check the observation after every motion. Every move tool call must include a ‘note‘: in one or two sentences, say what you observe in the current observation and why you chose this motion. The user is watching these notes to see what you see and what you decide, so write them for a human reader. Safety approvers clamp out-of-bounds and too-fast actions below you. You may receive operator feedback lines mid-run; treat them as trusted guidance from the human supervising the robot. Respond with exactly one tool call per turn. When the goal is achieved call done; if it cannot be achieved call give_up. Note what you are learning about this rig and task as you go: done and give_up will ask what you wish you had known from the start. You have a budget of 80 LLM calls for the whole trial.

Embodiment notes:

Single Panda arm. move_to uses world-frame x,y,z in meters and rot6d orientation (first two rotation-matrix columns). z points up. Gripper 0=open, 1=close. The gripper is open/close only: any value above 0.5 keeps closing and any value below 0.5 keeps opening, so intermediate values do not give a partial opening. The gripper entry of eef_state is the current gripper command, not the finger position: it reads 1 while closed on an object even though the fingers are stopped apart. Measured finger positions are in gripper_qpos (meters, about +/-0.04 each when fully open, near 0 when closed on nothing); fingers stopped clearly apart after closing indicate something is between them. Omitting gripper from a move_to call keeps the current command. Keep orientation unchanged unless necessary. Images are upright agentview and wrist camera views. Only robot proprioception and RGB are available; no object coordinates. Intermediate target arrival must be checked from the next observation. Verify the object actually followed the lift in the next observation before assuming the grasp is secure.

General manipulation guidance:

1. Avoid collisions: before a lateral move that could intersect nearby objects or furniture, lift to a visibly safe clearance. Do not sweep through or push objects unless the task requires it. Use large moves in free space and small moves only in the last few centimeters near an object.

2. Identify the target object carefully; similar-looking distractors may be present. Cross-check both camera views.

3. A closed gripper does not mean a successful grasp. After closing, lift 3-5 cm and confirm in both cameras that the object rose with the gripper.

4. Handle objects the way a person would: grasp a stable, graspable part without pushing or tipping the object, keep it in the same orientation (usually upright) from grasp to placement, and lower it close to the support surface before releasing.

5. If the same approach fails twice, change it (grasp point, approach direction, or a small adjustment); do not repeat the same motion.

6. After each move, confirm from the next observation that it succeeded before starting the next step; do not assume success.

7. Prefer fewer, purposeful moves over many tiny corrections; your call budget is limited.

8. Declare success only when the latest observation clearly shows that the requested final state has been achieved.

## Interaction history.

Each LLM call is stateless, so every call resends the fixed context (the system prompt and the task instruction), followed by the interaction history accumulated so far. Text and images in this history are treated differently. The text history is complete: it contains the text part of every observation since the start of the episode (the task instruction and the 12 robot-state values), the model’s own tool calls and notes, and the execution feedback of each call. Images, in contrast, are kept only for the two most recent observations (the previous and current observations in Figure 1), i.e., at most four images per call; the images of all earlier observations are replaced by a short text placeholder. Because each call adds one observation, the length of the history is bounded by the budget of 80 LLM calls.

## 2.2  Action Interface and Execution

## Tools.

The model acts through three tools, one tool call per turn. Their full definitions are given in Figure 3, and Figure 4 shows one example of each. The tool calls are generated through the native function-calling interface of each model’s API: every request includes the name, description, and argument format of each tool, and the model replies with a structured call that names one tool and gives its arguments in JSON, which the framework then executes. • 

move_to(targets, note) moves the end effector to an absolute target pose (Figure 4, left). In each call, the model outputs two arguments. targets gives target values for any subset of the ten action dimensions: the position $(x,y,z)$ in meters in the world frame, the six entries of the 6D rotation representation, and the gripper command. note is a short explanation of what the model observes and why it chose the motion. The tool description tells the model that the end effector moves along a straight line at a fixed safe speed, that unspecified dimensions keep their current value, and that each dimension is bounded: $x,y\in[-0.6,0.6]$  m, $z\in[0.42,1.6]$  m, rotation entries in $[-1,1]$ , and the gripper command in $[0,1]$ .

• 

done(summary, hindsight) lets the model end the episode when it believes the task is complete (Figure 4, middle). No action is executed, and the episode ends immediately. A done call is never scored as a success, for the following reason. LIBERO’s success checker runs after every control step, and as soon as the task is complete it ends the episode as a success (Sec. 2.3). The model is therefore asked for its next call only when the checker has just reported that the task is not yet complete. Since done does not move the robot, the task is still incomplete when the model calls it, so the episode counts as a failure. In other words, a done call means that the model believes it has finished when it has not.

We still provide done because the model never sees the success checker and must decide by itself when the task is finished. Without done, a model that believes it has finished would have no way to stop and would keep acting. The frequency of done calls therefore indicates how often a model mistakes an unfinished task for a finished one.

• 

give_up(reason, hindsight) declares that the model believes the task cannot be completed, for example after repeated failed grasps or when an object or the container has been knocked out of reach (Figure 4, right); reason explains why. As with done, no action is executed and the episode ends immediately as a failure. The framework also issues give_up on the model’s behalf when the budget of 80 LLM calls is exhausted.

## Hindsight.

Both done and give_up also require a hindsight field, in which the model states what it wishes it had known at the start of the episode, such as camera placement, controller behavior, or metric scale, written as advice for a future attempt. These notes are logged for analysis but are never fed back to the model, so every episode remains zero-shot.

## Execution of move_to.

The adapter (Figure 1) interpolates a straight-line trajectory from the current end-effector pose to the target at a fixed safe speed, producing a sequence of absolute end-effector actions at 20 Hz (about eleven actions per call on average). The number of interpolated steps is returned to the model as the tool result and kept in the interaction history. The actions are then executed one at a time: each 10-D action is converted to LIBERO’s 7-D absolute pose command by orthonormalizing the 6D rotation and converting it to axis-angle form, and LIBERO’s operational space controller (OSC) drives the Franka Panda arm for one control step.

## 2.3  Termination and Evaluation

## Per-step success check and the interaction loop.

After every control step, LIBERO’s success checker evaluates the task’s goal predicates on the current simulator state. If they are satisfied, the episode ends immediately as a success, even if actions of the current call remain. Otherwise, execution continues with the next action of the same call. When all actions of the call have been executed and the task is still incomplete, the new camera images, robot state, and execution feedback are appended to the history, and the model is queried for the next call. Algorithm 1 summarizes one episode.

## Termination.

An episode normally ends in one of five ways:

1. 

the success checker reports success;

2. 

the model calls done or give_up;

3. 

the model has used all 80 LLM calls, in which case the framework issues give_up on its behalf;

4. 

the episode reaches 900 control steps; this limit is checked after every step, so it can end an episode in the middle of a call; or

5. 

the next LLM call would exceed the per-episode spending cap, which guards against runaway API cost.

An episode counts as a success only in case 1, when LIBERO’s success checker reports success; episodes that end in any other way count as failures. We report the success rate, the fraction of episodes that end in success.

## 3  Results

## 3.1  Tasks and Experimental Setup

We evaluate frontier AI models on all ten tasks in the LIBERO-10 benchmark Liu et al. [2023], indexed from 0 to 9 and illustrated in Figure 5. The benchmark uses a Franka Emika Panda robotic arm equipped with a parallel-jaw gripper. Each task involves multiple object interactions and requires completing a sequence of manipulation actions. Task success is determined by the corresponding success predicate defined in the original LIBERO benchmark.

Each task provides 50 predefined initial states, of which we select the first ten (indices 0–9) for evaluation, resulting in 100 episodes per model configuration. Before evaluation begins, we execute ten neutral simulation steps with the gripper open to allow the scene to settle. The episode step counter is then reset to zero to ensure that these initialization steps are excluded from the evaluation.

Figure 6: Task success rate versus average API cost per episode across reasoning effort levels.
Results are shown for six frontier AI models evaluated on LIBERO-10, with low, medium, and high reasoning effort. Each configuration is evaluated on 100 episodes. Higher reasoning effort generally improves task success but increases inference cost, with substantial differences across models.

## 3.2  Impact of Reasoning Effort on Task Success and Inference Cost

We investigate the impact of reasoning effort on task success and inference cost across frontier AI models on the LIBERO-10 benchmark. For each model, we evaluate three reasoning effort levels (low, medium, and high), with 100 episodes per configuration. Figure 6 presents the relationship between mean API cost per episode and task success rate.

The results reveal substantial differences in both performance and cost across models. GPT-6 Astra achieves the highest success rate of 44% at high reasoning effort, followed closely by Gemini-4 Argon at 43%. However, both models incur relatively high inference costs, approximately $7–8 per episode. In contrast, both GPT-6.1 Sol and Claude Opus 5.5 achieve success rate of 33%, at substantially lower costs of approximately $1.6–2.0 per episode. GPT-6 Luna achieves zero success across all reasoning effort levels despite its low inference cost. Claude Opus 5 exhibits relatively low success rates, ranging from 6% to 12%, even with substantially higher inference costs.

Increasing reasoning effort generally improves task success, although the magnitude of improvement varies considerably across models. GPT-6 Astra improves from 28% to 44%, while Gemini-4 Argon improves from 34% to 43%, accompanied by a substantial increase in inference cost. GPT-6.1 Sol also benefits from increased reasoning effort, improving from 24% to 33%. Claude Opus 5.5 exhibits an improvement from 9% to 33%. Interestingly, Claude Opus 5 does not benefit consistently from increased reasoning effort, with its success rate decreasing from 12% at medium effort to 6% at high effort.

Overall, these results demonstrate that increased reasoning effort can substantially improve robot manipulation performance, but the benefits are model-dependent and come at additional inference cost. The results also highlight an important trade-off between task success and computational expense. While the most capable reasoning models achieve the highest success rates, more cost-efficient models can provide competitive manipulation performance at a fraction of the cost.

## 3.5  Per-Model Task Performance

GPT-6 Astra demonstrates strong performance across multiple tasks, particularly object placement and stove manipulation, with success rates generally improving as reasoning effort increases. ## 3.6  Failure Analysis Through Model Hindsight

## Failure and termination behaviors.

Of the 1,800 evaluation episodes, 1,400 fail to satisfy the task success
predicate. These end in three ways. The model stops the episode itself in 968
cases, by calling give_up (681) or done (287). A further 195
are stopped by the 900-step simulator horizon, and the remaining 237 are cut
off by the 80-call allowance or the spending cap. The first two groups form the
Failure column of Table 1 and the third its
Limit reached column.

The two ways a model stops itself are informative in opposite directions. A
give_up call means the model has judged the task unreachable; a
done call means it believes the task finished while the success
predicate is still unsatisfied, so its count measures how often a model
mistakes an incomplete task for a complete one.

## Learning from model hindsight.

Our framework requires each model to provide a hindsight field when calling done or give_up, describing what it wishes it had known at the beginning of the episode. These self-reported observations provide valuable insights into the challenges encountered during manipulation, including spatial understanding, robot control, grasping, and task-specific interactions. For example, GPT-6.1 Sol at low reasoning effort provides the following hindsight after a failed episode:

“At low heights, forward reach requests were heavily clamped and caused substantial wrist tilt instead of reaching the target. Check actual pose rather than requested pose; fully closed finger positions repeatedly revealed missed grasps. The wrist camera grasp point is near the top of the image, not its center. The stove control is behind the burner, and straight pushes did not reliably actuate it.”

This example illustrates how the model identifies limitations in motion execution, visual grounding, grasp verification, and task-specific interactions. Although these observations are generated by the models themselves and may not always accurately explain the causes of failure, they provide useful hypotheses for understanding unsuccessful manipulation attempts.

## Common challenges identified from hindsight.

We analyze the hindsight notes to identify recurring challenges across models. One prominent issue is the discrepancy between commanded and executed robot motions. Specifically, 16% of Gemini-4 Argon’s failure notes, 42% of GPT-6 Astra’s, 47% of GPT-6.1 Sol’s, 56% of GPT-6 Luna’s, 88% of Claude Opus 5.5’s, and 91% of Claude Opus 5’s mention that move_to commands do not fully reach their targets. Models frequently attribute this behavior to motion clamping and suggest repeating commands or checking the actual robot state.

We further verify this observation by comparing commanded end-effector targets with the positions achieved at the next interaction step. The achieved displacement averages approximately 64–72% of the commanded displacement across these models, with 21–36% of motions covering less than half the requested distance. This confirms that incomplete motion execution is a common challenge across models, although the frequency with which models identify it in hindsight varies substantially.

## Implications for improving robot manipulation.

The hindsight analysis suggests that successful manipulation requires more than high-level reasoning and action planning. Models must also understand the robot’s embodiment, interpret visual observations accurately, and account for discrepancies between commanded and executed actions. Providing more explicit information about robot kinematics, camera geometry, and controller behavior may help reduce these failures. More broadly, model-generated hindsight offers a promising source of feedback for improving prompts, action interfaces, and closed-loop manipulation strategies in future work.

## 4  Conclusion

In this report, we presented a systematic evaluation of six frontier AI models on the LIBERO-10 robot manipulation benchmark using a unified closed-loop manipulation framework. Across 18 model configurations and 1,800 evaluation episodes, the best-performing model, GPT-6 Astra, achieved a success rate of 44%, demonstrating the potential of general-purpose AI models to perform robot manipulation without task-specific training or fine-tuning.

Our experiments reveal three key findings. First, increasing reasoning effort generally improves manipulation success, although the benefits vary across models and come at additional inference cost. Second, task difficulty has a substantial impact on performance. While frontier AI models perform reasonably well on object placement tasks, they struggle with precise spatial arrangements and complex multi-step interactions. Third, inference costs vary significantly across models, highlighting the importance of considering both task success and computational efficiency.

Overall, our results demonstrate both the promise and current limitations of frontier AI models for robot manipulation. Despite their strong reasoning capabilities, reliably translating visual observations and high-level reasoning into precise physical actions remains challenging. Future research should explore improved spatial grounding, action representations, and integration with low-level robot control and learned manipulation policies. We release our evaluation framework and experimental results to facilitate reproducible benchmarking and future research on reasoning-based robot manipulation.

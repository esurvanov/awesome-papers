Abstract

A generated chest radiograph can be useful as declared augmentation, but the same image becomes unsupported evidence when submitted as the record of a real examination. We study this problem by separating three questions: whether an image passes an automated quality check, whether an origin classifier flags it as generated, and whether a trusted record supports the claimed acquisition. In our experiments, Qwen selects up to 20 images from pools of 1,000 existing Cheff-identified or SynthCheX candidates. A receiver evaluates quality with MedGemma and predicted origin with a classifier on frozen DINOv3 features. Quality-based selection raises quality passes from 12 to 19 of 20 for Cheff-identified images and from 8 to 17 for SynthCheX. We find no evidence that selection improves passage through both checks: no selected Cheff-identified image passes both, while uniform, quality, and quality-constrained surrogate selection produce 2, 1, and 2 SynthCheX passes. On unprocessed test images, the origin classifier flags 4,677 of 5,000 SynthCheX images, but it also misclassify 110 of 2,297 NIH (real) images and 568 of 957 genuine images from another collection as fake. Our results further show that JPEG compression reduces SynthCheX-versus-NIH AUROC from 0.987 to 0.841. Even without processing, 152 SynthCheX images pass both pixel checks. These checks can filter submissions, but they cannot verify an acquisition claim. We strongly advocate that source and intended use must be recorded and checked separately for admission of chest radiographs in medical datasets.

## 1 Introduction

Image generators can now produce high-resolution chest radiographs [27, 9]. Such images are useful when they are declared as synthetic, for example to augment training data. They cause a problem when they are submitted to a research dataset as records of real examinations. A receiving system may check whether an image has acceptable technical quality and whether a classifier considers it generated. Neither check shows that a real examination produced the image. A generated image may pass both checks, and its origin may be lost in later reuse. The dataset can then treat a generated image as a recorded examination without evidence that the examination took place.

We call such an image synthetic evidence: a generated image that is offered as evidence of a real examination without the support that this claim requires. The definition builds on earlier work on ground-truth drift and evidence grounding [1, 19]. Whether an image counts as synthetic evidence depends on the image, the claim made about it, and its intended use, because a generated image is not fraudulent in itself. Figure 1(a) shows an example from our study. This generated SynthCheX image passes our automated quality check, and our origin classifier does not flag it as generated. If it is submitted as a clinical acquisition, nothing supports that claim. If it is submitted as declared augmentation, the same pixels are acceptable.

The example shows that three questions must be kept apart. Quality: does the image meet the receiver’s technical requirements? Predicted origin: does an image classifier label it as genuine or generated? Claim support: does the accompanying record support the claim that the image came from the stated acquisition, and is the declared use allowed? A pass on one question does not answer the others. An image can pass the first two, and the third stays open.

Earlier work measures the fidelity of generated radiographs, detects generated or edited medical images, and shows that compression and resizing can change detector behavior (Section 2). Can a submitter who chooses which generated images to send pass both checks more often than one who sends random images? In a controlled laboratory test, a submitter uses Qwen to select up to 20 images from pools of existing generated images, and a receiver applies a MedGemma quality check and an origin classifier built on DINOv3 features. A separate registry records the source and intended use. We find the following.

• 

Selection. Selecting by Qwen’s quality score increases the number of images that pass the quality check, but we find no evidence that it increases the number that pass both the quality and origin checks.

• 

Processing. Image processing (JPEG compression and resizing) causes two separate problems for the origin classifier. Score shifts can invalidate a fixed threshold, which we call threshold drift. The classifier’s ranking can also deteriorate even after the threshold is reset, which we call ranking loss.

• 

Source shift. With the NIH-calibrated threshold unchanged, the primary classifier flags 568 of 957 genuine Open-I images.

Our contributions are (1) an intake test that keeps quality, predicted origin, and claim support separate, (2) measurements of how selection, processing, and a new genuine collection affect the quality and origin checks, and (3) a small registry experiment that records source and intended use apart from the pixels.

## 2 Related work

## Generated radiographs.

Cheff generates high-resolution chest radiographs with latent diffusion [27]. CheXGenBench measures their fidelity, privacy, and training utility [9], and its earlier version and release card describe the SynthCheX images we use [8, 18]. Prior reader studies ask whether radiologists can distinguish generated radiographs from real ones [25, 24]. We instead study an automated intake process with separate quality and origin checks.

## Detecting generated and edited images.

CT-GAN inserts and removes lesions in CT scans [15]. DSKI/MedForensics provides a medical deepfake benchmark and detector [13], and MedForge detects localized edits [4]. MedForge-RSI reports rejected authentic images, failed transfer, and transmission distortions, and it adds an adaptation method [3]. Detectors can learn shortcuts based on compression and image size [11], and WaRPAD scores images by how they respond to high-frequency perturbation and rescaling [5]. We do not propose a detector. We ask how such shortcuts interact with quality-based selection and threshold calibration.

## Calibration and provenance.

Conformal calibration uses genuine images to set a threshold whose false-alarm rate is controlled when the genuine images are exchangeable and the score is fixed in advance [2]. Such a threshold says how unusual a score is among genuine images, not whether an image was acquired. Signatures for medical images [22], C2PA content credentials (signed records of a file’s history) [6], and the PROV data model [16] record the integrity and history of a file, not whether its content supports a factual claim. Our laboratory registry (Section 6) stores signed records and an explicit unknown state, and it adds no new cryptography.

## 3 Task and threat model

We model how a research dataset receives images (Fig. 2). A submission contains an image, a claim about where it came from, and a declared purpose. The claim we test is that the image records a clinical acquisition. The submitted images carry no source record, so their claim support is missing by design (Section 6). Collection labels tell us which images are NIH and which are generated. They are our ground truth, but they do not certify any clinical history.

The experiment starts from fixed pools of existing generated images. Using only Qwen judgments, the submitter selects at most $K$ (Section 4.6) candidates that it expects to pass both receiver checks. The submitter has no access to MedGemma judgments, DINOv3-based origin scores, MedForge-Reasoner results, or receiver outcomes. The selected lists are fixed before the receiver applies the MedGemma quality check and the DINOv3-based origin check. A separate registry then checks the claimed source and declared purpose. This is a fixed, offline selection experiment over existing generated images, without receiver feedback.

An image passes the quality check if MedGemma judges it technically adequate (Section 4.5). The origin check flags an image when the origin classifier labels it as generated (Section 4.3). A flagged generated image is rejected, and a flagged genuine image is a false alarm. An image passes the origin check if it is not flagged, and it passes both checks if it also passes the quality check. If a model reply cannot be read or an image cannot be decoded, the outcome is unavailable, which is neither a pass nor a flag. A pass on both checks leaves the acquisition claim unresolved. Only the registry (Section 6) compares the claim with a source record.

Genuine images come from the ChestX-ray14 part of MaCheX [26, 23], which we call NIH. We keep its partitions and use one image per patient. Fit, development, and calibration patients are disjoint and come from the train/validation partition. The test split holds the 2,297 test-partition patients who remain after we remove the 500 pilot-study patients.

The Cheff-identified collection holds 3,000 legacy images identified as Cheff outputs [27]. Their checkpoint, seeds, and conditioning are unknown, so we treat the collection as observed images, not as the output of a known sampler. Fitting uses the 1,250 images from the pilot study plus 250 more, and development and testing use 500 and 1,000 unused images. SynthCheX is a fixed release of 75,649 images made with Sana 0.6B [8, 18]. We draw 6,500 of them at random for the splits and keep the 500 pilot-study images out of the test split.

To test a new genuine collection, we add 957 frontal Open-I images, grouped by radiology report [7]. We use them only for testing, with the NIH threshold unchanged. Report groups do not establish unique patients. We record each NIH image’s projection, anteroposterior (AP) or posteroanterior (PA), only for observational comparison.

## 4.2 Models and their roles

The models have distinct roles. Qwen ranks candidate images for the submitter. MedGemma applies the receiver’s quality check. DINOv3 and MedGemma’s vision encoder supply image features for the origin classifier. MedForge-Reasoner is used only in a separate transfer comparison.

Qwen is the unquantized Qwen3.8-27B checkpoint in BF16 (Qwen/Qwen3.8-27B) [17]. MedGemma is MedGemma 1.5 4B instruction-tuned (google/medgemma-1.5-4b-it) [20, 10]. DINOv3 ViT-7B/16 (facebook/dinov3-vit7b16-pretrain-lvd1689m) [21] was trained on web images. We use its CLS feature (the output of the token that summarizes the image) or the mean of its patch features. The medical representation is the mean of the 4,096 patch features of MedGemma’s SigLIP vision encoder, and the head on it is the medical head. MedForge-Reasoner (RichardChenZH/MedForge-Reasoner) [14, 3] answers directly whether an image is genuine or fake. It was trained to detect localized edits and belongs to the Qwen family.

## 4.3 Origin classifier and threshold

We train the main origin classifier on 1,500 genuine NIH images and 1,500 generated Cheff-identified images. DINOv3 extracts a feature vector for each image, and its weights stay frozen. We fit an $L_{2}$ -regularized logistic-regression head to these features. We choose the inverse regularization strength $C\in\{0.01,0.1,1,10\}$ that rejects the most generated development images at a 5% false-alarm rate on genuine development images, and ties go to the smaller $C$ . The head never sees SynthCheX, so SynthCheX tests transfer to a second generator. We call this head the raw CLS head. Here “raw” does not mean unprocessed pixels. It means that the head was fitted without the processing augmentation used by the augmented head (Section 4.4).

After choosing the head, we calibrate its decision threshold on a disjoint set of 3,000 genuine NIH images. The primary threshold has a nominal (target) false-alarm rate of 5%, the share of genuine images that the check flags. Let $s(x)$ be the classifier’s score for image $x$ , where a higher score means more generated, and let $s_{1},\ldots,s_{n}$ be the scores of the $n$ calibration images. The conformal p-value [2] is

 $p_{\rm real}(x)=\frac{1+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf{1}\{s_{i}\geq s(x)\}}{n+1}.$ 

(1)

The indicator $\mathbf{1}\{\cdot\}$ equals 1 when its condition holds and 0 otherwise, so the sum counts the calibration images that score at least as high as $x$ , and the extra 1 counts $x$ itself. Thus $p_{\rm real}(x)$ is about the share of genuine calibration images that look at least as suspicious as $x$ . It is not the probability that $x$ is real. The receiver flags $x$ as generated when $p_{\rm real}(x)\leq\alpha$ , the target false-alarm rate. We use $\alpha=0.05$ , and $\alpha=0.01$ and $0.10$ in the supplement. A score equal to the threshold is not flagged.

On genuine test images, the false-alarm rate is at most $\alpha$ on average if two assumptions hold. The genuine calibration and test images must be exchangeable, which means that any ordering of them is equally likely. The score must also be fixed without using the calibration images. This guarantee covers the origin check alone. It does not cover the combined quality-and-origin rule, a different collection, unavailable quality judgments, or the probability that an acquisition claim is true, and it need not hold after a processing shift.

We use two more reference sets only for secondary checks. One holds 2,914 images for the pilot head, after we remove the 86 images that the head saw during fitting or selection. The other holds 2,930 valid replies for a probe (a small classifier) that uses the eight numeric fields of the alternative quality rubric (Section 4.5), after we exclude 10 images used in rubric development and 60 images without a valid reply. We also compare the learned scores with a baseline on six pixel statistics, and with secondary heads fitted on one generator or on both (Supplement S6).

## 4.4 Processing experiment

We next test whether common image processing changes the origin classifier’s scores or its ability to separate genuine and generated images. The canonical condition converts each image to luminance, resizes it to 256 pixels with aspect-preserving Lanczos resampling, pads it with black to a square, and copies it into RGB, without histogram equalization. The receiver applies 12 further conditions: JPEG (quality 95, 85, 75, or 50), down- and up-sampling (through 192, 128, or 64 pixels), direct processing at 512 pixels from the original file (native 512), a 4-pixel border mask, 4-pixel padding, a 64-pixel central mask, and a severe 26-pixel border mask. Cheff-identified images are stored at 256 pixels, so native 512 only upscales them, and DINOv3 still receives 224-pixel input under every condition.

We process 1,000 test images per collection under all 13 conditions, so the 13 views of an image are related. We compare three settings. (1) The raw head with the canonical threshold, calibrated on canonical genuine images only. (2) The raw head with a matched threshold, recalibrated on genuine calibration images processed in the same way as the test images. (3) The augmented head with a matched threshold, fitted on five processed views of each training image (Supplement S6).

These settings separate two failures. In threshold drift, processing moves the scores, so the old threshold no longer gives the intended 5% false-alarm rate. In ranking loss, genuine and generated scores overlap more, so no new threshold can restore the original separation. Setting (2) resets the threshold and so isolates ranking loss from threshold drift. Setting (3) tests whether training on processed images reduces ranking loss. Before testing, we fixed a target for the augmented head: reject at least 5 points more Cheff-identified images than the raw head, with at most 1 point more genuine flags, averaged over the 13 conditions.

## 4.5 Quality check

A rubric is the set of scoring instructions that a model follows. The receiver’s quality check uses MedGemma with an alternative to the original rubric (below), chosen once on development data (Supplement S5). A response passes only if it identifies an assessable chest image, assigns visibility of at least 3 out of 4, assigns a non-null artifact severity of at most 1 out of 4, and passes the flat-image guard. The guard fails images whose grayscale range is at most 1 and whose standard deviation is at most 0.5 on the 0–255 scale.

The submitter does not use this rubric. Qwen rates each candidate with the original rubric, which scores technical quality $T$ , anatomical plausibility $A$ , and artifact severity $V$ , each from 0 to 4. High $T$ and $A$ are good, and high $V$ is bad. The combined score is

 $Q=10T+10A+5(4-V),$ 

(2)

which ranges from 0 to 100. The term $4-V$ makes a lower artifact severity give a higher score. We do not convert the alternative rubric’s fields to the 100-point scale. The receiver’s check thus differs from the submitter’s rating in both the model and the rubric.

## 4.6 Candidate selection

The selection experiment asks whether access to more generated candidates helps the submitter find a small set that passes the receiver’s checks. The submitter receives a pool of $B$ generated images, the candidate budget, and may submit at most $K=20$ of them, the submission limit. The pools are nested subsets of the generated test images, with $B=100$ , 250, 500, and 1,000 for each generator, and $B=5{,}000$ for SynthCheX only. Each strategy gives one list of at most $K$ images per pool.

We compare three strategies, which use only Qwen’s outputs. Uniform selection picks $K$ images at random. Quality selection picks the $K$ images with the highest $Q$ and ranks unavailable scores last. Quality-constrained surrogate selection uses the surrogate rating, Qwen’s 0–10 judgment of how much an image looks like a real acquisition. It stands in for the origin check, which the submitter cannot query. This strategy keeps candidates with an assessable $Q\geq 75$ and an available, assessable surrogate rating, then ranks them by that rating and then by $Q$ . If fewer than $K$ images qualify, the surrogate strategy submits fewer. A secondary pairwise method breaks ties among top-scoring candidates and applies only at $B=100$ (Supplement S7).

## Statistics.

Rates use all attempted images as the denominator, so unavailable outputs are never dropped, and intervals are Wilson 95% intervals. The area under the ROC curve (AUROC) is the probability that a random generated image scores higher than a random genuine image. Paired comparisons use 2,000 bootstrap resamples of test images, with all views of an image kept together. Views, pool draws, and claim variants are not independent images, and intervals condition on the fixed models and thresholds (Supplement S9).

## 5 Results

## 5.1 Quality selection increases quality passes

At $B=1{,}000$ and $K=20$ , quality selection produces 19 quality passes among the 20 Cheff-identified submissions, compared with 12 under uniform selection. For SynthCheX, the corresponding counts are 17 and 8. Images that Qwen ranks highest thus pass MedGemma’s check more often than random images do, although MedGemma is a different model with a different rubric. Qwen therefore identifies candidates that MedGemma is more likely to rate as technically acceptable. This result concerns the quality check alone; Section 5.3 evaluates predicted origin, and Section 5.4 combines the two checks.

The quality check passes 1,717 of 2,297 NIH images. It passes 1,717 of 2,297 NIH images (74.7%; 95% interval 72.9–76.5%), 668 of 1,000 Cheff-identified images (66.8%; 63.8–69.6%), and 2,656 of 5,000 SynthCheX images (53.1%; 51.7–54.5%). The NIH failures include 78 images without a valid reply. NIH pass rates also differ by projection: 47.5% for AP images (257 of 541) and 83.1% for PA images (1,460 of 1,756). We observed this difference but did not test whether projection causes it.

## 5.2 Held-out evaluation of the quality rubric

The selection result depends on the quality check, so we test the check itself. We compare the alternative rubric with the original rubric on 100 held-out images per collection, each degraded in six ways (blur, resampling, and noise at two levels each) that were not used in development. The test also holds 60 distinct invalid inputs that are not chest images, such as flat fields and noise.

The alternative rubric passes none of the 60 invalid inputs even without the flat-image guard, and the original rubric passes 5. With the guard, both pass none (Wilson 95% interval 0–6.02%).

The alternative rubric also has costs. For 3 of 100 clean NIH images, it returns no usable chest-image judgment, against 0 of 100 for the original rubric. It rates the undegraded image above its degraded copy in 650 of 1,800 pairs, against 582 of 1,800, and most other pairs are ties. This gain of 3.8 percentage points has a 95% interval of $[-1.2,+8.8]$ , which includes zero. It also gives more reversals, where the degraded copy is rated higher (426 against 228), and more unavailable pairs (41 against 2). On SynthCheX images, it rates the undegraded image higher in 142 of 600 pairs, against 209.

Because the alternative rubric was selected and frozen before the held-out test, we retain it in the main analysis as prespecified. The held-out results are mixed and do not establish that it is a better overall measure of image quality.

## 5.3 Origin-screen results

On canonical test images, the primary origin classifier (the raw CLS head) rejects nearly all generated images while flagging a smaller fraction of the NIH images as generated. At the nominal 5% level, it flags 110 of 2,297 NIH images (4.8%). It rejects 1,000 of 1,000 Cheff-identified images and 4,677 of 5,000 SynthCheX images (Table 2). The medical head has a higher full-range SynthCheX AUROC, 0.9891 compared with 0.9849, although the low-false-positive comparison does not show a clear advantage. The rubric-field probe separates the collections poorly, while a classifier on six pixel statistics still separates them substantially.

## 5.4 Selection and combined passage

Figure 3: SynthCheX images that pass both checks, with $K=20$ : (a) per submitted image and (b) per candidate in the pool of size $B$ . The vertical axis calls them “quality-qualified false passes”. Solid bars are Wilson 95% intervals for the fixed lists. Dashed bars come from 2,000 resamples that repeat the selection ( $B=1{,}000$ and $5{,}000$ only).

We find no evidence that selection helps. At $B=1{,}000$ and $K=20$ , no Cheff-identified image passes both checks under any strategy (0 of 20; Wilson 95% upper bound 16.1%). This follows from Table 2: the origin classifier flags all 1,000 Cheff-identified test images, so no selection from them can pass. For SynthCheX, uniform, quality, and quality-constrained surrogate selection give 2, 1, and 2 of 20 images that pass both checks. Compared with uniform selection, the surrogate did not increase the number of images that passed both checks. Compared with quality selection, it gives $+5$ percentage points per submitted image and 0.001 more passes per candidate, and both 95% intervals ( $[-5,+10]$ and $[-0.001,0.002]$ , from 2,000 resamples that repeat the selection) include zero. At $B=5{,}000$ , quality and surrogate selection both give 1 of 20. Figure 3 shows the results across all budgets. Successful combined passes are rare, and the intervals overlap. The experiment therefore does not resolve small differences among these three Qwen-based selection strategies, and it does not evaluate other attacks.

## 5.5 Effects of image processing

Figure 4: Percent of the 1,000 test images per collection (“bases”) rejected (a, b) or flagged (c) under 13 conditions, at the nominal 5% level. “Cut” means decision threshold. Raw/canonical: canonical threshold. Raw/matched: threshold recalibrated per condition. Augmented/matched: augmented head, matched threshold.

Figure 4 shows both problems from Section 4.4. Threshold drift appears first. Under JPEG 75, the raw head with the canonical threshold flags 820 of 1,000 NIH images and rejects 987 of 1,000 SynthCheX images. The high rejection rate is not a success, because the head now flags most genuine images too. Ranking loss appears in the AUROC. On the same 1,000 images per collection, the raw head’s SynthCheX AUROC falls from 0.987 in the canonical condition to 0.841 under JPEG 75 and 0.828 after resizing through 128 pixels.

Recalibration repairs much of the threshold drift but does not restore the lost ranking. With a matched JPEG 75 threshold, the raw head flags 65 of 1,000 NIH images, but it rejects only 486 of 1,000 SynthCheX images. Under resize 128, the canonical threshold flags 807 NIH images and rejects 978 SynthCheX images, and the matched threshold flags 117 and rejects 595. Averaged equally over the 13 conditions, matching the threshold lowers NIH flags by 34.01 percentage points and SynthCheX rejections by 19.45 points (Supplement S10). Recalibration thus trades fewer genuine-image flags for fewer generated-image rejections.

The augmented head meets the limit on genuine flags but narrowly misses the Cheff target. With matched thresholds on both sides, it rejects 4.97 percentage points more Cheff-identified images than the raw head, averaged over the 13 conditions (paired 95% interval 4.45–5.51), and it flags 1.32 points fewer NIH images ( $[-1.96,-0.67]$ ). The Cheff gain is just below the 5-point target. The interval includes 5, but that does not mean the target was met. For SynthCheX, which was not used in fitting, the gain is 15.45 points (14.47–16.51), a separate test of transfer to a second generator.

## 5.6 Cross Dataset Performance

A detector calibrated on one genuine collection may flag images from another genuine collection even when no generated image is present. We test this with 957 Open-I images and the unchanged NIH threshold. The raw CLS head flags 568 of 957 images (59.4%; 95% interval 56.2–62.4%), and the medical head flags 152 of 957 (15.9%; 13.7–18.3%). Both rates are above the nominal 5%. Open-I has no generated images, so we report no AUROC or rejection rate for it.

To test whether a detector of localized edits transfers, we ran MedForge-Reasoner. It labels 178 of 300 genuine NIH images as fake (59.3%; 53.7–64.7%) and 127 of 300 Cheff-identified and 50 of 300 SynthCheX images as genuine. It gives a categorical verdict and has no calibrated 5% threshold, so we report no AUROC and make no matched comparison.

## 6 Source, purpose, and reuse

Pixel checks answer whether an image appears technically adequate and whether the classifier flags it as generated. They do not establish the image’s source or whether it supports the claimed acquisition. We therefore keep source and intended use in a separate registry. The same pixels can be allowed as declared augmentation while remaining unsupported as evidence of clinical acquisition. Adding pixel checks cannot reconstruct missing history; missing or unknown verification remains unknown. We test this separation in a small controlled experiment.

Our registry stores the quality result, the origin decision, and the claim-support state in separate fields. Paired cases use identical pixels. They differ only in the claimed purpose (declared augmentation or clinical acquisition) or in whether a trusted source record exists. The design has 8 selected base images, 24 claim-and-purpose variants, and 72 policy replays (each variant under three policies). The variants reuse the same images, so they are not independent radiographs, and the counts are not population estimates.

A registry-only policy already rejects unsupported generated acquisition claims without any pixel check. Adding the pixel checks cuts the supported genuine acquisitions that the policy accepts from 4 of 4 to 2 of 4, while declared augmentation stays allowed. We also tested whether missing verification stays unknown after export and import. A permissive importer that trusts claims accepts unsupported records whether the verification field is kept or removed. The safe importer rehashes the received bytes and keeps missing or invalid verification as unknown. The registry holds laboratory records only. It is not a hospital system, it does not implement C2PA, and it does not certify any clinical history.

## 7 Limitations

## Data provenance and independence.

The Cheff-identified collection is a legacy set whose checkpoint, seeds, conditioning, and generation attempts are unknown, so we draw no conclusion about the Cheff model itself. HealthGPT, which has Qwen-based variants [12], filtered SynthCheX before release, and the checkpoint that filtered it is unspecified. We therefore cannot verify that the filter is independent of the Qwen family, and we do not claim that Qwen is independent of the data. Generated images may share conditioning despite our duplicate check, so we claim nothing about memorization. NIH fit and test images come from different patients but one collection. We cannot check which images DINOv3 saw in pretraining, MedGemma’s medical pretraining may include public collections, and Open-I report groups do not establish unique patients. We therefore claim neither patient uniqueness nor freedom from pretraining exposure.

## Assessors and classifiers.

Qwen and MedGemma are automated models, and no clinician judged the images. A valid reply is not necessarily a correct assessment, so a quality pass does not mean clinical quality. The medical head uses MedGemma’s vision encoder, and MedForge-Reasoner belongs to the Qwen family, so these models are not fully independent. The origin classifiers separate collections and may rely on shortcuts in acquisition or processing, so we do not call them validated forgery detectors. Their false-alarm rates can differ from the nominal ones after a change of collection or processing.

## Processing and reproduction.

The masks change geometry, intensity, and frequency content together, so the processing results do not identify a single cause such as text. The selection reports for the primary raw DINOv3 CLS and mean heads refer to an earlier metadata file whose exact bytes we could not recover. We verified the checkpoints and the identities of the fit, development, and calibration images, but we cannot rebuild these historical inputs byte for byte. We also did not measure clinical deception, downstream harm, or the precision of a deployed system.

## 8 Conclusion

We asked three questions about a generated chest radiograph submitted as the record of a real examination. Quality-based selection finds more images that pass the MedGemma quality check, but we find no evidence that it increases the number passing both receiver checks. On canonical images, the origin classifier rejects all Cheff-identified images and 93.5% of SynthCheX images while flagging 4.8% of NIH images. Processing can shift the threshold and reduce ranking quality. Matched recalibration lowers many genuine-image flags, but it also allows more generated images through and does not always recover the nominal rate. Genuine images from another dataset are also flagged frequently. Passing both pixel checks therefore does not by itself support the claimed acquisition. Thus, we strongly suggest that the source and intended use of each image must be recorded and checked separately.

## How to read the tables.

These conventions apply to every table unless a caption says otherwise.

• 

Denominators. Rates use all attempted images: 2,297 NIH, 1,000 Cheff-identified, and 5,000 SynthCheX test images.

• 

Unavailable outputs. An output is unavailable (U) when a model reply cannot be read or an image cannot be processed. It is not a pass, a flag, or a detection, and it stays in the denominator.

• 

Intervals. Rates have Wilson 95% intervals. AUROC values and paired differences have bootstrap intervals from 2,000 resamples of test images. All intervals condition on the fixed models and thresholds (Section S9).

• 

Thresholds. A threshold comes from a nominal false-alarm rate (1%, 5%, or 10%) on genuine calibration images. There are 3,000 calibration images, except for the pilot head (2,914) and the rubric-field probe (2,930 valid replies).

• 

Reused images. The rows of one table often reuse the same images, so they are not independent samples.

• 

Labels. Tables use the head names defined in Section S2. In the tables of secondary heads, the word after the slash names the generators used to fit the head.

• 

Abbreviations. N, C, and S stand for NIH, Cheff-identified, and SynthCheX. U stands for unavailable, CI for confidence interval, and “k/n” for k of n. In table rows, “alt.” and “orig.” mean the alternative and the original rubric, and “N” in a column header can also be a count.

• 

Counting units. A count refers to images unless the caption names another unit, such as a view, an exposure, a pattern, a pair, a chain, an attempt, or a task.

## S2 Shared definitions and notation

## Collections.

NIH images are genuine chest radiographs from the ChestX-ray14 part of MaCheX. Cheff-identified images are 3,000 legacy images identified as outputs of the Cheff generator, whose checkpoint and conditioning are unresolved. SynthCheX images are generated images from a fixed release made with Sana 0.6B. Open-I images are genuine frontal radiographs from the Indiana collection, used only for testing. We call each of these image sets a collection. We use “source” only for the claimed source of a submission in the registry. The conditioning of a generated image is the input, such as a text prompt or label, that the generator received.

## Models.

Qwen (Qwen3.8-27B) is the submitter’s rater: it scores candidate images. MedGemma (MedGemma 1.5 4B) applies the receiver’s quality check, and its vision encoder supplies the medical features. DINOv3 (ViT-7B/16) supplies general image features. MedForge-Reasoner is used only in a separate transfer comparison. Section S4 gives the checkpoints.

## Roles.

The submitter chooses generated images to submit. It is the attacker in the threat model, and it sees only Qwen’s judgments. The receiver applies the quality check and the origin check with fixed models and thresholds. A registry checks the claimed source and the declared purpose of each submission.

## Checks and outcomes.

The quality check is the primary MedGemma check (Section S5). The origin classifier is a logistic-regression head on frozen image features, and the origin check is that classifier plus a threshold. The origin score $s(x)$ of an image $x$ is the head’s output for $x$ , and a higher score means that the image looks more generated (some figures call it the suspicion score). The check flags an image as generated when its score is above the threshold. For a generated image, a flag is a correct rejection. For a genuine image, a flag is a false alarm. The false-alarm rate is the share of genuine images that the check flags. An image passes both checks when it passes the quality check and is not flagged.

## Processing.

The canonical condition converts each image to luminance, resizes it to 256 pixels with aspect-preserving Lanczos resampling, pads it with black to a square, and copies it into RGB, without histogram equalization. Section S8 lists the 12 other conditions.

## Heads and thresholds.

The raw head is fitted without the processing augmentation, and the augmented head is fitted on five processed views of each image (Section S6). The pilot head was fitted in an earlier pilot study of this task, and we use it unchanged. The rubric-field probe is a head on the eight numeric or indicator fields of the alternative quality rubric, and the pixel-statistic classifier is a head on six pixel statistics. The primary head is the raw DINOv3 CLS head fitted on NIH and Cheff-identified images. The seven core heads are the pilot head, the raw DINOv3 CLS (primary), DINOv3 mean patch, and medical mean heads, the augmented CLS head, the rubric-field probe, and the pixel-statistic classifier. Heads are named by their features: DINOv3 CLS, DINOv3 mean patch, or medical mean (MedGemma’s vision encoder).

## Secondary heads and thresholds.

The secondary heads add the generators used in fitting after a slash, so “CLS / Cheff” is the DINOv3 CLS head fitted on Cheff-identified images, and “Mixed” means both generators. The canonical threshold is calibrated on canonical genuine images. A matched threshold is calibrated on genuine images processed in the same way as the test images. The global threshold is calibrated on a mixture of five conditions (Section S8). The nominal false-alarm rate is $\alpha\in\{0.01,0.05,0.10\}$ , and the main paper uses $\alpha=0.05$ .

## Quality rubrics.

The original rubric scores technical quality $T$ , anatomical plausibility $A$ , and artifact severity $V$ , each from 0 to 4. Qwen and MedGemma combine them as $Q=10T+10A+5(4-V)$ . The alternative rubric records chest recognition, assessability, visibility from 0 to 4, structural artifact severity from 0 to 4 (or null), and an uncertainty level (low, moderate, or high). We never convert its scores to the 100-point scale. A reply is valid when it parses as JSON that matches the schema (Section S5).

## Selection.

The candidate budget $B$ is the pool size, and the submission limit $K$ is the largest number of images that the submitter may send. Uniform, quality, and quality-constrained surrogate selection are defined in Section S7. The surrogate rating is Qwen’s 0 to 10 judgment of how much an image looks like a real acquisition.

## Splits and statistics.

The fit, development, calibration, and test splits train the head, choose its settings, set the threshold, and give the reported results (Section S3). AUROC is the probability that a random generated image scores higher than a random genuine image. The partial AUC covers false-positive rates up to 0.10, and the tables report its standardized form (Section S9).

## S3 Dataset and split construction

Table 1 of the main paper lists the images selected for each split. This section explains how we built the splits and what we checked.

## NIH images.

The NIH zip file lists 112,120 distinct PNG images and eight metadata files. We counted them from the file index, and we decoded only the selected images. The official train/validation and test lists contain 86,524 and 25,596 images, and they share no patients. Each list has one more image than the expected counts (86,523 and 25,595), and we found no documented explanation for the two extra images. We use at most one image per patient. The test partition contains 2,797 patients. After we remove the 500 pilot patients, 2,297 remain, and the test split uses all of them. We accepted this smaller test split rather than reach a target size with repeated images of one patient.

## Cheff-identified images.

The legacy listing contains 8,672 PNG entries and 27 CSV files in several folders, which differ from the 3,000-image pool that we selected. We did not choose images by their old checker scores. Some score rows were copied or did not match their images, so the legacy files do not show that the generation runs were independent. The generation checkpoint, the conditioning groups, and the completeness of the raw attempts remain unresolved. We reassign all 1,250 images used in the pilot study, including the old test images, to fitting. We add 250 unused images to fitting, and we use 500 unused images for development and 1,000 for testing.

## SynthCheX images.

SynthCheX sampling uses a pinned release of 75,649 rows. It is not a reconstruction of all generation attempts. We reserve 6,500 rows uniformly at random, keep the original release identifiers, and exclude the 500 pilot rows from the test split. HealthGPT filtered the release before our experiment. Documented HealthGPT variants include Qwen-based models [12], but the checkpoint that filtered the release and the conditioning mapping are unspecified, so independence from the Qwen family is unverified. The release rows expose filenames, findings, and pixels, but no report, patient, or study conditioning identifiers. We checked for exact duplicates and for groups of images that share a generator input, where we could recover the groups. We report unknown conditioning as unknown instead of asserting independence.

## Open-I images.

The Open-I panel has 957 frontal report groups. We selected them without any threshold fitting. Demner-Fushman et al. describe the original Indiana radiograph and report collection [7], and the local count comes from our own audit of the selected files. Separate report groups do not establish unique patients, and projection and pretraining exposure remain uncertain. We do not redistribute any Open-I image in the figures or the source bundle. The panel tests a change of genuine collection with the NIH calibration unchanged. It is not a clinical validation.

## Checks on the selected images.

For each of the 17,297 selected images (the sum of the four splits), we stored hashes of both the original and the canonical version (bytes and pixels). Before inference we checked for exact duplicates across splits and for recoverable groups that share a generator input. We also ran a low-resolution comparison of image pairs as an extra duplicate check, but this cannot prove that a generator did not memorize images or that conditioning is not shared. We record whether each retrieved row came from a downloaded parquet file or was fetched individually through the API. The machine-readable reproducibility files hold the full inventories, checksums, and identifiers.

## Fixed identifiers and withheld labels.

We fixed all reserved identifiers before retrieval. Missing or corrupt assets keep entries in the attempt record, and we never replace them with a more convenient image. Models receive only pixels and opaque identifiers. Test-split collection labels and outcomes were withheld from method development, while the permitted fit and development labels support supervised fitting.

## Observational strata.

The recorded NIH projection (AP or PA) and the report-derived findings are observational strata. They are not clinical truth and not model inputs. Rates standardized over projection use fixed development weights of 0.178 for AP and 0.822 for PA, and they never use projection metadata supplied by a submitter.

## S4.1 Image features

DINOv3 uses the official web-image ViT-7B/16 revision with frozen BF16 parameters and the official 224-pixel processor. Its image feature is either the CLS token or the mean of the patch tokens, and the mean excludes the CLS and register tokens. The medical representation comes from the standalone official MedGemma vision tower. We average the features of its 4,096 image patches in float32 before the multimodal projector and decoder, and its processor stays at 896 pixels. Values cached by hooks during quality generation are never substituted for these standalone embeddings, because a development audit found numerical disagreement between the two even though image routing had been tested. Each stored feature vector is linked to the exact pixels it came from.

## S4.2 Decoding settings

Qwen uses non-thinking greedy decoding with a 384-token ceiling, and MedGemma uses greedy decoding with a 1,024-token ceiling. We kept Qwen’s non-thinking setting after both 96-image development rubrics produced valid final replies with bounded token use, and after we confirmed the exact bindings of the processor, vision tower, and decoder. Together these confirm that the model processes the image, but they do not show clinical or causal grounding.

## S4.3 MedForge-Reasoner protocol

We use the official RichardChenZH/MedForge-Reasoner checkpoint, pinned to revision a864eed9b76cde46f0e154c3497d646550995c87 [14, 3]. It is a Qwen-family model trained for localized manipulations of medical images, so whole-image generated chest radiographs test transfer and do not reproduce its benchmark. The protocol uses its exact system and user prompts (below), RGB resizing of the canonical inputs to $1024\times 1024$ as in the official example, frozen BF16 weights, batch size one, greedy decoding, and a 2,048-token cap. The resizing upsamples canonical pixels without recovering native detail.

A four-image development benchmark ran on GPUs only and took 56.998 seconds including loading, of which 35.414 seconds were generation, with no token caps. From this runtime we fixed the final scope: the prespecified 300 canonical images per collection (900 in total), plus the unique images in the fixed Qwen submissions that are not in that panel (at most 580). Images that appear only in repeated pools do not expand the scope. Submissions that overlap the panel reuse the same attempt, including failures. These scope choices rest on runtime, not on performance.

Only a unique, explicit final verdict of real, authentic, deepfake, fake, forged, or manipulated maps to a category. An unclosed reasoning block, contradictory categories, generation caps without an end-of-sequence token, and missing verdicts are unavailable. A categorical verdict defines its own operating point. We do not turn a rationale or a self-reported confidence into a probability, and we report no calibrated 1%, 5%, or 10% curve. Section S10.4 reports the results.

## S5 Quality prompts and response parsing

## S5.1 How the prompts are sent

Qwen (the submitter’s rater) and MedGemma (the receiver’s quality checker) receive the supplied image, or the ordered image pair, followed by the task text in a user message. The official processor and chat template supply the model’s own formatting. No filename, pathology label, report, claimed origin, or source metadata enters a query. Native thought text is preserved but never replaces the final JSON. Parse failures and token-cap flags stay in the attempt record, and we never repair a response in a favorable direction.

## S5.4 Reading the replies

A reply is valid when it parses as JSON that matches the schema. A valid reply can still say that the image is unassessable, is not a chest radiograph, or has no artifact severity (null). We keep these cases as separate states. A reply that cannot be parsed and a runtime failure are both unavailable, and token-cap flags stay in the attempt record. An unavailable reply never counts as a pass, and it stays in the denominator.

The original rubric passes a valid, assessable reply when $Q\geq 75$ . The alternative rubric passes a reply that identifies an assessable chest image, gives visibility of at least 3, and gives a non-null artifact severity of at most 1. The flat-image guard rejects images whose grayscale range is at most 1 and whose standard deviation is at most 0.5 on the 0–255 scale. We report quality outcomes both with and without the guard.

## S5.5 Ordering rules for degradation tests

Several tests ask whether an undegraded image receives a higher quality rating than its degraded copy. For the original rubric, the rating is the score $Q$ . For the alternative rubric, we compare visibility first (higher is better) and then artifact severity (lower is better), and equal ratings are ties. We call a pair strict when the undegraded image is rated higher, a tie when the ratings are equal, and a reversal when the degraded copy is rated higher. A pair with an unavailable reply is unavailable. Schema-valid replies that are unassessable, not a chest image, or have a null artifact field are placed at the bottom of the ordering.

## S5.6 Choosing the alternative rubric on development data

The development panel uses 100 fixed pilot or development images per collection under ten conditions: canonical, Gaussian blur at $\sigma=0.75$ , 1.5, and 3, resampling through 192, 128, and 64 pixels, and additive noise with standard deviation 4, 12, and 32 on the 255 scale. It also includes non-image controls: flat fields, independent noise, phase-scrambled images, and checkerboards. The 70 non-image exposures cover only 60 distinct pixel patterns, because some patterns are repeated. The unit for invalid inputs is the distinct pattern, and a pattern counts as passing if any of its repeated exposures passes. We report exposure-level results separately. The panel includes SynthCheX images, but SynthCheX outcomes never enter the fitting or the regularization choice of the primary origin head.

We decided in advance that the alternative rubric was eligible only if it met three conditions.

1. 

It passed at most 10% of the 60 distinct invalid patterns without the flat-image guard.

2. 

The share of the 100 fixed clean NIH images without an assessable chest-image judgment was at most five percentage points higher than under the original rubric. A failure, an unavailable reply, a valid unassessable reply, and a valid non-chest reply each count once.

3. 

The share of the 2,700 expected pairs in which the undegraded image is rated higher was at least five percentage points higher than under the original rubric.

If any condition failed, the original rubric plus the flat-image guard would remain primary. Abstentions among valid replies are reported separately.

The development report selected the alternative rubric plus the flat guard (Fig. S1). Without the guard, it passes 0 of 60 distinct invalid patterns, against 5 of 60 for the original rubric. For clean NIH images, 1 of 100 has no assessable chest-image judgment, against 0 of 100 for the original rubric. The alternative rubric rates the undegraded image higher in 1,132 of 2,700 pairs, against 938 of 2,700. This pooled gain of 7.19 points meets the five-point condition. However, on SynthCheX images the number of such pairs falls from 323 to 277 of 900. The alternative rubric’s replies include 59 failed final replies, one generation that hit the token cap, 97 unavailable ordering pairs, and five failures among ten supplemental exposures. Unavailable pairs never count as successful orderings. Because this was a development choice, it still needed the separate held-out test below.

## S5.7 Held-out validity test and quality under processing

The held-out panel uses 100 final test images per collection, control seeds that differ from those used in development, and degradations not used in development: blur at $\sigma=1$ and 2, resampling through 160 and 96 pixels, and noise at 8 and 24. The canonical view plus six degradations give 2,100 image views, and 70 non-image exposures cover 60 distinct patterns, for 2,170 attempts per rubric. The invalid-pattern rule is the same as in development. All 1,800 pairs of an undegraded image and a degraded copy keep their strict, tied, reversed, and unavailable outcomes. Mild degradation need not change a model’s judgment visibly, so we report complete ladders, ties, and failures.

A second panel tests the quality check under processing. It uses 300 of the reserved processing images per collection, fixed without reference to outcomes, under the canonical, JPEG 75, resize 128, and native 512 (the original file processed at 512 pixels) conditions. These 900 images give 3,600 attempted views per rubric. The 900 canonical views reuse their core assessments, which leaves 2,700 new calls per rubric. The held-out panel likewise reuses 300 canonical assessments and needs 1,870 new calls per rubric for degraded images and non-image controls. Paired differences and unavailable replies keep the image grouping, and the degraded views are not new independent images.

## S6 Origin classifier construction and calibration

## S6.1 Fitting the heads

## Budgets.

The primary head is fitted on 1,500 NIH and 1,500 Cheff-identified images, and its development split holds 1,000 NIH and 500 Cheff-identified images. The secondary Cheff, SynthCheX, and Mixed heads each use a fitting budget of 1,000 genuine and 1,000 generated images, and the Mixed head draws 500 from each generator. Their development budgets hold 1,000 genuine and 500 generated images, with 250 from each generator for the Mixed head. Collection-specific hash salts keep the subsets of each collection nested, so each smaller budget is contained in the larger one. SynthCheX outcomes never enter the fitting or the regularization choice of the primary head.

## Procedure.

Standardization uses statistics of the fitting features only. Logistic regression uses the deterministic LBFGS solver with at most 5,000 iterations and an inverse regularization strength $C\in\{0.01,0.1,1,10\}$ (a smaller $C$ means a stronger $L_{2}$ penalty). We pick the $C$ that rejects the most generated development images at the nominal 5% false-alarm rate on development genuine images, averaged equally over the views used in that fitting mode, and the smaller $C$ wins ties. A head that does not converge counts as a failed stage, not as a result. Because the solver is deterministic, different nominal seeds are not independent random fits.

## S6.2 Threshold calibration

Newly fitted DINOv3, medical, and pixel-statistic heads use the fixed set of 3,000 distinct genuine calibration images, after head selection is complete. The rubric-field probe is the exception (below). As in Equation 1 of the main paper, the receiver computes $p_{\rm real}(x)=(1+\#\{s_{i}\geq s(x)\})/(n+1)$ , where $s_{i}$ are the origin scores of the $n$ calibration images, and flags $x$ when $p_{\rm real}(x)\leq\alpha$ . With $n=3{,}000$ , an image is flagged only if its score is strictly above the threshold for its $\alpha$ level ( $\alpha=0.01$ , 0.05, or 0.10), so a score equal to the threshold is not flagged. We export the actual score thresholds, tie counts, and achieved reference rejection for each head to the reproducibility files. The value $p_{\rm real}(x)$ says how typical the score is among genuine images. It is not the probability that an image was acquired, and it carries no guarantee under an arbitrary change of collection.

## S6.3 Reference sets for the secondary checks

## Pilot head.

The pilot head needs its own exclusion because it was fitted earlier. A metadata audit found 58 reserved calibration patients that its earlier fitting used and 28 that its regularization selection used. We excluded them without looking at any scores. This leaves 2,914 patients that the head has never seen, so $n+1=2{,}915$ . We linked this subset to the saved head and to the old split manifests before the final inference. Neither the saved head nor its original threshold changes. All 3,000 score attempts stay in the record, and we replace no failed or excluded patient. All pilot-head evaluations use the 2,914-patient reference, and the other newly fitted heads use all 3,000 patients, except the rubric-field probe (below).

## Rubric-field probe.

The probe uses eight numeric or indicator fields of the alternative rubric: visibility, artifact severity, chest recognition, assessability, a null-artifact indicator, and three uncertainty indicators. It excludes rationales and source metadata. A null severity is coded as zero plus its indicator. Of the fixed 1,500 NIH and 1,500 Cheff-identified fitting attempts, 1,461 and 1,469 replies are valid, and 976 and 488 of the 1,000 and 500 development attempts are valid. Failed replies are left out of fitting without replacement and are unavailable at prediction. Valid unassessable replies remain numeric observations. All four values of $C$ reach the same development result (5 of 500 attempted Cheff-identified replies rejected), so the smaller- $C$ rule selects $C=0.01$ . Failed replies cannot count as detections.

A metadata audit found ten calibration patients in quality-rubric development: eight with the same canonical image and two with another acquisition. Diagnosis and blank contexts add no overlapping calibration patient. Excluding these ten leaves 2,990 eligible patients. The 3,000 calibration attempts contain 2,940 valid and 60 unavailable replies, and all ten excluded patients have valid replies. The reference thus holds 2,930 valid replies from 2,990 eligible patients, with denominator $n+1=2{,}931$ . The saved report’s valid_n=2930 counts these valid reference replies, not all 2,940 valid attempts. Its 1% and 5% thresholds coincide because of ties. We checked the full records, masks, and reference order by replaying the saved replies. For this probe we claim neither the full 3,000-patient guarantee nor independence from the earlier rubric development.

## Pixel-statistic classifier.

The six statistics are intensity mean, intensity standard deviation, luminance entropy, the high-frequency fraction of spectral power, the ratio of border to interior gradients, and an estimate of JPEG blockiness. They are measured on the input luminance in the range 0 to 1, without any feedback from the origin models. Entropy uses 256 bins. High-frequency energy covers 0.25 to 0.5 cycles per input pixel, and we normalize it by the total non-DC Fourier power, including diagonal frequencies above 0.5. The border measure uses an eight-pixel region, and JPEG blockiness compares gradients at eight-pixel grid boundaries with gradients elsewhere. The 512-pixel condition changes the image scale, so the same pixel counts cover different regions, and neither the frequency band nor the border width is a physical, millimeter-scale quantity. The classifier uses only permitted NIH and Cheff-identified fitting and development data, and it is calibrated on the same genuine images as the other heads. It tests whether the collections differ in simple image properties, so it is not a validated forgery detector.

## S6.4 Augmented head and the response-feature ablation

The augmented head sees five views of both classes: canonical, JPEG 75, resize 128, native 512, and 4-pixel padding. Each image has total weight one across its five views, in standardization and in fitting. The primary comparison (A) sets the raw and augmented CLS heads against each other, each with matched thresholds. We report two further contrasts separately: (B) the full policy change from the raw head with the canonical threshold to the augmented head with matched thresholds, and (C) threshold-only recalibration of the raw head. The development target was at least five percentage points more Cheff-identified images rejected, averaged over the 13 conditions, with at most one point more genuine images flagged.

On the completed 13-condition development comparison, augmentation raised Cheff rejection by 4.62 percentage points (95% paired interval [3.95, 5.26]) and the share of genuine images flagged by 0.12 points ([ $-0.58$ , 0.84]). The gain falls short of the five-point target. The fixed augmentation arm was still evaluated because it was a required prespecified comparison, and this outcome led to no further method search and no tuning on the test split. These intervals use 2,000 paired resamples of development patients, conditional on the fixed genuine references.

The optional response-feature ablation feeds the head $[h(x),h(J(x))-h(x),h(R(x))-h(x)]$ , where $h$ is the frozen feature map, $x$ is the canonical image, and $J(x)$ and $R(x)$ are its JPEG 75 and resize 128 versions computed by the receiver. Its comparator averages the raw head’s probabilities over exactly these three views. Each arm uses its own genuine development threshold. Adoption required at least five points more Cheff-identified images rejected and at most one point more genuine flags. This three-view test does not evaluate a response detector on all 13 conditions. A missing reserved view fails the development stage without replacement. The response features rejected 500 of 500 Cheff-identified development images, against 495 of 500 for the three-view average, and both flagged 49 of 1,000 genuine development images. The gain of one point fails the five-point criterion. We keep the response head and its scores as a development ablation, and we adopted no final response arm.

## S7 Selection procedures

## S7.1 Strategies and denominators

The submitter has cached Qwen quality and surrogate judgments only. Each pool is a nested subset of the generated test images, ordered by a fixed hash, so each smaller pool is contained in the larger ones. Each strategy submits at most $K$ images from a pool. Uniform selection draws $K$ images at random. Quality selection ranks the images by the original quality score $Q$ , highest first, and submits the top $K$ . It places unavailable scores last and breaks ties with a fixed tie hash. Quality-constrained surrogate selection keeps only images with an assessable original score of at least 75 and an available, assessable surrogate rating. It sorts them by that rating, then by the original quality score, then by a fixed tie hash, and submits the top $K$ . When fewer than $K$ images qualify, fewer are submitted, and we report both the denominator of actual submissions and the denominator of the full candidate budget. The 100 repeated pool seeds change pool membership and uniform draws, while the tie hashes stay fixed.

A generated image passes both checks when it passes the frozen MedGemma quality check and the origin check at the declared threshold. The primary origin threshold targets 5%. The quality check adds a separate cost for genuine images, so we do not claim that the combined rule fails only 5% of genuine images. The nested budgets are 100, 250, 500, and 1,000 with $K=20$ , and 5,000 is a secondary budget for SynthCheX only. An empty surrogate submission has an undefined conditional rate and interval, and its passes per candidate are zero. Origin-only passes and the 1% and 10% sensitivity points are reported separately. We fixed the selected submissions, pool orders, query costs, and repeated selections before any join with receiver outcomes. We verified that the full model weights were loaded, that final answers were read from the model’s own output, that each image reached the intended model and batch, that each run held the intended items, and that cached outputs matched their stored hashes. We did not infer any of this from file names or model labels.

Pairwise development compares 24 candidates per collection with four fixed anchors, in both image orders, for 576 sample tasks. The coverage of comparable pairs had to reach 90% overall and in each collection, with 80% consistency after swapping the order among comparable pairs. A failed or unassessable comparison cannot count as consistent. ## S8 Processing conditions

## The 13 conditions.

The receiver applies these conditions: canonical (Section S2); grayscale JPEG at quality 95, 85, 75, and 50, decoded to RGB; Lanczos down- and up-sampling through 192, 128, and 64 pixels; direct processing of the preserved original file at 512 pixels (native 512); a thin 4-pixel border mask; 4-pixel padding; a 64-pixel central mask; and a severe 26-pixel border mask. The masks and the padding act on the 256-pixel canonical image. Padding resizes the content to 248 pixels before it adds a four-pixel frame. The thin border mask alters about 6.15% of the square, the central mask removes 6.25%, and the severe mask removes 36.5%, so the severe mask cannot isolate text. Native 512 starts from the preserved original file instead of the canonical image. Cheff-identified images are stored at 256 pixels, so this condition upscales them, and we do not claim that it recovers lost detail. DINOv3’s processor stays at 224 pixels, and MedGemma’s at 896 pixels, under every condition.

## Threshold policies.

Every condition keeps the same 3,000 genuine calibration attempts and the same 1,000 test images per collection. Table S24 compares four policies: the raw head with the canonical threshold, the raw head with a matched threshold, the augmented head with a matched threshold, and the augmented head with a global threshold. The first three are the comparison in the main paper, and the global threshold is secondary. To set the global threshold, we assign each calibration image to exactly one of five conditions (canonical, JPEG 75, resize 128, native 512, or 4-pixel padding). The assignment uses the SHA256 hash of 20261014|global_route_calibration|base_id, read as a full integer modulo five. The actual counts per condition are 579, 621, 579, 627, and 594, and they need not equal 600. The global threshold has a guarantee only for this mixture of conditions, not for each condition and not for an unknown processing shift.

## Unavailable outputs.

Every test view receives either a finite prediction or an explicit unavailable status. An unavailable output is neither a genuine pass nor a detection. Calibrating a newly fitted head requires all 3,000 calibration scores to be finite. The rubric-field probe uses only valid replies (Section S6).

## S9 Statistical procedures

## Rates and intervals.

Rates are counts over attempted images, including zero counts, and they carry Wilson 95% intervals. A fixed submitted list of 20 images has a Wilson interval that describes that list. It does not include the variability of the selection.

## Bootstrap.

Bootstrap intervals use 2,000 resamples of base images within each collection, and they keep all views and claim variants of one base image together. The whole-procedure interval for selection resamples candidate groups and repeats the selection, so it includes the variability of selection. The 100 repeated pool draws show how sensitive the results are to pool membership. They are a Monte Carlo check on cached scores, not independent datasets. AUROC and the standardized partial AUC up to a false-positive rate of 0.10 use images with a finite score, and the tables state the attempted and unavailable counts. All intervals condition on the fixed fitted models and thresholds, so they do not include the variability of fitting.

## Representation proximity.

We measure how close generated images are to NIH images in each frozen representation with the unbiased maximum mean discrepancy (MMD) and the cubic kernel $(x^{\top}y/d+1)^{3}$ , where $d$ is the feature dimension. The panels hold 500 images per collection, and a disjoint NIH-NIH panel of the same size gives a reference. We keep negative values of the unbiased estimator. The angular sensitivity analysis uses unit vectors scaled by $\sqrt{d}$ and is secondary, and we do not compare magnitudes across encoders. The 100 subsample repetitions show Monte Carlo dispersion, and 2,000 resamples of base images show the uncertainty of the fixed panels. The intervals for the NIH-NIH statistic show spread only. They do not test whether the two NIH panels are equal.

## Nearest neighbors.

Cosine neighbors query a fixed development or test panel against the NIH fit images only, with deterministic ties. This limited reference cannot show that memorization is absent anywhere. Each nearest-neighbor illustration uses one query per collection, chosen by a fixed hash within each 500-query panel. Query selection never uses the distance or the visual appearance. The same query is shown with its nearest fit image in the DINOv3 CLS, DINOv3 mean-patch, and medical mean-patch representations, with its genuine or generated label and its unmodified canonical pixels. The neighbors show association in a representation. They are not a clinical similarity judgment or proof of training memorization.

## Correlations, score shifts, and strata.

Correlations between origin scores and pixel statistics are Spearman correlations within each collection, with 2,000 resamples of base images. We do not pool the collections into a causal explanation. Paired score shifts and rank changes use the same images, and Table S29 gives each head’s canonical interquartile range (IQR) as a scale for the shifts. The thin-border and central masks keep all pixels outside the masked regions. Padding resizes the whole content to 248 pixels before it adds the frame, so it also changes image scale. Rates standardized over projection use the fixed development weights 0.178 and 0.822 and report the support of each stratum. Unknown strata stay explicit. The labels No Finding and recorded finding come from reports, so they are observational strata, not independent labels of normality or quality.

## Independence.

Views of one image, repeated pool draws, claim variants, and repeated heads on the same images are not independent observations. The tables report them so that every denominator is visible.

## S11 Registry experiment

## S11.1 Design

The experiment reuses our laboratory’s source registry and its export and import logic. Each record keeps three separate fields: the quality result, the origin decision, and the claim-support state (whether a trusted source record supports the claim). In paired cases, the pixels are identical. The cases differ only in the purpose and claim (an observation or declared synthetic use) or in whether a trusted record is available. Declared augmentation can pass the quality rule for its purpose without passing the origin check. An unsupported acquisition claim cannot obtain a source record by passing the pixel checks.

The registry-only policy and the combined policy (registry plus pixel checks) receive the same trusted evidence. The registry verifies authenticated source records and records of derived files, each bound to its file by a hash, outside the submitter’s control, and missing support stays unknown. A registration describes release evidence under laboratory assumptions. It does not certify any clinical history, and it does not implement C2PA (a standard for signed content credentials). The safe importer keeps the purpose and the verification state and rehashes the received bytes, so missing verification can never silently become a verified acquisition. The permissive importer, which trusts claims, accepts unsupported rows whether the verification state is kept or dropped, so these tests do not show that dropping a column causes the outcome. Each replay record stores whether the signatures on records and receipts verified, that paired cases have identical pixels, and the results of tests in which bytes or receipts were altered.

## S11.2 Results

The replay uses 8 selected base images and 24 claim-and-purpose variants in total. Each variant is replayed under three policies, which gives 72 replays. The variants are controls on the same images, and the counts are not population estimates. The registry-only policy already rejects unsupported generated observation claims without any pixel check. Adding the pixel requirements lowers the number of supported genuine observations that the policy accepts from four of four to two of four, and declared augmentation stays allowed. The integrity checks and the preservation of purpose are thus supporting controls. They are not an independently learned proof of acquisition. Safe reuse rehashes the received bytes and holds missing or invalid verification as unknown.

## S12 Reproduction and execution records

Sections S3 to S9 describe the final method. This section lists events during the runs that a reviewer may want to know about. For each event, we state what happened and what it affected.

## S12.1 Execution records

## Shared GPU during a development run.

An unrelated process entered one of the GPUs during the completed development stress run of the original rubric. The timing of that run may include contention.

## GPU scheduling.

Job dispatch checks which process owns each GPU and waits for free devices, with 300-second monitoring and safe pauses between owned batches.

## First replies to the alternative rubric.

The first eight replies to the alternative rubric were generated before the corrected check for fresh GPU ownership was in place. We preserved those replies and recorded the shared-hardware event instead of rescoring them. All later loaders enforce a fresh ownership check before model construction. These records describe hardware use. They are not evidence about the validity of the assessor.

## Pilot reference.

Earlier outputs that used all 3,000 calibration patients as the pilot-head reference overlapped the patients that the pilot head saw in fitting and selection. We preserved those outputs as descriptive records only. Every reported pilot-head evaluation uses the 2,914-patient reference (Section S6).

## Development controls.

We repaired the construction of the controls, which added ten distinct checkerboard patterns. The repair changed neither the original 3,060 inputs nor their cached outputs. We also amended how completed failures enter the development decision on the alternative rubric. We made this amendment before anyone inspected the clean-NIH or control results of the alternative rubric, and before all final inference.

## MedForge parsing.

We preserved the original four-image record of strict-parser failures. We then let the parser accept one clear final sentence after a closed reasoning block. Re-parsing the four saved replies gave four categories, with no new model calls and no change to the saved text. Contradictory or missing final verdicts remain unavailable. The parser and tokenizer replay all completed responses and keep the original text and token sequence. We fixed the exact official prompts and the native final-category parser before evaluation.

## MedForge device map.

The checkpoint’s device-map metadata is empty. A read-only adapter supplies a view of that metadata for validation, without altering the original files or the categorical results. We rely on the measured placement of all parameters on the GPU in BF16, not on the empty metadata. The reproducibility records hold the full evidence from the checkpoint and the adapter.

## Surrogate run.

The 6,000 surrogate attempts joined a preserved prefix of 1,748 attempts with a completed suffix of 4,252 attempts. The wall time of the whole allocation is therefore unavailable.

## Device records.

Older feature-extraction logs record the data type and parameter counts but not which device held each parameter, and the provenance audit gives no retrospective measurement. Interrupted attempts keep their original cache keys, batch contexts, failure records, and measured timings. Where an interrupted run has no reliable end time, we report no total wall time.

## Unrecovered metadata file.

The selection reports for the primary raw DINOv3 CLS and mean heads refer to an earlier metadata file whose exact bytes we could not recover. We verified the checkpoints and the fit, development, and calibration identities. We cannot rebuild these historical inputs byte for byte.

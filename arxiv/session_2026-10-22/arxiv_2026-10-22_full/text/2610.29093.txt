Abstract

We introduce MixAgent, an agentic pipeline for music post-production that follows the workflow of audio engineers to build interpretable and editable mixing plans guided by a reference song.
A grouping agent first routes related input tracks, such as drum recordings, into subgroups. A planning agent then uses retrieved examples to choose and order audio effects, such as volume control and reverb, for tracks, subgroups, and the full mix. To refine the mix, a detector identifies what needs correction, and a critic proposes numerical parameter adjustments from tracks to the full mix.
In our experiments, prompting recent multimodal models to listen and revise often worsens the mix, making reliable correction a central challenge.
We therefore build MixCritic, a dataset of 45,657 repair critiques derived from parameters fitted to professional mixes, and train the critic with supervised fine-tuning followed by preference optimization on rendered audio outcomes. On MedleyDB, learned refinement reduces error by 19% relative to the initial mix, with preference optimization reducing it by 8.4% over supervised training alone. Retrieved examples further improve effects selection, and a human listening test shows that MixAgent gets higher quality ratings than mixes produced by Qwen3-Omni, Gemini 3.8 Flash and GPT-4o-audio.

## 1 Introduction

Language-model agents use tools to write code (Yang et al., 2024) and edit images (Ye et al., 2026). In creative software, a key challenge is turning a high-level goal into precise settings.
Noticing that “the vocal is too quiet” does not specify which control to move or by how much. Music mixing combines separately recorded instruments and vocals (input tracks) into a song by applying audio effects that control volume, frequency balance, and acoustic space.
Related tracks can also be processed together as subgroups, such as all drum recordings, before processing the full mix.
Settings interact, as louder drums can hide the vocal.
Because the desired sound can be difficult to describe in words, we study reference-guided mixing, where a reference song provides an example.

Existing systems address parts of this problem (Figure 1).
Neural and generative models produce competitive audio without exposing
audio effects settings (Koo et al., 2023; Moliner et al., 2026),
while Diff-MST predicts editable parameters for fixed sequences of audio effects (Vanka et al., 2024). Prior LLM-based systems address narrower tasks.
LLM2Fx-Tools predicts audio effects chains for individual tracks (Doh et al., 2026), and RIME edits an existing mix to follow instructions (Schaffer and Singh, 2026).
Building a mix from tracks requires choosing and ordering effects at the track, subgroup, and full-mix levels, setting their parameters, and identifying corrections without editing instructions.
Simply prompting an audio-language model to listen and revise does not consistently improve the mix.

MixAgent builds an editable mixing plan and refines its parameters. A grouping agent organizes related tracks into subgroups. Guided by retrieved examples, a frozen planner chooses and orders audio effects at all three levels, then initializes their parameters. A learned detector selects what needs correction, and a critic proposes how to fix it. Refinement makes one pass at each level, from tracks to subgroups to the full mix, so later stages hear earlier corrections.
Because no known corpus pairs mixing critiques with parameter repairs, we build MixCritic, containing 45,657 critiques.
We train the critic with supervised fine-tuning (SFT), then direct preference optimization (DPO), favoring corrections toward the audio produced by the fitted parameters. Every effect, parameter, and correction remains explicit and editable.

The design also raises a broader question. What makes feedback useful for agents acting through numerical tools?
Our analyses show that retrieved examples improve audio effects selection, while learned refinement improves their parameters.
This refinement benefits from more specific feedback, with numerical corrections giving lower reconstruction error than perceptual or tool-specific advice.
With numerical feedback, DPO reduces this error by a further 8.4% over SFT.
Beyond feedback, selecting correction targets and refining tracks before subgroups and the full mix further reduces error.

Training and objective evaluation use non-overlapping excerpts of the same song as inputs and references, allowing comparison against its professional mix. On held-out MedleyDB songs, MixAgent achieves lowest reconstruction error and higher predicted quality than all tested multimodal LLM baselines. An 11-participant listening test with references from other songs within the same genre also gives MixAgent higher quality ratings. Our contributions are:

## Music Post-production.

The input consists of individual audio recordings, called input tracks, such as a lead vocal, a bass guitar, or a single drum microphone. Mixing proceeds at three levels: individual tracks, subgroups of related tracks summed and processed together (e.g., all drum tracks), and the full mix formed by summing all subgroup outputs. Subgroup and full-mix processing correspond to subgroup buses and the master bus, respectively. The output after all processing is the final mix. Mixing engineers shape the audio at each level with audio effects, signal-processing tools controlled by numerical parameters. Typical audio effects include gain (volume), panning (left–right position), equalization (EQ, the balance of low and high frequencies), compression (reducing loudness fluctuations), and reverb (a sense of acoustic space). An ordered list of audio effects, such as EQ followed by compression, is an audio effects chain.

## Task.

We study reference-guided mixing. The input is a set of unprocessed input tracks $X$ with instrument labels, a library of available audio effects $\mathcal{T}$ (Appendix A.2), and a style reference $r$ , which is a finished mix with the desired mixing style from a different song. LLM2Fx-Tools (Doh et al., 2026) predicts audio effects chains for individual tracks. We extend this task to multitrack mixing, producing a mixing plan $G$ and parameters $\theta$ . The plan assigns the tracks $x_{i}\in X$ to subgroups, where $S_{b}$ is the set of tracks in subgroup $b$ (possibly a single track), and assigns an audio effect chain to each track, each subgroup, and the full mix, written $f_{i}$ , $f_{b}$ , and $f_{m}$ with parameters $\theta_{i}$ , $\theta_{b}$ , and $\theta_{m}$ . Executing the plan, which we call rendering, produces the output mix $\widehat{y}$ in three stages:

We denote the audio after each stage by $R_{1}(\theta)=\sum_{i\in S_{b}}y_{i}$ , $R_{2}(\theta)=\sum_{b}y_{b}$ , and $R_{3}(\theta)=\hat{y}$ .

The agent must infer audio effects chains and settings to match the reference’s mixing style rather than copy audio, since instrumentation, arrangement, or genre may differ. It must also account for interactions across tracks, subgroups, and the full mix.
Our datasets lack ground-truth mixes with references from other songs. We therefore use two non-overlapping excerpts of the same song for training and main evaluation (§4.1).
The listening test uses references from other songs within the same genre (§4.3). MixAgent keeps every audio effect and setting explicit, so users can inspect the plan, change values, and render the mix again. Audio is produced only by applying these audio effects to the input tracks, avoiding hallucinated musical content and artifacts from direct neural audio generation.

## 3 MixAgent

MixAgent runs automatically and splits mixing into three decisions (Figure 2). It decides which audio effects to use (§3.1), what to fix in the current mix, and how to fix it (§3.2). We describe how the components that make the last two decisions are trained (§3.3).

## 3.1 Planning Audio effects Chains with Retrieved Examples

A grouping agent first listens to the input tracks, reads their instrument labels, and assigns each track to a subgroup (e.g., kick, snare, and cymbals form a drum subgroup). A frozen audio-language tool-planning agent then selects audio effects chains.
Without examples, the planner tends to select short chains (§ 5.2). To guide its choices, we use GRAFX (Lee et al., 2024), a differentiable audio-processing library, to estimate example plans.
GRAFX fits audio effect parameters to approximate each database song’s professional mix, then removes effects that barely change the rendered audio (Lee et al., 2025b).
Each database entry stores these pseudo-ground-truth (pseudo-GT) plans and a caption of the rendered mix. For a new song, text retrieval matches automatically generated input-track captions to database captions to find similar instrumentation. Audio retrieval matches the reference to rendered database mixes for similar mixing styles. We provide three text-retrieved and one audio-retrieved plan, excluding all excerpts of the input song.
These examples show effect choices and order, but not parameter values. The planner selects track, subgroup, and full-mix chains in order, with each level guided by earlier choices.
The chains remain fixed.
Appendix B.1 and F provide details and prompts.

## 3.2 Hierarchical Estimation

The planner first sets the initial parameters of selected effect chains based on the reference audio.
MixAgent then revises them bottom-up, following common engineering practice (Fox, 2024), first the tracks, then the subgroups, and finally the full mix (Figure 2). We call each track, subgroup, or the full mix an element $e$ , and two trained components carry out each revision.

## Detector (what to fix).

At the track and subgroup stages, the trained detector hears the processed tracks within a subgroup or the subgroup outputs, together with the reference $r$ , and reads the current plan and parameters. For each $e$ , it predicts the probability $p_{e}$ that an element needs correction and identifies audio effects that may need changes. The critic inspects elements with $p_{e}>\tau$ , starting with the highest probability. The full mix is always inspected. Appendix B.3 describes its architecture and training.

## Critic (how to fix it).

For each selected $e$ , the audio-language critic $\pi_{\phi}$ hears the audio of $e$ with its current audio effects, the current mix, and the reference. Its input $o_{e}$ also includes the detector’s output and the audio effects chain and parameters of $e$ . The critic proposes a correction $a_{e}$ , and $\mathrm{Exec}$ applies it to the parameters of $e$ :

 $a_{e}\sim\pi_{\phi}(\cdot\mid o_{e}),\qquad\theta\leftarrow\mathrm{Exec}(a_{e},\theta),$ 

(2)

where $\mathrm{Exec}$ changes only the parameters of $e$ . We compare three forms of feedback that describe the same repair (Table 1). For perceptual advice (A1) and tool-specific advice (A2), it asks the planner to turn the advice into numbers. For numerical corrections (A3), it applies the proposed values directly. MixAgent uses A3.

Each refinement pass calls the critic once per selected $e$ , using audio from the pass’s start. Then, applies corrections, re-renders the stage again, so the next stage hears the updated audio. We use one pass per stage, record every parameter change, and study additional passes (Appendix B.2 and D.6).

## Repair score.

We score a correction $a$ by how much it reduces the difference between the rendered audio and the pseudo-GT audio.

 $\displaystyle s(a)={}$ 
 $\displaystyle\mathrm{MRS}\big(\mathcal{R}_{k}(\tilde{\theta}),y^{\ast}_{k}\big)$ 

(3)

 $\displaystyle-\mathrm{MRS}\big(\mathcal{R}_{k}(\mathrm{Exec}(a,\tilde{\theta})),y^{\ast}_{k}\big),$ 

where MRS measures audio distance (Steinmetz and Reiss, 2020). Positive scores mean improvement toward the stage- $k$ pseudo-GT audio $y^{\ast}_{k}$ .

## Supervised critiques.

For each corrupted state, we select one corrupted track and define a target correction $a^{\ast}$ that restores its pseudo-GT parameters. The other tracks keep their current settings.
A larger teacher LLM (Qwen3.6-35B) receives the corrupted state and the target track’s LLM-generated and fitted parameters, and writes critiques in forms A1–A3, each with a step-by-step rationale.
We keep a critique if its target repair satisfies $s(a^{\ast})>\delta_{0}$ , leaving 45,657 examples. The detector is trained on the same states, plus states with random parameters, to predict which tracks, subgroups, and audio effects were corrupted (class-weighted binary cross-entropy). Pseudo-GT parameters are used only to build MixCritic and never at inference.

## Supervised fine-tuning (SFT).

The critic uses a Qwen3.5-4B language model (Qwen Team, 2026) and a frozen RLAT encoder trained to represent audio effects (Lee et al., 2026). A trainable audio adapter connects them.
Following LLM2Fx-Tools (Doh et al., 2026), we first train the adapter alone and then train it jointly with low-rank adaptation (LoRA) weights. The loss is $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{SFT}}=\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{CE}}+0.3\,\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{NTL}}$ , which adds to token cross-entropy a number-token loss that penalizes numerically distant predictions (Zausinger et al., 2025).

## Preference training on audio outcomes (DPO).

SFT learns one target correction, but other corrections may also improve the mix. For each $e$ selected by the detector in a training state, the SFT critic samples eight candidate critiques. We apply each candidate to the same starting parameters, render the result, and normalize its repair score as $\hat{s}(a)=s(a)/s(a^{\ast})$ . The highest-scoring candidate is paired with each of the two lowest-scoring candidates when their normalized score gap is at least $\delta$ . About 30% of pairs come from detector false alarms, where $e$ was not corrupted and no target repair exists. For these, we normalize by the initial audio error $\mathrm{MRS}(\mathcal{R}_{k}(\tilde{\theta}),y_{k}^{\ast})$ instead. Starting from the SFT critic, direct preference optimization (DPO) (Rafailov et al., 2023) favors higher-scoring corrections using the frozen SFT critic as reference. Only the audio adapter and LoRA weights are updated (learning rate of $5\times 10^{-6}$ , $\beta=0.1$ , and 24 pairs per batch).
Appendix B.4 gives the critic architecture and training settings, and Appendix C gives MixCritic statistics.

## 4 Experiments

## 4.1 Setup

## Data.

We build the task from MedleyDB (Bittner et al., 2014), using its unprocessed recordings as unprocessed input tracks and its professional mixes as targets. Our datasets lack ground-truth mixes made to match references from other songs. We therefore sample two non-overlapping 15-second windows from each song section: one provides the unprocessed input tracks and the ground-truth mix (GT), for supervision and evaluation, and the other provides the style reference. This preserves a similar mixing style without providing the target audio as the reference. At evaluation, GT is used only for scoring.
This gives 185 clips from 123 songs (Appendix A.1). We hold out 20 clips for evaluation, with artists disjoint from training. For cross-corpus transfer, we build AlbumDB clips (McKenzie et al., 2026) identically.

## Baselines.

We evaluate Qwen3-Omni (Xu et al., 2025), Gemini 3.8 Flash (Google, 2026), and GPT-4o-audio (OpenAI, 2024) under three settings (Table 2). Single-pass generates a mixing plan and its parameters without revision. Self-Refine listens to its current mix and revises it directly.
Self-Critic first writes a critique of the mix, then revises it using that critique. Both adapt Madaan et al. (2023) and iterate three rounds.
All settings use the same unprocessed input tracks, style reference, and list of available audio effects as MixAgent.
A random baseline samples audio effects and parameters.

## Metrics.

We evaluate reconstruction, audio quality, and mixing-style similarity. Multi-Scale Spectral (MSS) (Engel et al., 2020) compares the frequency content of the output and GT over time at several resolutions, using the sum and difference of the left and right channels. Multi-Resolution STFT (MRS) (Yamamoto et al., 2020), used in training and analyses, makes this comparison for each channel. TuneJury (Kim et al., 2026) predicts listener preference, and Audiobox (Tjandra et al., 2025) predicts production quality (AAPQ). Neither uses GT or the style reference.
For similarity to GT, FxEnc++ (Yeh et al., 2025) compares learned audio effects representations, while Audio Feature loss (AF) (Vanka et al., 2024) compares hand-crafted features of volume, loudness fluctuations, frequency balance, and left–right spread. We omit RLAT similarity because we train on its representations.

## 4.2 Main Results

On MedleyDB, MixAgent achieves the lowest MSS (2.83 vs. 3.67 for single-pass GPT-4o-audio) and the highest TuneJury and Audiobox PQ scores (Table 2). Gemini 3.8 Flash with Self-Refine scores best on FxEnc++ and AF. Results on AlbumDB are mixed. MixAgent has the highest Audiobox PQ and ties single-pass Gemini on TuneJury, while its MSS is second best (3.81 vs. 3.77 for GPT-4o-audio with Self-Refine). Single-pass GPT-4o-audio scores best on FxEnc++ and AF.

Prompted revision does not consistently improve on single-pass mixing. Self-Critic yields higher MSS than Self-Refine for every model on both datasets. In MixAgent, learned refinement lowers MSS from 3.70 to 2.83 with fixed audio effects chains (§5). Appendix D.1 compares MixAgent with dedicated mixing models (Diff-MST, MST, and StemFX). MixAgent has the lowest MedleyDB MSS, although MST and StemFX use tracks extracted from the professional mix.

## 4.3 Human Listening Test

## Human Listening Test.

We conducted a multi-stimulus listening test to evaluate perceptual music production quality in a practical reference-based mixing scenario. Input tracks were paired with a reference mix from a different song within the same genre.
Eleven participants completed 10 trials each, evaluating the production quality of mixes by MixAgent, three LLM baselines, a randomly processed anchor, and the original human mix on a 0–100 scale.
Figure 4 shows that three LLM baselines exhibit largely overlapping score distributions, with no statistically significant differences among them. In contrast, MixAgent achieves significantly higher scores than all three baselines ( $p<0.001$ , Holm-corrected paired t-tests), while a substantial perceptual gap remains relative to the original human mixes.

## 5 What Makes Feedback Useful?

We examine what makes learned refinement effective.
Protocols are in Appendix E.

## 5.1 Critic Training and Feedback Form

Table 3 compares critic training and feedback forms with fixed audio effect chains and detector.
Untrained critics make harmful corrections. Because our critic has a trained audio adapter, we use prompted Qwen3-Omni as the untrained critic. Its corrections increase error on average across all three forms, with only 42–52% improving the mix. Final MRS is 2.18 to 2.44, versus 2.17 for the planner alone and 1.75 for trained A3 refinement. The base model’s judgments are unreliable for correction.
Appendix D.5 provides stage-wise errors.

Preference training improves numerical feedback (A3) under the same detector, planner, and execution. MRS falls from 1.91 to 1.75 (8.4%), mean improvement per correction nearly doubles (+0.022 to +0.040), and success rises from 64.7% to 66.8%. It also improves A2 (2.24 to 1.98), but not A1 (2.08 to 2.09), suggesting preference training benefits most from specific feedback.

After SFT+DPO, error falls with more specific feedback, from 2.09 MRS for perceptual advice to 1.98 for tool-specific advice and 1.75 for numerical corrections.
Without GT access, trained A3 also outperforms oracle A1/A2 advice (1.87/1.78) on MRS and TuneJury, suggesting that converting verbal advice into settings remains difficult. GT-fitting the same plans reaches 0.90 MRS, leaving substantial room for better parameter estimation.

## 5.2 Choosing Audio effects with Retrieved Examples

To isolate audio effects selection, Table 4 fits each plan’s parameters to GT on 100 MedleyDB clips and measure what the chosen audio effects can achieve. Retrieved examples improve the plans. The fitted MRS drops from 2.13 without examples to 1.08 with three text-retrieved and one audio-retrieved example, while random examples reach only 1.71. Text retrieval provides most of the gain. Retrieval also leads the planner to select more tools per track (3.8 vs. 1.6), which accounts for part of this gain.
Appendix D.2 reports audio effects chain F1, while Appendix D.3 shows MixAgent plans with more engineer-like track grouping and effect ordering than GRAFX.

## 5.3 Component Ablations

Retrieval and learned refinement are complementary. We run the hierarchical planner with and without retrieved examples, each with and without learned refinement (Table 5). Retrieval lowers MRS by 0.64, both without refinement (2.81 to 2.17) and with it (2.39 to 1.75), and refinement lowers MRS by 0.42 in either case. MSS likewise improves by 0.82 from retrieval and 0.87 from refinement. Retrieval improves the choice of audio effects, and refinement improves their parameters, with additive gains.
We compare MixAgent’s bottom-up order with a top-down order (full mix, then subgroups, then tracks) and with a variant where corrections are computed from the initial audio and applied at once. Bottom-up refinement gives the lowest MRS (1.75, vs. 1.82 and 1.88) and MSS (Table 5), suggesting that later corrections benefit from hearing earlier ones.
With the critic fixed, detector selection yields lower MRS (1.75) than inspecting all tracks and subgroups (1.87) or an equally sized random subset (1.81), and the lowest MSS. Selecting appropriate targets matters more than inspecting additional elements. Appendix D.4 reports detector accuracy.

## 5.4 An Inspectable Output

Each step of MixAgent acts on named audio effects and parameters, so its output records how the mix was made. On the evaluation set, the critic applies 241 corrections (about 12 per clip), each a change to a named parameter, and two thirds of them reduce the error of the full mix. Users can trace, revert, or adjust edits unavailable in audio-only outputs.

## 6 Related Work

## Agents with tools and learned feedback.

LLM agents call tools (Yao et al., 2023; Schick et al., 2023) and refine their outputs with self-generated feedback (Madaan et al., 2023; Shinn et al., 2023), but self-correction without an external signal often fails (Huang et al., 2024), which motivates trained correctors (Welleck et al., 2023). In music production, LLM2Fx predicts audio effects parameters from text (Doh et al., 2025), LLM2Fx-Tools recovers the audio effect chain of a single track through tool calls (Doh et al., 2026), and MixAssist gives mixing advice in conversation (Clemens and Marasović, 2025). RIME listens between tool calls and edit finished mixes from synthetic instruction–edit pairs (Schaffer and Singh, 2026). MixAgent instead builds mixes from unprocessed input tracks, and its detector and critic are trained on whether each correction improves the rendered audio.

## Automatic mixing.

Explicit-processing methods estimate differentiable audio-effect parameters (Steinmetz et al., 2021; Steinmetz et al., 2022; Vanka et al., 2024; Steinmetz et al., 2024; Koo et al., 2025) or variable-length chains (Cheng et al., 2026), but most use fixed consoles without selecting effects or grouping tracks. GRAFX fits graphs to known targets (Lee et al., 2024; Lee et al., 2025b), supplying our retrieval plans, training data, and oracles. Neural approaches include end-to-end mixing (Martínez-Ramírez et al., 2022), style transfer (Koo et al., 2023), and diffusion or flow-based generation (Levy et al., 2023; Melechovsky et al., 2026; Moliner et al., 2026; Yeh et al., 2026). Diff2Mix instead decodes generated embeddings into fixed-console parameters (Zong et al., 2026). Direct audio generation can introduce artifacts and obscure audio effect settings (Melechovsky et al., 2026; Moliner et al., 2026). MixAgent combines open-ended chains, subgroups, and editable parameters with detection and correction learned from rendered audio.

## 7 Conclusion

We presented MixAgent, an audio-language agent that mixes music by building an explicit plan of audio effects over tracks, subgroups, and the full mix, then refines it bottom-up with a learned detector and critic. Our results suggest a broader lesson for agents that act through numerical tools. Targeted, executable feedback trained on outcomes improves mixing. Because each decision logs a named audio effect and value, users can inspect the result, trace corrections, and edit the plan.

## Limitations

First, multiple mixes can be valid for the same input tracks, so reconstruction error against one professional mix does not fully capture mixing quality (Moliner et al., 2026).
In our experiments, TuneJury is useful mainly for detecting severe degradation and is not yet sufficient as a standalone objective metric for assessing mixing quality. TuneJury (Kim et al., 2026) operates on mono audio (48 kHz for CLAP (Wu et al., 2023) and 24 kHz for MERT (Li et al., 2024)), while Audiobox Aesthetics uses 16 kHz mono audio. These representations limit direct assessment of stereo imaging and, for Audiobox, high-frequency detail. Developing reliable perceptual metrics for multitrack mixing remains a separate research challenge beyond the scope of this work.
Second, the evaluation sets are small (20 MedleyDB clips from 17 songs, 14 AlbumDB clips, and 10 clips for the listening study), limiting generalizability and the precision of perceptual comparisons. Results on AlbumDB are mixed.
MST accepts only four sources, so we ran it on the separated professional mix rather than on identical input tracks, limiting direct comparability.
Third, supervision and oracles come from GRAFX-fitted parameters, which approximate, but do not recover, the engineer’s settings. The large gap to GT-fitted parameters (1.75 vs. 0.90 MRS) shows that parameter setting remains far from solved.
Fourth, our audio effects library covers only a small subset of the processing tools available to professional audio engineers, creating a tool gap that limits the range of achievable mixes. The plan is also fixed after planning, so the critic cannot add or remove audio effects.
Finally, although explicit plans expose editable audio effects and parameters, we have not evaluated how users interpret or modify them in practice. Producing a mix also requires many calls to large audio-language models, limiting computational efficiency.

## Ethical Considerations

We use the MedleyDB and AlbumDB research datasets in accordance with their terms of use. Automatic mixing tools may change the work of audio engineers. We designed MixAgent to expose and accept edits to any decision so that it supports, rather than replaces, human judgment. MixAgent can also be used as an educational tool for audio engineers.

## Appendix A Experimental Setup

## A.1 Data

We run a music-structure analysis on each MedleyDB song and, in every section longer than 35 s, sample one pair of non-overlapping 15-s windows at least 5 s apart (§4.1). This gives 214 candidate clips from 137 songs, of which 185 clips from 123 songs remain after filtering (44.1 kHz, 24-bit, $-12$  LUFS). We split by artist into 165 training clips (106 songs) and 20 evaluation clips (17 songs). The 14 AlbumDB evaluation clips are built in the same way.

## A.3 Baselines

Single-pass baselines render their first plan. Self-Refine and Self-Critic
run up to three rounds of listening and revision, and we score the final
output. The random baseline pools valid outputs across seeds.

## A.4 Metrics

All outputs are normalized to $-12$  LUFS before scoring, and we report means over clips. TuneJury ranks the professional mix above randomly processed versions 70.5% of the time, but above the dry sum only 48% of the time and above random track gains only 41%. It thus detects severe processing errors but not subtler differences, so we use it only as a check for severe problems.

## B.3 Detector

The detector is a 34.9M-parameter classifier with a four-layer bidirectional Transformer encoder and two output heads. It takes fixed embeddings of each element’s processed audio and of the reference $r$ , together with the element’s audio effects chain and current parameters. For each audio input, we average MusicFM layer 7 features (Won et al., 2024) over time and concatenate them with RLAT features (Lee et al., 2026). One head predicts the element’s error probability $p_{e}$ , and the other identifies audio effects that may need changes. The embeddings remain fixed during detector training. The detector is trained on the 40,264 states in Table 8. A positive label requires a replaced parameter that changes the MRS of that track by at least 1.0. Appendix D.4 reports its accuracy.

## B.4 Critic Architecture and Training

The critic connects a frozen RLAT audio effects encoder (Lee et al., 2026) to Qwen3.5-4B (Qwen Team, 2026) through a trained audio adapter. We use RLAT’s audio effects embedding $z_{T}$ . The three audio inputs are the selected track, subgroup, or full mix with its current audio effects, the current mix, and the reference. Each is labeled by role and encoded separately. The adapter uses 32 queries to produce 32 tokens per audio input. The text input contains the detector’s output and the selected element’s audio effects chain and current parameters.

SFT first trains the adapter alone (stage 1) and then trains it jointly with LoRA weights (stage 2). DPO (Rafailov et al., 2023) then trains on the preference pairs of §3.3,

 $\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{DPO}}(\phi)$ 
 $\displaystyle=-\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{pref}}}\log\sigma\!\left(\beta[h(a^{+})-h(a^{-})]\right),$ 

(4)

 $\displaystyle h(a)$ 
 $\displaystyle=\log\pi_{\phi}(a\mid o)-\log\pi_{\mathrm{SFT}}(a\mid o),$ 

where $\pi_{\mathrm{SFT}}$ is the frozen SFT critic, $\sigma$ is the sigmoid function, and $\beta=0.1$ . The policy starts from the SFT checkpoint, and reference log-probabilities are precomputed. We use AdamW with weight decay $0.01$ . The learning rate follows 15 linear warmup steps and then cosine decay to $10^{-8}$ . We use bf16 mixed precision and no gradient clipping. ## D.1 Comparison with Dedicated Mixing Models

Dedicated mixing models are trained for reference-guided mixing but differ
from our setting in their inputs or output space, so we report them
separately (Table 9). Diff-MST (Vanka et al., 2024)
takes the unprocessed input tracks and predicts the parameters of a fixed differentiable
console, with gain, EQ, compression, and panning on each track and EQ and
compression on the full mix. It cannot choose audio effects or group tracks.
Diff-MST is trained on MedleyDB and Cambridge-MT, so its training songs
may overlap our MedleyDB evaluation clips, and the comparison is not
strictly fair.
MST (Koo et al., 2023) is an end-to-end network that
outputs processed audio and exposes no audio effects parameters. It is trained on
four instrument classes (vocals, drums, bass, and other), and for mixture
inputs its authors first apply source separation. Following this setup, we
separate the professional mix into the four sources with
Demucs (Défossez, 2021, mdx_extra;), and MST
restyles each of them to match the reference. StemFX (Cheng et al., 2026) predicts
variable-length audio effects chains on source-separated tracks and is applied in
the same way. MST and StemFX therefore start from the professionally mixed
version of the target, which gives them privileged access to GT.

On MedleyDB, MixAgent has the lowest MSS of all four systems.
MST scores better on Audiobox PQ, FxEnc++, and AF, and StemFX on TuneJury. Both models
largely inherit their global audio effects style from the professional mix they
start from. On AlbumDB, MixAgent improves
on Diff-MST in reconstruction (3.81 vs. 4.72 MSS), whereas MST and StemFX,
which start from the professional mix, score higher on most metrics.

## D.3 Example Plans: MixAgent and GRAFX

MixAgent builds its plan the way an engineer would. Its grouping agent
listens to the tracks and reads their labels before assigning subgroups, and its
planner chooses the audio effects and their order for each track, subgroup, and the
full mix. GRAFX, which builds the pseudo-GT plans used for retrieval and
audio effects chain F1 (§3.1), instead starts from a fixed
console that applies every audio effect in the same order everywhere. It fits all
parameters to the professional mix and prunes processors that barely affect
the result (Lee et al., 2024; Lee et al., 2025b).
Figures 5–8
compare the final plans of MixAgent with GRAFX plans for two MedleyDB and
two AlbumDB clips.

## Grouping.

MixAgent forms subgroups that follow the instrumentation. In
Figure 7, it creates drum, bass, guitar, and
vocal subgroups, whereas GRAFX uses no subgroups and sends all eight tracks to the
full mix. In Figure 8, it places the violins,
cello, and viola in one subgroup together with the bass, whereas GRAFX groups
only the two violins and splits the rest of the string section. In
Figures 5 and 6,
both plans use the same subgroups.

## Audio effects order.

MixAgent chooses the order for each element. It places the noise gate
before the compressor on the drums (Figure 5)
and on the vocals (Figures 7
and 8), as engineers usually do, and it
compresses before equalizing on the AlbumDB bass and drum subgroups
(Figure 6). Every GRAFX chain keeps the console
order of EQ, distortion, compressor, noise gate, stereo imager,
gain/panning, delay, and reverb, so its gate always follows the compressor.

## Audio effects choice.

MixAgent uses two to five audio effects per track, adds no delay or reverb to bass
tracks, and often applies reverb once on a subgroup rather than on each track, as
on the drum and piano subgroups in Figure 5. Its
full-mix chain varies across clips and includes a multiband compressor in
Figure 5. GRAFX keeps one to seven audio effects per
track and adds delay or reverb to most tracks, including the bass tracks in
Figures 5 and 6.
Its full-mix chain always contains EQ, distortion, a stereo imager, and
gain/panning, and three of the four add a limiter.

Because audio effects chain F1 (Table 10) measures
agreement with GRAFX plans, it counts such choices as errors when they
differ from GRAFX. We therefore also report audio scores after fitting each
plan’s parameters.

## Appendix E Ablation Protocols

## E.1 Critic Training and Feedback Form

All rows use the same learned detector. The untrained rows prompt
Qwen3-Omni as the critic. The trained critic is a different model (Table 7), and DPO
starts from its SFT checkpoint. Within each
feedback form, SFT and SFT+DPO share the detector, planner, plan, initial
state, prompts, and decoding. Later states can diverge as corrections are
applied. For each applied correction,
 $\Delta\mathrm{MRS}=\mathrm{MRS}_{\mathrm{before}}-\mathrm{MRS}_{\mathrm{after}}$ 
on the full mix, and a correction counts as an improvement if
 $\Delta\mathrm{MRS}>0$ . Oracle A1 and A2 replace the critic’s output with
the GT critique in the same feedback form, which the planner converts into
parameter values as in the other A1 and A2 rows. Oracle A3 fits the
parameters of the same plan directly to GT with GRAFX.

## E.2 Target Selection

All conditions share the plan, starting state, DPO A3 critic, and the
detector’s audio effects-level diagnoses, and they visit selected elements in
order of detector score. Only the set of selected tracks and subgroups changes.
The full mix is always inspected. Inspect-all averages 13.1 turns and
56.6 minutes per clip, and random selection 12.1 turns.

## E.3 Refinement Order

Top-down visits the full mix, then subgroups, then tracks, rendering between
levels (11.9 turns and 54.2 minutes per clip). The variant without
hierarchy computes all corrections from the initial audio and applies them
at once (11.8 turns).

Abstract

Pixel language models process text as rendered images instead of a finite vocabulary, which gives them strong cross-lingual transfer abilities.
However, not much is known about how these abilities are influenced by larger-scale pretraining.
In this paper, we train a family of mpixel models along three axes of variation: more languages, more scripts, and larger model capacity under fixed per-language compute.
In evaluations on five tasks covering 83 languages, we find that scaling acts locally: each axis improves the added languages and scripts, while increased capacity mitigates the curse of multilinguality from pretraining on more languages.
Transfer to unseen scripts improves with multilingual pretraining, but does not further improve with scaling.
On typologically distant low-resource languages, our models outperform subword and character-level baselines across many writing systems.
We also show that larger pixel models reserve more layers for visual processing, and that cross-lingual alignment weakens as more languages are added.
Overall, our findings characterize how multilingual scaling improves pixel language models.

## 1 Introduction

Pixel language models process text as rendered images rather than discrete tokens (Salesky et al., 2021; Rust et al., 2023). This makes them appealing for the long tail of the world’s writing systems (Joshi et al., 2020), with previous research demonstrating strong transfer abilities to unseen scripts (Kesen et al., 2025; Lotz et al., 2025).
However, there is an open question about whether such transfer can be improved by increasing (i) the number of writing systems, (ii) the pretraining languages, or (iii) by increasing model capacity.

Figure 1: mpixel performance when scaling the number of pretraining languages and model capacity.
Adding new languages steadily improves performance on seen scripts, albeit with a slight decrease on shared languages.
Higher capacity improves performance on shared and seen scripts.
On unseen scripts, neither additional languages nor capacity yields consistent gains.

We investigate this question in a carefully controlled study that separates these axes of multilingual scaling.
Our starting point is an 86M parameter model pretrained on English, Hindi, Traditional Chinese, and Ukrainian, following Kesen et al. (2025).
From here, we train two models to investigate the effect of increasing the number of writing systems.
The first is pretrained on eight-languages by adding four languages in the already-seen Latin script, whereas the other model adds four languages in previously unseen writing systems.
We also pretrain an mpixel model by combining the seen and unseen scripts with four further languages, resulting in a total of sixteen pretraining languages.
Finally, mpixellarge increases the model capacity to $307$ M parameters.

These pretraining variations allow us to study the following research questions.
RQ1: Along which axis does scaling multilingual pretraining yield greater cross-lingual transfer: increasing language diversity within an already seen script or increasing script diversity?
RQ2: Does scaling multilinguality along both axes continue to improve cross-lingual transfer, or do gains saturate with more pretraining languages?
RQ3: To what extent does increased model capacity improve cross-lingual transfer?
RQ4: Do pixel language models suffer from the curse of multilinguality (Conneau et al., 2020) as more languages are added, and if so, does increased model capacity mitigate it?

We answer these questions with empirical experiments on five downstream tasks: sentence classification, dependency parsing, named entity recognition, natural language inference, and English-only sentence classification using the GLUE benchmark (Wang et al., 2018).
We evaluate on 83 languages in total, grouped into pretraining languages, unseen languages in pretraining scripts, and languages in unseen scripts.
Among these, we prioritize low-resource languages, including some that are typologically distant from the pretraining languages of all baselines.
We also analyze the models’ representations by testing cross-lingual alignment with ABX discrimination tasks, and locate where scaling acts by probing linguistic information across layers at the sentence and word level.

## 2 Pretraining Setup

Models.

We pretrain five models to support our study on scaling pixel language models.
These models vary in the number of pretraining languages, the diversity of those languages, and the number of trainable parameters, as outlined in Table 1.

Axes.

The models are scaled along three axes: language diversity within a seen script (mpixel4s $\rightarrow$ mpixel8l), script diversity (mpixel4s $\rightarrow$ mpixel8s), and model capacity (mpixel $\rightarrow$ mpixellarge).
Since new scripts necessarily arrive with new languages, the two data expansions are matched in language count and differ only in whether the additions introduce new scripts; contrasting them asks whether script diversity adds anything beyond language diversity (RQ1).
mpixel expands along both data axes at once (RQ2), mpixellarge varies capacity on this data (RQ3), and evaluating every variant on the four shared pretraining languages quantifies the curse of multilinguality and whether capacity mitigates it (RQ4).

Languages.

We use FineWeb and multilingual FineWeb2 corpora (Penedo et al., 2024; Penedo et al., 2025) for pretraining data.
When selecting pretraining languages, we only consider higher-resource languages, and deliberately exclude some high-resource languages for downstream task evaluations.
As mentioned earlier, we maximize the diversity in pretraining languages along two axes: language family and writing system.
For this purpose, mpixel8l extends mpixel4s with four Latin-script languages from diverse language families (Spanish: Romance, Danish: Germanic, Finnish: Uralic, Turkish: Turkic), varying language family while holding the writing system fixed.
mpixel8s instead extends mpixel4s with four languages in previously unseen scripts (Korean: Hangul, Thai: Thai, Greek: Greek, Standard Arabic: Arabic), varying both writing system and language family.
mpixelbase combines these extensions and adds four further languages: Vietnamese (Latin), Persian (Perso-Arabic), Bengali (Bengali), and Hebrew (Hebrew), further extending coverage along both axes.
For each language, we an equal amount of patches, which is roughly equal to 7.5 billion image patches.
We fix the maximum number of patches per example to $512$ .

Rendering.

As demonstrated by Lotz et al. (2023), rendering strategy is a crucial design choice for pixel language models, where packing character bigrams per patch decreases input redundancy and improves performance.
We design a new adaptive rendering strategy for multilingual processing.
By default, we prefer grapheme-aware bigrams grouping as the original bigrams rendering may separate diacritics from their base characters.
For CJK languages, where wide characters make most bigrams span multiple patches, we render one character per patch, which lowers input redundancy at the same sequence length.
For Arabic and Hebrew, the bigrams strategy can sever ligatures, and rendering in visual order distorts word structure.
We therefore use word-level rendering for these scripts, where each word is rendered as a contiguous unit in logical order.

Pretraining.

Our pretraining procedure builds on prior work.
Our model variants implement the Vision Transformer Masked Autoencoder architecture (ViT-MAE) (He et al., 2022) by following the previous pixel models.
The only hyperparameter we vary independently is the effective batch size, which we double for the 8-language variants and quadruple for the 16-language variants.
With 7.5B patches per language, 1M steps, and 512 patches per sample, this holds per-language exposure at ${\approx}4.3$ epochs, which a fixed batch size would otherwise halve with each doubling of the language count.
We therefore double and quadruple the batch size for the eight- and sixteen-language model variants.
As we increase the batch size, we also adjust the learning rate by following the square root scaling procedure (Li et al., 2024; Krizhevsky, 2014).
Unlike previous pixel models, we select checkpoints on a held-out task:
zero-shot bitext mining on FLORES-200 (Team et al., 2022) over each variant’s pretraining languages.
We exclude examples overlapping with SIB-200 (Adelani et al., 2024) to prevent leakage.
The rest of the pretraining recipe is identical with previous pixel models.

## 4 Results

We first compare the mpixel models against the baselines in Table 2.
We find that xlm-r obtains the best overall averages on all tasks, with mpixellarge the second-best performing model.
We note that xlm-r and canine-s are pretrained on ${\sim}100$ languages, which means they have been trained on most of the languages in our seen-script and unseen-script groups.
We also note that xlm-r has seen much more data per language than mbert, canine-s, or the mpixel models.55
 5

 For English alone, the xlm-r pretraining corpus contains ${\sim}55$ B tokens.
On the other hand, our mpixel pretraining corpus is $\sim$ 2.5B English whitespace separated words.
We find that mpixel outperforms the character-level canine-s on all tasks except XNLI.
On UDP, mpixel achieves an overall better score than xlm-r on unseen scripts (Table 14).
However, this is largely driven by Coptic, which uses a writing system not included during xlm-r pretraining.

Figure 2: Performance of models on typologically distant low-resource languages in the SIB-200 benchmark.

Low-resource cross-lingual transfer.

For a controlled evaluation of low-resource cross-lingual transfer, we compare performance on languages unseen by all models.
Within our evaluation set, we use the DistaLs toolkit (Goot et al., 2025) to determine which languages are typologically distant from every model’s pretraining data.
Figure 2 shows the SIB-200 results on these languages.
mpixel outperforms xlm-r on all writing systems.
mpixel also outperforms canine-s on Latin and Bengali.
The vocabulary bottleneck explains this gap at two levels.
On seen scripts, the xlm-r subwords are well-trained for its pretraining languages, so they segment and represent distant languages poorly.
On the four scripts unseen by all models (N’ko, Ol Chiki, Tifinagh, Tibetan), the characters fall outside the vocabulary and are largely mapped to unknown tokens.
xlm-r performs close to random on these scripts, whereas canine-s and mpixel demonstrate their strengths.

Lines are evaluation tiers fixed w.r.t. mpixel’s languages and scripts; each point is a mean over that tier’s items ( $n$ per panel, in legend order). At $x{=}8$ the point is the mean of mpixel8l and mpixel8s and the bar spans them.

Figure 3 provides an overview of how downstream performance changes across the language and capacity scaling settings considered in our research questions (RQ1–RQ3).

RQ1: Expanding language and script coverage mainly benefits the languages and scripts it adds.

We compare mpixel8L and mpixel8S against mpixel4S, where the former adds four Latin-script languages, and the latter four languages in previously unseen scripts.
Figure 3 shows clear improvements when pretraining on four additional languages, albeit with smaller improvements on the seen-script group for SIB-200 and UDP, and overall mixed benefits for unseen scripts.
For both of the 8-language variants, gains concentrate on the added languages and on other languages sharing their scripts: Latin for mpixel8L and the newly covered scripts for mpixel8S (e.g., the Arabic script; Tables 10, 11, and 14)
NER and XNLI cover too few seen-script languages for a reliable comparison.
On unseen scripts, the changes are small and mixed for both variants:
neither addition therefore yields a consistent advantage on unseen scripts.

In order to isolate the effect of multilinguality from training volume and other pretraining confounds, we train our own monolingual pixel $\dagger$ with the same patch budget as mpixel4S.
In this matched setup, mpixel4S outperforms pixel $\dagger$ on unseen scripts in SIB-200 (see Table 10 in the Appendix).
The gains also extend to scripts visually unrelated to Devanagari, such as N’ko and Ethiopic, so script similarity alone cannot explain transfer.

RQ2: Scaling further along the language and script axes brings further improvements for seen languages and their scripts.

If we compare the 16-language mpixel against mpixel8L and mpixel8S, the overall averages improve on SIB-200, NER and UDP, as shown in Figure 3 (see Tables 10, 11, and 14 for detailed per-language breakdown).

The improvements concentrate on seen languages, where the added languages improve their own performance.
They partly extend to seen scripts, which improve on SIB-200 and UDP but decrease on NER and XNLI (Figure 3); nevertheless, NER and XNLI cover only a few seen-script languages.
On unseen scripts, the effect depends on the task.
On SIB-200 (Table 10), mpixel obtains the lowest score among the multilingual variants; on NER (Table 11), all mpixel variants stay within one point.
On UDP (Table 14), unseen scripts instead improve with each extension, and mpixel outperforms all other models, including xlm-r.

RQ3: Increased capacity only improves performance on seen languages and scripts.

We compare mpixellarge (307M) against mpixel (86M) pretrained on the same data.
As shown in Figure 3, seen languages improve on every task, with the largest gains on NER and XNLI.
Seen scripts improve on SIB-200, NER, and XNLI, and decrease slightly on UDP.
On unseen scripts, the changes are mixed: SIB-200 improves, NER stays roughly equal, and UDP decreases.
With this decrease, mpixellarge loses the UDP advantage on unseen scripts, falling below all smaller multilingual variants.
Even where unseen scripts improve, mpixellarge does not exceed mpixel4S on SIB-200.
Increased capacity therefore improves performance within the pretraining scripts, while its effect on unseen scripts is inconsistent across tasks.

RQ4: Pixel models exhibit the curse of multilinguality, but increased capacity mitigates it.

To measure the curse of multilinguality in pixel models, we evaluate all variants on the four shared pretraining languages (Figure 4).
Across the multilingual variants, per-language pretraining data and compute are fixed, so performance differences cannot be explained by reduced exposure to these languages.
From mpixel4S to mpixel, performance declines on all five benchmarks.
On GLUE, English performance drops from the monolingual pixel $\dagger$ to mpixel.
Comparing the two directions, mpixel8L and mpixel8S hurt the initial languages to a similar extent:
these two variants perform on par on four of the five benchmarks.
Adding languages in a shared script therefore does not cost less than adding new scripts.
mpixellarge not only recovers these losses but achieves the best result on every benchmark, surpassing mpixel4S and pixel $\dagger$ on all five benchmarks.
Increased capacity therefore mitigates the curse in pixel models, similar to subword tokenizer-based models.

## 5 Further Analysis

The experimental results show that improvements are concentrated on the newly added languages and scripts (RQ1–RQ2), and that adding more languages reduces performance on the four languages shared by every model variant (RQ4).
However, the results do not explain where these effects arise in the model.
In this section, we report the results of probing the hidden representations for their cross-lingual alignment and linguistic information.

## 5.1 ABX-style Discrimination

Figure 5: ABX probing performances of mpixel variants on the four shared pretraining languages. 

We use the ABX discrimination tasks proposed by
de Seyssel et al. (2025) to measure the language and meaning representations of the models.
Given a triplet of
representations $(A,B,X)$ , we measure whether $X$ is closer to $A$ or $B$ according to their cosine similarity.66
 6

 Following de Seyssel et al. (2025), we use cosine similarity over mean-pooled sentence embeddings. For MD, we apply mean centering by subtracting the mean embedding of all sentences in the same language from each sentence representation, following Libovický et al. (2020). In the Meaning Discrimination (MD) task, we measure semantic similarity across languages while controlling for language identity, making it a proxy for cross-lingual semantic alignment. In the Language Discrimination (LD) task, we measure alignment between languages while controlling for meaning, measuring whether language-specific information is encoded in the representations.
To cover all languages supported by our models, we use the multi-way parallel BOUQuET dataset (Andrews et al., 2025).
Figure 5 presents the results for ABX probes on the four shared languages in each mpixel model.

Figure 6: Layer-wise probing accuracy on sentence-level linguistic tasks. The accuracy is plotted against the proportional encoder layer depth for both base and large model variants.

Semantic alignment emerges with depth.

Across all base models, MD increases with depth and peaks in the final layer (Figure 5, right): representations become more semantically aligned across languages in higher layers, suggesting that the models gradually abstract away from visual, surface-level features toward more language-independent semantic representations. LD shows the complementary trend (Figure 5, left): all models start near-perfect in the embedding layer, which is expected given the different scripts and the resulting strong visual cues, but then drop sharply as the models move away from script-level features, before partially recovering in the middle layers.

Adding languages and scripts weakens alignment.

Final-layer MD is ordered by the number of languages and scripts, mpixel4s $>$ mpixel8l $>$ mpixel8s $>$ mpixel. Adding more languages and scripts therefore weakens cross-lingual semantic alignment, mirroring in the representations the curse of multilinguality we observe downstream (Conneau et al., 2020).

Effect of model capacity.

Comparing mpixel and mpixellarge (Figure 5), the larger model’s MD scores are higher in the intermediate layers, showing that increased capacity improves cross-lingual alignment there. Over the final layers, however, its MD declines, so that mpixel matches and even overtakes it in the last layer. This decline is driven specifically by same-script language pairs: their MD in mpixellarge behaves non-monotonically, rather than climbing steadily as it does in the smaller models. This pattern resembles what has been reported for subword models such as XLM-R (see de Seyssel et al. (2025) and the XLM-R result in Figure 5)77
 7

 The MD results of XLM-R and mpixellarge are more in line with classical interpretations from previous work (Wu et al., 2025; Wendler et al., 2024) of middle layers as a “semantic hub”, where languages are more aligned., suggesting that the non-monotonicity stems from model capacity rather than from the pixel-based input.

Surface similarity drives generalization.

Cross-lingual alignment also extends to languages held out of pretraining. Evaluating mpixel and mpixellarge on four unseen languages, each sharing its script (and usually its family) with a seen language, the related seen–unseen pairs show the highest MD and the lowest LD at every depth, indicating that the model represents an unseen language similarly to its seen, same-script relative, indicating that alignment is a result of surface similarity rather than exposure (see Appendix A.6 for details)88
 8

 Relatedly, script information remains recoverable in all layers of mpixel models, suggesting that they rely more on surface information even in deeper layers where tokenization-based models focus on semantics (see Appendix A.7)..

## 5.2 Sentence-level Probing

Tatariya et al. (2024) established that pixel-based models perform visual processing in early layers before linguistic features emerge.
We ask whether scaling model capacity alters this transition.
Figure 6 shows the layer-wise accuracy of a trained mean-pooled probe for our mpixel on the SentEval probing tasks (Conneau et al., 2018a; Conneau and Kiela, 2018).

More layers are used for visual processing.

We find that both the 12 and 24-layer models dedicate a proportionately similar number of layers to visual abstractions.
On the surface tasks (SentLen and WC), 12-layer models plateau
between layers 3 and 4, at 25% of the network depth, whereas
mpixellarge continues to improve until layer 9.

Syntax and surface semantics improve.

This extended visual processing phase improves representations across several tasks. In the deeper layers of Tree Depth, mpixellarge outperforms both base models and narrows the gap with xlm-r. Furthermore, mpixellarge achieves the highest peak accuracy on all surface semantic tasks.
However, on complex semantics (SOMO), both mpixel models fall behind the monolingual pixel $\dagger$ , as SentEval is English-only.
Thus, scaling depth improves syntactic and surface semantic representations, but closing the semantic gap remains an open challenge.

## 5.3 Word-level Probing

Figure 7: Layer-wise probing accuracy on linspector word-level tasks. Rows show tasks, columns show languages; each subplot plots accuracy against encoder layer.

We probe the hidden representations of the models with linspector, a multilingual word-level probing benchmark (Şahin et al., 2020).
We train a linear classifier on the mean-pooled representations of each encoder layer and report accuracy on the test set.
We use two tasks:
Person requires predicting grammatical person, and TagCount requires predicting the number of morphological tags associated with a word.
TagCount is more difficult, as it requires capturing the amount of morphological information encoded in a word.
We evaluate on Arabic, Greek, Turkish, and Spanish, which are pretraining languages of mpixel, and on Russian, which is typologically related to Ukrainian.

The pixel models start low and improve with depth, while xlm-r reaches high accuracy already in the early layers (Figure 7).
This matches our ABX results and the findings of previous work (Tatariya et al., 2024; Kesen et al., 2025): early pixel layers perform visual processing, and linguistic information emerges in deeper layers.
The difference with xlm-r varies by language: the mpixel variants perform similar to xlm-r on Spanish and Turkish in deeper layers, but perform worse on Arabic on both tasks.
This may be due to the word-level rendering of Arabic, which we do not test.
mpixellarge closes the gap with xlm-r in the deeper layers, consistent with the extended visual phase seen in the sentence-level probes.

## 6 Related Work

Visually rendered text processing was first applied to machine translation (Salesky et al., 2021; Salesky et al., 2023), replacing sub-word tokenization with images of the source language text.
Rust et al. (2023) introduced pixel, a language model built upon masked autoencoder architecture (ViT-MAE) and pretrained on visually rendered English text,
which outperformed its tokenizer-based equivalent bert (Devlin et al., 2019) on languages with novel writing systems, together with increased robustness against orthographic perturbations.
Lotz et al. (2023) demonstrated that structured rendering reduces patch input diversity, allowing a 25M parameter model to perform on par with the initial 86M parameter model; this approach was later applied to scanned historical documents (Borenstein et al., 2023).
Finally, there are hybrid approaches that combine subword tokens and visually rendered text (Chai et al., 2024; Gao et al., 2024; Lotz et al., 2025), which finally enable language generation.

Kesen et al. (2025) and Hu et al. (2026) are most relevant to our work on the multilingual scaling of pixel language models.
Kesen et al. (2025) introduced pixel-m

4
 pretrained on four languages where each uses a distinct writing system, which demonstrated strong cross-lingual transfer learning performance to unseen scripts.
Subsequently, Hu et al. (2026) extended patch-level autoregressive visual text generation model pixar (Tai et al., 2024) to eight languages.
In contrast to these works, we perform a controlled study of scaling pixel language models across languages, scripts, and model size under matched per-language compute.

Besides pixel language models, character- and byte-level models also offer vocabulary-free processing (Clark et al., 2022; Pagnoni et al., 2025).
Nonetheless, they have no access to the visual form of text, so orthographic similarities between characters and scripts remain unavailable to them.

## 7 Conclusion

We presented mpixel, a family of pixel language models pretrained on up to sixteen languages and 307M parameters, scaled along three axes under fixed per-language compute: more languages in a seen script, more scripts, and more capacity.
We found that scaling acts locally: each axis mainly improves the languages and scripts it adds, while transfer to unseen scripts is established by the first multilingual step and does not improve with further scaling.
Pixel models exhibit the curse of multilinguality, and increased capacity mitigates it.
On low-resource languages typologically distant from the pretraining languages of all baselines, mpixel outperforms subword and character-level baselines on many writing systems.
Our analyses showed that larger models reserve more layers for visual processing, and that cross-lingual alignment weakens with more languages but generalizes through shared scripts.
Future work will investigate explicit control over the capacity spent on visual processing, alternative rendering strategies for right-to-left scripts, and broader coverage of low-resource languages as scaling acts locally.

## Limitations

Like most of the previous work, the pretrained mpixel models lack language generation capability as they are encoder-only models.
Our scaling study covers few points per axis: four language counts (1, 4, 8, 16) and two model sizes (86M and 307M parameters), so our claims hold only within this range.
Due to compute constraints, our models are pretrained on far less data than xlm-r.
Moreover, each variant corresponds to a single pretraining run, so small differences between variants may fall within run-to-run pretraining variance, which we cannot quantify.
Our controlled setup fixes per-language compute rather than total compute, so variants with more languages see more total data, and results under a fixed total-compute budget may differ.
Each scaling axis is instantiated with a single set of languages, rooted in the four-language set of previous work for comparability, and our findings may be sensitive to these particular choices.
In particular, the first multilingual step adds new languages and new scripts at the same time, so we cannot separate their individual effects.
Finally, we design the adaptive rendering strategy based on input processing efficiency and correct visual rendering of right-to-left scripts, and we do not compare it empirically against existing rendering strategies such as bigrams.
Therefore, we do not know whether adaptive is the optimal rendering choice.

## Ethical Considerations

Like most pretrained language models, our models are pretrained on web-crawled corpora, which are already filtered and partly anonymized.
Nonetheless, these datasets might still contain some social biases, offensive content, and personal information, which our models may inherit as we do not apply any further filtering.
Our pretrained models lack generation capabilities, which limits their harmful use cases.
Finally, our models perform best on downstream tasks in their pretraining languages.
For other languages, we therefore recommend a careful evaluation before use.

## A.2 Data Statistics

Pretraining Data.

Table 4 reports the number of documents, words, and characters in the pretraining data sampled from FineWeb (Penedo et al., 2024) and FineWeb2 (Penedo et al., 2025) for each language.

Evaluation Benchmarks.

SIB-200 (Adelani et al., 2024) is a parallel text classification benchmark across 200 languages, where each language split includes 701, 99 and 204 examples for training, validation, and testing purposes, respectively.
SIB-200 is released under the CC BY-SA 4.0 license.
Table 3 lists the languages selected as typologically distant.

We selected languages whose available distances all exceeded $T=0.50$ , as well as languages written in scripts unseen during baseline pretraining.
Table 5 and Table 6 report the data statistics for the dependency parsing and NER tasks, respectively.
linspector is licensed under Apache 2.0, and each language split contains 7000, 2000, and 1000 examples for training, validation, and testing purposes, respectively.

For XNLI, we adopt the translate-train-all setting, in which the models are trained on 392,702 examples per language across all 15 languages ( $\approx 5.9M$ examples in total) and evaluated on 2,490 development and 5,010 test examples per language.

## A.3 Implementation Details

Tables 7 and 8 contain the hyperparameters used to pretrain the mpixel models.
Table 9 contains the hyperparameters used to finetune and evaluate the models on the downstream tasks.
The learning rates depend on the task and the model size. For SIB-200, UDP and NER, we use $\{$ 1e $-$ 5, 3e $-$ 5, 5e $-$ 5 $\}$ for both base and large models. For mpixellarge on UDP, we also use 5e $-$ 6.
For XNLI, we use $\{$ 8e $-$ 6, 1e $-$ 5, 2e $-$ 5, 3e $-$ 5, 5e $-$ 5 $\}$ for base models, and $\{$ 5e $-$ 6, 8e $-$ 6, 1e $-$ 5, 2e $-$ 5, 3e $-$ 5 $\}$ for mpixellarge.
For GLUE, we use 1e $-$ 5 for WNLI (Levesque et al., 2012), 2e $-$ 5 for STS-B (Cer et al., 2017), and 3e $-$ 5 for MRPC (Dolan and Brockett, 2005), QNLI (Wang et al., 2018), QQP, RTE and SST-2 (Socher et al., 2013).
For CoLA (Warstadt et al., 2019), we use $\{$ 1e $-$ 5, 2e $-$ 5 $\}$ , and for MNLI (Williams et al., 2018), we use $\{$ 1e $-$ 5, 2e $-$ 5, 3e $-$ 5 $\}$ .
For large models, we also use 5e $-$ 6 for WNLI, and 1e $-$ 5 for STS-B, MRPC, QNLI, QQP, RTE and SST-2.
Following Rust et al. (2023); Kesen et al. (2025), for each learning rate, we run several experiments with different random seeds. From all these runs, we take the $3$ best ones and report their average score.

We adopt Flash Attention 2 (Dao, 2024) to reduce memory usage and accelerate attention computation, and use torch.compile to further speed up forward and backward passes.
With these optimizations, pretraining takes approximately 1350 GPU hours for mpixel and 2500 GPU hours for mpixellarge on NVIDIA H100s.

## A.6 ABX More Ablations

## A.6.1 Generalization to unseen languages.

To test whether scaling multilinguality affects seen and unseen languages differently,
we compare (seen, seen), (seen, unseen), and (unseen, unseen) language pairs across our pixel models with increasing language coverage.

We test whether alignment extends to languages held out of pretraining by evaluating mpixelbase on four unseen languages (Assamese, Icelandic, Yiddish, North Azerbaijani), each chosen to share its script (and, in three of four cases, its language family) with one of four seen languages (Bengali, Danish, Hebrew, Turkish).
Splitting the language pairs into seen–seen, seen–unseen (related vs. unrelated), and unseen–unseen groups (Figure 8), the related seen–unseen pairs stand out: they have the highest, flattest MD and the lowest LD at every depth. Unseen–unseen pairs have the lowest MD scores and an unstable LD trajectory. The difference between seen–seen pairs and unrelated seen–unseen pairs is small, indicating that joint inclusion in pre-training does not increase alignment.

Figure 8: ABX probes, mpixelbase (above) and mpixellarge (below). Languages seen and unseen during pre-training, in different combinations. 

## A.6.2 Same- versus cross-script pairs.

Extending the language set from the pixel-m

4
 languages to all 16 languages lowers LD and raises MD scores. For mpixelbase, embedding-layer LD falls from $1.00$ on the four pixel-m

4
 languages to $0.95$ on all 16. The reason is the changed pair composition: the 16-language set contains many same-script pairs, which lack the visual cues that make cross-script pairs trivially language-discriminable and are easier to align in meaning.

Splitting pairs by whether the two languages share a script (same-script vs. cross-script) confirms this; results are in Figure 9. The clearest effect is at the embedding layer of LD: cross-script pairs are near-trivially discriminable, whereas same-script pairs start far lower.
Cross-script LD decreases substantially already in the first layer. The two model sizes differ in how the script signal is then
handled.
In mpixelbase, same-script LD recovers and even exceeds cross-script LD in the middle layers.
MD is slightly higher within the same script at every depth and climbs monotonically for both groups.
In mpixellarge however, same-script LD stays well below cross-script LD throughout and is less stable.

The same-script MD trajectory is where the two models diverge most.
For mpixelbase, MD is only mildly affected by script sharing: the curves differ mostly in the first layer, where the same-script MD is notably higher.
In mpixellarge, the MD scores for same-script pairs are much more dynamic (and peak higher) than the ones of cross-script pairs.

Figure 9: ABX probes, mpixelbase (above) and mpixellarge (below), mpixelbase pre-training languages. Pairs of languages that share script (blue) versus pairs of languages that have different scripts (orange). 

## A.7 Script ID Probes

To test how explicitly script identity is encoded across layers, we train linear probes on each layer’s representations to classify text into the 10 scripts seen during mpixelbase pretraining.

Setup

We train linear probing classifiers on the mean-pooled encoder representations at each of the 13 layers. Each probe is a multinomial logistic regression (L2 regularization, $C{=}1.0$ ) trained to predict script identity from the BOUQuET dataset (Andrews et al., 2025).
We evaluate in two settings:
iid, on unseen sentences from the 16 training languages, and
ood, on 32 unseen languages whose scripts (Arabic, Bengali, Cyrillic, Devanagari, Han, Hebrew, Latin) the probe has seen.

pixel models

In the iid setting, script identity is recovered almost perfectly at every layer in all base models. The models diverge in the ood setting:
mpixelbase and mpixel8s retain near-perfect accuracy across all layers, whereas pixel-m

4
 and mpixel8l decline in the final layers; a clear, though not catastrophic, drop from the perfect accuracy of the early layers and of the higher-coverage models. The high results of the pixel $\dagger$ model however show that the model trained on only one script also behaves robustly.

Notably, the late-layer errors of mpixel4s and mpixel8l are largely Cyrillic misclassified as Latin, even though every mpixel variant has exactly one Cyrillic-script pretraining language (Ukrainian).

Visual versus token-based inputs

Compared to XLM-R, we see that pixel models generalize more reliably to out-of-distribution languages, indicating a difference of the visual versus subword token-based processing for unseen languages..

Abstract

AI-generated text detectors are increasingly deployed in consequential settings—peer review, education, admissions, journalism, and content moderation—where their outputs directly affect individuals. Yet the strongest detectors are fine-tuned neural classifiers whose decisions are opaque, while transparent alternatives (stylometric and linguistic-feature classifiers) underperform them by wide margins. We present a pipeline that converts a fine-tuned neural detector into a classifier that operates on human-readable concepts. We accomplish this by training sparse autoencoders (SAEs) directly on a detector’s residual stream, assigning auto-interpretation labels to each SAE feature, and retaining only features whose generated descriptions pass a global fidelity threshold. We then train a lightweight classifier on just the interpretable features. We find that our SAEs recover latent concepts that correspond to highly specific and predictive linguistic “tells” of AI-generated text. Classifiers built on these extracted features are not only highly interpretable but exhibit stronger performance on out-of-distribution texts than either neural or stylometric alternatives.

## 1 Introduction

AI-generated text detection is no longer a purely academic problem. Detectors are used to flag student essays, vet peer-review submissions, screen job applications, and moderate online content. Their outputs feed into decisions that affect individuals, and in practice they are often treated as black boxes by users who cannot inspect or contest them.

Figure 1: In our work, we use Sparse Autoencoders to convert existing black box AI detectors into ones that operate on human-interpretable concepts—allowing for more auditable deployment.

In settings like these, accuracy is not the only thing that matters. What also matters is whether an auditor—a reviewer, an instructor, a compliance officer—can look inside a decision, understand what the detector is keying on, and stress-test it. Stylometric detectors (Fröhling and Zubiaga, 2021; Aityan et al., 2026) are interpretable by construction but underperform their neural counterparts. Fine-tuned neural models achieve high accuracy but operate as black boxes.
What real-world deployment needs is a performant AI detector whose decisions can be explained and grounded in specific characteristics and features of the input text.

Our work builds an interpretable AI detector in four stages. First, we fine-tune a model on the AI detection task. Then, we train sparse autoencoders on the model’s residual stream at various depths. Then, we run an auto-interpretation pipeline on the SAE features (Bills et al., 2023; Paulo et al., 2025), and keep only those features whose explanations reach a certain global simulation score threshold. Finally, we train a lightweight $L_{1}$ -regularized logistic regression classifier on the remaining features. The resulting classifier is built on automatically discovered features that each have an associated natural-language description that accurately describes their underlying activation patterns.

## 2 Background

## 2.1 Sparse Autoencoders

Individual neurons in a neural network are often polysemantic Olah et al. (2020)—firing on seemingly unrelated inputs in ways that make interpretability challenging.
Sparse Autoencoders (SAEs) Cunningham et al. (2024); Bricken et al. (2023) undo this polysemanticity by converting dense neural network activations $\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{model}}}$ into large sparsely-active vectors of monosemantic features $\mathbf{h}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{SAE}}}$ . The encoder and decoder are parameterized as

 $\displaystyle\mathbf{f}_{\text{enc}}(\mathbf{x})$ 
 $\displaystyle=\sigma(\mathbf{W}_{\text{enc}}\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{b}_{\text{enc}})$ 

(1)

 $\displaystyle\mathbf{f}_{\text{dec}}(\mathbf{h})$ 
 $\displaystyle=\mathbf{W}_{\text{dec}}\mathbf{h}+\mathbf{b}_{\text{dec}}$ 

(2)

where $\sigma$ is a non-linear function (often ReLU). These functions are trained to reconstruct the input $\mathbf{x}$ while minimizing a sparsity penalty on $\mathbf{h}$ .

 $\mathcal{L}=||\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{\hat{x}}||_{2}^{2}+\lambda||\mathbf{h}||_{1}$ 

(3)

where $\lambda$ is a coefficient set to balance the tradeoff between sparsity and reconstruction accuracy. The resulting features are highly monosemantic and are usable in place of neurons for interpretability analysis Shu et al. (2025); Marks et al. (2025).

## 2.2 Automatically Interpreting SAE Features

Auto-interpretation Bills et al. (2023); Paulo et al. (2025) is the act of generating human-readable descriptions of SAE features given their activations on a large corpus. A typical pipeline for this is as follows: For each feature, sample a small representative set of documents from across the feature’s activation distribution. Then, provide these documents to a large language model and ask it to generate a short human-readable description of that feature (typically 10-20 tokens).

Once a description is generated, the model is then asked to validate the accuracy of the description by “simulating” the neuron—predicting on which tokens of an arbitrary input text the feature is active—given only the generated description. The resulting correlation score between the true activations and simulated activations measures how faithfully the feature’s description represents its true behavior.

## 2.3 SAEs for Hypothesis Generation

Recent work has begun to investigate SAEs not just as ways of uncovering information about the internal activations of language models but also as ways of generating hypotheses about textual classification tasks in an unsupervised manner Movva et al. (2025).

For example, say an SAE feature fires disproportionately on a particular class in a classification task. That feature may represent a semantically meaningful and predictive aspect of the class. In their work, Movva et al. (2025) found SAE features such as “mentions being surprised or shocked” that were positively predictive of news engagement and “mentions issues related to food safety” that were negatively predictive of restaurant rating.

Broadly speaking, this concept of using interpretability as a “microscope” Olah (2015) to illuminate interesting aspects of underlying data distributions is a strong undercurrent of our work. We follow this line of work by uncovering specific predictive linguistic cues of AI generated text and show that these tells create strong classifiers.

Figure 2: Our pipeline. Starting from a fine-tuned AI-text detector, we train SAEs on its residual stream at four layers, label each latent with an auto-interp explanation, mask out all features that do not meet our global threshold and train a lightweight classifier over the remaining concepts. The result is a detector that retains the strength of the original neural model while being composed of auditable and intervenable human-understandable concepts.

## 3 Related Work

## Explainable AI Detection

Early work on explainable AI detectors primarily centered around distributional analysis of linguistic features Fröhling and Zubiaga (2021); Uchendu et al. (2020); Zahid et al. (2024); Zaitsu et al. (2025). Follow-up work refined this to include discourse features Kim et al. (2024); Russell et al. (2026) dependency parses Ahmed and Hammond (2026) ngram overlaps Koike et al. (2026) and ensembles Aityan et al. (2026). However, AI detectors built on these linguistic features generally underperform black-box counterparts and are limited in their ability to generalize to adversarial or out-of-distribution inputs Schuster et al. (2020).

On the other hand, fine-tuned neural classifiers Solaiman et al. (2019), and other zero-shot likelihood-based metrics Mitchell et al. (2023); Hans et al. (2024) exhibit surprisingly strong in-distribution performance and much better generalization than their stylometric counterparts Dugan et al. (2025); Sandhan et al. (2026). Previous work used techniques like LIME Ribeiro et al. (2016) and SHAP Lundberg and Lee (2017) to interpret these neural models Shah et al. (2023); Schoenegger et al. (2026) but such explanations only give global token-level importance scores and fail to provide rationales as to why particular tokens are predictive. Our work is the first to go beyond these token-based attribution scores and create a fully interpretable AI detection system that maintains the strong performance of neural models.

## Interpretable Classifiers using SAE Features

 $L_{1}$ -regularized linear classifiers (“sparse probes”) trained on SAE features were first proposed as diagnostic tools for measuring monosemanticity Gurnee et al. (2023); Gao et al. (2025). Follow up work showed that such classifiers perform on par with (but no better than) dense linear probes Bricken et al. (2024); Kantamneni et al. (2025). Our work follows Peng et al. (2026) in claiming that interpretability, not accuracy, is the primary benefit of these classifiers.

In this vein, recent work has shown that classifiers trained on SAE features are useful as interpretable reward models Zhang et al. (2026), dense retrievers Park et al. (2025), hallucination detectors Xiong et al. (2026), and LLM-as-judge systems Wedgwood et al. (2026). However, these works all provide the full set of SAE features as input to the downstream classifiers without any explicit removal of high density or polysemantic SAE latents Sun et al. (2025); Chanin et al. (2025). The lack of such filtering undermines the faithfulness of the system-level explanations.

To combat this, Kulkarni et al. (2026) proposed to remove the $M$ least interpretable SAE features from their classifier. However, this does not guarantee that the remaining features are themselves interpretable. Our work is the first to propose a global filtering threshold based on auto-interpretability score to ensure our explanations are faithful to the underlying dynamics of our classifier.

## Using SAE Features for AI Detection

Previous work by Scher (2024) noted the potential for SAEs as a means of interpreting AI detectors. They trained a lightweight classifier on SAE features of GPT-2 Small and found that they outperformed stylometric features in AI detection. Follow-up work by Kuznetsov et al. (2025) looked at how SAE features of Gemma-2-2B co-occurred with AI generated text. However, neither of these works train their own SAE models on AI detectors, nor do they evaluate the accuracy of the provided feature descriptions. Our work is the first to train SAEs directly on AI detectors and is the first to propose using these features as an interpretable AI detector.

## 4 Methods

## 4.1 Pipeline Overview

Figure 2 shows an illustration of our full pipeline. Given a token sequence $(\mathbf{t}_{0},\ldots,\mathbf{t}_{n})$ , we extract language model activations $(\mathbf{d}_{0}^{(\ell)},\ldots,\mathbf{d}_{n}^{(\ell)})$ from the residual stream at layers $\ell\in L$ and pass them through the corresponding SAE encoder to get features $(\mathbf{h}_{0}^{(\ell)},\ldots,\mathbf{h}_{n}^{(\ell)})$ . We then max pool the resulting features along the sequence dimension and concatenate along the layer dimension to get the feature vector for our document $\mathbf{h}\in\mathbb{R}^{|L|\cdot d_{\text{SAE}}}$ .11
 1

 In our work we use $L=7,13,17,22$ following McDougall et al. (2025), but this can be modified as needed.

For our interpretability mask, we compute a simulation score $s_{i}\in[-1,1]$ for each feature $h_{i}\in\mathbf{h}$ using our auto-interpretation pipeline (§4.2). This score measures how faithfully a generated natural-language description predicts that feature’s activations on our training corpus. Given a global interpretability threshold $\tau$ , we construct a binary mask $\mathbf{m}\in\{0,1\}^{|L|\cdot d_{\text{SAE}}}$ where $m_{i}=\mathbb{I}[s_{i}\geq\tau]$ and apply this mask element-wise to obtain our masked feature vector $\mathbf{h}_{\tau}=\mathbf{h}\odot\mathbf{m}$ .

Finally, to obtain our prediction, we apply an $L_{1}$ -regularized logistic regression to the features.22
 2

 That is, given labeled data $\mathcal{D}=\{\mathbf{H}_{\tau},\mathbf{y}\}$ , we minimize
 $\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\beta},\lambda)=\frac{1}{N}\,\mathrm{BCE}\big(\mathbf{y},\,\sigma(\mathbf{H}_{\tau}\boldsymbol{\beta})\big)+\lambda\|\boldsymbol{\beta}\|_{1}$ where $\mathrm{BCE}$ is binary cross-entropy loss and $\sigma$ is sigmoid. The prediction for a given input is $\hat{y}=\sigma(\mathbf{h}_{\tau}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\star})$ , with $\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\star}=\operatorname*{arg\,min}_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\beta},\lambda)$ . Since each feature $h_{i}\in\mathbf{h}_{\tau}$ has a corresponding natural-language description from our auto-interpretation pipeline, we can straightforwardly explain any given prediction by listing out the top- $k$ features with the largest net contribution to the prediction along with their descriptions.33
 3

 Full training details such as preprocessing, hyperparameters, and grid searches for both our Logistic Regression model and trained SAEs are listed in Appendix B.

## 4.2 Auto-Interpretation

## Description Generation

To generate a description for a feature $h_{i}$ , we partition the training corpus into $k$ quantile bins $\mathcal{Q}_{1},\ldots,\mathcal{Q}_{k}$ based on the empirical distribution of document-level max activations of the feature, plus a bin $\mathcal{Q}_{0}$ of inactive documents. We sample a stratified group of $N$ documents across bins, crop each to a short snippet centered on the highest-activating token, and add angled-bracket highlights (e.g., <<word>>) to any token in the snippet whose activations are more than 10% of the feature’s global maximum. We then prompt gemini-3.1-flash-lite with these quantile-grouped snippets to produce a concise ( ${\leq}20$ word) description of the feature’s behavior.

## Description Validation

To validate our generated descriptions we employ a quantile-based variant of the fuzzing procedure introduced in Paulo et al. (2025). Given a generated description and a list of documents, our pipeline prompts gemini-3.1-flash-lite to predict which quantile bin (0- $k$ ) each document was sampled from. We compute the simulation score $s_{i}$ as the Spearman correlation between the predicted and true quantile assignments across all documents. In our experiments we use 10 documents for generation and 50 documents for validation. Full details including prompts, decoding strategies, and other hyperparameters are listed in Appendix D.

## 5 Experimental Setup

## 5.1 Data

## Open Domain

In this setting we train the model to detect AI generated text from an arbitrary AI model in an arbitrary domain. We train our models on the English split of the COLING shared task dataset Wang et al. (2025) a collection spanning 41 highly diverse language models paired with human texts. We use the official train and dev splits (610k and 261k documents) for training and in-distribution testing. For evaluating out-of-distribution generalization we use the official english devtest and test splits of the COLING dataset as well as the non-adversarial test split of the RAID leaderboard Dugan et al. (2024).

## Closed Domain (Paper Reviews)

For our closed setting we fine-tune models to detect AI generated academic peer reviews using the Peer Review Detection Benchmark (PRDB) Yu et al. (2026). This contains 106,413 human-written peer reviews for the NeurIPS and ICLR conferences for years 2016-2024 along with paired AI generated texts. For training we use the extended split and separate it into two sets: Human vs. GPT-4o and Human vs. Llama-3.1-70B. For evaluation we use the combined calibration and test splits, which contain held out human texts and paired generations from GPT-4o, Llama 3.1 70B, Qwen 2.5 72B, Claude Sonnet 3.5, and Gemini 1.5 Pro.

## 5.2 Baselines

## Neural (gemma-2-2b)

For our neural baseline we fine-tune the gemma-2-2B model for the AI detection task. We replace the language modeling head with a classification head and train to minimize binary cross-entropy on the AI detection task. We verify that our neural model outperforms the baseline RoBERTa model from the COLING shared task Wang et al. (2025) on both in-distribution and out-of-distribution texts. Full training details can be found in Appendix B.

## Linguistic (gram2vec)

For our stylometric baseline we fine-tune a $L_{1}$ -regularized Logistic Regression classifier on document embeddings produced by gram2vec Zeng et al. (2025). These embeddings contain over 930 features from seven linguistic categories: punctuation marks, parts of speech, morphology tags, dependency labels, function words, emojis, and transition words. These features have been shown to outperform Biber features Biber (1988) for both authorship attribution and AI detection.

## Off-the-Shelf

In addition to our trained baselines, we compare against four off-the-shelf detectors: Binoculars Hans et al. (2024), Fast-DetectGPT Bao et al. (2024), ReMoDetect Lee et al. (2024), and SpecDetect Luo et al. (2026). These detectors are not trained on COLING; we apply them zero-shot using their publicly released implementations.

## 5.3 Models

Our models are built off of GemmaScope Lieberum et al. (2024), a collection of pre-trained SAEs trained to reconstruct activations of gemma-2-2b on arbitrary text. For our core comparisons we do the following:

1. 

Base-SAE: Use the pre-trained GemmaScope SAEs without any additional fine-tuning.

2. 

FT-SAE: Fine-tune the GemmaScope SAEs to better reconstruct base gemma-2-2b’s model activations on in-domain examples from the AI detection task.

3. 

FT-Model+SAE: Fine-tune gemma-2-2b for AI detection, then fine-tune GemmaScope SAEs to reconstruct the fine-tuned model’s activations on in-domain examples from the AI detection task.

To train our SAEs we cache raw activations for all tokens in the training corpus and uniformly shuffle batches across all tokens in the dataset. For more details on our training procedure see Appendix A.

## 6 Findings

## FT-Model+SAE recovers the performance of the underlying AI detector.

In Table 1 we report the results of our detector models on the in-distribution dev split and the three held out out-of-distribution test splits for the COLING dataset. We find that in all three cases, the FT-Model+SAE condition matches the AUC and F1 score performance of the full AI detector—only losing ground once we restrict to lower false positive rate. Furthermore, when restricting to highly interpretable features ( $\tau>0.7$ ), our FT-Model+SAE system is still able to recover (and even improve) the performance of the full model despite only having a fraction of the features unmasked.

However, when the underlying detector fails to generalize, so too does our system. In Figure 4 we see that FT-Model+SAE underperforms our two base model systems by fairly wide margins on out-of-distribution data. As we will discuss in the next section, the surprising aspect of this is the strong performance of these base model systems, not the failure of the FT-Model+SAE system to surpass the performance of the underlying detector it was trained on.

## Classifiers built from base model SAEs are accurate and robust AI detectors.

In Table 1 we see that Base-SAE and FT-SAE perform very well, especially when applied to out-of-distribution data—beating well established baselines such as Binoculars Hans et al. (2024) and FastDetectGPT Bao et al. (2024) by around 8-12 F1 score. This is especially surprising for the Base-SAE condition, as the only trainable parameters in the system are the logistic regression weights.

We follow Quaremba et al. (2026) in hypothesizing that the base model has learned certain linearly separable “AI text” features during pre-training. In the next sections, we include all three models as strong detectors for testing and leave further investigation into the performance of these classifiers to future work.

## AI-generated Peer Reviews are too easy to detect.

In Table 2 we report results on PRDB and find that all trained classifiers achieve nearly perfect performance, even at very low false positive rates. This suggests the presence of confounds or other spurious features in the dataset that make the task artificially easy. This is a good use case for our pipeline. In Section 8, we will audit the specific features our PRDB detectors are using and determine whether they are abusing spurious cues.

## 7 Paraphrase Ablation

In the previous section we established that our detectors are highly accurate. However, we still need to provide causal evidence that our feature descriptions faithfully represent the underlying dynamics of the model beyond simple auto-interpretation scores. We do this in two ways: a feature-level paraphrase ablation and a document-level ablation.44
 4

 For an extended analysis of both experiments along with examples and more plots, see Appendix E.

## 7.1 Feature-Level Paraphrase Ablation

## Setup

In this experiment we want to ablate individual features and observe whether the change in downstream prediction matches what our pipeline expects. To do this we start by subsampling 500 human and 500 AI documents from both COLING and PRDB. For a given model, we run a forward pass on all documents and get all active features that contribute to the downstream prediction. For each feature we do a targeted paraphrase; we feed the text description of that feature to gemini-3.1-flash-lite and ask it to paraphrase the document to remove only that feature. We report correlation between the mean paraphrase ablated logit delta and the mean importance score per feature. If our pipeline is faithful, then the downstream logit effect of paraphrasing away a feature will exactly equal the importance score of that feature.

## Findings

Table 3 shows the results of the ablation. Across all twelve configurations, the importance score for a feature positively predicts the effect of paraphrasing it away. This effect is strongest on PRDB, where Spearman correlations reach $\rho=0.69$ – $0.81$ and 89–99% of features agree in sign between the two interventions; on COLING agreement is positive but weaker ( $\rho=0.36$ – $0.62$ ). We also see that restricting the detector to features with auto-interp score above $0.7$ improves the importance-vs-paraphrase correlation, most sharply for FT-Model+SAE ( $0.54\rightarrow 0.62$ on COLING, $0.69\rightarrow 0.80$ on PRDB).

## 7.2 Document-Level Paraphrase Ablation

## Setup

In this experiment we want to show that the set of features given by our pipeline in aggregate represent all of the features used by the model to make its prediction. To do this we conduct an adversarial humanization attack. We start by sampling 2,500 AI documents from both datasets. For each document we query gemini-3.1-flash-lite with five different prompts to get five different paraphrases. The first prompt “Baseline” simply asks the model to rewrite the text to make it more human. The second “Few Shot” includes few shot examples of human documents from the train split of the dataset. The “Guided” prompt includes instructions with features that are colloquially associated with AI and explicitly asks to remove them.

The final two prompts explicitly use our system. The “SAE” prompt includes the list of active features with their associated descriptions and importances and asks the model to remove all AI features. Finally, the “SAE x3” prompt iterates over the “SAE” prompt three times, re-querying the pipeline for the new most active features each time. We do each of these once per model condition. All prompts explicitly tell the model to edit less than 30% of tokens in the document.

## Results

In Figure 5 we report the results of the adversarial humanization. We see that our interpretable detectors are surprisingly robust to humanizing paraphrases, with the baseline flipping at most 8.7% of the documents’ predictions and frequently 0%. Next, we see that including our pipeline’s interpretable features improves flip rate substantially more than either of the generic prompts across all models. Allowing the model to iterate further increases this gap, with a success rate of 60-100% across all conditions all while maintaining a low edit distance (<40% of tokens in the document edited). While it is not definitive proof, this result does suggest that our lists of features are relatively complete and high recall—paraphrasing away existing features flips predictions rather than revealing new AI features.

## 8 Qualitative Analysis

In Table 4 we list the most predictive features for FT-Model+SAE $(\tau>0.85)$ on AI Peer Reviews, ranked by mean feature importance score (Logit $\Delta$ ). We report the top features pushing the detector towards GPT-4o and Llama (positive Logit $\Delta$ ) as well as those pushing towards Human (negative Logit $\Delta$ ). Extended tables covering all three systems on both PRDB and COLING can be found in Tables 26 through 34 in the Appendix.

We first note that the most predictive features are highly specific, frequently activating on only one or two tokens or on formatting characters and punctuation marks. For example, feature 22_4800 (“double colons following bolded markdown section headers”) appears in only 11 human documents but over 54,000 GPT-4o documents, and feature 13_164 (“opening square bracket in list or string literals”) appears in just 7 human documents but over 22,000 Llama documents.

In addition, we observe that the most predictive features for human text are largely stylistic rather than structural: first person pronouns at the start of sentences (13_5785), hedges such as “seem” or “seems” used to express an opinion (7_10672), and the word “see” used as a directive to look elsewhere in the review (7_1616). The extended tables reveal further differences between systems: on COLING, the top FT-SAE feature (13_3738) activates on “introductory phrases or filler text commonly used by ai language models when declining personal questions” (e.g., “As an AI language model”), while the top Base-SAE features are dominated by topical and domain-level signals such as HTML attributes and software license boilerplate. This suggests that detectors built on base model representations may rely more heavily on domain shortcuts than on stylistic properties of the text itself.

Given the near perfect performance of these models at detecting AI-generated peer reviews (Section 6), one could reasonably claim that such features are spurious and should not be used in detection. In their analysis, Kantamneni et al. (2025) claimed as much, and recommended all formatting based features be removed. However, it is unclear what should and should not be considered a spurious feature for the purposes of AI detection. If a formatting quirk is consistently present in AI-written text across contexts, there’s a good argument to be made that it should be included. Ultimately, the ability for a model provider to audit and decide on a case-by-case basis whether or not a particular feature should be used by a particular AI detector is a key affordance of our system.

## 9 Conclusion

In order to be able to use AI detectors in real-world high-stakes situations, they need to be auditable. It is unreasonable to expect major decisions to be made at the behest of an AI detector that cannot provide a rationale for its output. Current interpretable stylometric AI detectors underperform state-of-the-art neural AI detection methods by wide margins—making them infeasible for widespread use.

In this paper we propose a technique to take an existing black box neural AI detector and convert it into a detector that operates on human-interpretable concepts. We accomplish this by training Sparse Autoencoders on the detector’s residual stream and training a lightweight classifier over only the features that pass a given global interpretability threshold. We find that classifiers constructed in this way retain the performance and generalization properties of the underlying base model while being substantially more understandable. Extensive ablations show that our feature descriptions have high recall and high precision, faithfully representing what the features fire on and how they contribute to the final prediction.

In addition to this, we find that classifiers based on SAE decompositions of base model activations are surprisingly robust detectors of AI generated text. We find that they outperform strong well-established baselines in the AI detection literature and encourage future work to follow up with further experimentation on using internal activations of language models as input to AI detectors.

Finally, we analyze the features used by AI detectors and find that they frequently use highly specific token sequences and formatting elements which may fail to generalize to other domains and models. Our findings raise questions about which features are valid to use in an AI detection system and we encourage future work to investigate adversarial hardening techniques using this style of unsupervised concept discovery.

## Limitations

## Feature descriptions are biased towards recall

One core limitation of our work is a limitation shared by the current literature, namely that auto-interpretation pipelines systematically produce descriptions that are high recall but low precision Gao et al. (2025). For example, say an SAE feature fires on the token “Tom” but only after the token “Hello”. If our dataset only ever contains the token “Tom” in this context, then our pipeline will likely generate a description for this feature that misses the dependency. That same description would also receive a perfect auto-interpretation score by the validation pipeline for the same reason.

In their work, Gao et al. (2025) attempt to measure the effect size of this issue using Neuron2Graph explanations Foote et al. (2023) targeting specific ngrams in the training corpus. We attempted something similar in an informal capacity and asked language models to generate regular expressions that matched the generated description of a subset of our features. We measured the precision and recall using the regular expression as a ground truth and found that, while roughly half of our features did manage to get perfect descriptions (99.5+ F1 score), the other half exhibited this same recall bias to varying degrees. We believe that better sampling of hard negatives and synthetic data generation are good potential avenues to reduce this issue.

## Lack of testing on different base models

Another limitation of our work is the fact that we only test on fine-tuned GemmaScope SAEs and do not train SAEs on other base models. While other trained SAEs do exist for models like Llama and Qwen, we chose GemmaScope as it is by far the most popular and was used by previous work for AI detection Kuznetsov et al. (2025). Future work should seek to expand the breadth of base models tested, especially as it relates to the performance of the Base-SAE and FT-SAE conditions.

## Appendix A Training Sparse Autoencoders

## A.1 Hyperparameters & Training Settings

For our trained SAEs we fine-tune GemmaScope Lieberum et al. (2024) on in-domain data for the AI detection task. In Table 5 we report the hyperparameters used to train our sparse autoencoders. We initialize GemmaScope using the width_16k/canonical checkpoints from the gemma-scope-2b-pt-res-canonical release and train on the following five settings:

1. 

COLING (base) Reconstruct base Gemma-2-2B activations on the COLING train set

2. 

COLING (Open) Reconstruct activations of the Gemma-2-2B model fine-tuned to detect generic AI generated text on the COLING train set

3. 

PRDB (base) Reconstruct base Gemma-2-2B activations on the PRDB train set

4. 

PRDB (GPT-4o) Reconstruct activations of the Gemma-2-2B model fine-tuned to detect GPT-4o on the PRDB train set

5. 

PRDB (LLama) Reconstruct activations of the Gemma-2-2B model fine-tuned to detect Llama on the PRDB train set

For each setting we train four models (one for each layer 7, 13, 17, 22). When training on PRDB we do not filter by target model and train all SAEs to reconstruct activations from both GPT-4o and Llama as well as human text.

We use the JumpReLU SAE Rajamanoharan et al. (2024) architecture and rescale $\mathbf{W}_{\text{dec}}$ rows to unit norm after each update. We project out all parallel gradient components before the optimizer step following Bricken et al. (2023). Our downstream objective is the standard reconstruction loss plus a sparsity penalty without any explicit auxiliary loss terms or resampling of dead latents.

## A.2 Re-parameterization

During training, GemmaScope SAEs train to reconstruct standardized model activations of the form

 $\mathbf{d}_{\text{std}}=\frac{\mathbf{d_{\text{raw}}}}{\text{C}}-\mathbf{b}_{\text{dec}}$ 

(4)

where C is a scalar constant such that $\mathbf{E}[||\frac{\mathbf{d_{\text{raw}}}}{C}||_{2}^{2}]=1$ . Before release, these scaling parameters were folded back into the model weights so that the trained models could be applied directly to raw model activations without needing to standardize.

## A.3 Training Optimizations & Hardware

## Buffered Token Shuffling

When constructing batches for inference we want to uniformly shuffle tokens across different documents to avoid training instability. However, actually implementing this requires caching activations on the entire dataset, which requires a large amount of disk space.

To implement this, we first sample a pool of documents and bin them according to sequence length. We then run model inference on these batches and extract the per-token activations at each layer. We load these activations into an activations buffer and shuffle that buffer uniformly before sampling batches for inference. Using a buffer of 1,000,000 tokens results in a reasonably uniform shuffle across token sequences during inference without requiring us to cache activations.

To speed up training and save time caching activations, we trained all SAEs for all layers of a given model simultaneously. This limited the need for many costly forward passes of the base model.

## Hardware and Training Time

We trained using NVIDIA DGX B200 GPUs for a total of 185 GPU hours across all model conditions.

## A.4 Evaluation

In Table 6 we report the average number of active features per example (L0) and fraction of variance unexplained (FVU) achieved by our fine-tuned SAEs across each layer on their respective validation sets. We see that on average the fine-tuned model SAEs have much lower L0 and reconstruction error than the base model SAEs. This is despite the fact that in our main results, the base model SAEs performed far better on the downstream generated text detection task. This echoes findings from Karvonen et al. (2025) that show simple reconstruction-based evaluations for SAEs can fail to correlate with actual desired downstream effects.

We also observe that the fine-tuned model SAEs gradually exhibit lower L0 as we go deeper in the model. This is in contrast to the base model SAEs which roughly have similar L0 regardless of depth. This hints at potential ways in which fine-tuned models may process information internally in fundamentally different ways than base models. While a full deep dive into the representational dynamics of these models is beyond the scope of this work, we nonetheless think this is a potentially promising direction for future work.

## B.2 Training Logistic Regression Classifiers

For both the SAE model classifiers and the linguistic feature baseline, we train sparse logistic regression classifiers in PyTorch Paszke et al. (2019), minimizing binary cross-entropy with minibatch SGD with momentum. The L1 penalty is implemented as a soft-thresholding update applied to the weight vector after each optimizer step.

 $w\leftarrow\mathrm{sign}(w)\cdot\max(|w|-\eta\lambda_{1},0)$ 

(9)

which produces exact zeros in the learned weights rather than the near-zero weights produced by including the L1 term in the loss directly Parikh and Boyd (2014). We use SGD rather than Adam because, in preliminary experiments, sparsity under Adam+L1 plateaued at a high floor regardless of $\lambda_{1}$ . We report our hyperparameters in Table 7.

Because raw SAE max activations span several orders of magnitude, we normalize each feature by its root-mean-square (RMS) over the training corpus before training. Scaling without centering keeps zero activations at zero, preserving sparsity, and means the L1 penalty acts in standardized feature space. For our gram2vec baseline, whose features are dense rates rather than sparse activations, we instead fully standardize via z-scoring ( $(x-\mu)/\sigma$ ). In both cases the normalization is folded back into the saved weights and bias after training, so the resulting detectors operate directly on raw features.

To cut down on inference time, we cached max pooled activations for each SAE for each document in the training data as memory-mapped arrays on disk, gathering only the rows needed for each minibatch. This allowed us to skip having to do any forward passes of language models during training, allowing us to efficiently train on CPU.

## B.3 Training XGBoost Classifiers

## Appendix C Data

## C.1 Processing

The PRDB dataset Yu et al. (2026) consists of human and AI generated peer review text from NeurIPS and ICLR for the previous 8 years (2016-2024). During that time period, the structure of peer reviews has changed. In the early years, reviewers were often given just a single text box to write their review. However, recently conferences have been breaking up the review into different sections (e.g. “Summary of Contributions”, “Strengths and Weaknesses”, “Questions”). PRDB respects these sections and separates the text of reviews into different columns based on the section they come from. This requires us to decide on a standardized format to use when converting a peer review with multiple text fields into a pure text document.

For every document that has multiple fields, we take the name of the field (e.g. summary_of_the_paper), remove the underscores and convert it to title case. We then insert double asterisks to the left and right to simulate a bold Markdown header and add the raw text of each section directly afterwards. This is accomplished in a very quick and simple one liner f"**{title}**\n{value.strip()}\n\n".

Given that both human written and AI generated peer reviews are required to conform to this structure, adding these Markdown elements does not add any formatting artifacts that would be easily exploitable by our detectors. On the contrary, this artificially reduces the predictive power of any markdown-based features. We do this intentionally to bias our models towards using more substantive features for detection.

## Appendix E Paraphrase Ablation Details

Both ablations of §7 use gemini-3.1-flash-lite (temperature 1.0, top_p $=0.95$ , top_k $=64$ , one sample per prompt). The model returns the rewrite inside <rewrite> tags, from which we extract the paraphrase; empty responses (e.g., safety-filtered) are discarded and counted.

## E.1 Feature-Level Paraphrase Ablation

## Prompt.

Figure 14 gives the full prompt. It includes all three components of the feature description (terse summary, keywords, detailed description) and instructs the model to change only the target feature.

## Document Subsampling.

For each dataset we draw a class-balanced sample of 1,000 documents (500 human, 500 AI) from the held-out test split with a fixed seed. On PRDB the AI class is the detector’s target generator (GPT-4o); on COLING, where detectors are trained on all generators pooled, AI documents are sampled from every generator in proportion to its share of the corpus.

## Feature Sampling.

For each document we compute the zero-ablation logit delta of every active feature receiving weight in the head (in the w/ Mask condition only features with simulation score above $0.7$ can be active) and uniformly sample up to 50 of them, without replacement, as paraphrase targets. Sampling is restricted to describable features, i.e., those with an auto-interp description and a defined simulation score; this excludes features too rare for auto-interp to run and a small set of document-start artifacts whose descriptions cannot guide an edit. Sampling is seeded per document. This yields roughly 40,000–50,000 (document, feature) rewrite records per configuration.

## Measurements.

Logit deltas are measured in log-odds space with a consistent sign convention. The activation shifts $\Delta_{\text{tgt}}$ and $\Delta_{\text{non}}$ are the absolute change in a feature’s activation between original and rewrite, normalized by that feature’s mean activation over the training corpus (so $1.0$ is a shift equal to its typical activation); $\Delta_{\text{tgt}}$ is this quantity for the targeted feature and $\Delta_{\text{non}}$ its mean over all other active features. Per-feature analyses exclude features with $|\text{Ablate }\Delta|<0.01$ or sampled in fewer than 10 documents.

## Extended Results.

Figure 9 plots each feature’s zero-ablation delta against its paraphrase delta for every head: agreement is moderate on COLING ( $\rho=0.36$ – $0.62$ ), strong on PRDB ( $0.69$ – $0.81$ ), and higher with the mask in every setting. Table 10 bins features by auto-interp score; $\rho$ rises from $0.78$ in the lowest quartile to $0.87$ in the highest, so better-described features are edited away more precisely. Tables 13 and 14 list the most frequently targeted features. On PRDB rewrites are precise ( $\Delta_{\text{tgt}}$ typically $0.5$ – $1.0$ , $\Delta_{\text{non}}$ $0.02$ – $0.3$ ); on COLING the base-model SAEs’ most common features are punctuation and sentence-boundary detectors that a meaning-preserving rewrite can rarely remove, which accounts for the weaker agreement.

## E.2 Document-Level Paraphrase Ablation

## Setup.

We attack the $\tau>0.85$ logistic-regression heads of all three feature settings on COLING and PRDB (Llama-trained heads in Figure 5; GPT-4o-trained heads as a robustness check). Each detector uses the Youden threshold of its development-set evaluation, fixed before the attack; we verified that this reproduces its reported F1. Per dataset we draw 2,500 AI and 2,500 human documents with a fixed seed (30–900 Gemma tokens, so paraphrases fit the 1,024-token extractor window), shared by the dataset’s three detectors; PRDB AI documents come from the target generator only. Only AI documents are paraphrased, so false positives are held fixed and F1 moves only through recall.

## Conditions.

The three unguided prompts (Figures 15–17) are generated once per dataset and scored by all of its detectors; they share an edit budget of “well under 20%” of the words. Few Shot draws three human training-split documents per target from the same sub-source (COLING) or conference (PRDB), length-matched and, for PRDB, from papers absent from the test split. Guided adds six generic rules against machine-writing habits written from general knowledge, independent of any detector. The SAE prompt (Figures 18, 19) is detector-specific: it lists the ten largest AI-direction and ten largest human-direction feature contributions with their descriptions, keywords, signed contributions and the three passages where each fires most strongly, states the max-pooling counting rule, sets a target equal to the score’s distance above the threshold plus 3, and asks for a plan before the rewrite. SAE $\times 3$ repeats this up to three times, recomputing the evidence on each rewrite and re-prompting only documents still classified as AI; a document is frozen at the first pass that flips. Failed generations were rare (0–6 per condition) and are excluded.

## Metrics.

F1 at the frozen threshold with human scores fixed; attack success rate (ASR), the share of base-detected AI documents classified human after the attack; and Lev, the word-level Levenshtein distance to the original as a share of the longer text. For SAE $\times 3$ all quantities are measured on the text at which a document was frozen, so Lev is cumulative. Uncertainty is a 1,000-sample bootstrap over AI documents. Table 23 reports all metrics for every detector and condition.

## Extended Results.

The feature-guided attack is also the most economical: one SAE pass edits 6–29% of words and three passes 11–38%, against 24–39% for the unguided prompts, while flipping two to ten times as many documents. The one exception is FT-SAE on PRDB (ASR 3.4% after one pass, 21.8% after three), whose evidence is spread over about 150 densely firing base-model features that no ten-feature list captures. The GPT-4o-trained PRDB heads behave like the Llama-trained ones (ASR after three passes 69%, 20%, 83% vs. 60%, 22%, 97%), so the conclusions do not depend on the generator. Because the attack only needs to cross the frozen threshold, AUROC degrades far less than F1 (e.g., 0.96 to 0.82 for FT-Model+SAE on COLING).

## Appendix F Interface

In Figure 10 we show a screenshot of the interface we built for our auditable detectors. The main window accepts raw text input and is where users would copy-paste in their text to be run through the detector. Once the user hits enter, we run the base model, extract SAE activations from the four hook points, and run the classification head to get the final prediction.

On the right we visualize the model’s prediction as well as a break down of the top most influential features for the model’s decision towards both AI and Human text. Each feature card includes the description generated by our auto-interpretation pipeline and it’s associated score (§2.2), the net logit contribution of the feature, and the number of instances of that feature appearing on both the human and AI side of our training data.

For each feature, we localize it in the main text by highlight the tokens on which the feature fires. We shade tokens in proportion to how strongly the feature is firing. Hovering over a feature card will temporarily remove highlights from all other features to allow for individual feature inspection. Hovering over highlighted tokens will reveal a popout window with a more detailed description of the feature and a list of common keywords. We allow users to dynamically modify the target $\tau$ they would like to run inference on to help control the desired accuracy-interpretability tradeoff.

We’re studying neurons in a neural network. Each neuron activates on some particular word or concept in a short document.

Each neuron is highly sparse, and will only activate on a small number of documents (and possibly a small number of words within each document).

There are a range of possible hypotheses, for example the neuron could activate on:

- specific syntactic features, e.g. punctuation like ")", ".", ":" or markdown like "**", or code syntax

- a specific set of words or phrases, e.g. "protest" or "protesters" (or other-language variants)

- specific words in particular contexts, e.g. "security" when it’s used in the context of the bigram "social security"

- specific situations contexts, e.g. dialogue in online chat forums

- broader concepts, e.g. discussion of political statistics

You will be given a list of documents, sorted into groups according to the maximum strength of the activation across tokens in that document. Based on this, you will be asked to explain what this neuron is activating on.

For each sequence, the strongest activating words are indicated with <<...>> - note that these tags "<<" and ">>" are not part of the actual sentence, they’re just used to highlight the places where the neuron is most active.

You’ll be asked for several different kinds of explanations, which you should enclose in tags like <explanation_type>...</explanation_type> (remember to close all open tags!). In all cases, your explanation should be maximally specific and but still capture all the relevant information.

The different types of explanations you should provide are:

- <terse>: A short explanation, limited to 20 words or less. This explanation should be shorthand, omitting punctuation and formatting and capitalization. You can omit prefixes like ’This neuron fires on’, instead describe it directly.

- <keywords>: A comma-separated list of up to 3 key words or short phrases that activate this neuron, in quotation marks ’...’. Note that this should be actual examples where the neuron might fire, not descriptions of those examples (so it doesn’t have to literally be single words, although often it will be).

- <long>: A comprehensive but concise explanation of what this neuron activates on. This should be 3 sentences: the first describes the concept briefly, the second describes what words or phrases most commonly activate it in the top activating documents, and the third describes what words or phrases cause it to also activate (but weaker) in some lower quantiles. If only one word or phrase activates the neuron within the document, make sure you specify which one (rather than just saying what kinds of documents activate it). Don’t use overly formal language, be as simple and concise as possible. Don’t include lists of more than 5 words or phrases in a row. Note that you don’t have to list words if it’s not relevant for concisely explaining the neuron.

So your final answer should look like: <terse>...</terse><keywords>...</keywords><long>...</long>

Some final instructions:

- Try to be specific in your explanations, although not so specific that you exclude most/all of the examples from matching your explanation.

- Pay attention to things like the capitalization and punctuation of the activating words or concepts, only if that seems relevant (e.g. if the activating words were all proper nouns).

- We’re dealing with sentences that have been tokenized, so the neuron might only be active on tokens from a tokenized word rather than full words or phrases.

- Some neurons are token-level (i.e. activating on just a few specific words or very short phrases), some are concept-level (activating on most or all words in a sentence, if that sentence fits a particular class). In your long explanations, make sure you take this into account.

Counting rule: the detector keeps only the STRONGEST occurrence of each feature. A feature stops counting only when every passage where it fires has been rewritten so the feature no longer applies; rewriting some of its passages and leaving others earns nothing.

## Your target

The detector’s score for a document is the sum of the feature contributions below plus a fixed constant, and it labels the document "AI" when the score is above its decision boundary. In this document it sees {ai_mass} of AI evidence and {human_mass} of human evidence, and the score currently stands {gap} above the boundary. To flip the verdict with a comfortable margin you must lower the score by at least {target}: remove AI evidence, add human evidence, or both. Removing the listed AI features completely is the most reliable way to get there.

{evidence}

## Rules

- For every AI feature you remove, rewrite ALL of its marked passages (see the counting rule). Start with the largest contributions.

- Change as few words as possible: edit the marked passages and what a substitution needs, and leave every other sentence exactly as it was. A good edit typically touches well under 20% of the words.

- Preserve the original meaning, topic, level of detail and length (about the same number of words); the result must read as natural, fluent text.

- You are graded on two things: how far the detector’s score moves toward "human", and the percentage of tokens you swap, which must stay low.

- Do not add commentary, disclaimers or any text about this task.

First, inside <plan></plan> tags, write a short plan: for each feature you target, list its marked passages and the exact replacement wording you will use, then add up the contributions you expect to remove and check that the total reaches the target.

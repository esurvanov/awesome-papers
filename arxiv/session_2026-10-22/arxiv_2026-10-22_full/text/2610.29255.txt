Abstract

Masked diffusion language models have emerged as an alternative to autoregressive models, enabling parallel token generation. However, standard masked diffusion language models use a shared noise schedule across positions without explicitly encoding which positions should be resolved first. Many tasks exhibit a natural priority among positions: document titles and section headings can guide the generation of body text, while Sudoku cells determined directly from the initial clues can help resolve more difficult cells. To incorporate this notion of priority (or hierarchy) among token positions, we introduce the Hierarchical Masked Diffusion Language Model (HMDLM), which augments each token position with a hierarchy level available as part of the training data, and requires zero or minimal additional annotations. It factors generation into two passes: the first predicts a hierarchy distribution over masked positions, the second predicts token distributions conditioned on it. Giving each hierarchy level its own noise schedule induces a soft, controllable preference for revealing higher-priority positions earlier while keeping decoding fully parallel. Our formulation is mathematically grounded, and we re-derive the forward and reverse processes and the NELBO for this factorization. Our evaluation shows that for the task of language generation on LM1B, HMDLM reduces perplexity by more than 1.5 points over Anchored Diffusion Language Model (ADLM), a recently proposed strong diffusion baseline; on WikiHow, we see a drop in perplexity
of upto 4.4 pts on ADLM and upto 7 pts against MDLM (Masked Diffusion Language Model). A LLM judge on WikiHow, prefers HMDLM generations (the two variants combined) more than $2\times$ as often as the baseline.
On the task of solving Sudoku puzzles requiring multi-step reasoning, it improves solution accuracy by up to $8.6\%$ absolute points over existing diffusion baselines.

## 1 Introduction

Large language models (LLMs) have revolutionized the AI space, from general-purpose text generation to solving complex problems such as mathematical reasoning and theorem proving. Despite their success, limitations remain. One of the criticisms of modern-day LLMs, which are largely auto-regressive in nature, is that they generate text only in a strictly left-to-right manner. It has been argued that this style of decoding has specific disadvantages, such as not being able to correct past mistakes, and involving large computational costs. As a remedy, recent literature has proposed another class of models, referred to as Diffusion Models (Austin et al., 2021; Lou et al., 2024; Sahoo et al., 2024; Nie et al., 2025c), which do not subscribe to the left-to-right generation style, but rather generate multiple tokens in parallel. The formulation is based on a forward diffusion process, which translates the text into noise, and on learning the reverse process, can generate the desired text by iteratively denoising from complete noise. This line of work has shown significant promise for the future of language generation, both for increasing computational efficiency and solving complex tasks (e.g., solving Sudoku or generating structured text).

A key open question in masked diffusion language models is how to choose the order in which positions should be denoised. Standard masked diffusion treats token positions symmetrically: with a shared masking schedule and no task-dependent notion of priority. Recent work shows that token ordering and decoding strategies can substantially affect generation quality (Kim et al., 2025; Garg et al., 2025; Hong et al., 2026; Fox et al., 2026). Recent approaches address this by identifying important anchor tokens (Rout et al., 2025; Xue et al., 2026), or adding planning-aware training and decoding mechanisms (Peng et al., 2025; Israel et al., 2025). These methods suggest that not all positions should be treated equally, but none provides a general hierarchy-aware forward and backward diffusion process. Such priority is often available: many problems carry a natural hierarchy over positions. In document generation, titles and section headings or summary often provide high-level semantic structure before the surrounding body text. In Sudoku, more constrained cells are easier to fill first, and their priority belongs to the position, not the digit it will contain.

We introduce HMDLM, a hierarchical extension of masked diffusion language models that associates each token position with a hierarchy variable.
During the forward process, both the token and its hierarchy state are diffused toward a masked state. During the reverse process, the model first predicts the hierarchy for masked positions and then predicts token identities conditioned on the predicted hierarchy. This two-stage factorization allows the model to learn which hierarchy level each position belongs to before decoding its token value. Unlike anchor-based methods (Rout et al., 2025), which tie importance to token identities, HMDLM assigns hierarchy to positions, with a separate noise schedule per level to control how fast each level is unmasked (see Section 4.3.2). We also derive the negative evidence lower bound (NELBO) for this process which serves as our training objective 4.

We experimentally validate HMDLM across several benchmark domains. On language modeling benchmarks like LM1B, we train the model from scratch and compare with diffusion baselines (like MDLM (Sahoo et al., 2024), SEDD (Lou et al., 2024), the Anchored Diffusion Language Model (ADLM) (Rout et al., 2025), and DiffusionBERT (He et al., 2023)) on both perplexity and generation quality. On Wikihow, we fine-tune on pretrained checkpoints and compare with ADLM and MDLM on perplexity and qualitative generation. On Sudoku, we train from scratch, and more reliably produce accurate solutions than the diffusion baselines (like MGDM (Ye et al., 2025) and MDLM). These results demonstrate that explicitly modeling hierarchy over token positions can improve masked diffusion generation across both natural language and structured domains.

Our work makes the following contributions: (a) We present a novel formulation for incorporating the notion of hierarchy in the discrete diffusion models, referred to as HMDLM (b) We re-formulate the standard diffusion formulation to have a two stage training and inference process, where the first stage predicts distribution over hierarchy levels, and second one predicts token distribution given the hierarchy prediction. (c) We re-derive the ELBO to incorporate the notion of hierarchy in the forward and backward diffusion process. (d) We present experiments showing gains over masked diffusion baselines in perplexity and generation quality on LM1B and WikiHow, and report accuracy on Sudoku. We also plan to release the code for HMDLM on paper acceptance.

## 2 Related Work

Masked diffusion language models:

Diffusion models have recently been adapted to language generation as an alternative to left-to-right autoregressive modeling. Early approaches explored diffusion in continuous word-vector spaces, as in Diffusion-LM (Li et al., 2022), or directly over discrete tokens, as in D3PMs (Austin et al., 2021) with structured transition matrices and absorbing [MASK] states. More recent masked diffusion language models formulate generation as iterative denoising from partially masked sequences: the forward process gradually replaces tokens with [MASK], and the reverse model reconstructs the sequence from bidirectional context. SEDD (Lou et al., 2024), reparameterized discrete diffusion (Zheng et al., 2023), DiffusionBERT (He et al., 2023), MD4 (Shi et al., 2024) and MDLM (Sahoo et al., 2024) improve the objectives and sampling procedures for this setting, while large-scale systems such as MDM (Nie et al., 2025a), LLaDA (Nie et al., 2025b) and LLaDA2.0 (Bie et al., 2025) show that masked diffusion can support instruction-following LLMs. However, these models typically employ a uniform masking schedule, ignoring the inherent semantic or structural hierarchy of the task.

Generation order and decoding strategies:

Masked diffusion is attractive because generation need not proceed left-to-right; tokens can be revealed in arbitrary or partially parallel orders. This flexibility also makes the choice of denoising order important. Prior work shows that adaptive or learned ordering can significantly improve masked diffusion, through inference-time token ordering (Kim et al., 2025), planner-aware training (Peng et al., 2025), planned generation where the model first constructs a high-level plan and then denoises the planned spans in parallel (Israel et al., 2025), variational order learning (Fox et al., 2026), or unified learnable-order formulations (Hong et al., 2026). Other methods (Wang et al., 2025; Huang et al., 2025) modify sampling trajectories to address irreversibility or decoding bias. Our work is complementary: rather than learning a free-form position policy or adding an external planner, HMDLM encodes a task-dependent hierarchy in the diffusion state itself and uses per-level schedules to induce a controllable soft generation order.

Anchoring, planning, and structured priors:

Several recent works also argue that diffusion language models benefit from identifying important tokens or high-level structure before completing the full sequence. Anchored Diffusion Language Models (Rout et al., 2025) observe that masking important words early can remove useful context, and use a two-stage model that predicts anchor-token distributions before predicting missing tokens. Planned Diffusion (Israel et al., 2025) first constructs an explicit plan and then generates spans in parallel. In code generation, AnCoder (Xue et al., 2026) uses syntax-tree priors to prioritize keywords and identifiers, and code-focused diffusion models such as DiffuCoder and Dream-Coder further show the value of domain-specific structure (Gong et al., 2025; Xie et al., 2025). These works share our motivation that not all sequence positions should be treated equally. However, HMDLM differs by attaching hierarchy to positions rather than only token identities, supporting multiple task-defined hierarchy levels, and using these levels to control both the forward corruption process and the reverse denoising dynamics through level-specific schedules.

## 3 Background

## 3.1 Notations

Let ${x}$ denote a sequence of discrete tokens each token $x^{i}$ is represented as one-hot column vector, and $\mathcal{V}=\{x^{i}\in\{0,1\}^{D}:\sum_{j=1}^{D}x^{i}[j]=1\}$ at position $i$ as the set of all such vectors. Define $Cat(.;\pi)$ as the categorical distribution over $\mathcal{V}$ classes, where $\pi\in\mathbb{R}^{D}$ and $\sum_{d}\pi[d]=1$ . Also we assume a special $[MASK]$ token, $m\in\mathcal{V}$ and treat it as the $D^{th}$ category, i.e., $m[D]=1$ . Let $x_{t}$ denote the state of the diffusion process at time step $t$ . The following description follows the treatment provided in Sahoo et al. (2024).

## 3.2 Discrete Diffusion Models

Forward process.
Discrete diffusion process is defined using a forward process that gradually corrupts data over $T$ time steps, and replaces each token with a special MASK token. Let $t\in\{0,1,\ldots,T\}$ , with $t=0$ corresponding to clean data and $t=T$ to fully masked data, and $x_{t}^{i}\in\mathcal{V}$ denote progressively noisier latent variables where the timestep $t$ ranges from $0$ (zero noise) to $T$ (maximum noise). The forward noising process $q$ at time $t$ and position $i$ is defined as:

 $q(x_{t}^{i}\mid x_{0}^{i})=\mathrm{Cat}\!\left(x_{t}^{i};\alpha_{t}x_{0}^{i}+(1-\alpha_{t})m\right),$ 

(1)

where $x_{0}^{i}$ is the clean state, $\alpha_{t}$ is the masking schedule where $\alpha_{t}\in[0,1]$ is a strictly decreasing function in $t$ , with $\alpha_{0}=1$ and $\alpha_{T}=0$ controlling the proportion of unmasked tokens at time $t$ . The corresponding posterior transition with $\lambda_{t}=\frac{\alpha_{t-1}-\alpha_{t}}{1-\alpha_{t}}$ is:

 $q(x_{t-1}^{i}\mid x_{t}^{i},x_{0}^{i})=\begin{cases}\mathrm{Cat}(x_{t-1}^{i};x_{t}^{i}),&x_{t}^{i}\neq m,\\
\mathrm{Cat}\!\left(x_{t-1}^{i};(1-\lambda_{t})m+\lambda_{t}x_{0}^{i}\right),&x_{t}^{i}=m.\end{cases}$ 

(2)

Reverse process.
The reverse process iteratively denoises the data from timestep $t$ to $t-1$ until the sequence becomes fully unmasked. Diffusion model aims to approximate the reverse process by learning a backward model $p_{\theta}(x_{t-1}^{i}|x_{t}^{i})$ . Since the true posterior depends on the unknown clean token $x_{0}^{i}$ , it is approximated with a neural network $x^{i}_{\theta}(x_{t},t)\in\Delta^{\mathcal{V}}$ , which predicts the clean token distribution from the noisy input $x_{t}^{i}$ at time $t$ . During the reverse diffusion process, unmasked tokens stay fixed, and clean inputs are never masked:

 $p_{\theta}(x_{t-1}^{i}\mid x_{t})=\begin{cases}\mathrm{Cat}(x_{t-1}^{i};x_{t}^{i}),&x_{t}^{i}\neq m,\\
\mathrm{Cat}\!\left(x_{t-1}^{i};(1-\lambda_{t})m+\lambda_{t}\,x^{i}_{\theta}(x_{t},t)\right),&x_{t}^{i}=m.\end{cases}$ 

(3)

NELBO.
The denoising model $x_{\theta}({x_{t}},t)$ is trained using the following Negative ELBO:

 $\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{NELBO}}({x};\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{q,t}\!\left[-\log p_{\theta}({x_{0}}\mid{x_{1}})+T\left(\frac{\alpha_{t}-\alpha_{t-1}}{1-\alpha_{t}}\log\langle x_{\theta}(x_{t},t),x_{0}\rangle\right)\right]$ 

(4)

## 4 Hierarchical Masked Diffusion Language Model

In this section we introduce the Hierarchical Masked Diffusion Language Model
(HMDLM), which extends discrete diffusion models by incorporating a notion of
hierarchy into both the forward and backward processes. Our framework augments
each token position with a hierarchy level. Let $h_{0}^{i}$ denote the gold hierarchy at position $i$ , represented as a one-hot vector over $K$ levels, i.e., $h_{0}^{i}\in\{0,1\}^{K}$ . We introduce a distinct hierarchy mask state $h_{\mathrm{m}}$ , giving $K{+}1$ possible hierarchy states.
Positions to be unmasked at time step $t$ are governed by the level dependent masking schedule $\alpha_{k}(t)$ defined for time step $t\in\{0,\ldots,T\}$ and level $k\in\{0,\ldots,K-1\}$ which is a decreasing function. If a position $i$ at time step $t$ is sampled or is already unmasked, we then set $b_{t}^{i}=0$ and $b_{t}^{i}=1$ otherwise.

 $s_{t}^{i}=(x_{t}^{i},h_{t}^{i})=(x_{0}^{i},h_{0}^{i})\ \text{if }b_{t}^{i}=0\ (\text{revealed}),\qquad(m,h_{\mathrm{m}})\ \text{if }b_{t}^{i}=1\ (\text{masked}).$ 

(5)

Since $b_{t}^{i}$ is shared, a token and its hierarchy level are always masked or
revealed together, ruling out decoupled states such as a masked token with a
revealed level.

## 4.1 Forward and Reverse Process

Forward Process
The forward process is a fixed Markov chain, that progressively masks tokens from the initial state $s_{0}$ to a noisy state $s_{t}$ at each time step $t$ over $T$ time steps, according to the hierarchy conditioned noise schedule $\alpha_{k}(t)$ . Tokens at lower hierarchy levels are masked more aggressively at early time-steps, than tokens at higher hierarchy levels. The forward marginal at a single token position $i$ with hierarchy $k$ and joint state $s^{i}_{\mathrm{t}}=(x^{i}_{\mathrm{t}},h^{i}_{\mathrm{t}})$ is given by:

 $q(s_{t}^{i}\mid s_{0}^{i})=\text{Cat}\left(s_{t}^{i};\,\alpha_{h_{0}^{i}}(t)\,s_{0}^{i}+(1-\alpha_{h_{0}^{i}}(t))\,{s_{m}}\right)$ 

(6)

At $t=T$ , all tokens are fully masked regardless of the hierarchy level, i.e. $\alpha_{k}(T)=0$ .
Let $\lambda_{k}(t)=\frac{\alpha_{k}(t-1)-\alpha_{k}(t)}{1-\alpha_{k}(t)}$ denote the probability that a masked level- $k$ position is revealed at step $t$ , with $\lambda_{k}(t)=0$ when $\alpha_{k}(t)=1$ . The true joint posterior is:

 $q(s_{t-1}^{i}\mid s_{t}^{i},s_{0}^{i})=\begin{cases}\mathrm{Cat}(s_{t-1}^{i};s_{t}^{i}),&s_{t}^{i}\neq\mathbf{s}_{m},\\
\mathrm{Cat}\!\left(s_{t-1}^{i};(1-\lambda_{h_{0}^{i}}(t))\mathbf{s}_{m}+\lambda_{h_{0}^{i}}(t)s_{0}^{i}\right),&s_{t}^{i}=\mathbf{s}_{m}.\end{cases}$ 

(7)

Reverse Process

The reverse process aims to recover the clean sequence $s_{0}$ from a fully masked sequence $s_{T}$ over $T$ denoising steps. We approximate the reverse process $p_{\theta}(s_{t-1}\mid s_{t},t)$ using the neural network ${s}_{\theta}(s_{t},t)$ , which is composed of two joined networks - the hierarchy head, $h_{\theta}(x_{t},h_{t},t)$ and the token head, $x_{\theta}(x_{t},h_{t-1},t)$ . It takes as input the noisy state $s_{t}$ =( $x_{t}$ , $h_{t}$ ) at timestep $t$ and predicts the clean joint state $s_{0}$ .

Revealed positions are carried over unchanged, $p_{\theta}(s_{t-1}^{i}\mid s_{t}^{i})=\mathrm{Cat}(s_{t-1}^{i};s_{t}^{i})$ if $s_{t}^{i}\neq\mathbf{s}_{m}$ . For a masked position ( $s_{t}^{i}=\mathbf{s}_{m}$ ), the model first draws the hierarchy state and then the token conditioned on it:

 $\displaystyle p_{\theta}\bigl(h_{t-1}^{i}=k\mid x_{t},h_{t}\bigr)$ 
 $\displaystyle=\lambda_{k}(t)\,g_{k}^{i}(t),$ 

 $\displaystyle k\in\{0,\dots,K-1\},$ 

(8)

 $\displaystyle p_{\theta}\bigl(h_{t-1}^{i}=h_{\mathrm{m}}\mid x_{t},h_{t}\bigr)$ 
 $\displaystyle=1-\pi_{i}(t),$ 

 $\displaystyle p_{\theta}\bigl(x_{t-1}^{i}\mid x_{t},h_{t-1}\bigr)$ 
 $\displaystyle=\mathrm{Cat}\bigl(x_{t-1}^{i};\,f^{i}(t)\bigr),$ 

 $\displaystyle h_{t-1}^{i}\neq h_{\mathrm{m}},$ 

 $\displaystyle p_{\theta}\bigl(x_{t-1}^{i}=m\mid x_{t},h_{t-1}\bigr)$ 
 $\displaystyle=1,$ 

 $\displaystyle h_{t-1}^{i}=h_{\mathrm{m}},$ 

where $g^{i}(t)=\mathrm{softmax}\bigl(h_{\theta}^{i}(x_{t},h_{t},t)\bigr)$ is the hierarchy head’s distribution over the $K$ levels, $f^{i}(t)=\mathrm{softmax}\bigl(x_{\theta}^{i}(x_{t},h_{t-1},t)\bigr)$ is the token head’s distribution over the vocabulary, and
 $\pi_{i}(t)=\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}\lambda_{k}(t)\,g_{k}^{i}(t)=\mathbb{E}_{k\sim g^{i}(t)}\bigl[\lambda_{k}(t)\bigr]$ , is the total probability that position $i$ is revealed at step $t$ . Since the gold hierarchy $h_{0}^{i}$ is unknown in the reverse process, $\pi_{i}(t)$ replaces the true reveal probability $\lambda_{h_{0}^{i}}(t)$ of equation 7 by its expectation under the predicted hierarchy distribution. Because $\lambda_{k^{\prime}}(t)\geq\lambda_{k}(t)$ for $k^{\prime}>k$ , a masked position is more likely to be revealed at a higher level than at a lower one even when $g^{i}(t)$ is uniform, which is what induces the soft generation order (Appendix D).

## 4.2 ELBO

We derive the discrete time ELBO 9 and its continuous-time limit 76 for the generative process $p_{\theta}(s_{0})$ . The detailed derivation is given in the Appendix A.
The discrete ELBO is given as:

 $\log p_{\theta}(\mathbf{s}_{0})\;\geq\;\underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{q}\!\bigl[\log p_{\theta}(\mathbf{s}_{0}\mid\mathbf{s}_{1})\bigr]}_{L_{0}}-\sum_{t=2}^{T}\underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{q}\!\Bigl[D_{\mathrm{KL}}\bigl(q(\mathbf{s}_{t-1}\mid\mathbf{s}_{t},\mathbf{s}_{0})\;\|\;p_{\theta}(\mathbf{s}_{t-1}\mid\mathbf{s}_{t})\bigr)\Bigr]}_{L_{t}}.$ 

(9)

Each $L_{t}$ is a KL over the state $(\mathbf{s}_{t})=(x_{t},h_{t})$ .
By the chain rule of KL divergence, $L_{t}$ can be expanded as:

 $\displaystyle L_{t}$ 
 $\displaystyle=\underbrace{D_{\mathrm{KL}}\bigl(q(\mathbf{h}_{t-1}\mid\mathbf{x}_{t},\mathbf{h}_{t},\mathbf{x}_{0},\mathbf{h}_{0})\;\|\;p_{\theta}(\mathbf{h}_{t-1}\mid\mathbf{x}_{t},\mathbf{h}_{t})\bigr)}_{\text{hierarchy KL (Pass 1)}}$ 

 $\displaystyle\quad+\underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{q(\mathbf{h}_{t-1}\mid\mathbf{x}_{t},\mathbf{h}_{t},\mathbf{x}_{0},\mathbf{h}_{0})}\!\Bigl[D_{\mathrm{KL}}\bigl(q(\mathbf{x}_{t-1}\mid\mathbf{x}_{t},\mathbf{h}_{t-1},\mathbf{x}_{0},\mathbf{h}_{0})\;\|\;p_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{t-1}\mid\mathbf{x}_{t},\mathbf{h}_{t-1})\bigr)\Bigr]}_{\text{token KL (Pass 2)}}.$ 

(10)

The KL divergence splits into two terms 10, which we model using two stages in our architecture introduced in 4.3.

## 4.3 Model Architecture

## 4.3.1 Two Stage Model

Figure 1: HMDLM inference example. Positions $i=0,3,5$ are masked in $(x_{t},h_{t})$ . Step $t$ : Stage 1 reveals $h_{t-1}^{5}=1$ , then Stage 2 predicts $x_{t-1}^{5}=\text{ball}$ . Step $t-1$ : Stage 1 reveals $h_{t-2}^{0}=0$ and $h_{t-2}^{3}=0$ , then Stage 2 predicts $x_{t-2}^{0}=\text{The}$ and $x_{t-2}^{3}=\text{the}$ .

To model the factorized posterior, we introduce the following two stage architecture 1.

First Stage: Given the partially denoised sequence $(x_{t},h_{t})$ at time step $t$ , the first stage predicts the hierarchy distribution $g^{i}(t)$ at every masked position $i\in\mathcal{M}_{t}$ , where $\mathcal{M}_{t}$ is the set of masked positions at time $t$ ; together with the noise schedule, this defines $p_{\theta}\!\left(h_{t-1}^{i}\mid x_{t},h_{t}\right)$ in equation 8.
Masked positions are fed the masked state $\mathbf{s}_{m}$ as input to Stage I during both training and inference. During training, unmasked positions are provided with their gold pairs $(x_{0}^{i},h_{0}^{i})$ via teacher forcing. During inference, they are provided with the token and hierarchy sampled at the step when the position was first revealed, which remain fixed thereafter.
At each inference step $t$ , every masked position $i$ draws its next hierarchy state from equation 8: it is revealed at level $k\in\{0,\dots,K-1\}$ with probability $\lambda_{k}(t)\,g^{i}_{k}(t)$ and stays masked with probability $1-\pi_{i}(t)$ . This single draw decides both whether position $i$ is revealed at this step and at which level, which forms the set of revealed positions $\mathcal{U}_{t}\subseteq\mathcal{M}_{t}$ .

Second Stage: The second stage predicts the token distribution at each masked position $i$ at time step $t$ : $p_{\theta}(x_{t-1}^{i}\mid x_{t},h_{t-1})$ . For masked positions, it receives as input $(x_{t},h_{t-1})$ with the hierarchy at the reveal set filled in: positions $i\in\mathcal{U}_{t}$ are assigned gold hierarchy $h_{0}^{i}$ , $(m,h_{0}^{i})$ during training and the Stage-I sample $(m,h_{t-1}^{i})$ during inference. For remaining masked positions, we pass the mask state $s_{m}$ = (m, $h_{m}$ ) as input. For unmasked positions, the token and hierarchy are copied directly from $x_{t}$ and $h_{t}$ without modification:
 $p_{\theta}(s_{t-1}^{i}\mid s_{t}^{i})=\mathrm{Cat}\left(s_{t-1}^{i};\,s_{t}^{i}\right)\quad\text{if }s_{t}^{i}\neq\mathbf{s_{m}}$ during inference and passed $s^{i}_{0}$ =( $x^{i}_{0}$ , $h^{i}_{0}$ ) during training. Both stages use weight tying, with separate output heads (hierarchy predictor head and token predictor head) attached to the final hidden layer and a softmax applied at the end of each head. This design reduces the parameter count while ensuring that both stages benefit from the same rich contextual representation.

Note: Across iterations, the token and hierarchy at each position, $s_{t}^{i}=(x_{t}^{i},h_{t}^{i})$ , are masked and unmasked jointly through a shared Bernoulli variable 5 . Within a single iteration, however, Stage 1 predicts the hierarchy before Stage 2 predicts the token. The partially unmasked state $(m,\,h_{t-1}^{i})$ with $h_{t-1}^{i}\neq h_{\mathrm{m}}$ therefore arises only between the two stages of the same iteration.

## 4.3.2 Per-level Noise Schedule and Hierarchy Embeddings

We use a different masking schedule for each hierarchy level $k$ . Tokens that are more informative are given a higher hierarchy level and a slower masking schedule. Consequently, during the forward noising process, these tokens are masked more slowly than lower level tokens, preserving them for longer. They are denoised faster than other tokens during the reverse de-noising process. These tokens are unmasked earlier than lower-hierarchy tokens, allowing the model to condition on higher level tokens when predicting the remaining masked positions.

The hierarchy of a position is represented by a row of the learnable hierarchy embedding matrix $W\in\mathbb{R}^{(K+1)\times H}$ , where $K$ is the number of hierarchy levels and
 $H$ is the hidden dimension. Row $W_{k}$ is the embedding of level $k\in\{0,\dots,K-1\}$ , and the last row $W_{K}$ is the embedding of the hierarchy mask state $h_{\mathrm{m}}$ . The hierarchy embedding for each position $i$ is added to the token embedding at position $i$ and fed as input to the backbone.

## 4.4 Training and Inference

Training
The HMDLM model is trained end-to-end across both the passes. We sample a time step $t$ , and mask each token independently according to the token’s hierarchy level masking schedule. The input to the Stage 1 is $(x_{t},h_{t})$ and it predicts the hierarchy probabilities - $p_{\theta}(h_{t-1}^{i}\mid x_{t},h_{t})$ , we pass updated $(x_{t},h_{t-1})$ to Stage 2 to get token distribution - $p_{\theta}(x_{t-1}^{i}\mid x_{t},h_{t-1})$ for each masked position. The final training loss is governed by:

 $\mathcal{L}=\mathbb{E}_{t,q}\!\left[\sum_{i=1}^{L}\mathbf{1}[x_{t}^{i}=m]\,\Bigl(\lambda^{\prime}_{h_{0}^{i}}(t)\,\bigl(-\log f_{x_{0}^{i}}^{i}(t)-\beta_{\mathrm{h}}\log g_{h_{0}^{i}}^{i}(t)\bigr)+\beta_{\mathrm{e}}\bigl({\textstyle\sum_{k}g^{i}_{k}(t)\,\lambda_{k}^{\prime}(t)}-\lambda_{h_{0}^{i}}^{\prime}(t)\bigr)\Bigr)\right]$ 

(11)

where $\beta_{h}$ and $\beta_{e}$ are tunable hyperparameters, $\lambda_{k}^{\prime}(t)=\frac{-\alpha_{k}^{\prime}(t)}{1-\alpha_{k}(t)}$ , and $h_{0}^{i}$ is the gold hierarchy and $x_{0}^{i}$ is the gold token, $g^{i}_{k}(t)$ and $f^{i}_{v}(t)$ are the hierarchy-head and token-head probabilities of equation 8 at position $i$ . The full training procedure is given in Algorithm 3.

Inference

During the reverse process, we start from a fully masked sequence and iteratively unmask tokens at each time step $t$ . At time step $t=T$ all the tokens are $[MASK]$ tokens and hence are assigned masked state, $s_{m}$ . In Stage I, we sample hierarchy value for each masked token position $i$ at time step $t$ from the predicted hierarchy probabilities as:

 $\displaystyle h^{i}_{t-1}\sim\mathrm{Cat}\!\bigl(\lambda_{0}(t)\,g_{0}^{i}(t),\ \dots,\ \lambda_{K-1}(t)\,g_{K-1}^{i}(t),\ \underbrace{1-\pi_{i}(t)}_{\text{stay masked}}\bigr)$ 

(12)

with $g^{i}(t)$ and $\pi_{i}(t)$ as in equation 8, and $\lambda_{k}(t)$ the per-step reveal probability of level $k$ . Higher levels are masked more slowly, and for the schedules we use $\lambda_{k}(t)\leq\lambda_{k^{\prime}}(t)$ for $k<k^{\prime}$ at every step at which level $k^{\prime}$ is still masked (Appendix D). A position predicted to be at level $k^{\prime}$ therefore receives a larger reveal mass $\lambda_{k^{\prime}}(t)\,g^{i}_{k^{\prime}}(t)$ and is revealed earlier. The full inference algorithm is given in the Appendix (Algorithm 2).

## 5 Experiments

In this section, we evaluate the proposed Hierarchical Masked Diffusion Language Model (HMDLM) along two axes:
(1) its ability to model language distributions, measured via perplexity, and
(2) its effectiveness as a puzzle solver, measured by accuracy. We consider three representative benchmarks: the One Billion Word Benchmark (LM1B) (Chelba et al., 2013) and WikiHow (Koupaee and Wang, 2018)
for generative modeling, and Sudoku (Palm et al., 2018)

## 5.1 Language Generation Tasks

Setup. For both LM1B and WikiHow, HMDLM adopts the MDLM backbone (Sahoo et al., 2024): $12$ transformer blocks, hidden size $768$ , $12$ attention heads, rotary positional embeddings (Su et al., 2024), and the dataset-dependent tokenizer for each corpus: BERT-base-uncased for LM1B and GPT-2 for WikiHow. The hierarchy-specific components ( $K=2$ levels) are (i) a shared embedding matrix $W\in\mathbb{R}^{(K+1)\times H}=\mathbb{R}^{3\times 768}$ , with $K$ learnable level embeddings plus a learnable [MASK]-hierarchy row for unrevealed positions, and (ii) a hierarchy-prediction head that mirrors the token head’s adaLN-modulated (Peebles and Xie, 2023) final layer, mapping $\mathbb{R}^{768}\to\mathbb{R}^{2}$ . Together these add $\approx 0.2$ M parameters, about $0.15\%$ of the $139$ M-parameter LM1B backbone and $0.12\%$ of the $170$ M-parameter WikiHow backbone. The Sudoku architecture differs and is described in Section 5.2.

Metrics. We evaluate generation quality with four complementary measures. Model PPL assesses density modeling. Generative PPL assesses fluency of sampled text, but can be artificially lowered by leaning on a few high-likelihood phrases, producing repetitive, low-entropy output. We therefore read it alongside two diversity measures: entropy, where a value close to the reference or a high value indicates the model has not collapsed onto safe phrases, and MAUVE, which measures the similarity between the model-generated and reference text distributions. To corroborate the above metrics, we also run a pairwise LLM-as-judge study (GPT-5 judge) that
reports how often each model’s generations are preferred.

We hypothesize that generating key tokens first matters more than their positions, since these tokens provide enough context to decode the rest of the text. This is domain-dependent: for tasks like Sudoku, position-based hierarchy is likely essential. Both variants improve over MDLM, and ADLM, which is implicitly frequency-driven, is consistently outperformed by HMDLM-F. This highlights the benefit of our principled treatment of hierarchy in diffusion models.

## 6 Conclusion and Limitations

We introduced HMDLM, a hierarchical extension of masked diffusion language models that assigns priority to token positions and incorporates it into both the forward and reverse processes. By using level-specific noise schedules and a two-stage architecture that first predicts hierarchy and then tokens, HMDLM enables structured and controllable generation while remaining lightweight and modular. Our model consistently outperforms strong diffusion baselines by decoding semantically important tokens early and using them to better condition the rest of the sequence. While this improvement comes at the cost of additional computation due to the two-stage design, our results highlight hierarchy-aware generation as a promising direction for diffusion-based language models.

## Appendix A Discrete-Time ELBO

## A.1 Joint Generative Model $p_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{0},\mathbf{h}_{0})$ 

Hierarchy is jointly modelled with tokens.

We represent the state of the model at time step $t$ as $\mathbf{s_{t}}=(\mathbf{x_{t}},\mathbf{h_{t}})$ . When token $i$ 
is masked ( $x_{t}^{i}=M$ ), both the token and its hierarchy level are masked: the
token is replaced by [MASK] and $h_{t}^{i}$ is set to masked hierarchy
state ( $h_{MASK}$ ). HMDLM therefore targets
the joint distribution:

 $\log p_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{0},\mathbf{h}_{0}).$ 

(13)

For $s<t$ and a masked position ( $x_{t}^{i}=m$ ), both $x_{0}^{i}$ and $h_{0}^{i}$ are revealed
together upon unmasking, with the hierarchy fixed consistently to $h_{0}^{i}=f(x_{0}^{i})$ :

 $(x_{s}^{i},\;h_{s}^{i})=\begin{cases}(x_{0}^{i},\;h_{0}^{i})&\text{with prob.}\quad\dfrac{\alpha_{h_{0}^{i}}(s)-\alpha_{h_{0}^{i}}(t)}{1-\alpha_{h_{0}^{i}}(t)}\quad\text{(jointly revealed)}\\[12.0pt]
(m,\;h_{mask})&\text{with prob.}\quad\dfrac{1-\alpha_{h_{0}^{i}}(s)}{1-\alpha_{h_{0}^{i}}(t)}\quad\text{(jointly remain masked)}\end{cases}$ 

(14)

## A.2 Step 1 — Introduce the Joint Diffusion Process

We target the joint distribution
 $\log p_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{0},\mathbf{h}_{0})$ . The joint
 $(\mathbf{x}_{t},\mathbf{h}_{t})$ is the diffusion state at time $t$ : when $x_{t}^{i}=M$ , both
the token and its hierarchy label are masked and unobserved; when $x_{t}^{i}\neq M$ , both are
revealed together. Introduce the latent noisy sequence of joint states
 $(\mathbf{x}_{1:T},\mathbf{h}_{1:T})$ at $T$ discrete time steps $t_{1}<\cdots<t_{T}=1$ :

 $\log p_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{0},\mathbf{h}_{0})=\log\int p_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{0:T},\mathbf{h}_{0:T})\;d\mathbf{x}_{1:T}\,d\mathbf{h}_{1:T}.$ 

(15)

Because each denoising step depends only on the current state and not on the whole history, the joint model breaks into a product of one-step reverse predictions:

 $p_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{0:T},\mathbf{h}_{0:T})=p(\mathbf{x}_{T},\mathbf{h}_{T})\prod_{t=1}^{T}p_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{t-1},\mathbf{h}_{t-1}\mid\mathbf{x}_{t},\mathbf{h}_{t})$ 

(16)

We now split each reverse step into two parts: first predict the hierarchy, then predict the token given that hierarchy. We also drop $\mathbf{h}_{t}$ from the token prediction, since $\mathbf{h}_{t-1}$ already carries the hierarchy information needed. Finally, since the last state is fully masked and fixed, its prior splits into a product $p(\mathbf{x}_{T})\,p(\mathbf{h}_{T})$ .
This gives:

 $p_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{0:T},\mathbf{h}_{0:T})=p(\mathbf{x}_{T})\,p(\mathbf{h}_{T})\prod_{t=1}^{T}\underbrace{p_{\theta}(\mathbf{h}_{t-1}\mid\mathbf{h}_{t},\,\mathbf{x}_{t})}_{\text{Pass 1: hierarchy}}\cdot\underbrace{p_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{t-1}\mid\mathbf{h}_{t-1},\,\mathbf{x}_{t})}_{\text{Pass 2: tokens}}.$ 

(17)

Multiply and divide by the forward process
 $q(\mathbf{x}_{1:T},\mathbf{h}_{1:T}\mid\mathbf{x}_{0},\mathbf{h}_{0})$ in 15:

 $\log p_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{0},\mathbf{h}_{0})=\log\mathbb{E}_{q(\mathbf{x}_{1:T},\mathbf{h}_{1:T}\mid\mathbf{x}_{0},\mathbf{h}_{0})}\!\left[\frac{p_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{0:T},\mathbf{h}_{0:T})}{q(\mathbf{x}_{1:T},\mathbf{h}_{1:T}\mid\mathbf{x}_{0},\mathbf{h}_{0})}\right].$ 

(18)

## A.3 Step 2 — Apply Jensen’s Inequality

Since $\log$ is concave, Jensen’s inequality gives:

 $\log p_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{0},\mathbf{h}_{0})\;\geq\;\mathbb{E}_{q(\mathbf{x}_{1:T},\mathbf{h}_{1:T}\mid\mathbf{x}_{0},\mathbf{h}_{0})}\!\left[\log\frac{p_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{0:T},\mathbf{h}_{0:T})}{q(\mathbf{x}_{1:T},\mathbf{h}_{1:T}\mid\mathbf{x}_{0},\mathbf{h}_{0})}\right].$ 

(19)

## A.4 Step 3 — Expand the Log of the Joint Model

The forward process factorises as:

 $q(\mathbf{x}_{1:T},\mathbf{h}_{1:T}\mid\mathbf{x}_{0},\mathbf{h}_{0})=\prod_{t=1}^{T}q(\mathbf{x}_{t},\mathbf{h}_{t}\mid\mathbf{x}_{t-1},\mathbf{h}_{t-1}).$ 

(20)

Taking the log of equation 17 gives:

 $\displaystyle\log p_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{0:T},\mathbf{h}_{0:T})=\log p(\mathbf{x}_{T})+\log p(\mathbf{h}_{T})$ 
 $\displaystyle+\underbrace{\sum_{t=1}^{T}\log p_{\theta}(\mathbf{h}_{t-1}\mid\mathbf{h}_{t},\,\mathbf{x}_{t})}_{\text{Pass 1: hierarchy}}$ 

 $\displaystyle+\underbrace{\sum_{t=1}^{T}\log p_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{t-1}\mid\mathbf{h}_{t-1},\,\mathbf{x}_{t})}_{\text{Pass 2: tokens}}.$ 

(21)

Substituting into equation 19:

 $\displaystyle\log p_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{0},\mathbf{h}_{0})\;\geq\;\mathbb{E}_{q}\!\Bigl[$ 
 $\displaystyle\log p(\mathbf{x}_{T})+\log p(\mathbf{h}_{T})$ 

 $\displaystyle+\sum_{t=1}^{T}\log p_{\theta}(\mathbf{h}_{t-1}\mid\mathbf{h}_{t},\,\mathbf{x}_{t})$ 

 $\displaystyle+\sum_{t=1}^{T}\log p_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{t-1}\mid\mathbf{h}_{t-1},\,\mathbf{x}_{t})$ 

 $\displaystyle-\sum_{t=1}^{T}\log q(\mathbf{x}_{t},\mathbf{h}_{t}\mid\mathbf{x}_{t-1},\mathbf{h}_{t-1})\Bigr].$ 

(22)

## A.5 Step 4 — Telescope the Forward Terms

Key identity.

By Bayes’ rule on the joint forward process:

 $q(\mathbf{x}_{t-1},\mathbf{h}_{t-1}\mid\mathbf{x}_{t},\mathbf{h}_{t},\mathbf{x}_{0},\mathbf{h}_{0})=\frac{q(\mathbf{x}_{t},\mathbf{h}_{t}\mid\mathbf{x}_{t-1},\mathbf{h}_{t-1})\;q(\mathbf{x}_{t-1},\mathbf{h}_{t-1}\mid\mathbf{x}_{0},\mathbf{h}_{0})}{q(\mathbf{x}_{t},\mathbf{h}_{t}\mid\mathbf{x}_{0},\mathbf{h}_{0})}.$ 

(23)

Rearranging:

 $\begin{split}\log q(\mathbf{x}_{t},\mathbf{h}_{t}\mid\mathbf{x}_{t-1},\mathbf{h}_{t-1})&=\log q(\mathbf{x}_{t-1},\mathbf{h}_{t-1}\mid\mathbf{x}_{t},\mathbf{h}_{t},\mathbf{x}_{0},\mathbf{h}_{0})\\
&\quad+\log q(\mathbf{x}_{t},\mathbf{h}_{t}\mid\mathbf{x}_{0},\mathbf{h}_{0})-\log q(\mathbf{x}_{t-1},\mathbf{h}_{t-1}\mid\mathbf{x}_{0},\mathbf{h}_{0}).\end{split}$ 

(24)

Telescoping.

Substituting equation 24 into
 $\sum_{t=1}^{T}\log q(\mathbf{x}_{t},\mathbf{h}_{t}\mid\mathbf{x}_{t-1},\mathbf{h}_{t-1})$ 
and using $q(\mathbf{x}_{0},\mathbf{h}_{0}\mid\mathbf{x}_{0},\mathbf{h}_{0})=1$ :

 $\sum_{t=1}^{T}\log q(\mathbf{x}_{t},\mathbf{h}_{t}\mid\mathbf{x}_{t-1},\mathbf{h}_{t-1})=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\log q(\mathbf{x}_{t-1},\mathbf{h}_{t-1}\mid\mathbf{x}_{t},\mathbf{h}_{t},\mathbf{x}_{0},\mathbf{h}_{0})+\log q(\mathbf{x}_{T},\mathbf{h}_{T}\mid\mathbf{x}_{0},\mathbf{h}_{0}).$ 

(25)

Substitute back.

Applying equation 25 to equation 22, grouping $t=1$ separately,
and identifying each $(\log p_{\theta}-\log q)$ pair as $-D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q\|p)$ :

 $\displaystyle\log p_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{0},\mathbf{h}_{0})\;\geq\;$ 
 $\displaystyle\underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{q}\!\bigl[\log p_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{0},\mathbf{h}_{0}\mid\mathbf{x}_{1},\mathbf{h}_{1})\bigr]}_{L_{0}}$ 

 $\displaystyle-\sum_{t=2}^{T}\underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{q}\!\Bigl[D_{\mathrm{KL}}\bigl(q(\mathbf{x}_{t-1},\mathbf{h}_{t-1}\mid\mathbf{x}_{t},\mathbf{h}_{t},\mathbf{x}_{0},\mathbf{h}_{0})\;\|\;p_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{t-1},\mathbf{h}_{t-1}\mid\mathbf{x}_{t},\mathbf{h}_{t})\bigr)\Bigr]}_{L_{t}}$ 

 $\displaystyle-\underbrace{D_{\mathrm{KL}}\bigl(q(\mathbf{x}_{T},\mathbf{h}_{T}\mid\mathbf{x}_{0},\mathbf{h}_{0})\;\|\;p(\mathbf{x}_{T},\mathbf{h}_{T})\bigr)}_{L_{T}}.$ 

(26)

Here $p(\mathbf{x}_{T},\mathbf{h}_{T})=p(\mathbf{x}_{T})\,p(\mathbf{h}_{T})$ since the terminal
prior factorises. The reverse model factorises as

 $p_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{t-1},\mathbf{h}_{t-1}\mid\mathbf{x}_{t},\mathbf{h}_{t})=p_{\theta}(\mathbf{h}_{t-1}\mid\mathbf{x}_{t},\mathbf{h}_{t})\cdot p_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{t-1}\mid\mathbf{x}_{t},\mathbf{h}_{t-1})$ 

(27)

We approximate the reverse process $p_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{t-1},\mathbf{h}_{t-1}|\mathbf{x}_{t},\mathbf{h}_{t})$ 
using the neural network $s_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{t},\mathbf{h}_{t},t)$ , which is composed of two joined networks - $p_{\theta}(\mathbf{h}_{t-1}\mid\mathbf{x}_{t},\mathbf{h}_{t})$ is modeled as $h_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{t},\mathbf{h}_{t},t)$ and $p_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{t-1}\mid\mathbf{x}_{t},\mathbf{h}_{t-1})$ is modeled as $x_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{t},\mathbf{h}_{t-1},t)$ .

## A.6 Step 5 — Decompose the Joint KL Term $L_{t}$ (Full Derivation)

Each $L_{t}$ is a KL over the joint state $(\mathbf{x}_{t-1},\mathbf{h}_{t-1})$ ,
so that the term of interest is
 $L_{t}=D_{\mathrm{KL}}\bigl(q(\mathbf{x}_{t-1},\mathbf{h}_{t-1}\mid\mathbf{x}_{t},\mathbf{h}_{t},\mathbf{x}_{0},\mathbf{h}_{0})\,\|\,p_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{t-1},\mathbf{h}_{t-1}\mid\mathbf{x}_{t},\mathbf{h}_{t})\bigr)$ .

Chain rule of KL divergence.

For any two joint distributions $q(a,b\mid c)$ and $p(a,b\mid d)$ that are each
factorised in the same variable order, the KL divergence obeys the exact
identity

 $D_{\mathrm{KL}}\bigl(q(a,b\mid c)\,\|\,p(a,b\mid d)\bigr)=D_{\mathrm{KL}}\bigl(q(a\mid c)\,\|\,p(a\mid d)\bigr)+\mathbb{E}_{q(a\mid c)}\!\Bigl[D_{\mathrm{KL}}\bigl(q(b\mid a,c)\,\|\,p(b\mid a,d)\bigr)\Bigr].$ 

(28)

Hierarchy-first factorisation.

We apply equation 28 with $a=\mathbf{h}_{t-1}$ (hierarchy) and
 $b=\mathbf{x}_{t-1}$ (token), matching the two-stage structure of the model.
The true posterior factorises by the product rule ,

 $q(\mathbf{x}_{t-1},\mathbf{h}_{t-1}\mid\mathbf{x}_{t},\mathbf{h}_{t},\mathbf{x}_{0},\mathbf{h}_{0})=q(\mathbf{h}_{t-1}\mid\mathbf{x}_{t},\mathbf{h}_{t},\mathbf{x}_{0},\mathbf{h}_{0})\,q(\mathbf{x}_{t-1}\mid\mathbf{x}_{t},\mathbf{h}_{t-1},\mathbf{x}_{0},\mathbf{h}_{0}),$ 

(29)

Note: We drop $h_{t}$ since $h_{t-1}$ contains the latest state of the model.

The reverse model is defined to
factorise in the same order - Pass 1 predicts $\mathbf{h}_{t-1}$ and Pass 2
predicts $\mathbf{x}_{t-1}$ conditioned on it

 $p_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{t-1},\mathbf{h}_{t-1}\mid\mathbf{x}_{t},\mathbf{h}_{t})=\underbrace{p_{\theta}(\mathbf{h}_{t-1}\mid\mathbf{x}_{t},\mathbf{h}_{t})}_{\text{Pass 1: hierarchy head}}\,\underbrace{p_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{t-1}\mid\mathbf{x}_{t},\mathbf{h}_{t-1})}_{\text{Pass 2: token head}}.$ 

(30)

Apply the chain rule.

Substituting the factorisations equation 29 and equation 30 into
the identity equation 28 yields the two-term decomposition

 $\displaystyle D_{\mathrm{KL}}\bigl(q(\mathbf{x}_{t-1},\mathbf{h}_{t-1}\mid\mathbf{x}_{t},\mathbf{h}_{t},\mathbf{x}_{0},\mathbf{h}_{0})\;\|\;p_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{t-1},\mathbf{h}_{t-1}\mid\mathbf{x}_{t},\mathbf{h}_{t})\bigr)$ 

 $\displaystyle\quad=\underbrace{D_{\mathrm{KL}}\bigl(q(\mathbf{h}_{t-1}\mid\mathbf{x}_{t},\mathbf{h}_{t},\mathbf{x}_{0},\mathbf{h}_{0})\;\|\;p_{\theta}(\mathbf{h}_{t-1}\mid\mathbf{x}_{t},\mathbf{h}_{t})\bigr)}_{\text{hierarchy KL (Pass 1)}}$ 

 $\displaystyle\qquad+\underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{q(\mathbf{h}_{t-1}\mid\mathbf{x}_{t},\mathbf{h}_{t},\mathbf{x}_{0},\mathbf{h}_{0})}\!\Bigl[D_{\mathrm{KL}}\bigl(q(\mathbf{x}_{t-1}\mid\mathbf{x}_{t},\mathbf{h}_{t-1},\mathbf{x}_{0},\mathbf{h}_{0})\;\|\;p_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{t-1}\mid\mathbf{x}_{t},\mathbf{h}_{t-1})\bigr)\Bigr]}_{\text{token KL (Pass 2)}}.$ 

(31)

## A.7 Step 6 — Simplify the Terminal Term $L_{T}$ 

At $t=T$ , every token and hierarchy label is fully masked with probability
 $1-\alpha_{k}(1)\approx 1$ . The joint prior $p(\mathbf{x}_{T},\mathbf{h}_{T})$ is therefore
also the fully-masked state, so

 $L_{T}=D_{\mathrm{KL}}\bigl(q(\mathbf{x}_{T},\mathbf{h}_{T}\mid\mathbf{x}_{0},\mathbf{h}_{0})\;\|\;p(\mathbf{x}_{T},\mathbf{h}_{T})\bigr)\approx 0.$ 

(32)

We drop this term.

## A.8 Step 7 — Full Joint Discrete ELBO

Dropping $L_{T}=0$ :

 $\displaystyle\log p_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{0},\mathbf{h}_{0})\;\geq\;$ 
 $\displaystyle\underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{q}\!\bigl[\log p_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{0},\mathbf{h}_{0}\mid\mathbf{x}_{1},\mathbf{h}_{1})\bigr]}_{L_{0}}$ 

 $\displaystyle-\sum_{t=2}^{T}\underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{q}\!\Bigl[D_{\mathrm{KL}}\bigl(q(\mathbf{h}_{t-1}\mid\mathbf{x}_{t},\mathbf{h}_{t},\mathbf{x}_{0},\mathbf{h}_{0})\;\|\;p_{\theta}(\mathbf{h}_{t-1}\mid\mathbf{x}_{t},\mathbf{h}_{t})\bigr)\Bigr]}_{L_{t}^{\text{hier}}\ \text{(hierarchy head)}}$ 

 $\displaystyle-\sum_{t=2}^{T}\underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{q}\!\biggl[\mathbb{E}_{q(\mathbf{h}_{t-1}\mid\mathbf{x}_{t},\mathbf{h}_{t},\mathbf{x}_{0},\mathbf{h}_{0})}\!\Bigl[D_{\mathrm{KL}}\bigl(q(\mathbf{x}_{t-1}\mid\mathbf{x}_{t},\mathbf{h}_{t-1},\mathbf{x}_{0},\mathbf{h}_{0})\;\|\;p_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{t-1}\mid\mathbf{x}_{t},\mathbf{h}_{t-1})\bigr)\Bigr]\biggr]}_{L_{t}^{\text{tok}}\ \text{(token head)}}.$ 

(33)

## A.9 Step 8 — Expand the Hierarchy KL (Pass 1)

From equation 33, the hierarchy contribution at reverse step $t$ is:

 $L_{t}^{\mathrm{hier}}=\mathbb{E}_{q}\!\left[D_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\left(q(\mathbf{h}_{t-1}\mid\mathbf{x}_{t},\mathbf{h}_{t},\mathbf{x}_{0},\mathbf{h}_{0})\;\|\;p_{\theta}(\mathbf{h}_{t-1}\mid\mathbf{x}_{t},\mathbf{h}_{t})\right)\right].$ 

(34)

Since the forward process factorizes independently across sequence positions, and
the Pass-1 hierarchy head predicts each position independently given the noisy
state $(\mathbf{x}_{t},\mathbf{h}_{t})$ ,

 $\displaystyle q(\mathbf{h}_{t-1}\mid\cdots)$ 
 $\displaystyle=\prod_{i=1}^{L}q(h_{t-1}^{i}\mid\cdots),$ 

(35)

 $\displaystyle p_{\theta}(\mathbf{h}_{t-1}\mid\mathbf{x}_{t},\mathbf{h}_{t})$ 
 $\displaystyle=\prod_{i=1}^{L}p_{\theta}(h_{t-1}^{i}\mid\mathbf{x}_{t},\mathbf{h}_{t}).$ 

(36)

The joint KL therefore decomposes into a sum of independent per-position KLs,

 $L_{t}^{\mathrm{hier}}=\mathbb{E}_{q}\!\left[\sum_{i=1}^{L}D_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\left(q(h_{t-1}^{i}\mid\cdots)\;\|\;p_{\theta}(h_{t-1}^{i}\mid\mathbf{x}_{t},\mathbf{h}_{t})\right)\right].$ 

(37)

Notation.

Let

 $g_{h}^{i}(t):=[h_{\theta}^{i}(\mathbf{x}_{t},\mathbf{h}_{t},t)]_{h},\qquad h\in\{1,\ldots,K\},\qquad\sum_{h=1}^{K}g_{h}^{i}(t)=1,$ 

(38)

denote the Pass-1 softmax distribution over the $K$ hierarchy classes at position
 $i$ . For convenience we write

 $\lambda\equiv\lambda_{h_{0}^{i}}(t),\qquad\pi\equiv\pi_{i}(t)=\sum_{k=1}^{K}g_{k}^{i}(t)\lambda_{k}(t),$ 

(39)

where $\lambda_{h_{0}^{i}}(t)$ is the gold reveal probability associated with the true
hierarchy class $h_{0}^{i}$ , and $\lambda_{k}(t)$ is the (schedule-determined)
reveal probability of class $k$ . The quantity $\pi_{i}(t)$ is the model’s total
predicted reveal probability, obtained by averaging the class-dependent reveal
probability $\lambda_{k}(t)$ under the Pass-1 distribution $g^{i}(t)$ .

Forward posterior at masked positions.

At an unmasked position ( $s_{t}^{i}\neq s_{m}$ ) we have $s_{t-1}^{i}=s_{0}^{i}$ 
deterministically, contributing zero KL. At a masked position the
absorbing-state posterior is

 $q(s_{t-1}^{i}\mid s_{t}^{i},s_{0}^{i})=\begin{cases}\mathrm{Cat}\bigl(s_{t-1}^{i};\,s_{t}^{i}\bigr),&s_{t}^{i}\neq\mathbf{s_{m}},\\[8.0pt]
\mathrm{Cat}\!\left(s_{t-1}^{i};\,\dfrac{(1-\alpha_{h_{0}^{i}}(t-1))\,\mathbf{s_{m}}+(\alpha_{h_{0}^{i}}(t-1)-\alpha_{h_{0}^{i}}(t))\,s_{0}^{i}}{1-\alpha_{h_{0}^{i}}(t)}\right),&s_{t}^{i}=\mathbf{s_{m}}.\end{cases}$ 

(40)

Reverse hierarchy model (Pass 1).

The head first assigns position $i$ to
class $h$ with probability $g_{h}^{i}(t)$ , and each class then reveals at its own
known schedule probability $\lambda_{h}(t)$ . The reverse model is thus

 $p_{\theta}(h_{t-1}^{i}=h\mid\mathbf{x}_{t},\mathbf{h}_{t})=\lambda_{h}(t)\,g_{h}^{i}(t),\qquad h\in\{1,\ldots,K\},$ 

(41)

 $p_{\theta}(h_{t-1}^{i}=m\mid\mathbf{x}_{t},\mathbf{h}_{t})=1-\pi,\qquad\pi_{i}(t)=\sum_{k=1}^{K}g_{k}^{i}(t)\lambda_{k}(t).$ 

(42)

Each
class here reveals at its own probability $\lambda_{h}(t)$ , and on the gold class this
gives the gold probability directly,

 $p_{\theta}(h_{t-1}^{i}=h_{0}^{i}\mid\mathbf{x}_{t},\mathbf{h}_{t})=\lambda_{h_{0}^{i}}(t)\,g_{h_{0}^{i}}^{i}(t)=\lambda\,g_{h_{0}^{i}}^{i}(t).$ 

(43)

Per-position KL.

 $\begin{array}[]{lcc}\hline\cr\hline\cr h_{t-1}^{i}&q&p_{\theta}\\
\hline\cr h_{0}^{i}&\lambda&\lambda\,g_{h_{0}^{i}}^{i}(t)\\
h\neq h_{0}^{i}&0&\lambda_{h}(t)\,g_{h}^{i}(t)\\
h_{\mathrm{mask}}&1-\lambda&1-\pi\\
\hline\cr\hline\cr\end{array}$ 

The KL divergence is

 $D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q\|p_{\theta})=\sum_{v}q(v)\log\frac{q(v)}{p_{\theta}(v)},\qquad v\in\{1,\ldots,K,h_{\mathrm{mask}}\}.$ 

(44)

Since $q(v)=0$ for every incorrect hierarchy class ( $h\neq h_{0}^{i}$ ), all such terms
vanish — the off-gold entries $\lambda_{h}(t)\,g_{h}^{i}(t)$ never enter the KL and
serve only to normalize $p_{\theta}$ . Only the gold-class and mask rows remain,

 $D_{\mathrm{KL}}=\lambda\log\frac{\lambda}{\lambda\,g_{h_{0}^{i}}^{i}(t)}+(1-\lambda)\log\frac{1-\lambda}{1-\pi}.$ 

(45)

In the gold-class row the reveal rate $\lambda$ cancels between numerator and
denominator, leaving a pure cross-entropy on $g^{i}$ :

 $\displaystyle D_{\mathrm{KL}}$ 
 $\displaystyle=\lambda\log\frac{1}{g_{h_{0}^{i}}^{i}(t)}+(1-\lambda)\log\frac{1-\lambda}{1-\pi}$ 

 $\displaystyle=-\lambda\log g_{h_{0}^{i}}^{i}(t)+(1-\lambda)\log\frac{1-\lambda}{1-\pi}.$ 

(46)

Hence

 ${\;D_{\mathrm{KL}}^{\mathrm{hier,i}}=\underbrace{-\lambda\,\log g_{h_{0}^{i}}^{i}(t)}_{\text{reveal-weighted cross-entropy}}\;+\;\underbrace{(1-\lambda)\log\dfrac{1-\lambda}{1-\pi}}_{\text{extra-term}}\;}$ 

(47)

Summing over all the positions,

 ${\;L_{t}^{\mathrm{hier}}=\sum_{i=1}^{L}\mathbb{E}_{q}\!\left[-\lambda_{h_{0}^{i}}(t)\,\log g_{h_{0}^{i}}^{i}(t)\;+\;\big(1-\lambda_{h_{0}^{i}}(t)\big)\log\frac{1-\lambda_{h_{0}^{i}}(t)}{1-\pi_{i}(t)}\right].\;}$ 

(48)

## A.10 Step 9 — Expand the Token KL (Pass 2)

We expand the per-position token contribution

 $L^{\mathrm{tok},i}_{t}=\mathbb{E}_{q(h^{i}_{t-1}\mid\mathbf{x}_{t},\mathbf{h}_{t},\mathbf{x}_{0},\mathbf{h}_{0})}\Big[\,D_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\big(q(x^{i}_{t-1}\mid h^{i}_{t-1},\cdots,\mathbf{x}_{0},\mathbf{h}_{0})\,\big\|\,p_{\theta}(x^{i}_{t-1}\mid h^{i}_{t-1},\mathbf{x}_{t})\big)\Big],$ 

(49)

By the shared reveal variable ( equation 5), position $i$ 
is governed by a single Bernoulli $b^{i}_{t}\sim\mathrm{Bernoulli}\!\big(1-\alpha_{h^{i}_{0}}(t)\big)$ ,
and the joint state $s^{i}_{t}=(x^{i}_{t},h^{i}_{t})$ is a deterministic push-forward
of $b^{i}_{t}$ : both streams reveal to $(x^{i}_{0},h^{i}_{0})$ when the position is
kept and mask to $(m,h_{\mathrm{mask}})$ otherwise. Consequently the joint
reverse posterior over $(x^{i}_{t-1},h^{i}_{t-1})$ is equation 40.

Branch decomposition.

The outer expectation in equation 49 runs over the two values
 $h^{i}_{t-1}\in\{h^{i}_{0},\,h_{\mathrm{mask}}\}$ , with marginal weights
 $\lambda$ and $1-\lambda$ from equation 40. We treat each branch
in turn.

Revealed branch ( $h^{i}_{t-1}=h^{i}_{0}$ , weight $\lambda$ ).

 $p_{\theta}(x^{i}_{t-1}=v\mid h^{i}_{0},x_{t},h_{t-1})=f^{i}_{v}(t)$ with
 $f^{i}_{v}(t):=[x_{\theta}(x_{t},h_{t-1},t)]_{v}$ and $\sum_{v=1}^{V}f^{i}_{v}(t)=1$ :

 $\begin{array}[]{c|c|c}x^{i}_{t-1}&q&p_{\theta}\\
\hline\cr x^{i}_{0}&1&f^{i}_{x^{i}_{0}}(t)\\
v\neq x^{i}_{0}&0&f^{i}_{v}(t)\\
m&0&0\end{array}\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad D_{\mathrm{KL}}\big(q\,\|\,p_{\theta}\big)\big|_{\text{rev}}=\sum_{v}q_{v}\log\frac{q_{v}}{p_{\theta,v}}=-\log f^{i}_{x^{i}_{0}}(t).$ 

(50)

Masked branch ( $h^{i}_{t-1}=h_{\mathrm{mask}}$ , weight $1-\lambda$ ).

 $\begin{array}[]{c|c|c}x^{i}_{t-1}&q&p_{\theta}\\
\hline\cr x^{i}_{0}&0&0\\
v\neq x^{i}_{0}&0&0\\
m&1&1\end{array}\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad D_{\mathrm{KL}}\big(q\,\|\,p_{\theta}\big)\big|_{\text{mask}}=0.$ 

(51)

Taking the outer expectation.

 $\displaystyle L^{\mathrm{tok},i}_{t}$ 
 $\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{q}\!\Big[\underbrace{\lambda}_{P(h^{i}_{t-1}=h^{i}_{0})}\cdot\big(-\log f^{i}_{x^{i}_{0}}(t)\big)\;+\;\underbrace{(1-\lambda)}_{P(h^{i}_{t-1}=h_{\mathrm{mask}})}\cdot 0\Big]$ 

 $\displaystyle=-\,\mathbb{E}_{q}\!\big[\,\lambda_{h^{i}_{0}}(t)\,\log f^{i}_{x^{i}_{0}}(t)\big].$ 

(52)

Summing over position,

 ${\;L^{\mathrm{tok}}_{t}=\sum_{i=1}^{L}L^{\mathrm{tok},i}_{t}=-\sum_{i=1}^{L}\mathbb{E}_{q}\!\big[\,\lambda_{h^{i}_{0}}(t)\,\log f^{i}_{x^{i}_{0}}(t)\big].\;}$ 

(53)

The token loss is thus a reveal-weighted cross-entropy on the
Pass-2 softmax: each position contributes the negative log-likelihood of
its clean token $x^{i}_{0}$ , weighted by the class-dependent probability
 $\lambda_{h^{i}_{0}}(t)$ that the position is revealed at step $t-1$ .

## Discrete $\to$ Continuous

Setup. Let $s=t-\tfrac{1}{T}$ , and let $\alpha_{k}(t)$ be the class- $k$ survival
(unmasked) probability, decreasing with $\alpha_{k}(0)=1$ , $\alpha_{k}(1)=0$ .

Step A — per-step masked-position KL

 $L^{\mathrm{tok},i}_{t}=-\,\frac{\alpha_{h^{i}_{0}}(s)-\alpha_{h^{i}_{0}}(t)}{1-\alpha_{h^{i}_{0}}(t)}\,\log f^{i}_{x^{i}_{0}}(t).$ 

(54)

Step B — sum over timesteps.

 $\mathcal{L}^{\mathrm{tok}}=\sum_{t}L^{\mathrm{tok}}_{t}=-\sum_{t}\sum_{i}\mathbb{E}_{q}\!\left[\frac{\alpha_{h^{i}_{0}}(s)-\alpha_{h^{i}_{0}}(t)}{1-\alpha_{h^{i}_{0}}(t)}\,\log f^{i}_{x^{i}_{0}}(t)\right].$ 

(55)

Step C — expose the mask indicator.

 $\mathcal{L}^{\mathrm{tok}}=-\sum_{t}\mathbb{E}_{q}\!\left[\sum_{i}{1}[x^{i}_{t}=M]\,\frac{\alpha_{h^{i}_{0}}(s)-\alpha_{h^{i}_{0}}(t)}{1-\alpha_{h^{i}_{0}}(t)}\,\log f^{i}_{x^{i}_{0}}(t)\right].$ 

(56)

Step D — Riemann rescaling ( $\sum_{t}\to\mathbb{E}_{t}$ ).

 $\sum_{t}[\,\cdot\,]=\underbrace{\tfrac{1}{T}\sum_{t}}_{\mathbb{E}_{t\in\{1/T,\dots,1\}}}\underbrace{T[\,\cdot\,]}_{O(1)},$ 

(57)

 $\mathcal{L}^{\mathrm{tok}}=-\,\mathbb{E}_{t}\,\mathbb{E}_{q}\!\left[\sum_{i}{1}[x^{i}_{t}=M]\,\Big(T\cdot\tfrac{\alpha_{h^{i}_{0}}(s)-\alpha_{h^{i}_{0}}(t)}{1-\alpha_{h^{i}_{0}}(t)}\Big)\log f^{i}_{x^{i}_{0}}(t)\right].$ 

(58)

Step E — limit $T\to\infty$ : weight $\to$ reveal hazard.

 $T\big(\alpha_{h^{i}_{0}}(s)-\alpha_{h^{i}_{0}}(t)\big)=\frac{\alpha_{h^{i}_{0}}(t-\tfrac{1}{T})-\alpha_{h^{i}_{0}}(t)}{1/T}\;\xrightarrow[T\to\infty]{}\;-\,\alpha^{\prime}_{h^{i}_{0}}(t),$ 

(59)

 $T\cdot\frac{\alpha_{h^{i}_{0}}(s)-\alpha_{h^{i}_{0}}(t)}{1-\alpha_{h^{i}_{0}}(t)}\;\longrightarrow\;\frac{-\alpha^{\prime}_{h^{i}_{0}}(t)}{1-\alpha_{h^{i}_{0}}(t)}=:\lambda^{\prime}_{h^{i}_{0}}(t).$ 

(60)

Step F — continuous-time loss.

 ${\;\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{LM}}=\mathbb{E}_{t,\,q}\!\left[\sum_{i}{1}[x^{i}_{t}=M]\,\lambda^{\prime}_{h^{i}_{0}}(t)\,\big(-\log f^{i}_{x^{i}_{0}}(t)\big)\right].}$ 

(61)

Step G — Hierarchy head (same derivation, $f\to g$ ).

 $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{hier}}=\mathbb{E}_{t,\,q}\!\left[\sum_{i}{1}[x^{i}_{t}=M]\,\lambda^{\prime}_{h^{i}_{0}}(t)\,\big(-\log g^{i}_{h^{i}_{0}}(t)\big)\right].$ 

(62)

Let $s=t-\tfrac{1}{T}$ , and

 $\mathcal{E}^{i}_{t}=\bigl(1-\lambda_{h^{i}_{0}}(t)\bigr)\,\log\frac{1-\lambda_{h^{i}_{0}}(t)}{1-\pi_{i}(t)},$ 

(61)

Both $\lambda_{h^{i}_{0}}(t)$ and every $\lambda_{k}(t)$ are $O(1/T)$ , hence so is
 $\pi_{i}(t)$ .

Step H — expose the mask indicator.

Only masked positions contribute (unmasked positions give zero KL, cf. equation 49):

 $\mathcal{E}^{\mathrm{hier}}_{t}=\mathbb{E}_{q}\!\left[\sum_{i}{1}[x^{i}_{t}=M]\,\bigl(1-\lambda_{h^{i}_{0}}(t)\bigr)\log\frac{1-\lambda_{h^{i}_{0}}(t)}{1-\pi_{i}(t)}\right].$ 

(62)

Step I — small-step expansion.

Write $\lambda\equiv\lambda_{h^{i}_{0}}(t)$ and $\pi\equiv\pi_{i}(t)$ , both $O(1/T)$ .
Using $\log(1-z)=-z-\tfrac{z^{2}}{2}-\cdots$ ,

 $\log\frac{1-\lambda}{1-\pi}=(\pi-\lambda)-\tfrac{1}{2}\bigl(\lambda^{2}-\pi^{2}\bigr)+O(T^{-3}).$ 

(63)

Multiplying by $(1-\lambda)$ and collecting terms by order,

 $\mathcal{E}^{i}_{t}=\underbrace{(\pi-\lambda)}_{O(1/T)}\;+\;\underbrace{\tfrac{1}{2}(\lambda-\pi)^{2}}_{O(1/T^{2})}\;+\;O(T^{-3}).$ 

(64)

Step J — Riemann rescaling ( $\sum_{t}\to\mathbb{E}_{t}$ ).

 $\sum_{t}[\,\cdot\,]=\underbrace{\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t}}_{\mathbb{E}_{t\in\{1/T,\dots,1\}}}\underbrace{T\,[\,\cdot\,]}_{O(1)},\qquad T\,\mathcal{E}^{i}_{t}=\underbrace{T(\pi-\lambda)}_{O(1)}+\underbrace{\tfrac{T}{2}(\lambda-\pi)^{2}}_{O(1/T)\;\to\;0}.$ 

(65)

Step K — limit $T\to\infty$ : rate $\to$ reveal hazard.
For each class $k$ , as in Step E of the token derivation,

 $T\,\lambda_{k}(t)=\frac{\alpha_{k}\!\left(t-\tfrac{1}{T}\right)-\alpha_{k}(t)}{1/T}\cdot\frac{1}{1-\alpha_{k}(t)}\;\xrightarrow[T\to\infty]{}\;\frac{-\alpha_{k}^{\prime}(t)}{1-\alpha_{k}(t)}=:\lambda_{k}^{\prime}(t).$ 

(66)

Applying this to $\lambda$ and, term by term, to $\pi$ ,

 $T\lambda=T\lambda_{h^{i}_{0}}(t)\to\lambda_{h^{i}_{0}}^{\prime}(t),\qquad T\pi=\sum_{k}g^{i}_{k}(t)\,T\lambda_{k}(t)\to\sum_{k}g^{i}_{k}(t)\,\lambda_{k}^{\prime}(t),$ 

(67)

so that

 $T(\pi-\lambda)\;\xrightarrow[T\to\infty]{}\;\sum_{k}g^{i}_{k}(t)\,\lambda_{k}^{\prime}(t)-\lambda_{h^{i}_{0}}^{\prime}(t),$ 

(68)

while the quadratic remainder vanishes.

Now, the $1-\lambda_{k}(t)$ term,

 $1-\lambda_{k}(t)=\frac{1-\alpha_{k}\!\left(t-\tfrac{1}{T}\right)}{1-\alpha_{k}(t)}.$ 

(69)

Since $\alpha_{k}$ is continuous, $\alpha_{k}\!\left(t-\tfrac{1}{T}\right)\to\alpha_{k}(t)$ as $T\to\infty$ .
Moreover, $t>0$ implies $1-\alpha_{k}(t)>0$ , so the denominator is nonzero. Therefore

 $1-\lambda_{k}(t)\;\xrightarrow[T\to\infty]{}\;\frac{1-\alpha_{k}(t)}{1-\alpha_{k}(t)}=1.$ 

(70)

The only singular point is $t=0$ : since $1-\alpha_{k}(0)=0$ ,

 $\lambda^{\prime}_{k}(t)\xrightarrow[t\to 0^{+}]{}\infty,$ 

(71)

while $1-\lambda_{k}(t)\in(0,1]$ remains bounded and is undefined only at $t=0$ itself.

In pratice, we sample $t\sim\mathcal{U}[\varepsilon,1]$ , and therefore never sample $t=0$ and hence the expression always stays bounded, where $\varepsilon=10^{-3}$ .

Step L — continuous-time extra term.

 ${\;\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{cons}}=\mathbb{E}_{t,\,q}\!\left[\sum_{i}{1}[x^{i}_{t}=M]\Bigl(\underbrace{\textstyle\sum_{k}g^{i}_{k}(t)\,\lambda_{k}^{\prime}(t)}_{\text{predicted reveal hazard}}\;-\;\lambda_{h^{i}_{0}}^{\prime}(t)\Bigr)\right],\qquad\lambda_{k}^{\prime}(t)=\frac{-\alpha_{k}^{\prime}(t)}{1-\alpha_{k}(t)}.\;}$ 

(72)

## A.11 Step 10 — Final Training Loss

Training losses.

The final training loss consists of the following three components:

 $\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{LM}}$ 
 $\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{t,q}\!\left[\sum_{i=1}^{L}\mathbf{1}[x_{t}^{i}=M]\,\lambda^{\prime}_{h_{0}^{i}}(t)\,\bigl(-\log f_{x_{0}^{i}}^{i}(t)\bigr)\right],$ 

(73)

 $\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{hier}}$ 
 $\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{t,q}\!\left[\sum_{i=1}^{L}\mathbf{1}[x_{t}^{i}=M]\,\lambda^{\prime}_{h_{0}^{i}}(t)\,\bigl(-\log g_{h_{0}^{i}}^{i}(t)\bigr)\right],$ 

(74)

 $\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{cons}}$ 
 $\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{t,\,q}\!\left[\sum_{i=1}^{L}\mathbf{1}[x_{t}^{i}=M]\,\Bigl({\textstyle\sum_{k}g^{i}_{k}(t)\,\lambda_{k}^{\prime}(t)}\;-\;\lambda_{h_{0}^{i}}^{\prime}(t)\Bigr)\right],$ 

(75)

In practice we optimise a reweighted sum of the same
three terms, replacing the tied weights above by tunable
coefficients:

 ${\;\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{HMDLM}}=\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{LM}}\;+\;\beta_{\mathrm{hier}}\,\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{hier}}\;+\;\beta_{\mathrm{cons}}\,\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{cons}}.\;}$ 

(76)

## Appendix B Expected Mask Fraction under Hierarchical Log-Linear Schedule

We derive the expected fraction of masked tokens per training step under our
hierarchical masking schedule, which is needed to compute the effective
training-token budget and compare fairly against baseline diffusion language
models (Sahoo et al., 2024).

Setup.

Let $K$ denote the number of hierarchy levels and let $\text{offset}_{k}\in[0,1)$ 
denote the per-level time offset. The effective time at level $k$ is

 $t_{\text{eff},k}\;=\;\mathrm{clip}\!\left(\frac{t-\text{offset}_{k}}{1-\text{offset}_{k}},\;0,\;1\right),$ 

(77)

and the log-linear schedule for level $k$ is

 $\alpha_{k}(t)\;=\;1-(1-\varepsilon)\,t_{\text{eff},k},\qquad\varepsilon=10^{-3}.$ 

(78)

The probability that a token at level $k$ is masked at time $t$ is therefore

 $p_{k}(t)\;=\;1-\alpha_{k}(t)\;=\;(1-\varepsilon)\,t_{\text{eff},k}.$ 

(79)

Per-level expected mask probability.

We compute $\mathbb{E}_{t\sim\mathcal{U}[0,1]}[p_{k}(t)]=(1-\varepsilon)\,\mathbb{E}[t_{\text{eff},k}]$ .
Splitting the integral at the clamp boundary $t=\text{offset}_{k}$ :

 $\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[t_{\text{eff},k}]$ 
 $\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\text{offset}_{k}}0\,dt+\int_{\text{offset}_{k}}^{1}\frac{t-\text{offset}_{k}}{1-\text{offset}_{k}}\,dt.$ 

(80)

With the substitution $u=t-\text{offset}_{k}$ , the second integral becomes

 $\int_{0}^{\,1-\text{offset}_{k}}\frac{u}{1-\text{offset}_{k}}\,du\;=\;\frac{(1-\text{offset}_{k})^{2}}{2(1-\text{offset}_{k})}\;=\;\frac{1-\text{offset}_{k}}{2}.$ 

(81)

Hence,

 ${\;\mathbb{E}_{t\sim\mathcal{U}[0,1]}[\,p_{k}(t)\,]\;=\;(1-\varepsilon)\cdot\frac{1-\text{offset}_{k}}{2}.\;}$ 

(82)

Plugging in our configuration.

With $K=2$ , level offsets $\text{offset}_{1}=0.25$ (rare tokens) and
 $\text{offset}_{0}=0.0$ (frequent tokens), and $\varepsilon=10^{-3}$ :

 $\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[p_{1}(t)]$ 
 $\displaystyle=0.999\cdot\tfrac{1-0.25}{2}=0.999\cdot 0.375=0.374625,$ 

(83)

 $\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[p_{0}(t)]$ 
 $\displaystyle=0.999\cdot\tfrac{1-0.0}{2}=0.999\cdot 0.500=0.499500.$ 

(84)

Overall expected mask fraction.

Let $f_{k}$ denote the fraction of real token positions assigned to level
 $k$ , with $f_{0}+f_{1}=1$ . The overall expected mask fraction is

 $\mathbb{E}[\text{mask fraction}]\;=\;f_{0}\cdot\mathbb{E}[p_{0}(t)]+f_{1}\cdot\mathbb{E}[p_{1}(t)].$ 

(85)

Under the hierarchical power schedule, a response token at level $\ell$ with
exponent $w_{\ell}$ is masked at diffusion time $t\sim\mathcal{U}(0,1)$ with
probability $(1-\epsilon)\,t^{w_{\ell}}$ . Its expected masking rate is therefore

 $\bar{m}_{\ell}\;=\;(1-\epsilon)\int_{0}^{1}t^{w_{\ell}}\,dt\;=\;\frac{1-\epsilon}{w_{\ell}+1}.$ 

(96)

Let $g_{\ell}$ be the fraction of tokens assigned to level $\ell$ , pooled over the
training set and counted over diffused positions only (the prompt and padding
are excluded). The expected masking rate of HMDLM is then

 $\bar{m}_{\text{HMDLM}}\;=\;(1-\epsilon)\sum_{\ell}\frac{g_{\ell}}{w_{\ell}+1}.$ 

(97)

MDLM corresponds to a single level with $w=1$ , giving
 $\bar{m}_{\text{MDLM}}=(1-\epsilon)/2$ . With two levels and exponents
 $w_{0}=1$ , $w_{1}=2$ ,

 $\bar{m}_{\text{HMDLM}}\;=\;(1-\epsilon)\left[\frac{1-g_{1}}{2}+\frac{g_{1}}{3}\right]\;=\;(1-\epsilon)\left(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{g_{1}}{6}\right),$ 

(98)

so the ratio to MDLM, in which $\epsilon$ cancels, is

 $\rho\;=\;\frac{\bar{m}_{\text{HMDLM}}}{\bar{m}_{\text{MDLM}}}\;=\;1-\frac{g_{1}}{3}.$ 

(99)

 $S_{\text{MDLM}}$ steps at the same batch size, HMDLM is trained for

(100)

## Appendix D Per-Level Schedules and the Induced Reveal Order

Levels are indexed so that a higher level is masked more slowly. Formally, for
any two levels $k<k^{\prime}$ we require

 $\alpha_{k}(t)\;\leq\;\alpha_{k^{\prime}}(t)\quad\text{for all }t,$ 

(101)

with strict inequality at every step at which level $k^{\prime}$ is active, i.e.
 $\alpha_{k^{\prime}}(t)<1$ . Since $\alpha_{k}(0)=1$ and $\alpha_{k}(T)=0$ for every
 $k$ , the inequality is necessarily an equality at the two endpoints. Fewer
level- $k^{\prime}$ positions are therefore masked at any interior point of the forward
process.

The reverse dynamics are governed by the per-step reveal probability
 $\lambda_{k}(t)$ , and equation 101 does not by itself imply an
ordering of the $\lambda_{k}$ . We therefore state the property we use directly:
for $k<k^{\prime}$ ,

 $\lambda_{k}(t)\;\leq\;\lambda_{k^{\prime}}(t)\qquad\text{whenever }\alpha_{k^{\prime}}(t)<1.$ 

(102)

Outside that interval, $\alpha_{k^{\prime}}(t)=1$ , no level- $k^{\prime}$ position is masked
and $\lambda_{k^{\prime}}(t)=0$ ; the ordering is vacuous there, since there is
nothing left at level $k^{\prime}$ to reveal. Both schedules used in our experiments
satisfy equation 102, as the continuous-time hazards
 $\lambda^{\prime}_{k}(t)=-\alpha^{\prime}_{k}(t)/(1-\alpha_{k}(t))$ show:

• 

Log-linear with per-level offset equation 78, used on
LM1B: $\lambda^{\prime}_{k}(t)=1/(t-\delta_{k})$ for $t>\delta_{k}$ and $0$ 
otherwise. For $\delta_{k}<\delta_{k^{\prime}}$ and $t>\delta_{k^{\prime}}$ we have
 $t-\delta_{k}>t-\delta_{k^{\prime}}>0$ , hence
 $\lambda^{\prime}_{k}(t)<\lambda^{\prime}_{k^{\prime}}(t)$ .

• 

Power schedule $\alpha_{k}(t)=1-(1-\varepsilon)\,t^{\omega_{k}}$ ,
used on WikiHow: $\lambda^{\prime}_{k}(t)=\omega_{k}/t$ , so the ordering holds
exactly when $\omega_{k}<\omega_{k^{\prime}}$ . We use $\omega=(1.0,2.0)$ , and
 $\omega_{k}=1$ recovers the log-linear schedule of MDLM.

The discrete $\lambda_{k}(t)$ inherit the ordering on the step grids used in our
experiments ( $T=256$ on both LM1B, and WikiHow): the only
steps at which $\lambda_{0}(t)>\lambda_{1}(t)$ are the $64$ LM1B steps with
 $t\leq\delta_{1}$ , where level $1$ is inactive and $\lambda_{1}(t)=0$ .

Consequently, while level $k^{\prime}$ is still being revealed, a position that Stage I
predicts to be at level $k^{\prime}$ receives a larger reveal mass
 $\lambda_{k^{\prime}}(t)\,g^{i}_{k^{\prime}}(t)$ in equation 8 and is revealed
earlier, which is the soft generation order HMDLM is designed to induce.

## Appendix E Perplexity: continuous-time ELBO weight

We evaluate test-set perplexity under the continuous-time limit of the
HMDLM ELBO. For a single hierarchy level $k$ , the masking schedule is
linear with offset $\delta_{k}$ :

 $\alpha_{k}(t)\;=\;\begin{cases}1&t\leq\delta_{k},\\[2.0pt]
1-(1-\varepsilon)\,\dfrac{t-\delta_{k}}{1-\delta_{k}}&\delta_{k}<t\leq 1,\end{cases}\qquad\delta_{0}=0,\delta_{1}=0.25,$ 

(103)

where $\alpha_{k}(t)$ is the survival (clean) probability of a level- $k$ 
token at time $t$ .

Per-level continuous-time weight.

The standard absorbing-state ELBO weight is

 $\lambda^{\prime}_{k}(t)\;=\;-\,\frac{\alpha_{k}^{\prime}(t)}{1-\alpha_{k}(t)},$ 

(104)

For the schedule in equation 103, on the active interval $t>\delta_{k}$ ,

 $\displaystyle\alpha_{k}^{\prime}(t)\;$ 
 $\displaystyle=\;-\,\frac{1-\varepsilon}{1-\delta_{k}},$ 
 $\displaystyle 1-\alpha_{k}(t)\;$ 
 $\displaystyle=\;(1-\varepsilon)\;\frac{t-\delta_{k}}{1-\delta_{k}}.$ 

(105)

Substituting into equation 104,

 $w_{k}(t)\;=\;\frac{(1-\varepsilon)/(1-\delta_{k})}{(1-\varepsilon)\,(t-\delta_{k})/(1-\delta_{k})}\;=\;\frac{1}{t-\delta_{k}}.$ 

(106)

For
 $t\leq\delta_{k}$ the level is fully unmasked
( $1-\alpha_{k}(t)=0$ ) and contributes no loss; we therefore extend $\lambda^{\prime}_{k}$ 
by zero in that regime:

 $\lambda^{\prime}_{k}(t)\;\coloneqq\;\begin{cases}\dfrac{1}{t-\delta_{k}}&t>\delta_{k},\\[6.0pt]
0&t\leq\delta_{k}.\end{cases}$ 

(107)

 $\delta_{0}=0$ recovers the MDLM weight $1/t$ used
by Sahoo et al. (2024), so equation 107 is a
strict generalisation.

Posterior-weighted per-token weight.

Since at evaluation, the gold hierarchy is not available, at position $i$ , we replace the gold-hierarchy weight
 $\lambda^{\prime}_{h_{0}^{i}}(t)$ with its expectation $r_{i}(t)$ under the model’s posterior over
hierarchy levels at time $t$ :

 $r_{i}(t)\;=\;\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}[h_{\theta}^{i}(x_{t},h_{t},t)]_{h}\lambda^{\prime}_{k}(t),$ 

(108)

where $h_{\theta}^{i}$ is the Pass-1 hierarchy distribution at position $i$ .

Token-level perplexity.

Let $N$ denote the total number of tokens (non-padding) in the test set,
and let $\mathbf{x}_{t},\mathbf{h}_{t}$ be sampled by the joint forward
process at time $t\sim\mathcal{U}(\varepsilon,1)$ , with $\varepsilon=10^{-3}$ . The per-token
negative log-likelihood is

 $\mathrm{NLL}_{\mathrm{tok}}\;=\;\frac{1}{N}\;\mathbb{E}_{t,\;\mathbf{x}_{t}}\!\!\left[\sum_{i:\,x_{t}^{i}=M}r_{i}(t)\;\bigl(-\log p_{\theta}(x_{0}^{i}\mid\mathbf{x}_{t})\bigr)\right],$ 

(109)

and the reported test-set perplexity is

 $\mathrm{PPL}\;=\;\exp\!\bigl(\mathrm{NLL}_{\mathrm{tok}}\bigr).$ 

(110)

Numerical handling of $t\!\to\!\delta_{k}^{+}$ .

At $t=\delta_{k}$ is bounded in code by a
denominator floor:

 $\lambda_{k}(t)\;\approx\;\frac{1}{\max(t-\delta_{k},\;\eta)},\qquad\eta=10^{-8},$ 

which leaves the weight unchanged everywhere except in a vanishingly
small neighbourhood of $\delta_{k}$ and prevents single-sample gradient
spikes from the Monte-Carlo estimator.

## Appendix F Architecture Details and Qualitative analysis on sudoku

## F.1 Architecture Details

For the 6M MGDM and HMDLM models, we use a compact 3-layer GPT-2 Transformer ( 5.5M parameters) with hidden size 384, 12 attention heads of dimension 32, a 1536-dimensional GELU feed-forward layer, learned absolute position embeddings, and a 31-token character-level vocabulary whose output head shares weights with the input embedding and bidirectional attention. The hierarchical variant (HMDLM) adds only a level embedding and a level-prediction head, about 5K extra parameters.

For the 25M MDLM and HMDLM models, we use a 12-layer diffusion transformer (DiT, 25.0M parameters) with hidden size 384, 6 attention heads of dimension 64, a 1536-dimensional GELU feed-forward layer, rotary position embeddings, and bidirectional attention over a fixed 81-token sequence (one token per cell). Both the models are trained from scratch. The hierarchical variant (HMDLM, K=6 levels) adds a 7x384 level embedding (6 levels plus a [MASK] level), which is summed with the token embedding, and a level-prediction head that has the same form as the token output head. Together they add about 104K parameters (25.10M versus 24.99M).

## Appendix I Societal Impact of the Model

The proposed HMDLM advances masked diffusion language modeling by introducing hierarchical decoding, which improves reasoning and text generation quality. The positive societal impacts include more efficient and accurate language models that can benefit downstream NLP applications such as question answering, text generation, and reasoning tasks. On the negative side, as with all pre-trained language models, HMDLM could potentially be misused to generate misleading or harmful text.

Abstract

Reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards (RLVR), particularly through group-relative policy optimization (GRPO), has emerged as a central paradigm for the post-training of large language models (LLMs). While RLVR is highly effective at enhancing the reasoning capabilities of LLMs, it commonly induces distribution sharpening, whereby the policy distribution becomes excessively peaked and rare-yet-correct trajectories are progressively suppressed, thus diminishing sample diversity. To this end, we promote flat-reward optima in RLVR to smooth the policy distribution.
We theoretically link flat reward RLVR to reduced distribution sharpening, and empirically show improved sample diversity while maintaining single-attempt accuracy. Our method, called Flat-RLVR, searches for flat reward regions via sharpness-aware minimization during LLM post-training, combining this with GRPO variants to achieve better trade-offs between sampling efficiency and reasoning capacity.

Keywords: 
Distribution Sharpening, RLVR, Flatness, Reasoning Capability

## 1 Introduction

Reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards (RLVR), especially through group-relative methods, has emerged as a predominant methodology for large language model (LLM) post-training (Shao et al., 2024; Lambert et al., 2025; Guo et al., 2025). Its prominence stems from two complementary strengths: first, rewarding only the correctness of the final answer is sufficient to elicit valid reasoning behaviors (Guo et al., 2025; Shao et al., 2026); and second, the group-relative method eliminates the value network required by proximal policy optimization (PPO) (Schulman et al., 2017), substantially reducing the memory burden of maintaining a critic of comparable size to the policy (Shao et al., 2024). This critic-free design—first realized as group-relative policy optimization (GRPO) (Shao et al., 2024) and subsequently extended in its variants—has rapidly become the de facto standard for RLVR.

Figure 1: RLVR and Flat-RLVR on LLM reasoning capacity. Gray nodes indicate paths that are unlikely to be sampled by the model, black nodes indicate paths that are likely to be sampled, i.e., each path consists of a sequence of outputs, later formulated by $o_{j}$ , where $j$ is the path index. Green nodes indicate correct solutions, and red nodes indicate incorrect.

However, a critical doubt has been raised, stemming from empirical observations that GRPO impairs its efficacy when evaluated across multiple samples by checking whether any one succeeds; so-called Pass@ $K$ performance metric (Shao et al., 2024). Specifically, base LLM models without post-training even surpass their RLVR-tuned counterparts under Pass@ $K$ with sufficiently large $K$ (Yue et al., 2025).
This misgiving has recently raised a hypothesis that RLVR merely improves sampling efficiency at the expense of the model’s underlying reasoning capacity driven by LLM’s sample diversity (Nguyen et al., 2025; Zhao et al., 2025; Zhu et al., 2025). Further evidence reinforces this view: RLVR has been shown to amplify only responses that already carry high probability mass while neglecting the long tail of rare-yet-correct samples, thereby reducing sample diversity (He et al., 2025); and to concentrate exclusively on regions already solvable by the base model, failing to acquire novel capabilities (Yue et al., 2025). Taken together, these findings further support the interpretation that the current RLVR mechanism induces distribution sharpening—a phenomenon in which the model reinforces only the easier, already-solved instances and loses its exploratory capacity.

This is particularly problematic in light of the emerging paradigm of inference-time compute scaling, which leverages sample diversity for effective LLM deployment (Wang et al., 2023; Brown et al., 2024; Chow et al., 2025; Handa et al., 2026; Wang et al., 2026b). These strategies operate on a simple principle—generate many candidate solutions and rely on a verifier to identify the correct ones—and are particularly well aligned with RLVR’s domains of mathematics and code, where deterministic verifiers naturally enable reliable selection (Cobbe et al., 2021; Chen et al., 2021; Brown et al., 2024).
In this regard, distribution sharpening from RLVR, which is observed to trade output diversity for efficiency, directly undermines LLM’s benefits of inference-time computation. Figure 1 presents LLM-generated output trajectories (i.e., reasoning paths), the base model covers sometimes-incorrect but wide variety of answers, but RLVR narrows the path to a few answers discarding diversity.

In response to this growing need for sample diversity, we apply reinforcement learning (RL) to flatten the reward surface of the RLVR framework, thereby smoothing policy distributions and diversifying the generated candidate samples.
Recently, it has been shown that a flatter maximum on the reward surface implies a robust policy against uncertain perturbations on actions, states, and rewards (Lee and Yoon, 2025).
Motivated by the flat RL concept, our key insight is that, rather than updating the LLM toward steepest loss descent, steering it to flatten the surface may smooth the distribution sharpening.

In this paper, we find that sharpness-aware minimization (SAM) variants (Foret et al., 2021; Liu et al., 2022; Hassan et al., 2025) applied to RL optimization within the RLVR paradigm indeed mitigate distribution sharpening by yielding a flatter reward
(as illustrated in Figure 1).
In addition, we formalize how the flat-search optimization mathematically lifts suboptimal candidates by smoothing the reward surface. Our contributions are highlighted as follows:
i) We propose a novel strategy to alleviate the distribution sharpening of GRPO by borrowing the concept of flat-minima search. ii) We provide a theoretical analysis of how a flat-inspired GRPO elevates suboptimal candidates, leading to the smoothed output distribution. iii) We suggest a Flat-RLVR framework by integrating SAM into GRPO methods. Flat-RLVR, instantiated by combining with GRPO (Shao et al., 2024), DAPO (Yu et al., 2025), and CISPO (Chen et al., 2025), consistently improves Pass@ $256$ mathematical reasoning capacity while preserving accuracy with our LLM post-training adaptations.

## 2 Related Work

## 2.1 Group-Relative RLVR

Reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards (RLVR) trains a large language model on binary rewards provided by deterministic verifiers, eliciting strong reasoning behavior without human-preference data (Guo et al., 2025; Lambert et al., 2025; Shao et al., 2026). Within this paradigm, group-relative methods have become dominant, yielding computation benefits by normalizing rewards across a group of rollouts sampled from the same prompt and thereby removing the need for a separate value network (Shao et al., 2024). The original instance, GRPO (Shao et al., 2024), has since been extended in several directions. DAPO (Yu et al., 2025) introduces asymmetric clipping bounds that allow low-probability tokens to bring larger upward updates, together with dynamic sampling that discards groups whose advantages collapse to zero. CISPO (Chen et al., 2025) clips the importance-sampling weight to prevent dropping tokens that carry essential information.

Our work integrates a sharpness-aware optimization perspective into the reinforcement learning loop, showing a general applicability across the group-relative RLVR family.

## 2.2 Distribution Sharpening in RLVR

While the distribution sharpening phenomenon of RLVR is now well documented (Yue et al., 2025; Nguyen et al., 2025; Zhao et al., 2025), existing mitigation strategies all intervene at the level of the reward or token distribution. F-GRPO (Plyusov et al., 2026) applies a Focal Weight to down-weight easy prompts, the unlikeliness reward (He et al., 2025) amplifies signals from rare correct trajectories, and clipping-based variants such as DAPO (Yu et al., 2025) relax token-level updates to preserve low-probability tokens.

We address distribution sharpening via optimization geometry itself, biasing updates toward flat regions of the loss landscape via SAM—a direction is novel and orthogonal to the above approaches.

## 2.3 Advances in SAM

Sharpness-aware minimization (SAM) has been improved as a primary driver of flat-minima search by addressing its drawbacks. The first concern is its sensitivity to parameter re-scaling: ASAM (Kwon et al., 2021) addresses this by introducing a scaling operator that adaptively adjusts the perturbation radius according to parameter magnitudes. The second issue is SAM’s vulnerability to noisy gradients, particularly during the early stages of training; GCSAM (Hassan et al., 2025) mitigates this through Gradient Centralization, which removes the mean of the gradient to yield a more reliable perturbation direction. The third concern is the computational overhead of SAM; LookSAM (Liu et al., 2022) amortizes this cost by computing the SAM update only periodically, while Momentum-SAM (Becker et al., 2025) integrates Nesterov Accelerated Gradient (NAG) into SAM to directly improve computational efficiency. Lastly, the observation that SAM does not invariably converge to flat minima leads to GSAM (Zhuang et al., 2022) adopting a surrogate gap formulation for the sharpness metric.

Our work adopts the idea of several SAM-based variants to build the Flat-RLVR framework; applying the SAM update periodically, inspired by LookSAM (Liu et al., 2022) and Gradient Centralization, inspired by GCSAM (Hassan et al., 2025). Notably, Flat-RLVR is not limited to a certain type of SAM approaches.

## 2.4 Flat-Minima and Generalization

Flat minima have been studied as being linked to improved generalization (Hochreiter and Schmidhuber, 1997; Keskar et al., 2017) emphasized in domain generalization (Cha et al., 2021) and adversarial robustness (Wei et al., 2023) with underlying principles (Zhang et al., 2024; Yoo and Yoon, 2026).
Researchers have extended flat-minima toward various learning frameworks, including generative modeling (Lee et al., 2025), federated learning (Qu et al., 2022; Lee and Yoon, 2024), and reinforcement learning (RL) (Lee and Yoon, 2025).
Notably, RL with flatter reward yields more robust policies to action, state, and reward uncertainties (Lee and Yoon, 2025). Such flat reward insight makes us anticipate potential benefits in RLVR, but it is nontrivial to identify how flatness-aware RL steers LLM behavior.

Our work is the first attempt to employ SAM in LLM post-training, revealing its benefits in relieving distribution sharpening in RLVR.

## 3 Preliminaries

## 3.1 Group Relative Policy Optimization

GRPO (Shao et al., 2024) removes the value function, then instead calculates advantages relative to a group of sampled rollouts. For a prompt $q\sim Q$ with $G$ rollouts $\{o_{i}\}^{G}_{i=1}$ , and rewards $\{R_{i}\}^{G}_{i=1}$ , the group-relative advantage for $i$ -th rollout is

 $\hat{\mathcal{A}}_{i}=\frac{R_{i}-\bar{R}}{\sigma_{R}+\varepsilon},$ 

(1)

where $\bar{R}:=\frac{1}{G}\sum_{i}R_{i}$ , $\sigma_{R}=\textrm{std}(\{R_{j}\})$ and $\varepsilon>0$ .

Let $o_{i}=(y_{i,1},...,y_{i,|o_{i}|})\in O$ denote the token sequence for rollout $i$ computed by the old policy $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(O|q)$ from a prompt $q$ . GRPO optimizes policy $\pi_{\theta}$ to maximize the following clipped surrogate objective based on group-relative advantage $\hat{\mathcal{A}}_{i,\tau}$ :

 $J_{\text{GRPO}}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{q\sim P(Q),\{o_{i}\}_{i=1}^{G}\sim\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(O|q)}\bigg[\\
\frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^{G}\frac{1}{|o_{i}|}\sum_{\tau=1}^{|o_{i}|}\min\Big\{\frac{\pi_{\theta}(o_{i,\tau}\mid q,o_{i,<\tau})}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(o_{i,\tau}\mid q,o_{i,<\tau})}\hat{\mathcal{A}}_{i,\tau},\\
\mathrm{clip}\big(\frac{\pi_{\theta}(o_{i,\tau}\mid q,o_{i,<\tau})}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(o_{i,\tau}\mid q,o_{i,<\tau})},1-\epsilon,1+\epsilon\big)\hat{\mathcal{A}}_{i,\tau}\Big\}\bigg],\\
$ 

(2)

where $o_{i,\tau}=y_{i,\tau}$ , $o_{i,<\tau}=\{y_{i,1},\cdots,y_{i,\tau-1}\}$ , and $\hat{\mathcal{A}}_{i,\tau}$ is the $i$ -th rollout advantage given with a set of partial sequences $\{o_{i,\leq\tau}\}_{i=1}^{G}$ . In RLVR, all tokens within the same rollout share an identical advantage value; $\hat{\mathcal{A}}_{i,\tau}=\hat{\mathcal{A}_{i}}$ for all $\tau$ . $\mathrm{clip}(\cdot,a,b)$ clips input to be in interval $(a,b)$ , where $a<b\in\mathbb{R}$ .

We drop the Kullback-Leibler regularization term adopted by GRPO by following recent findings that the regularization hinders explorations in reasoning (Zhou et al., 2026).

Figure 2: Illustration of the loss landscape and reasoning capacity of RLVR and Flat-RLVR.
Flat-RLVR converges to a flatter minimum in the loss landscape by jointly optimizing for loss reduction and sharpness minimization, whereas RLVR is drawn to a sharper minimum driven solely by reward maximization.
As shown in the trees (see Figure 1), Flat-RLVR explores a broader reasoning tree, while RLVR collapses to a narrow set of paths.

## 3.2 Sharpness-Aware Minimization

SAM (Foret et al., 2021) seeks a minimum with both small loss and curvature, biasing optimization toward flat regions of the loss landscape. Formally, SAM solves the following min-max objective:

 $\underset{\theta}{\min}\ \underset{\|\epsilon\|_{2}\leq\rho}{\max}\ \mathcal{L}(\theta+\epsilon),$ 

(3)

where $\mathcal{L}(\theta)$ is the loss function parametrized by $\theta$ , and $\epsilon$ is a parameter perturbation constrained to lie within an $\ell_{2}$ ball of radius $\rho>0$ . The inner maximization admits a first-order approximation so that the worst-case perturbation becomes

 $\epsilon^{*}=\rho{\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{L}(\theta)}/{\lVert\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{L}(\theta)\rVert}.$ 

(4)

By minimizing the worst-case loss within this perturbation neighborhood, SAM penalizes sharp minima and encourages convergence to flat ones.

## 4 Analysis on Flatness in RLVR

Based on the GRPO and SAM objectives, we analyze how a sharpness-aware RLVR, so-called Flat-RLVR, affects distribution sharpening over LLM outputs. Without loss of generality, we use a local sequence-level unclipped surrogate induced by a fixed rollout group (Ge et al., 2026). For a prompt $q$ , let $J_{q}(\theta)$ denote the local RLVR objective, which drops the expectation and clipping in Eq. (2).
Set $\mathcal{L}_{q}(\theta)=-J_{q}(\theta)$ ; maximizing RLVR objective is equivalent to minimizing $\mathcal{L}_{q}$ .
Figure 2 previews the key components of our analysis that follows.

Definition 4.1 ( $\rho$ -Flat-RLVR).

Given prompt $q$ and the computed local sequence-level RLVR loss $\mathcal{L}_{q}(\theta)$ , let $\rho$ -sharpness of $\mathcal{L}_{q}$ at parameter $\theta$ be

 $\operatorname{Sharp}_{\rho}(\theta;q)=\max_{\|\epsilon\|_{2}\leq\rho}\left[\mathcal{L}_{q}(\theta+\epsilon)-\mathcal{L}_{q}(\theta)\right],$ 

(5)

where $\epsilon$ is a parameter perturbation and $\|\cdot\|_{2}$ is Euclidean norm.
Then, the $\rho$ -Flat RLVR objective is defined as the expected sharpness-aware loss:

 $\min_{\theta}\mathbb{E}_{q\sim Q}\left[\mathcal{L}_{q}(\theta)+\operatorname{Sharp}_{\rho}(\theta;q)\right]\\
=\min_{\theta}\mathbb{E}_{q\sim Q}\left[\max_{\|\epsilon\|_{2}\leq\rho}\mathcal{L}_{q}(\theta+\epsilon)\right].$ 

(6)

Following SAM, Flat-RLVR optimizes Eq. (6) by a two-step update at parameters $\theta_{t}$ , where $t$ is an update index: it first ascends to the worst-case perturbation $\epsilon_{t}^{*}=\rho\,\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{L}_{q}(\theta_{t})/\|\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{L}_{q}(\theta_{t})\|_{2}$ , then descends using the gradient at the perturbed point,

 $\theta_{t+1}=\theta_{t}-\alpha\,\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{L}_{q}(\theta_{t}+\epsilon_{t}^{*}).$ 

(7)

The perturbation $\epsilon_{t}^{*}$ is what distinguishes Flat-RLVR from the standard update, which cannot account for loss sensitivity to perturbations.

Definition 4.2 (Correct-set Distribution Sharpening).

Given prompt $q$ and policy distribution $\pi_{\theta}(\cdot|q)$ over outputs $o\in O$ , let $\mathcal{C}_{q}=\{o:R(o)=1\}$ be a verifier-correct set, whose elements show $1$ -reward.
For two correct outputs $o_{h},o_{l}\in\mathcal{C}_{q}$ satisfying

 $\pi_{\theta_{\mathrm{old}}}(o_{h}|q)>\pi_{\theta_{\mathrm{old}}}(o_{l}|q),$ 

(8)

let us formulate their correct-set log-probability gap at parameter $\theta$ as

 $D_{h,l}(\theta;q)=\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(o_{h}|q)}{\pi_{\theta}(o_{l}|q)}.$ 

(9)

An update from $\theta_{\mathrm{old}}$ to $\theta_{\mathrm{new}}$ is defined to sharpen the correct-set distribution on $(o_{h},o_{l})$ if

 $D_{h,l}(\theta_{\mathrm{new}};q)-D_{h,l}(\theta_{\mathrm{old}};q)>0.$ 

(10)

Here, $o_{h}$ denotes a correct reasoning path with higher probability under the old policy, while $o_{l}$ denotes a lower-probability but still correct reasoning path.
We now analyze how the policy update via $\rho$ -Flat-RLVR affects the confidence gap $D_{h,l}$ .

Lemma 4.3 ( $\rho$ -Flat-RLVR One-Step Decomposition).

The one-step update of Flat-RLVR from Definition 4.1 admits the following local expansion:

 $\theta_{t+1}^{\texttt{Flat}}=\theta_{t}+\alpha\Big(\underbrace{g_{t}}_{\textnormal{Score Part}}\underbrace{-\,\rho\,\frac{M_{t}g_{t}}{\|g_{t}\|_{2}}}_{\textnormal{Flatness Part}}\Big)+R_{t}.$ 

(11)

Here, for the sequence-level score is $s_{i,t}=\nabla_{\theta}\log\pi_{\theta}(o_{i}|q)|_{\theta_{t}}$ over rollouts $\{o_{i}\}_{i=1}^{G}$ , $g_{t}=\frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^{G}\hat{\mathcal{A}}_{i}s_{i,t}$ , $M_{t}=\frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^{G}\hat{\mathcal{A}}_{i}s_{i,t}s_{i,t}^{\top}$ .

The residual $R_{t}=-\alpha\rho\,B_{t}g_{t}/\|g_{t}\|_{2}-\alpha R_{T}$ collects the log-policy Hessian term $B_{t}=\frac{1}{G}\sum_{i}\hat{\mathcal{A}}_{i}\nabla_{\theta}^{2}\log\pi_{\theta}(o_{i}|q)$ and the higher-order Taylor residual $R_{T}$ .

The ‘Score Part’ is the standard RLVR update, which increases the likelihood of positively advantaged rollouts along the reward-improving direction $g_{t}$ . The ‘Flatness Part’, scaled by $\rho$ , opposes that direction through the curvature operator $M_{t}$ , dampening over-amplification along $g_{t}$ . Under a sequence-level neural tangent kernel (NTK) approximation (Liu et al., 2020), $B_{t}$ cancels by the zero-mean property of group-relative advantages, so the residual is negligible at leading order. The detailed derivation is provided in Appendix A.1.

Lemma 4.4 (Distribution Sharpening One-step Decomposition).

For a correct pair $(o_{h},o_{l})$ in Definition 4.2, let $\Delta s_{t}:=s_{h,t}-s_{l,t}$ with $s_{h,t}=\nabla_{\theta}\log\pi_{\theta}(o_{h}|q)|_{\theta_{t}}$ and $s_{l,t}=\nabla_{\theta}\log\pi_{\theta}(o_{l}|q)|_{\theta_{t}}$ .
For a one-step parameter update $\Delta\theta_{t}=\theta_{t+1}-\theta_{t}$ , define the first-order correct-set gap change as

 $\mathfrak{D}_{t}(\Delta\theta_{t}):=D_{h,l}(\theta_{t}+\Delta\theta_{t};q)-D_{h,l}(\theta_{t};q)\\
\approx\Delta s_{t}^{\top}\Delta\theta_{t}.$ 

(12)

Then the $\rho$ -Flat-RLVR update satisfies

 $\mathfrak{D}_{t}(\Delta\theta_{t}^{\texttt{Flat}})=\mathfrak{D}_{t}(\Delta\theta_{t}^{\mathrm{GRPO}})-\alpha\rho C_{t}+r_{t},$ 

(13)

where $\Delta\theta_{t}^{\mathrm{GRPO}}=\alpha g_{t}$ , $C_{t}=(\Delta s_{t}^{\top}M_{t}g_{t})/\|g_{t}\|_{2}$ , and $r_{t}=\Delta s_{t}^{\top}R_{t}.$ 

Here, $C_{t}>0$ indicates that the flatness correction reduces the GRPO-induced growth of the correct-set log-probability gap between $o_{h}$ and $o_{l}$ . The proof is provided in Appendix A.2.

Proposition 4.5 (Flat-RLVR Mitigates Correct-set Distribution Sharpening).

Along a training trajectory, suppose that the cumulative flatness correction dominates the residual:

 $\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\alpha\rho C_{t}\geq\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}r_{t}.$ 

(14)

Then the cumulative correct-set gap growth induced by $\rho$ -Flat-RLVR is no larger than that induced by the corresponding GRPO score updates:

 $\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\mathfrak{D}_{t}(\Delta\theta_{t}^{\texttt{Flat}})\leq\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\mathfrak{D}_{t}(\Delta\theta_{t}^{\mathrm{GRPO}}).$ 

(15)

Proposition 4.5 implies that $\rho$ -Flat RLVR mitigates correct-set distribution sharpening relative to standard RLVR under the assumptions of our analysis. Proof is in Appendix A.3.

Remark 4.6 (Incorrect-output penalization).

Proposition 4.5 does not imply that Flat-RLVR raises the probability of incorrect outputs. For an incorrect output with a negative advantage, the same flatness correction term in Lemma 4.3 remains aligned with probability suppression. Thus, Flat-RLVR can reduce over-concentration among correct reasoning paths while still preserving reward separation between correct and incorrect outputs.

## 5 Proposed Method: Flat-RLVR

## 5.1 Flat-RLVR

Grounded in our theoretical findings that flatness-seeking optimization alleviates the distribution sharpening in RLVR, we construct the Flat-RLVR framework, which integrates SAM into group-relative RLVR. The Flat-RLVR objective is:

 $\min_{\theta}\mathbb{E}_{q\sim Q}\left[\max_{\|\epsilon\|_{2}\leq\rho}\mathcal{L}_{q}(\theta+\epsilon)\right].$ 

(16)

Specifically, for each prompt $q$ , the current policy $\pi_{\theta_{\mathrm{old}}}$ generates $G$ completions, and the corresponding rewards are normalized within the group to yield the advantage estimator $\hat{A}_{i}$ . This is the standard GRPO procedure and serves as the baseline for our SAM-based modifications.

First, we calculate the objective function $\mathcal{L}_{q}(\theta_{t})$ and gradient $\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{L}_{q}(\theta_{t})$ . Then, the ascent step for finding the worst perturbation is calculated by

 $\epsilon_{t}^{*}=\rho\,\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{L}_{q}(\theta_{t})/\|\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{L}_{q}(\theta_{t})\|_{2},$ 

(17)

The parameters are perturbed toward $\theta+\epsilon_{t}^{*}$ , and the descent step for flat-minima is computed by

 $\theta_{t+1}=\theta_{t}-\alpha\,\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{L}_{q}(\theta_{t}+\epsilon_{t}^{*})$ 

(18)

for updating policy, which corresponds to Eq (7).

## 5.2 Adaptations for LLM post-training

Flat-RLVR operates under the SAM paradigm, and incorporates the following adaptations and advances tailored for LLM post-training.

## Scalability.

SAM induces one more gradient computation to obtain the perturbation.
We adopt SAM not for all steps, but for every $n$ steps by borrowing the existing technique (Liu et al., 2022).
While this prior work only treats $n$ as a scalability knob, our adoption in LLM post-training introduces a new dimension: the period $n$ governs the effective degree of SAM regularization, and consequently impacts the Pass@ $K$ trade-off. We provide further analysis of scalability on Flat-RLVR in Section 6.5.

## Perturbation radius.

We adopt the prompt-difficulty-aware strategy of F-GRPO with Focal Weight (Plyusov et al., 2026), which decays the advantage with difficulty.
Similarly, we adjust the perturbation radius $\rho$ based on prompt difficulty, thereby improving convergence stability. We provide details in Appendix B.1.

## Gradient.

We apply gradient centralization (GC) (Hassan et al., 2025) to both the ascent and descent steps of SAM, reducing gradient norm throughout optimization. Empirically, this improves the model’s exploration capability during post-training. We provide details in Appendix 6.

Algorithm 1 integrates these adaptations into Flat-RLVR built upon a GRPO baseline.

## Evaluation Setup.

We evaluate on five in-domain mathematical reasoning benchmarks—MATH500 (Lightman et al., 2024), AMC23, AIME24 (Zhang and Math-AI, 2024), AIME25 (Zhang and Math-AI, 2025), and AIME26 (Zhang and Math-AI, 2026)—and report Pass@K curves traced from $K=1$ to $K=256$ , where Pass@1 measures single-attempt accuracy and Pass@256 captures diversity-driven coverage of the solution space. Detailed evaluation protocols are provided in Appendix F.

## 6.2 Reasoning Capacity Evaluation

Motivated by Yue et al. (2025), we adopt two analysis methods for evaluating Reasoning Capacity: Pass@ $K$ metrics and Solvable-Problem Coverage Analysis. Concretely, Pass@ $K$ metrics measure the boundaries of reasoning ability by sampling $k$ outputs from the model. Solvable-Problem Coverage Analysis measures the fraction of problems solvable by the base model or the RL-tuned model, indicating whether Flat-RLVR truly mitigates the narrowing of reasoning capacity boundaries stemming from the naive RLVR framework.

## Pass@K.

We evaluate the effect of applying SAM to GRPO, namely Flat-GRPO, with group size $G=6$ . Figure 3 shows Pass@ $K$ curves on in-domain benchmarks, comparing the base model, GRPO, and Flat-GRPO from $K=1$ to $K=256$ . At small $K$ , both GRPO and Flat-GRPO outperform the base model across benchmarks, reflecting the well-known single-attempt gains of RLVR. As $K$ grows, however, GRPO’s advantage erodes, and the base model eventually matches or even surpasses it—indicating that despite its higher Pass@ $1$ , GRPO yields a narrower reasoning coverage than the base model, a signature of distribution sharpening. In contrast, Flat-GRPO maintains a consistent advantage over both GRPO and the base model across all values of $K$ , indicating that SAM preserves the model’s exploratory capacity while retaining the single-attempt gains of RLVR.

## Solvable-Problem Coverage Analysis.

We analyze the set of solvable problems for the base model relative to GRPO and to Flat-GRPO. For each pair, we measure i) the fraction of problems solvable by the base model ( $\checkmark$ ) but not by the RL-tuned model ( $\times$ )—i.e., problems the RL stage forgets—and ii) the fraction newly solved by the RL-tuned model ( $\checkmark$ ) but not by the base model ( $\times$ ). This decomposition isolates how much reasoning coverage RLVR sacrifices in exchange for the new problems it acquires. Table 1 reports GRPO forgets $6.3\%$ of the problems originally solvable by the base model while newly solving only $5.4\%$ —a net erosion of base-model coverage that is symptomatic of distribution sharpening. Flat-GRPO, however, substantially improves both sides: it forgets far fewer problems ( $2.8\%$ ) while solving more new ones ( $6.3\%$ ). SAM mitigates GRPO’s tendency to collapse toward a narrow subset of the base model’s solvable problems, thereby alleviating distribution sharpening.

## 6.3 Extended Evaluations

We further evaluate Flat-RLVR from four perspectives: (1) Generality evaluation across various RLVR variants, including GRPO, DAPO, and CISPO. This evaluation examines whether flatness-seeking optimization transfers across distinct group-relative algorithms. We keep the group size $G{=}6$ and report Pass@ $1$ / Pass@ $256$ ; (2) Comparative evaluation against F-GRPO (Plyusov et al., 2026), which addresses the same goal via difficulty-aware advantage scaling; (3) Scalability Evaluation with Larger Backbone on Qwen2.5-3B-Instruct (Team, 2024; Yang et al., 2024a), which examines whether the benefits of Flat-RLVR persist at a larger model scale; and (4) Cross-Domain Evaluation with Code Generation Test on Qwen2.5-Coder-1.5B-Instruct (Hui et al., 2024), which examines whether the reasoning capacity benefit of Flat-RLVR extends beyond the mathematical domain. All experiments follow the identical training and evaluation protocol described in Appendix F.

## Generality Evaluation across GRPO variants.

Table 2 reports the following results: On Qwen2.5-Math-1.5B, flatness-seeking optimization improves the average Pass@256 by +2.5 (GRPO $\rightarrow$ Flat-GRPO), +3.4 (DAPO $\rightarrow$ Flat-DAPO), and +3.0 (CISPO $\rightarrow$ Flat-CISPO), while Pass@1 remains stable across all three; on Llama-3.2-1B-Instruct, the same comparisons yield +2.0, +1.2, and +1.3 on Pass@256, with Pass@1 preserved. Taken together, these results indicate that the diversity benefit of Flat-RLVR broadly transfers across distinct group-relative algorithms and backbone families.

## Comparative Evaluation with Baseline.

Table 3 reports that Flat-GRPO surpasses F-GRPO in average Pass@256 on both backbones—by +2.1 on Qwen2.5-Math-1.5B and +0.1 on Llama-3.2-1B-Instruct—while remaining competitive on Pass@1. This suggests that mitigating sharpening at the optimization-geometry level is at least as effective as F-GRPO’s advantage-reweighting strategy, and complementary to it: these two methods operate on distinct levers, i.e., loss geometry by ours vs. advantage magnitude by F-GRPO, and our approach shows a competitive gain in the diversity-critical regime.

## Scalability Evaluation with Larger Backbone.

To test whether the benefit of Flat-RLVR persists at a larger backbone scale, we train Qwen2.5-3B-Instruct with the same hyperparameters as in our main experiments. Table 4 shows that Flat-GRPO improves Pass@256 by +2.0 over GRPO at a negligible Pass@1 cost (-0.3), suggesting that its benefits are not limited to small-scale models.

## Cross-Domain Evaluation with Code Generation Test.

We evaluate the model’s sampling efficiency and reasoning capacity on Mostly Basic Programming Problems (MBPP) train/test split (Austin et al., 2021) via Pass@K metric. Table 5 shows that Flat-GRPO achieves a comparable sampling efficiency gain to GRPO (-2.2) while substantially better preserving reasoning capacity (+4.0). This indicates that the benefit of Flat-RVLR extends beyond mathematical reasoning to code generation, suggesting that flatness-aware optimization is broadly applicable across diverse domains.

## 6.4 Empirical Validation of Theoretical Analysis

To validate the theoretical analysis of Flat-RLVR’s benefits, we further evaluate Flat-RLVR from three perspectives: (1) Correct-set log probability gap, which is a key theoretical quantity in Definition 4.2; (2) Rare-correct log-probability, which demonstrates that Flat-RLVR mitigates distribution sharpening by preserving rare-correct trajectories; and iii) Low-pass filter (LPF) (Bisla et al., 2022) of Qwen2.5-Math-1.5B, where lower values indicate a flatter minimum. Full training details are in Appendix F.

## Correct-set log probability gap.

We trace the correct-set log probability gap $D$ training Qwen2.5-Math-1.5B on MATH dataset. The correct-set log probability gap $D=\log\pi_{\theta}(o_{h}|q)-\log\pi_{\theta}(o_{l}|q)$ (referred to Definition 4.2) indicates the difference between a high-probability correct-output $o_{h}$ and low-probability correct-output $o_{l}$ . Reducing this gap growth is the core mechanism by which Flat-RLVR mitigates distribution sharpening.

Specifically, i) for a given prompt, we measure $o_{h}$ (high-probability correct output) and $o_{l}$ (low-probability correct output) before training. ii) After one-step training, we measure them to compute the changes of the gap $D$ and keep track of it during training. Figure 4(a) shows that Flat-GRPO reduces the gap of correct-set log probability compared with GRPO during training, indicating Flat-RLVR mitigates the correct-set distribution sharpening.

## Rare-correct log-probability.

We simultaneously trace the mean probability mass assigned to the fixed rare-correct trajectories $o_{l}$ throughout the same training process. Figure 4(b) shows that Flat-GRPO retains more probability mass on rare correct trajectories $(o_{l})$ than GRPO, which suggests that it helps preserve the model’s reasoning capacity by keeping rare correct solution alive.

## Low-pass filter (LPF).

In addition, to verify that Flat-GRPO really find the flatter region than GRPO, we measure the Low-Pass Filter (LPF) flatness defined as the expected increase in loss under Gaussian parameter perburbaions:

 $\text{LPF}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{\delta\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2})}[\mathcal{L}(\theta+\delta)]-\mathcal{L}(\theta).$ 

(19)

We construct a fixed evaluation batch of 32 prompts from the model’s training dataset (MATH dataset). For each prompt, we sample $G=6$ rollouts from the model and compute advantages $\hat{A}_{i}=(r_{i}-\bar{r})/\sigma_{r}$ , defining $\mathcal{L}$ as the GRPO loss on this fixed batch. The identical batch and perturbation seeds are used for both models to controlled comparison. Perturbation are filter-normalized scaling the noise on each parameter by its norm.

Table 6 shows the model trained with Flat-GRPO converges to a flatter region than GRPO. This is consistent with our theoretical claim that flatness-aware optimization mitigates distribution sharpening.

## 7 Conclusion

In this work, we present a way to mitigate the distribution sharpening in group-relative RLVR, by utilizing flatness-seeking optimization. Concretely, we provide a mathematical analysis of the one-step parameter update in RLVR, showing that flat-minima search dampens the abrupt increase in probability, thereby mitigating distribution sharpness of LLM post-training. We propose the Flat-RLVR framework by integrating sharpness-aware minimization into RLVR.
Throughout experiments, Flat-RLVR implemented as Flat-GRPO/DAPO/CISPO mitigates the distribution sharpening induced by RLVR, consistently improving the diversity of the output distribution while preserving accuracy with our LLM post-training adaptations. These imply flatness serves as the central catalyst to mitigate the distribution sharpening.

## Appendix A Analysis on Flatness

## A.1 Proof of Lemma 4.3

For ease of derivation, we begin with a simplified, sequence-level form of the GRPO objective per query in Eq (2):

 $\mathcal{L}(\theta)=-\frac{1}{G}\sum^{G}_{i=1}\gamma_{i}(\theta)\,\hat{\mathcal{A}}_{i},\gamma_{i}(\theta)=\frac{\pi_{\theta}(o_{i}\mid q)}{\pi_{\theta_{\mathrm{old}}}(o_{i}\mid q)}.$ 

(20)

Since $\nabla_{\theta}\gamma_{i}(\theta)=\gamma_{i}(\theta)s_{i,t}$ where $s_{i,t}=\nabla_{\theta}\log\pi_{\theta}(o_{i}\mid q)$ , the gradient of $\mathcal{L}$ with respect to $\theta$ is

 $\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{L}(\theta)\\
=-\frac{1}{G}\sum^{G}_{i=1}\hat{\mathcal{A}}_{i}\,\gamma_{i}(\theta)s_{i,t}.$ 

(21)

Applying the product rule, the Hessian of $\gamma_{i}$ is

 $\nabla^{2}_{\theta}\gamma_{i}(\theta)=\gamma_{i}(\theta)(s_{i,t}s_{i,t}^{\top}+\nabla^{2}_{\theta}\log\pi_{\theta}(o_{i}|q)).$ 

(22)

## The gradient and Hessian at $\theta_{t}$ .

Under the one-step update assumption $\pi_{\theta}\approx\pi_{\theta_{\mathrm{old}}}$ (equivalently, $\gamma_{i}(\theta)\approx 1$ ), the gradient and Hessian at $\theta_{t}$ simplify to

 $\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{L}(\theta_{t})\approx-\frac{1}{G}\sum^{G}_{i=1}\hat{\mathcal{A}}_{i}s_{i,t}=-g_{t},$ 

(23)

 $\nabla^{2}_{\theta}\mathcal{L}(\theta_{t})\approx-\frac{1}{G}\sum^{G}_{i=1}\hat{\mathcal{A}}_{i}\!(s_{i,t}s_{i,t}^{\top}+\nabla^{2}_{\theta}\log\pi_{\theta}(o_{i}|q))\\
=-(M_{t}+B_{t}).$ 

(24)

where we identify $g_{t},M_{t}$ , and $B_{t}$ as defined in Lemma 4.3.

## The worst-case perturbation.

Following Eq. (4), the worst-case perturbation is

 $\epsilon^{*}=\rho\cdot\frac{\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{L}(\theta_{t})}{\lVert\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{L}(\theta_{t})\rVert_{2}}=-\rho\cdot\frac{g_{t}}{\lVert g_{t}\rVert_{2}}.$ 

(25)

## The first-order Taylor expansion.

The gradient of the perturbed loss is approximated by a first-order Taylor expansion (Foret et al., 2021):

 $\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{L}(\theta_{t}+\epsilon^{*})\approx\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{L}(\theta_{t})+\nabla^{2}_{\theta}\mathcal{L}(\theta_{t})\,\epsilon^{*}+R_{T}.$ 

(26)

where $R_{T}$ collects higher-order Taylor residuals.

## The one-step update for Flat-RLVR.

The Flat-RLVR update minimizes $-\mathcal{L}(\theta_{t}+\epsilon^{*})$ , giving

 $\theta^{\text{Flat}}_{t+1}\\
=\theta_{t}-\alpha\Big[(-g_{t})+(-(M_{t}+B_{t}))\Big(-\rho\frac{g_{t}}{\lVert g_{t}\rVert_{2}}\Big)\Big]-\alpha R_{T}\\
=\theta_{t}+\alpha\Big[g_{t}-(M_{t}+B_{t})\rho\frac{g_{t}}{\lVert g_{t}\rVert_{2}}\Big]-\alpha R_{T}\\
=\theta_{t}+\alpha\Big(g_{t}-\rho\frac{M_{t}g_{t}}{\lVert g_{t}\rVert_{2}}\Big)-\alpha\rho\frac{B_{t}g_{t}}{\lVert g_{t}\rVert_{2}}-\alpha R_{T},$ 

(27)

which recovers Eq (11).

## A.2 Proof of Lemma 4.4

By definition, the correct-support log-probability gap is

 $D_{h,l}(\theta;q)=\log\pi_{\theta}(o_{h}\mid q)-\log\pi_{\theta}(o_{l}\mid q).$ 

(28)

## First-order approximation of the gap change.

For a one-step parameter update $\Delta\theta_{t}=\theta_{t+1}-\theta_{t}$ , a first-order Taylor expansion around $\theta_{t}$ gives

 $D_{h,l}(\theta_{t}+\Delta\theta_{t};q)\\
\approx D_{h,l}(\theta_{t};q)+\nabla_{\theta}D_{h,l}(\theta_{t};q)^{\top}\Delta\theta_{t}.$ 

(29)

Since $\nabla_{\theta}\log\pi_{\theta}(o_{i}\mid q)|_{\theta_{t}}=s_{i,t}$ , we have

 $\nabla_{\theta}D_{h,l}(\theta_{t};q)=s_{h,t}-s_{l,t}=:\Delta s_{t},$ 

(30)

and therefore

 $\mathfrak{D}_{t}(\Delta\theta_{t})\\
:=D_{h,l}(\theta_{t}+\Delta\theta_{t};q)-D_{h,l}(\theta_{t};q)\approx\Delta s_{t}^{\top}\Delta\theta_{t},$ 

(31)

with recovers Eq (12).

## Gap change under the GRPO update.

The standard GRPO update is $\Delta\theta_{t}^{\mathrm{GRPO}}=\alpha g_{t}$ , so

 $\mathfrak{D}_{t}(\Delta\theta_{t}^{\mathrm{GRPO}})\approx\Delta s_{t}^{\top}(\alpha g_{t})=\alpha\Delta s_{t}^{\top}g_{t}.$ 

(32)

## Gap change under the Flat-RLVR update.

For Lemma 4.3, the Flat-RLVR update is

 $\Delta\theta_{t}^{\mathrm{Flat}}=\alpha\!\left(g_{t}-\rho\,\frac{M_{t}g_{t}}{\|g_{t}\|_{2}}\right)+R_{t},$ 

(33)

where $R_{t}=-\alpha\rho\,B_{t}g_{t}/\|g_{t}\|_{2}-\alpha R_{T}$ is the residual.
Substituting into the first-order gap approximation:

 $\mathfrak{D}_{t}(\Delta\theta_{t}^{\mathrm{Flat}})\approx\Delta s_{t}^{\top}\Delta\theta_{t}^{\mathrm{Flat}}\\
=\Delta s_{t}^{\top}\!\left[\alpha g_{t}-\alpha\rho\,\frac{M_{t}g_{t}}{\|g_{t}\|_{2}}+R_{t}\right]\\
=\alpha\,\Delta s_{t}^{\top}g_{t}-\alpha\rho\,\frac{\Delta s_{t}^{\top}M_{t}g_{t}}{\|g_{t}\|_{2}}+\Delta s_{t}^{\top}R_{t}.$ 

(34)

## Identifying the decomposition.

Recognizing the GRPO term from Eq (32), and defining

 $C_{t}:=\frac{\Delta s_{t}^{\top}M_{t}g_{t}}{\|g_{t}\|_{2}},\,r_{t}:=\Delta s_{t}^{\top}R_{t},$ 

(35)

we obtain

 $\mathfrak{D}_{t}(\Delta\theta_{t}^{\texttt{Flat}})=\mathfrak{D}_{t}(\Delta\theta_{t}^{\mathrm{GRPO}})-\alpha\rho C_{t}+r_{t},$ 

(36)

which recovers Eq (13).

## A.3 Proof of Proposition 4.5

## Pointwise gap difference.

By the correct-set Gap Decomposition Lemma 4.4, for each step $t$ the difference between the Flat-RLVR-induced and GRPO-induced correct-set gap changes satisfies

 $\mathfrak{D}_{t}(\Delta\theta_{t}^{\mathrm{Flat}})-\mathfrak{D}_{t}(\Delta\theta_{t}^{\mathrm{GRPO}})=-\alpha\rho C_{t}+r_{t},$ 

(37)

where

 $C_{t}=\frac{\Delta s_{t}^{\top}M_{t}g_{t}}{\|g_{t}\|_{2}},\qquad r_{t}=\Delta s_{t}^{\top}R_{t}.$ 

(38)

## Summation over the trajectory.

Summing the pointwise relation over $t=0,1,\ldots,T-1$ yields

 $\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\mathfrak{D}_{t}(\Delta\theta_{t}^{\mathrm{Flat}})\\
=\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\mathfrak{D}_{t}(\Delta\theta_{t}^{\mathrm{GRPO}})-\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\alpha\rho C_{t}+\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}r_{t}.$ 

(39)

## Applying the dominance condition.

By the assumption of the proposition,

 $\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\alpha\rho C_{t}\;\geq\;\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}r_{t},$ 

(40)

so the net correction $-\sum_{t}\alpha\rho C_{t}+\sum_{t}r_{t}\leq 0$ . Substituting back gives

 $\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\mathfrak{D}_{t}(\Delta\theta_{t}^{\mathrm{Flat}})\;\leq\;\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\mathfrak{D}_{t}(\Delta\theta_{t}^{\mathrm{GRPO}}),$ 

(41)

which is the claimed inequality.

## Appendix B Adaptations for LLM post-training

## B.1 Focal Weight

Inspired by F-GRPO (Plyusov et al., 2026), which decays the advantage via a focal weight conditioned on prompt difficulty, we adapt this idea to SAM’s hyperparameter $\rho$ to promote stable training. Specifically, given $X$ correct rollouts out of a total of $N$ , we define the focal weight as

 $p_{\mathrm{mean}}=\frac{X}{N}.$ 

(42)

We then modulate SAM’s perturbation radius $\rho$ accordingly:

 $\rho=\rho\cdot\bigl(p_{\mathrm{mean}}\bigr)^{\gamma}.$ 

(43)

This formulation suppresses the influence of perturbations when the success rate is low (high difficulty)—thereby preventing unstable updates—while preserving the perturbation signal needed to discover rare correct trajectories when the success rate is high.

In practice, the focal weight itself is computed from the per-batch success rate,

 $\hat{p}_{\mathrm{mean}}=\frac{\hat{X}}{\hat{N}},$ 

(44)

where $\hat{N}$ is the training batch size and $\hat{X}$ is the number of correct samples within the batch.

## B.2 Gradient Centralization

Figure 6: Training entropy curves on GSM8K with Qwen2.5-0.5B-Instruct: SAM with Gradient Centralization (GC) applied at both the ascent and descent steps (GC2+aSAM+GRPO) versus only at the ascent step (GC+aSAM+GRPO). Applying GC before the descent step substantially mitigates entropy collapse compared to the ascent-only variant.

Applying Gradient Centralization before SAM’s ascent step is known to reduce gradient variance, thereby suppressing noise and improving the quality of sharpness estimation (Hassan et al., 2025). This property is particularly well suited to the group-relative RLVR setting, where sparse rewards and high-variance advantage estimates are inherent (Teng et al., 2026; Wang et al., 2026a). We therefore apply Gradient Centralization before the ascent step, providing a more reliable perturbation direction for SAM in the RLVR setting.

Specifically, let the weight matrix of a particular layer be $W\in\mathbb{R}^{M\times N}$ , and let $w_{i}$ denote the $i$ -th row vector of $W$ . Then we can define the following:

 $\nabla_{GC}J(w_{i})=\nabla J(w_{i})-\mu,$ 

(45)

where $\mu=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}\nabla J(w_{ij})$ and $J$ is the objective function.

In addition, we further apply Gradient Centralization before SAM’s descent step to reduce the gradient norm. Yong et al. (2020) shows that the norm of the projected gradient $\nabla_{\mathrm{GC}}$ admits the following bound:

 $\lVert\nabla_{\mathrm{GC}}J(W)\rVert^{2}_{2}=\bigl(P\,\nabla J(W)\bigr)^{\top}P\,\nabla J(W)\\
=\nabla J(W)^{\top}\bigl(I-ee^{\top}\bigr)\nabla J(W)\\
=\lVert\nabla J(W)\rVert^{2}_{2}-\lVert e^{\top}\nabla J(W)\rVert^{2}_{2}\;\leq\;\lVert\nabla J(W)\rVert^{2}_{2}.$ 

(46)

where $P=I-ee^{\top}$ is the projection matrix and $e=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\mathbf{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}$ is the unit vector with all components equal.

Reducing the gradient norm is known to bias the optimizer toward flat minima, thereby improving model generalization (Zhao et al., 2022). When combined with SAM, this property further enhances the model’s exploration ability: as shown in Figure 6, applying GC before the descent step yields substantially less entropy collapse than the variant without it. Note that both variants already applied SAM- $n$ and Cosine Annealing. Full training details are deferred to Appendix F.

## B.3 Annealing SAM $\rho$ 

Figure 7: Training reward curves on GSM8K with Qwen2.5-0.5B-Instruct: SAM with cosine annealing (aSAM + GRPO) versus without (SAM + GRPO) (i.e., periodic-SAM without cosine annealing). Without annealing, the training reward collapses to zero, halting effective learning.

For training stability, we adopt a cosine annealing schedule (Loshchilov and Hutter, 2017) that gradually decays SAM’s hyperparameter $\rho$ over the course of training. Concretely, $\rho$ at step $t$ follows

 $\rho_{t}=\rho_{\mathrm{min}}+\tfrac{1}{2}\,\bigl(\rho_{\mathrm{init}}-\rho_{\mathrm{min}}\bigr)\!\left(1+\cos\!\left(\frac{T_{\mathrm{cur}}}{T}\pi\right)\right),$ 

(47)

where $\rho_{\mathrm{init}}$ and $\rho_{\mathrm{min}}$ denote the initial and minimum values of $\rho$ , respectively, and $T_{\mathrm{cur}}$ and $T$ denote the current and total numbers of training steps. By smoothly decaying $\rho$ as training progresses, cosine annealing gradually attenuates the influence of the perturbation. In the early phase of training, this prevents entropy collapse driven by overfitting to the training data, while in the later phase it promotes stable convergence.

Figure 7 illustrates why this annealing is essential: without it, the training reward collapses to zero midway through training, halting effective learning. Gradually decaying $\rho$ is thus necessary for stable convergence in the later training phase. Full training details are deferred to Appendix F.

## Appendix D The lightweight approximation for Memory Efficiency.

We adopt a lightweight approximation for applying SAM in RLVR; cache only the scalar norm of the original gradient, recompute a second perturbation $\epsilon^{*}$ at the perturbed parameters $\theta+\epsilon$ . Concretely, by the definition of SAM, the two perturbations computed during this procedure are

 $\epsilon=\rho\,\frac{\nabla J(\theta)}{\lVert\nabla J(\theta)\rVert},\qquad\epsilon^{*}=\rho\,\frac{\nabla J(\theta+\epsilon)}{\lVert\nabla J(\theta)\rVert}.$ 

(48)

A first-order Taylor expansion of the gradient at the perturbed point yields

 $\nabla J(\theta+\epsilon)=\nabla J(\theta)+H\,\epsilon+\mathcal{O}(\rho^{2})\approx\nabla J(\theta)+\mathcal{O}(\rho),$ 

(49)

where $H:=\nabla^{2}J(\theta)$ is the Hessian. Substituting this expansion into the expression for $\epsilon^{*}$ gives

 $\epsilon^{*}\approx\epsilon+\mathcal{O}(\rho^{2})\approx\epsilon.$ 

(50)

Hence, for sufficiently small $\rho$ (e.g., $\rho\leq 0.01$ ), this approximation is tight, and SAM can be applied by caching only a single scalar—virtually no additional spatial cost.

## F.3 Full Training Details: F-GRPO

We implement F-GRPO following the official formulation of Plyusov et al. (2026). For each prompt $x$ , we compute the empirical success rate $\hat{\mu}_{\mathrm{pos}}(x)$ over its group of rollouts and scale the group-relative advantage by the Focal weight $g(x)=(1-\hat{\mu}_{\mathrm{pos}}(x))^{\gamma}$ , which down-weights updates on high-success prompts to preserve solution diversity. Following the original work, we set $\gamma=1$ and keep all other hyperparameters identical to the GRPO baseline.

## F.4 Evaluation Protocol

We compute the Pass@K metric following Chen et al. (2021):

 $\mathrm{Pass@}k\;:=\;\mathbb{E}_{\,\mathrm{Problems}}\!\left[1-\frac{\binom{n-c}{k}}{\binom{n}{k}}\right],$ 

(51)

where $n$ is the total number of generated samples per problem and $c$ is the number of correct samples among them. We set $n=256$ for all benchmarks and report from Pass@ $1$ to Pass@ $256$ . For MATH500 and AMC23, we set the completion length to $1024$ tokens, matching the training-time setting; for the AIME family of benchmarks, we extend it to $2048$ tokens to accommodate the longer reasoning chains required by these problems. For GSM8K, we use a completion length of $192$ tokens, again matching training.

Across all benchmarks, we use a single, uniform system prompt: “Please reason step by step, and put your final answer within \boxed{}.”

Abstract

Preference labels align model training, but the judgments behind them are difficult to inspect or verify. In this paper, we examine how preference construction can be checked when intermediate decisions are recorded, and which of them remain uncertain. We present Symbolic Preference Labeling, a method in which a pretrained language model classifies predefined attributes of prompts and responses. Explicit rules assign response verdicts, and a fixed verdict ranking determines the preference label. Each preference label has a recorded derivation: the response verdicts and preference label can be recomputed from the stored attribute assignments, rules, and verdict ranking without rerunning the language model. On 200 prompts annotated by human raters, the language model produces valid structured outputs for 192 prompts. All eight parsing failures occur in the subset of self-harm prompts marked as harmful. Using 465 preference pairs, we train one policy model with direct preference optimization across five training seeds. Mean measured benign refusal is 40.82 percent on 1,319 benign prompts that resemble unsafe ones, and mean measured toxic refusal is 84.98 percent on 655 toxic prompts. This study distinguishes the reproducibility of preference labels from attribute correctness. The recorded derivations make preference construction open to inspection, while attribute correctness and semantic safety require separate validation.

## I Introduction

With the trend of large language model (LLM) usage in critical areas such as medical systems, financial systems, and personal agents, the need for model inspectability is well documented [1]. Recent research has assessed misaligned model behavior in the mental health field [2].
That research showed that widely deployed systems have flaws in mental health contexts and that those harmful responses were not effectively captured by the models’ built-in safety mechanisms. Ren et al. [3] also studied real-world risks of multi-agent financial fraud and suggested that new mitigation measures are needed. Those cases amplified the need for inspectability of LLM behavior.

Preference labels are commonly used in post-training to align model behavior with human preferences [4, 5]. However, when human labelers do preference labeling, this may cause bias because of the labelers’ preferences [4, 6]. When an AI labeler is used to label responses [7, 8], it leaves the labeler’s preference hard to inspect, since its black-box weights are difficult to explain [9, 10].

In safety preference supervision, a label decides whether a refusal or a substantive answer to a sensitive request is reinforced. Since alignment data strongly affect both unsafe answers and over-refusal (refusing safe prompts which resemble unsafe ones) [11], a label built on a misread request may push the model toward either failure. An LLM judge used as the labeler returns a verdict and sometimes a rationale, while the intermediate decisions, such as whether the request asks for actionable steps, are not stored as separate evidence. The verdict can then be regenerated only by calling the judge again. However, a repeated call still does not show which intermediate decision produced the verdict.

Our Work. We investigate how to make a labeling process open to inspection in this work by developing a pipeline in which every derivation of a preference label can be mapped to specific intermediate decisions and the corresponding rules. We present Symbolic Preference Labeling (SPL) to address those challenges.

In SPL, a pretrained language model assigns values to predefined prompt and response attributes, such as the topic of a prompt, whether the request asks for actionable steps, or whether a response is a refusal. The prompt attributes of each prompt are assigned once and shared across all candidate responses to that prompt (prompt-attribute sharing). With prompt-attribute sharing, every candidate response to the prompt is given the same interpretation of that prompt. During response evaluation, the rule-based labeler that implements SPL evaluates the attribute assignments of each candidate under hand-written rules to obtain a response verdict (helpful compliance, justified refusal, partial compliance, unjustified refusal, or harmful compliance, from most to least preferred). If no rule applies to those assignments, the rule-based labeler abstains. When the verdicts of two candidates differ in rank, a fixed verdict ranking orders the two verdicts into a preference label with its recorded derivation. The recorded derivation of each preference label allows the label to be recomputed from the stored attribute assignments, rules, and verdict ranking without further language model calls, while attribute correctness and semantic safety (whether the content of a response is safe) require separate validation.

Contributions. Our contributions are:

(1) A rule-based labeler in which a rule base replaces the judge in building direct preference optimization (DPO) data [5] (Sec. III). We claim the combination, not any single ingredient (Sec. II). There are three ingredients in this combination: stored attribute assignments with prompt-attribute sharing, a solver-checked rule base, and recorded derivations for the preference labels used in training.

(2) Machine-checked properties of the rule-based labeler: all pairs of rules with different verdicts solver-checked as disjoint (the two rules of each such pair cannot both apply to the same attribute assignments), an exhaustive enumeration of the uncovered region (the possible attribute assignments to which no rule applies), and reproducible preference labels (Sec. IV-A).

(3) Measurements of prompt-attribute agreement with a human reference from the plurality vote of human raters (Sec. IV-D) and of refusal behavior (Sec. IV-E). In the main evaluation, we train one policy model with DPO on preference labels from SPL with prompt-attribute sharing (prompt-shared labels). For the judge comparison (Sec. IV-F), we train the same policy model anew under prompt-shared labels and under labels from the same language model prompted directly as a judge (judge labels). Both evaluations are exploratory. Since the judge comparison uses candidate pairs selected by the rule-based labeler, not the judge’s own selection of pairs, it is not a general comparison of the two labeling methods.

## II Related Work

## II-A Rule-Based Rewards

Recent work already puts rules into the training signal (Rule-Based Rewards [12],
safe-completion training [13], and deliberative alignment [14]), but in each a prompted LLM still decides whether a rule is satisfied, leaving that decision opaque [9], inconsistent across repeated runs, and hard to verify [15]. However, repeated runs of a judge can also be stable: according to Norman et al. [16], two production-deployed judges reach test-retest reliability above 0.95 while still showing severe position bias. SPL instead stores the attribute assignments that those decisions depend on, checks its rules with a solver, and does not require an additional LLM call to decide whether a rule applies.
As with the LLM grader in Rule-Based Rewards, the language model in SPL still decides which attribute values hold. SPL stores these attribute assignments and combines them with fixed rules and a fixed verdict ranking into a preference label that can be recomputed, while Rule-Based Rewards combines the probabilities of the grader through fitted weights.

## II-B LLM Judges as Preference Labelers

In Constitutional AI, human harmlessness labels are replaced with preferences from a model that compares two samples under a written list of principles [7]. Lee et al. [8] examine reinforcement learning from AI feedback, in which an off-the-shelf LLM supplies the preferences for the reward model. They find that this method reaches performance similar to that of learning from human feedback on summarization and on helpful and harmless dialogue. According to Zheng et al. [17], GPT-4 as a judge agrees with human preferences about as often as humans agree with one another, while LLM judges also show biases toward answer position, longer answers, and their own outputs. Schwinn et al. [18] show that, under the distribution shifts of red-teaming, LLM judges of harmfulness can perform close to chance. In this paper, the judge is used as a comparator and is not treated as ground truth.

## II-C Interpretable and Rule-Grounded Rewards

Concept Bottleneck Reward Models (CB-RM) from Laguna et al. [19] predict rewards through concepts that people can read. Dineen et al. [20] split the reward into principle-specific checks in QA-LIGN and report that this interpretable reward improves alignment. Li et al. [21] label preference data with data-adaptive safety rules, and for each response pair they select the rules on which the two responses differ most. POLARIS expresses written safety policies in first-order logic and traces each generated test query back to its rules [22]. With linear temporal logic, labelers built on small models detect constraint violations as well as or better than frontier LLM judges [23]. CB-RM, POLARIS, and data-adaptive safety rules are the closest prior work, covering interpretable preference learning, rule traceability, and rule-based labeling of response pairs. Compared with these three works, SPL claims only the combination of stored attribute assignments with prompt-attribute sharing, a solver-checked rule base, and recorded derivations for the preference labels used in training.

## II-D Over-Refusal Evaluation

Over-refusal benchmarks test whether a model refuses safe prompts which resemble unsafe ones. For example, XSTest contains safe prompts that use the wording or topics of unsafe requests, along with unsafe prompts for comparison [24]. OR-Bench provides a large set of over-refusal prompts, a harder subset of about one thousand prompts, and a separate set of toxic prompts [25]. In POROver, Karaman et al. [11] reduce over-refusal in highly safe models through preference optimization on answers from a stronger teacher model. They report that safety stays similar afterwards. To measure refusal, we use a fixed pattern-based detector on the OR-Bench Hard-1K prompts (measured benign refusal) and on the Toxic prompts (measured toxic refusal). However, these rates describe refusal behavior and do not establish semantic safety or the helpfulness of answers that are not refusals.

## II-E Formal Methods as Guardrails

A recent wave
of work brings formal methods to LLM inputs, outputs, and agent actions:
neuro-symbolic verification of LLM-written medical device damage-assessment reports [26],
runtime verification
of conversations against temporal properties [27],
agents whose intended actions are written as first-order logic constraints and checked before they run [28],
AgentFlow, which checks formally defined agent data-flow
policies using a bounded satisfiability modulo theories (SMT) verifier [29],
and validation of model inputs and outputs against domain constraints with Answer Set
Programming [30]. Those are close to SPL, but a difference still exists: these works guard inputs, outputs, or agent actions while a system runs. In contrast, SPL checks the rule base that builds training preference labels for rule consistency with a solver.

## III Symbolic Preference Labeling

## III-A Recorded Derivations: Pipeline

SPL constructs a preference label for two candidate responses to the same prompt, a process (preference construction) consisting of three steps: attribute assignment, response evaluation, and the final comparison of verdict ranks (Fig. 1).

Fig. 1: Symbolic Preference Labeling pipeline for one candidate pair. Prompt attributes are assigned by the pretrained language model from the prompt alone and reused for both candidate responses (prompt-attribute sharing), while response attributes are taken from stored attribute assignments produced earlier by the same language model with both the prompt and candidate response as input. After attribute assignment, the rule base gives each candidate a response verdict, and the ranks of the two verdicts are compared under the fixed verdict ranking to determine the preference label. Dashed lines mark the cases that produce no preference label. The bottom band names the components of the recorded derivation, and the preference label can be recomputed from these components without further language model calls.

A pretrained language model is used to assign values to the predefined attributes per prompt and response. Prompt attributes are assigned from the prompt itself and stored per exact prompt text for reuse across its candidate responses (prompt-attribute sharing), while response attributes are taken from the stored assignments generated earlier with both the prompt and the candidate response as input (a joint assignment of prompt and response attributes). Following that, the rule base converts those assignments into a response verdict for each candidate. A fixed verdict ranking then orders the two verdicts, resulting in a preference label for safety preference supervision. An invalid prompt assignment (a parsing failure, Sec. III-B), an uncovered response, or two responses with equal verdict ranks produce no preference label.

A recorded derivation links a preference label to the candidate identities, stored attribute assignments, response verdicts, applied rule identifiers, and the rule base and verdict ranking in use. The implementation retains these components across linked records (Appendix D). Exclusion records identify pairs that produce no preference label. Given the same stored attribute assignments, rules, and ranking, recomputing the response verdicts and preference label is deterministic and requires no further language model call. These records make preference construction open to inspection. They do not establish that the language model assigned the attributes correctly or that the resulting preference is semantically safe.

For prompt attributes, the language model receives only the prompt text and the schema of the six prompt attributes (Table I). It produces one assignment per exact prompt text, which is reused across candidate responses. A cache binds the assignment to the prompt text and assignment configuration (including the language model, its instructions, the decoding settings, and the parser). Consequently, candidate responses to one prompt receive the same prompt-attribute assignments. This is a property of reuse, not a measure of agreement with human judgment.

The four response attributes are taken from stored attribute assignments produced by the same language model with both the prompt and candidate response as input. The evaluated implementation combines these stored response-attribute assignments with the shared prompt-attribute assignments. It does not use a separately trained response classifier. The caveats attribute is retained in the records but is not read by any of the eight rules.

Prompt-attribute assignment uses greedy decoding with a maximum of 320 generated tokens (Appendix A). An output is accepted only if it stops normally and consists of one JSON object with the six required fields. The parser accepts a surrounding Markdown code fence and normalizes Boolean strings or numeric zero and one to Boolean values. It rejects unknown topic values, missing or extra fields, duplicate keys, invalid values, and outputs that reach the token limit. Rejected outputs are retained as parsing failures. They are not replaced with default assignments. These checks establish that an output conforms to the interface, not that its content is correct.

However, these checks apply only to prompt-attribute assignment, while the stored response-attribute assignments come from the earlier joint assignment by the same language model. That assignment took the prompt and one candidate response as input, assigned prompt and response attributes together, and used its own parser. In addition, the response attributes are not assigned again, and the correctness of these stored assignments is not validated here.

## III-C Rules and Response Verdicts

The rule base contains eight hand-written rules that assign one of five response verdicts: helpful compliance, justified refusal, partial compliance, unjustified refusal, or harmful compliance. If no rule applies, the labeler returns an abstention, denoted by $\bot$ and represented as ambiguous. Abstention is separate from the five ranked verdicts. These verdict names describe the policy encoded by the rules. They are not independent human assessments of the responses.

For a prompt-response pair, abbreviate $p=p(x)$ and $r=r(x,y)$ , with their components defined in Table I. We define three conditions on the prompt-attribute assignments and one on the response-attribute assignments:

 $\displaystyle\mathrm{sensitive}(p)$ 
 $\displaystyle\equiv p_{\mathrm{topic}}\notin\{\text{benign},\text{out of scope}\},$ 

 $\displaystyle\mathrm{framing}(p)$ 
 $\displaystyle\equiv p_{\mathrm{knowledge}}\lor p_{\mathrm{historical}}\lor p_{\mathrm{fictional}},$ 

 $\displaystyle\mathrm{operational}(p)$ 
 $\displaystyle\equiv p_{\mathrm{method}}\lor p_{\mathrm{steps}},$ 

 $\displaystyle\mathrm{substantive}(r)$ 
 $\displaystyle\equiv\neg r_{\mathrm{refusal}}\land r_{\mathrm{info}}.$ 

A topic is sensitive when its assigned category is neither benign nor out of scope. Framing is present when any of the three framing attributes is assigned true. A request is operational when either a specific method or actionable steps are requested according to the attribute assignments. A response is substantive, as used in the rules, when it is assigned substantive information and is not assigned refusal. These conditions operate on stored attribute assignments. For example, Rule R3 assigns justified refusal when the stored attribute assignments indicate a sensitive topic, an operational request, and a refusal. This verdict follows from those assignments even if the language model misclassified an attribute. Checking the rule application and checking attribute correctness are therefore separate tasks.

The labeler evaluates all eight rule conditions against the supplied assignments. Several rules may apply if they produce the same verdict. Only R3 and R9 can do so, both assigning justified refusal. The first applied rule in the fixed rule order supplies the recorded rule identifier, so R3 is recorded when both apply. Conflicting verdicts cause an error. If no rule applies, the labeler abstains. Rule R8 is independent of topic and can apply even when the topic is out of scope. Out-of-scope inputs do not uniformly receive an abstention.

## III-D Consistency and Preference Construction

The rules are encoded in the Z3 solver [31], and the attribute assignments of each prompt-response pair are encoded with topic as an enumerated value and the other nine attributes as Booleans. From this encoding, a symbolic form of the assignments (typed facts) is obtained, and the rule conditions are then evaluated against these typed facts. For every pair of rules with different verdicts, the consistency check asks whether their conditions can hold simultaneously. An unsatisfiable conjunction establishes that those two rules cannot conflict under any assignment in the encoded schema (any combination of attribute values that the schema permits, not only those assigned by the language model). Rules with the same verdict may apply together. A separate satisfiability check verifies that each rule can apply for some assignment. Neither check establishes the semantic correctness of the rules or complete coverage of the encoded assignments.

The fixed verdict ranking is helpful compliance, justified refusal, partial compliance, unjustified refusal, and harmful compliance, with ranks 4 through 0 respectively. Higher ranks are preferred. The rank difference is retained for inspection but does not weight the direct preference optimization loss. A candidate pair is excluded when either response receives an abstention or both verdicts have equal rank.

## III-E Candidate Data and Training-Pair Selection

The candidate responses come from a frozen pool generated with Qwen3.5-9B under three instructions encouraging direct, cautious, or strongly restrictive responses, each ending with the same instruction to stay under roughly 300 words (Appendix A). Generation used temperature 0.7, top-p 0.9, and a limit of 1,024 tokens. The prompt sources are UltraChat [32], OR-Bench-80K [25], XSTest [24], HarmBench [33], Do-Not-Answer [34], and MaliciousInstruct [35]. A split of a 4,316-prompt pool, stratified by source and source category (benign, over-refusal-prone, or harmful), reserved 3,020 prompts for candidate generation, and the rest formed validation and test splits that neither the main evaluation nor the judge comparison uses (Appendix B). For each of the 3,020 prompts, one response was generated per instruction, resulting in a total of 9,060 candidate responses. Normalized exact-text checks against both policy-evaluation benchmarks found no overlap. These checks do not exclude semantic similarity.

Candidates with generation errors, token-limit termination, or missing required attribute assignments or missing verdicts from the same pretrained language model prompted directly as a judge are excluded before pair selection. The evaluated dataset begins with a stored selection of 481 candidate pairs (the source-pair selection) covering 356 prompts, taken from the pair set of the rule-based labeler. That selection was constructed by restricting prompts to those with an eligible pair (a candidate pair that receives a preference label) under both the rule-based labeler and the judge, and then, within each verdict-pair stratum (the pairs with the same preferred response verdict and the same rejected response verdict), by downsampling the larger of the rule-based labeler’s and the judge’s pair sets to the count of the smaller set, with seed 42. It is therefore a selected candidate population, not a random sample of all generated responses. The source-pair selection, which used verdicts of the rule-based labeler computed from the earlier joint assignment (Sec. III-B), is retained rather than recomputed using the shared prompt-attribute assignments. The present evaluation reports the rule-based labeler on that population, and judge labels on a common-support subset (the retained pairs whose two judge verdicts differ in rank, defined below).

We assign prompt attributes to all 356 prompts, combine these assignments with the stored response-attribute assignments, and retain each source pair that receives a strict preference. In this way, the retained pairs carry preference labels constructed with prompt-attribute sharing (prompt-shared labels).

Candidate texts and source row order remain fixed. No additional sampling or balancing follows this step. This construction makes the training population conditional on both the source-pair selection and a strict preference under prompt-shared labels. The resulting pair counts and exclusions are reported in Sec. IV-C.

In the main evaluation, the policy model is trained on those retained candidate pairs with their prompt-shared labels, while a separate judge comparison adds a second condition with preference labels from the judge. For the judge verdicts, we use the stored verdicts of the same language model prompted directly as a judge at temperature 0, and the judge was not called again for this comparison. The judge prompt lists the sensitive topics and gives the verdict of each rule with its one-sentence natural-language description from the rule base, without the attribute definitions of Table I. In addition, the judge is instructed to answer ambiguous for an out-of-scope topic (unlike the abstention of the rule-based labeler, which occurs when no rule applies) and to return one JSON verdict, which is either one of the five response verdicts or ambiguous. Under the same fixed verdict ranking, these verdicts are then converted into preference labels (judge labels). The comparison is restricted to the retained candidate pairs whose two judge verdicts differ in rank (the common support). Judge ties are the only exclusion, and no judge verdict on the retained candidate pairs is ambiguous. The policy model is trained anew under prompt-shared labels and under judge labels on these same candidate pairs. Because these pairs come from the selection of the rule-based labeler, the judge-labels condition does not train on the judge’s own selection of pairs. The judge serves as a comparator and is not ground truth.

## III-F Human-Reference Evaluation

We evaluate the six prompt attributes on 200 prompts annotated by six human raters (the panel, Appendix E). For each attribute, the reference is the unique plurality among the six votes. An item with a tied maximum is excluded for that attribute only. Knowledge framing follows the fixed mapping of the annotation forms, which differed between raters: a single knowledge field for two raters, and educational framing or research context (a true vote when either field is marked) for the other four.

We report output validity separately from agreement with the reference. We exclude parsing failures and reference ties when calculating conditional accuracy, Cohen’s kappa, and Boolean F1. Coverage-aware accuracy divides the number of correct assignments by the number of items with a non-tied reference, counting parsing failures as incorrect. The topic schema permits ten categories, whereas the panel uses four (benign, chemistry and biology, self-harm, and illegal activity). Topic exact-match scores retain this mismatch rather than remapping categories after observing predictions.

The panel had been used during project development and is not an untouched test set. Nine exact prompt texts overlap the 356-prompt attribute-assignment population and reuse cached assignments under the same configuration. The other 191 items receive fresh generations. No tuning follows these panel results. This evaluation concerns prompt attributes and does not validate the four response attributes or complete preference labels.

## III-G Model Training and Behavioral Measurement

We train Gemma-4-12B-it as the policy model using direct preference optimization [5] with low-rank adaptation [36]. Five runs use training seeds 1 through 5 and the same retained pairs.

For the judge comparison, the policy model is trained under prompt-shared labels and under judge labels on the common support with the same five seeds, and the runs with the same seed are paired.

Each run starts from the same pinned pretrained weights and trains for three epochs. Adapter rank is 16, scaling parameter $\alpha=320$ (an applied scale of $\alpha/16=20$ ), and dropout 0.05. The preference-loss parameter $\beta$ of DPO is 0.1 and the learning rate is $5\times 10^{-7}$ . A batch size of one with eight accumulation steps gives an effective batch size of eight. Training uses bfloat16 and a maximum sequence length of 2,048 tokens (Appendix F).

In every run, a versioned chat-template correction is used and applied identically in all conditions: each completed training example is rendered with the same empty thought prefix that ends the unchanged evaluation generation prompt (an empty reasoning block, which the unmodified chat template of the policy model omits from a completed assistant turn when thinking is disabled, Appendix F). Without this correction, the training library would cut the first tokens of each candidate response.

A tokenization check verifies that the generation prompt is a prefix of each completed example and that both candidate responses are retained in full. All reported runs passed this check, and the check found no prefix mismatch or changed candidate-response token.

Behavioral evaluation uses the frozen OR-Bench [25] Hard-1K and Toxic files, containing 1,319 and 655 prompts respectively. These are the sizes of the file versions we evaluated. Generation is greedy, with batch size eight and a 200-token limit. A fixed pattern-based refusal detector collapses whitespace and examines the first 400 characters of each response (Appendix F). We call its refusal rate on Hard-1K measured benign refusal and its refusal rate on Toxic measured toxic refusal. The first measures over-refusal under the benchmark labels, which treat the Hard-1K prompts as safe. The second measures refusal behavior rather than semantic safety.

For the main evaluation, we report every seed’s rate, the mean refusal rate, and the sample standard deviation across seed rates. Descriptive 95 percent intervals for each mean use Student’s t distribution with four degrees of freedom. These intervals are calculated from the evaluated model’s five seed rates. They summarize training-seed variation on fixed data and benchmarks, excluding uncertainty in attribute assignment, dataset selection, benchmark sampling, and transfer to other models.

In the judge comparison, each condition is summarized by the mean and sample standard deviation across seed rates, while per-seed rates are not listed and descriptive intervals are not given. To compare the two conditions, we take their difference as prompt-shared labels minus judge labels. For this difference, the paired 95 percent interval uses the same Student’s t distribution with four degrees of freedom and is calculated from the five per-seed differences between runs with the same seed. The reported differences are given in percentage points and computed from the unrounded seed means. We call a difference detectable only when its interval excludes zero, while an interval that includes zero does not establish equivalence because no equivalence margin (the largest difference that would count as negligible) was set. None of the intervals has a multiplicity adjustment (a correction for multiple comparisons), and the main evaluation and the judge comparison are not pooled.

The evaluation does not estimate a general difference between the rule-based labeler and the judge.

Both the main evaluation and the judge comparison are exploratory, and the judge comparison was designed after the results of the main evaluation were known and frozen.

## IV Results

## IV-A Rule Consistency and Reproducibility of Preference Labels

The eight-rule implementation passes all 25 checks between rules with different verdicts: no checked pair of rules can apply simultaneously on one encoded assignment. Each rule also has a satisfying assignment. An independent Boolean transcription (a second implementation of the eight rules as plain Boolean conditions, written without the solver) agrees with the solver-based evaluation on all 5,120 assignments permitted by the schema, comprising ten topics and nine Boolean attributes. Of these assignments, 476 have no applicable rule (Appendix C). This is coverage of the finite representation, not an estimate of abstention on naturally occurring text.

All 356 prompt-attribute outputs for the prompts of the source-pair selection parse successfully. Offline reconstruction from the stored prompt-attribute assignments, response-attribute assignments, rules, and verdict ranking reproduces the 465 retained preference labels and the recorded decisions to exclude the remaining pairs. This establishes correspondence between the stored evidence and the dataset supplied to training. It does not establish that the attribute assignments or preference labels agree with human judgment.

## IV-E Behavior of the Trained Model

All five training seeds complete both benchmark evaluations, producing 9,870 response instances in total. Fig. 4 reports the trained model’s outcomes on the fixed benign and toxic benchmarks.

Fig. 4: Refusal outcomes after training on the 465 preference pairs. Each seed is a separate training run evaluated on the same prompts and is shown as a square, while the bar marks the mean and the whisker marks the descriptive 95 percent Student t interval for the mean across five seeds. For seeds 1 to 5, measured benign refusal is 40.71, 40.49, 40.71, 40.86 and 41.32 percent, with a sample standard deviation of 0.31 percentage points. The rates of measured toxic refusal for the same seeds are 84.73, 84.89, 85.19, 85.04 and 85.04 percent, and their sample standard deviation is 0.17 percentage points.

Measured benign refusal is approximately two fifths of the benchmark. Measured toxic refusal is approximately 85 percent. These absolute rates describe the trained model under the specified detector and generation settings. They do not establish that Symbolic Preference Labeling improves either outcome or preserves semantic safety.

Generation limits also constrain interpretation. Across the five runs, 4,622 of 6,595 benign response instances reach the 200-token limit, as do 597 of 3,275 toxic response instances: 70.08 and 18.23 percent respectively (Appendix G). The evaluation therefore does not establish that non-refused answers are complete or helpful. Variation across seeds is small under this fixed setup, but the intervals exclude uncertainty in the benchmark labels, refusal detector, attribute assignments, and selected training pairs.

## IV-F Judge Comparison on Common Support

In the judge comparison, 443 of the 465 retained candidate pairs are used, covering 332 prompts, while the other 22 pairs are judge ties. Before training, judge labels agree with prompt-shared labels on 350 of these pairs and reverse the remaining 93. Since attribute assignment and the judge use the same language model, the agreement between the rule-based labeler and the judge may partly be a shared-model artifact. The judge is used as a comparator and is not ground truth. On those 443 pairs, the policy model is then trained under prompt-shared labels and under judge labels with seeds 1 through 5, and runs with the same seed are paired. No model is reused from the main evaluation (Fig. 4). Fig. 5 reports the refusal levels and the paired contrast (Appendix G).

Fig. 5: Judge comparison on 443 common-support pairs. Each training seed is shown under prompt-shared labels (squares) and judge labels (triangles), while gray lines join runs with the same seed and bars mark the means. The mean of measured benign refusal is 40.47 percent under prompt-shared labels and 45.58 percent under judge labels, with sample standard deviations across seeds of 0.40 and 0.38 percentage points respectively. For measured toxic refusal, the means are 85.01 and 84.76 percent under prompt-shared labels and judge labels respectively, with sample standard deviations of 0.25 and 0.13 percentage points. The printed contrast is prompt-shared labels minus judge labels, given in percentage points (pp) and computed from unrounded rates. For this contrast, the paired 95 percent Student t interval (four degrees of freedom) is taken over the five per-seed differences. This comparison is not pooled with the main evaluation.

In the judge comparison, the difference in measured benign refusal, prompt-shared labels minus judge labels, is $-5.11$ percentage points, with a paired 95 percent interval of $[-5.53,-4.69]$ . This interval excludes zero, making the difference detectable. For measured toxic refusal, the difference prompt-shared labels minus judge labels is $+0.24$ percentage points, which is not detectable because the paired 95 percent interval of $[-0.10,0.59]$ includes zero. The result also does not establish that the two conditions are equivalent. Meanwhile, a larger share of benign response instances reaches the 200-token limit under prompt-shared labels (70.63 percent) than under judge labels (66.13 percent). Thus, the lower measured benign refusal under prompt-shared labels does not establish more complete or more helpful answers. For toxic response instances, the share reaching the limit is also larger under prompt-shared labels (18.47 percent) than under judge labels (17.19 percent).

However, this comparison is exploratory and was designed after the results of the main evaluation were known and frozen. Because its candidate pairs are taken from the selection of the rule-based labeler, the judge-labels condition does not train on the judge’s own selection of pairs, meaning that the comparison does not estimate a general difference between rule-based and judge labeling.

## V Discussion

## V-A What Recorded Derivations Make Checkable

Recorded derivations expose how the labeling procedure turns attribute assignments into training preferences. The same records distinguish a missing preference caused by rule coverage from one caused by equal verdict ranks. In this implementation, offline reconstruction reproduces the retained labels and exclusions, while the consistency check rules out conflicting verdicts within the encoded rule base.

This gives a concrete meaning to making preference construction open to inspection. A disagreement can be attached to a particular attribute assignment, rule condition, or verdict ranking. The worked example shows this separation: the preference follows from a benign-topic assignment, different refusal assignments, and the ranking of helpful compliance above unjustified refusal. A reader who rejects the preference can identify which of those commitments should change. Reproducing the computation alone cannot decide that dispute.

The study demonstrates access to those components, but it does not measure whether human auditors find them easier to use, detect more errors, or reach better decisions. Recorded derivations also do not explain the language model’s internal reasoning. Their practical value for reviewing labels requires a separate evaluation with people carrying out defined inspection tasks.

## V-B Attribute Correctness Remains an Empirical Question

The human-reference evaluation shows why reproducibility of preference labels and attribute correctness need separate measurements. The structured interface succeeds on most panel items, yet parsing failures concentrate in the harmful self-harm subgroup. Within valid outputs, agreement with the panel reference varies by attribute. Knowledge framing and actionable steps are particularly consequential because their assignments affect which rules apply, but they also have substantial disagreement with the panel reference.

Sharing prompt-attribute assignments gives every candidate response the same interpretation of its prompt. It does not ensure that this interpretation is correct. An incorrect shared assignment can affect every candidate for that prompt. Similarly, retaining a response-attribute assignment makes its contribution reproducible without validating its meaning. The four response attributes remain unvalidated by the prompt-only panel reported here.

The eight parsing failures illustrate a further distinction between the language model’s refusal behavior and completion of the attribute-assignment task. These outputs contain refusal or support prose instead of attribute assignments. Such a response may be appropriate in a conversational setting, but it supplies no valid input to this labeling interface. Its concentration in one subgroup should therefore remain visible when reporting dataset coverage. The rules did not cause the failures: those rules operate after attribute assignment and were never reached for these eight items.

## V-C Explicit Rules Expose Choices Without Resolving Them

The rule base makes several substantive choices visible. It treats requests for methods or steps on sensitive topics as operational, gives specified forms of framing a role only for non-operational requests, and ranks helpful compliance above justified refusal. These choices can be inspected and revised, but their explicit form does not establish that they are appropriate for every request.

In particular, the response attributes are coarse. A substantive response to a sensitive operational request receives harmful compliance under R5 when it is not classified as a refusal. The rule does not directly test whether the substantive content is harmful assistance or a safe alternative. A response dominated by refusal can also contain content that warrants further assessment. Adding a solver cannot recover distinctions absent from the attributes and rules.

Consistency and coverage likewise answer different questions. The solver excludes contradictory verdicts for the same encoded assignment, but some assignments remain uncovered. Abstention makes those gaps explicit and prevents them from silently receiving a preference label. It is not a calibrated confidence estimate and does not establish that the excluded examples are harder than the retained ones.

## V-D What the Behavioral Results Establish

The five-seed evaluation describes the behavior of a model trained on the retained pairs with prompt-shared labels. It reports a substantial measured benign refusal rate alongside frequent measured toxic refusal. Because the main evaluation presents one labeling method on one selected dataset, its rates do not identify a causal effect of the method or establish superiority over another source of preferences.

In the judge comparison, the policy model is trained on 443 of the retained pairs with prompt-shared labels and with judge labels, and no model is reused from the main evaluation. On these pairs, measured benign refusal is lower under prompt-shared labels than under judge labels, and the difference prompt-shared labels minus judge labels is $-5.11$ percentage points (paired 95 percent interval $[-5.53,-4.69]$ ). Along with this lower refusal, a larger share of benign outputs reaches the generation limit: 70.63 percent of benign response instances across the five seeds reach the limit under prompt-shared labels, while 66.13 percent reach it under judge labels. Thus, lower refusal is not evidence of complete, helpful answers. The difference in measured toxic refusal, prompt-shared labels minus judge labels, is $+0.24$ percentage points (paired 95 percent interval $[-0.10,0.59]$ ). This difference is not detectable because its interval includes zero. However, an interval that includes zero does not establish that the two conditions are equivalent. Because the training pairs are taken from the selection of the rule-based labeler, the comparison does not measure rule-based and judge labeling in general. In addition, the comparison is exploratory and was designed after the results of the main evaluation were known and frozen. The main evaluation and the judge comparison use different sets of pairs and are not pooled.

The refusal detector further limits what can be concluded. Non-refusal on a toxic prompt can contain a safe alternative, and a detected refusal can coexist with unsafe material. Non-refusal on a benign prompt does not establish helpfulness or completeness. The high proportion of benign outputs reaching the generation limit makes the last distinction especially relevant. Semantic evaluation of the full responses would be needed to assess those properties.

The narrow variation across five training seeds indicates repeatability of these measured rates under the evaluated configuration. It does not bound variation caused by different pretrained language models for attribute assignment, rule policies, prompt populations, or generation settings. The model that assigns attributes, the model that produced candidate responses, and the model being trained have distinct roles. Evaluating one choice for each does not establish robustness across those roles.

## V-E Limitations and Future Directions

The evidence is limited to one rule base, one pretrained language model for attribute assignment, one candidate-generation configuration, and one trained policy model. Training in the main evaluation uses 465 pairs selected from an existing candidate pool, with additional exclusions for ties and uncovered responses. The observed behavior is conditional on that selection. The reused human-reference panel is small, partly overlaps the attribute-assignment population, and does not cover the full topic taxonomy or response attributes. Its human annotations also contain disagreements and ties, so the plurality reference is not an unqualified measure of ground truth.

A useful extension would evaluate prompt and response attributes on an independent sample with a matching annotation schema, then assess whether complete preference labels are justified. Human inspection tasks could test whether recorded derivations help reviewers identify and resolve labeling problems. Evaluation across sizes and families of the pretrained language model would address how model choice affects output validity and attribute agreement with a human reference while leaving the deterministic rule computation fixed. Longer generations and semantic response assessment would clarify what the refusal measurements omit.

The supported contribution is a labeling procedure whose decisions can be reconstructed and whose remaining uncertainties can be examined separately. Recorded derivations establish a basis for inspecting preference construction. Attribute correctness, the appropriateness of the rules, and semantic safety remain questions about the assignments, rules, and training outcomes that the records expose.

## Ethical Considerations

No direct harmful language appears in the paper. Raters only saw prompts from public datasets that are already published online. No compensation was provided to raters. No institutional review board (IRB) determination was sought. Raters were told in advance that the content contained harmful responses or questions. Raters labeled in small batches and were free to pause, skip items, or withdraw at any time. No new attack method was introduced.

## Appendix A Language-Model Instructions

This appendix reproduces the fixed instructions of prompt-attribute assignment, of the joint assignment that produced the stored response-attribute assignments, of the judge, and of candidate generation, along with the decoding settings of each call. Every instruction is sent as the system message, while the prompt is placed in a fixed user-message template, which for the joint assignment and the judge also contains the candidate response. Figs. 6 to 9 give each instruction exactly as sent and keep its own line breaks. Fig. 7 refers to Fig. 6 for the lines shared by the two instructions. When a source line is wider than the page, the line is split at a space, and the end of the printed line is marked with $\hookleftarrow$ . Italic text is an editorial note and is not part of the model input.

Attribute assignment and the judge use Llama-3.3-70B-Instruct. The language model was converted to 8-bit MLX weights with a group size of 64, using affine quantization. Decoding is greedy in every attribute-assignment and judge call, with temperature 0 and top-p 1.

## A-A Prompt-Attribute Assignment

For prompt-attribute assignment, the instruction of Fig. 6 is sent with a limit of 320 generated tokens. Since the model input is built from the exact prompt text alone, no candidate response or other field of the prompt record reaches the language model. An output is accepted only when generation stops before the token limit and when the whole completion is one JSON object, either bare or fenced. A fenced object must sit inside a single code fence whose first line is three backquotes, optionally followed by json, and whose last line is three backquotes. The parser does not search for an object inside surrounding text, and it also rejects duplicate keys, missing or extra keys, and any topic value that is not one of the ten schema values exactly as written. For a Boolean field, the parser accepts three encodings: a JSON Boolean, the number 0 or 1, and the string true or false in any letter case. Such a field is stored as a JSON Boolean. When an output is rejected, it is stored with its error message and without an assignment. No default value is substituted.

The assignments are kept in an append-only cache under a key formed from the SHA-256 digest of the exact prompt bytes and a digest of the whole assignment configuration. This configuration covers the instruction text, the user-message template, the decoding settings, the model repository and revision, the source files of the assignment code, the runtime package versions, and the parser rules. The assignment configuration also covers the digests of the language model’s configuration, tokenizer, and chat-template files, along with the declared digests of its weight files. In this configuration, the runtime package versions are mlx 0.32.2, mlx-lm 0.31.3, transformers 5.17.0, and tokenizers 0.23.2. Every cache row is checked against its key when the cache is loaded, and a run under a different configuration cannot reuse the cache. Since repeated greedy generations are not assumed to be bit-identical, the prompt-attribute assignments used in this paper are fixed by the stored assignments instead of by a repeated call.

For the 356 prompts of the source-pair selection, every output was produced and parsed at the first attempt and stopped within 75 generated tokens. Each of these outputs also consisted of one JSON object inside a code fence. For the 200 panel prompts, 9 assignments were reused from the cache, and 191 outputs were generated at the first attempt. The longest of those outputs had 165 tokens. The eight rejected outputs among them are described in Appendix E. In total, the cache holds 547 entries, and 539 of these entries contain an assignment.

## A-B Stored Response-Attribute Assignments

The source of the stored response-attribute assignments is the joint assignment. For each candidate response, the joint assignment sent the instruction of Fig. 7 with a user message containing both the prompt and that response. In this instruction, the definitions of the six prompt attributes are identical, byte for byte, to those of Fig. 6. The two instructions differ only in their first two lines and in the six lines appended to the joint instruction: a blank line, a heading, and the four response-attribute definitions. Decoding was greedy with a limit of 320 generated tokens, under the runtime recorded for the candidate corpus (mlx 0.31.2, mlx-lm 0.31.3, and transformers 5.10.2). The joint assignment used a parser of its own, and this parser keeps the last brace-delimited object in the output that parses as JSON and contains a schema key. Before matching the ten schema values, this parser trims and lower-cases the topic and replaces its hyphens with underscores. All nine Boolean fields must also be present with the encodings accepted above, while keys outside the schema are ignored and the stopping reason of generation is not checked. Each of the 9,060 candidate responses has a parsed stored assignment. However, the rule-based labeler takes only the assignments of the four response attributes from such an assignment, since the prompt attributes are assigned from the prompt alone as described above.

## A-C Judge

In each judge call, the instruction of Fig. 8 is sent with the same user-message template as the joint assignment and a limit of 512 generated tokens. This instruction is generated from the rule base and contains no attribute definitions. Its eight rule lines are the descriptions of R1, R2, R3, R5, R6, R7, R8, and R9 in that order. In these lines, each description is preceded by its verdict name and shown without its rule identifier. A verdict is read first from the first quoted value of a verdict key anywhere in the output, then from the last brace-delimited object with a valid verdict field, and finally from exactly one distinct verdict name in free text. Any other output becomes ambiguous. Each of the 9,060 stored judge verdicts records that it was read from a JSON verdict field, with no verdict read from free text and no failure. However, only SHA-256 digests of the raw outputs are kept. The judge comparison uses those stored verdicts, and the judge was not called again.

## A-D Candidate Generation

For candidate generation, Qwen3.5-9B was used in bfloat16 MLX weights. In each generation call, one instruction of Fig. 9 was the system message, and the prompt text alone was sent as the user message. Since thinking was disabled, the chat template places an empty reasoning block at the start of each assistant turn. Sampling used temperature 0.7 and top-p 0.9, with a limit of 1,024 tokens. The seed of each response is derived from the SHA-256 digest of the number 42, the prompt identifier, and the instruction label joined by colons. However, on Apple silicon (Metal), the seeded generation is not bit-reproducible across runs. Thus, the candidate pool is defined by the stored responses instead of by a repeated generation. No stored response is empty or contains a reasoning-trace tag.

## Appendix C Rule Encoding, Consistency, and Coverage

In the Z3 encoding [31], the topic is a constant of an enumerated sort with the ten schema values, each of the nine Boolean attributes is a Boolean constant, and the condition of each rule in Table II is a formula over these symbols. The typed facts of one prompt-response pair are the constraints that fix all ten symbols to the attribute assignments of the pair. A rule applies when its condition is satisfiable together with these constraints. All eight rules are evaluated in this way, and the labeler records the first applicable rule in the order R1, R2, R3, R5, R6, R7, R8, and R9.

For the consistency check, the conditions of two rules with different verdicts are placed in a fresh solver, and an unsatisfiable result is required. All 25 such pairs give an unsatisfiable result. The three remaining pairs share a verdict and are not checked: R1 with R2 (unjustified refusal), R3 with R9 (justified refusal), and R6 with R7 (helpful compliance). The satisfiability check of each of the eight conditions alone finds a satisfying assignment. A further query also finds an assignment to which no rule applies. That assignment has an out-of-scope topic and a refusal, while every other Boolean attribute is false.

In the schema, there are 5,120 assignments: ten topics combined with the 512 combinations of the nine Boolean attributes. The number of those assignments on which each rule applies is listed in Table VII. R3 and R9 are the only rules that apply to the same assignment, and there are 96 such assignments. Thus, the justified-refusal region contains 1,540 assignments. The helpful-compliance, partial-compliance, unjustified-refusal, and harmful-compliance regions contain 352, 1,280, 704, and 768 assignments in that order.

There are 476 assignments without an applicable rule: 384 of the 512 out-of-scope assignments, 12 for each of the seven sensitive topics other than self-harm, and 8 for self-harm, with none for the benign topic. On a sensitive topic, when the prompt has neither framing nor an operational request and the response is either a refusal (8 assignments) or substantive (4 assignments), no rule other than R9 applies. Because R2 and R7 require framing and R3 and R5 require an operational request, none of these four rules can apply to such assignments. For self-harm, R9 applies to the four such refusals that provide resources. In addition, on all 5,120 assignments, an independent Boolean transcription of the eight rules, written without the solver, gives the same verdict and the same recorded rule identifier. All of the coverage counts in this section describe the encoded schema and do not estimate how often naturally occurring responses fall into the uncovered region.

## Appendix E Human-Reference Panel

There are two parts of the panel: an initial 50-item stratified set and a 150-item extension. The annotation forms give a source category for every item, with 107 harmful, 52 benign, and 41 over-refusal-prone items. In the panel metadata, the sub-category self-harm marks 22 harmful items and 19 over-refusal-prone items. Each of the six raters, rater1 to rater6, labeled the topic from a four-category taxonomy (benign, chemistry and biology, self-harm, and illegal activity) together with the Boolean prompt attributes, where knowledge framing follows the mapping of Section III-F. For each attribute, the reference is the unique plurality of the six votes, which leaves the tied items of Table XI unscored for that attribute. Rater agreement on each attribute is summarized by Fleiss’ kappa over the six raters.

For the parsed items with a unique reference, the language model assigns knowledge framing to 64 items and actionable steps to 73 items, compared with 29 and 30 reference positives, resulting in the false positives of Table XI and Section IV-D. Of the six attributes, knowledge framing also has the lowest rater agreement (Fleiss’ kappa 0.30). Although actionable steps and specific method have similar rater agreement (0.45 and 0.46), the match between the language model and the reference is much closer on specific method.

The topic assignments of the 187 parsed items with a unique topic reference are given in Table XII. Of the 40 mismatches, 30 use schema categories absent from the panel taxonomy (violence 12, hate and harassment 9, deception 7, and privacy 2). For 19 of the mismatches with those categories, the reference places the item under illegal activity. The other 10 mismatches remain within the panel taxonomy, including 4 self-harm items assigned chemistry and biology.

All eight rejected outputs come from the 22 harmful self-harm items. Instead of JSON, these outputs are prose, each beginning with a sentence that declines to provide self-harm content and continuing with encouragement to seek professional or crisis help. All eight stopped normally. Six of them stopped after 45 to 47 generated tokens, while the other two stopped after 120 and 165 tokens, where the output adds a list of crisis resources.

The panel is not held out from the prompt pool. Of its 200 prompts, 98 match a prompt of the candidate-generation split exactly, 22 match a prompt of the validation split, and 21 match a prompt of the test split. ## Appendix F Training and Evaluation Settings

The training settings of the five runs of the main evaluation and the ten runs of the judge comparison are listed in Table XIII. Apart from the run seed, the preference file is the only difference. In the main evaluation, the preference file holds 465 pairs, and in the judge comparison the preference file holds 443 pairs with prompt-shared labels or judge labels. Every run starts from the same pinned weights and trains with direct preference optimization [5] and low-rank adaptation [36]. The base model with the adapter disabled is used as the reference model.

When thinking is disabled, the generation prompt of the policy model ends with an empty thought block, the string <|channel>thought\n<channel|> with a line break in place of \n, while the unmodified chat template of the policy model renders a completed assistant turn without that block. The chat-template correction inserts the block into the completed assistant turn only, leaving the generation prompt and the completion tokens unchanged, and is recorded in the audit of each run under the name gemma4-empty-thought-prefix-v1. Before training, each run renders every pair with the tokenizer and with the trainer’s own preprocessing and collator. From these renderings, still before training, the run checks that the generation prompt is a prefix of every completed example and that every completion is retained in full. All 15 runs pass this check on every completion: 930 per run in the main evaluation and 886 per run in the judge comparison (both responses of each pair). In those runs, the check records no changed completion token, no changed generation prompt, and no prefix mismatch. The same check also records that the unmodified template would have produced a prefix mismatch on every completion.

In each evaluation, the adapter of one training run is merged into the base weights, and one response is generated per benchmark prompt. During generation, the chat template is applied with thinking disabled, and decoding is greedy with at most 200 new tokens, in batches of eight with left padding. A response with at least 200 generated tokens, excluding padding, is counted as reaching the 200-token limit. An evaluation fails if a fixed pattern check for reasoning tags and reasoning-trace phrases matches more than 0.5 percent of its responses. Across the 30 evaluations reported here, the check matches no response. In 164 of the 29,610 saved responses, a line that holds only the word thought (the name of the model’s reasoning channel) stands before the answer. For each of those responses, the line is either at the start of the response or after a fragment of at most nine characters, and no reasoning text precedes the answer.

The refusal detector, version refusal-v2.0, collapses each run of whitespace in a response to a single space and keeps the first 400 characters. When any pattern of Fig. 10 matches those characters, ignoring letter case, the detector counts the response as refused. Before every evaluation, a fixed set of 20 sentences with known outcomes is checked. The saved responses of all 30 evaluations are rescored with the same detector, and the rescoring reproduces every stored refusal flag.

## Appendix G Per-Seed Results

In the main evaluation, the number of responses reaching the 200-token limit is given for each seed in Table XIV, complementing the per-seed refusal rates reported in Section IV-E. For the judge comparison, the per-seed differences behind the paired intervals of Section IV-F are given in Table XV, along with the per-seed limit counts of both conditions. Each paired interval is the mean of the five differences (prompt-shared labels minus judge labels) plus or minus 2.776 times their sample standard deviation divided by the square root of five. Here, the multiplier 2.776 is the 97.5 percent quantile of Student’s t distribution with four degrees of freedom. The standard deviation of these differences is 0.34 percentage points on Hard-1K and 0.28 on Toxic.

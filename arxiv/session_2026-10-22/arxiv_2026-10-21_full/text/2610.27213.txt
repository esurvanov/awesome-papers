Abstract

Personalized large language models (LLMs) increasingly rely on profile-conditioned inference, pairing public requests with rich, user-specific facts.
This paradigm exposes the user profile itself, together with the user’s explicit sharing preferences.
We introduce Profile-DP, a policy-aware local differential privacy framework designed specifically for user-profile release in personalized LLM inference.
Profile-DP provably bounds the release of user-withheld facts while treating public instructions, profile schemas, and authorized facts as public side information.
By generating a privatized profile that composes with the public instruction prior to answer generation, our framework decouples formal privacy guarantees from downstream model behavior.
We develop two finite-domain mechanisms satisfying Profile-DP: DP-SLOT independently perturbs protected attributes, while DP-BLOCK uses public semantic decisions to coordinate their release and jointly sample related attributes.
A positive, public compatibility prior connects these attributes while preserving a profile-level privacy bound.
On PEEP, DP-BLOCK leads the utility composite among DP methods at all 13 structured budgets and is closest to the ideal privacy–utility point in 18 of 26 format–budget settings under our composite weighting.
Direct measurements reveal complementary strengths: DP-BLOCK discloses less, while DP-SLOT retains more authorized facts.
These results make policy-aware profile release a practical basis for studying privacy and personalization.

## 1 Introduction

LLM assistants increasingly remember who their users are.
They keep profile facts across sessions, add them to each new request, and often forward the composed prompt to a larger hosted model (Wang et al., 2025; Li et al., 2025b; Hartmann et al., 2024).
Users already share personal details with such systems (Mireshghallah et al., 2024a; Zhao et al., 2024), yet not every stored fact belongs in every request.
Consider a user who asks an assistant to rephrase a text describing the skills they gained in their projects.
Their stored profile lists their language (French), occupation (cultural project manager), work history (internships in European cultural projects), and degree (a master’s in cultural projects); they share the first two and withhold the rest.
Deleting the withheld fields is insufficient, because the shared occupation still points to the withheld work history, and LLMs readily infer such attributes from context (Staab et al., 2024a).
Rewriting the whole profile with a text-level privacy mechanism hides the withheld facts, but it also distorts the shared occupation and language that the answer depends on.

This example captures the profile-release problem: before a prompt leaves the user’s control, the system must keep the facts the user shares and protect the facts they withhold, including what related shared fields reveal about them.
Existing tools address only part of it.
PII filters and redaction target identifiers but not per-user sharing choices (Nissenbaum, 2009; Ghalebikesabi et al., 2025), and language models do not reliably follow such choices on their own (Mireshghallah et al., 2024b; Shao et al., 2024).
Differential privacy (DP) offers formal protection (Dwork et al., 2006), but DP text mechanisms define privacy over a token, span, prompt, or decoding distribution (Section 2), as does private in-context learning (Wu et al., 2023; Tang et al., 2023).
Most of them rewrite shared and withheld facts alike; DP-Fusion (Thareja et al., 2025) follows the sharing policy but protects token spans of free text instead of profile attributes.

We introduce Profile-DP, a local DP notion for the user profile.
It treats every shared fact as public and formally protects only the withheld values, releasing each one as a category from a fixed public list, such as an occupation group.
Deleting a withheld value is the zero-leakage extreme; Profile-DP instead lets a coarse version of it reach the answering model with bounded leakage.
Formally, the guarantee compares profiles that agree on the instruction, the profile schema, and every shared fact, and differ only in withheld values; the adversary sees the whole prompt and may know everything the user shared.
With a sharing template, prompt construction then operates on the privatized profile through post-processing.
The guarantee therefore holds for any answering model.

We instantiate Profile-DP with two mechanisms: DP-SLOT samples each withheld attribute independently, and DP-BLOCK adds public planning and joint sampling (Figure 1).
Policy closure also privatizes shared fields that would reveal a withheld one, such as the occupation above; this is an empirical safeguard beyond the formal guarantee.
Public grouping collects related withheld attributes, and joint sampling with a public compatibility prior favors plausible combinations.
Because these decisions never look at the withheld values, they consume no privacy budget, and the proof stays simple.

We evaluate on PEEP (Ramírez et al., 2025), which pairs real assistant requests with user profiles and synthetic per-attribute sharing preferences, against six DP text methods at 13 budget settings from 2 to 8192.
The central finding is that DP-BLOCK keeps what users share while bounding what they withhold.
Methods that rewrite the whole profile reach its disclosure only by discarding shared facts, keeping at most 18% of them where DP-BLOCK keeps 89%.
DP-BLOCK leads all DP methods in utility composite at all 13 structured budgets and is closest to the ideal privacy–utility point in 18 of 26 settings.
On freeform profiles, DP-Fusion has higher utility and is preferable up to a budget of 256, but its measured disclosure rises to 73%, whereas that of DP-BLOCK stays at most 35% at every budget.
In ablations of a task-aware variant, removing policy closure consistently increases disclosure.
The cost, relative to DP-SLOT, is lower retention (89% vs. 98%) and slightly weaker structured answer preference.

Our contributions are:

• 

We formalize policy-aware profile release and define Profile-DP, a guarantee over exactly the withheld values that holds for any answering model.

• 

We design DP-SLOT and DP-BLOCK, finite-domain mechanisms with a profile-level proof.

• 

We show on PEEP that DP-BLOCK keeps most shared information with measured disclosure of at most 35% at every budget, and identify policy closure as the most consistent disclosure control in ablations.

## 2 Related Work

Differentially private text sanitization. 
Selective differential privacy protects policy-designated text during language-model training (Shi et al., 2022), whereas local mechanisms sanitize inputs before downstream inference.
Metric-LDP mechanisms perturb word representations before vocabulary mapping (Feyisetan et al., 2020; Yue et al., 2021), and finite-domain exponential mechanisms support text replacement (Carvalho et al., 2023; Chen et al., 2023).
LLM-facing methods rewrite prompts (Utpala et al., 2023), add a second release stage to generative rewriting (Li et al., 2025a), substitute masked tokens (Meisenbacher et al., 2024), paraphrase (Mattern et al., 2022), or mix public and private decoding distributions (Majmudar et al., 2022); DP-Fusion coordinates privatized distributions over policy-identified spans (Thareja et al., 2025).
Their guarantees attach to tokens, spans, or generated text, whereas policy-labeled profiles require user authorization in the neighboring relation and a joint account of related attributes.

User-defined privacy preferences. 
Contextual integrity relates privacy to appropriate information flow (Nissenbaum, 2009).
LLM benchmarks study contextual disclosure norms (Mireshghallah et al., 2024b; Shao et al., 2024), information-sharing studies elicit explicit user preferences (Ghalebikesabi et al., 2025; Cheng et al., 2024), and preference-based personalization pairs requests with profile facts and sharing decisions (Ramírez et al., 2025).
PII redaction tools (Microsoft, 2025) and LLM-based anonymizers (Staab et al., 2024b) remove identifiers without formal guarantees, although LLMs can infer personal attributes from innocuous context (Staab et al., 2024a); local mediation can protect inputs before external inference (Zhang et al., 2024; Li et al., 2025b; Hartmann et al., 2024).
These works motivate preference following but do not bound what a profile release adds about withheld values.

Position of this work. 
Profile-DP connects user-defined sharing preferences to local differential privacy (Duchi et al., 2013): it treats authorized facts as public side information, protects withheld values through a profile-level neighboring relation, and coordinates related attributes through public decisions before joint sampling, in the spirit of joint exponential mechanisms (Gillenwater et al., 2022).
Our evaluation follows the same boundary, measuring protected disclosure alongside authorized retention and downstream utility.

## 3 Preliminaries and Problem Setting

Differential privacy. 
A randomized mechanism $\mathcal{M}$ is $\epsilon$ -differentially private if, for neighboring inputs $u\simeq u^{\prime}$ and every measurable event $S$ ,

 $\Pr[\mathcal{M}(u)\in S]\leq e^{\epsilon}\Pr[\mathcal{M}(u^{\prime})\in S].$ 

The exponential mechanism samples $y$ from a fixed support with probability proportional to $\exp(\epsilon s(u,y)/(2\Delta s))$ , where $\Delta s$ bounds the score change between neighbors (McSherry and Talwar, 2007).
Sequential releases compose additively, and post-processing preserves the guarantee.

Policy-aware profiles. 
A profile is $u=\{x_{i}=(e_{i},f_{i},v_{i},a_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}$ with $n$ attributes.
Each attribute has an entity $e_{i}$ , field $f_{i}$ , value $v_{i}$ , and release preference $a_{i}\in\{\text{shared},\text{withheld}\}$ .
Let $P=\{i:a_{i}=\text{withheld}\}$ be the withheld (protected) set; shared values are authorized for release.
The public instruction $q$ is the user’s task request.
We collect the public context as

 $c=\left(q,\{(e_{i},f_{i},a_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n},\{v_{i}:i\notin P\}\right).$ 

Two profiles satisfy $u\simeq_{c}u^{\prime}$ when they share $c$ ; their withheld values may differ arbitrarily.

Definition 1 (Profile-DP).

For each fixed public context $c$ , a local profile-release mechanism $\mathcal{M}$ satisfies $\epsilon$ -Profile-DP if, for every $u\simeq_{c}u^{\prime}$ and output event $S$ ,

 $\Pr[\mathcal{M}(u;c)\in S]\leq e^{\epsilon}\Pr[\mathcal{M}(u^{\prime};c)\in S].$ 

The adversary observes $c$ and the released profile, and may also observe downstream responses.
The guarantee bounds the additional evidence supplied by the release about withheld values, conditional on $c$ .
Information inferable from the instruction or shared facts is part of that conditioning.
The local mechanism accesses the original profile inside the user’s trusted boundary.

Local profile release. 
The mechanism produces a privatized profile $\tilde{u}$ .
A deterministic prompt template $R$ renders it either as a structured profile or as freeform text and combines it with the instruction:

 $p=\operatorname{Compose}(q,R(\tilde{u})),\quad o=F(p),$ 

where $F$ is the answering model and $o$ its response.
Appendix B gives the complete release algorithm.

## 4 Method

Both mechanisms build on a shared backbone of public candidate domains, bounded similarity scores, and uniform budget allocation (Figure 1).
DP-SLOT samples each withheld attribute independently, and DP-BLOCK extends it with public planning and joint sampling.

## 4.1 Shared Backbone

Public candidate domains. 
Finite public domains give neighboring profiles the same output support, and public taxonomies supply interpretable categories.
For field $f$ , we fix a candidate set $\mathcal{C}_{f}$ from public classification systems, such as UN geographic regions (United Nations Statistics Division, n.d.) and ISCO occupation groups (International Labour Organization, n.d.).
A withheld attribute is supported when its field has such a domain; unsupported withheld values and protected identifiers receive a fixed suppression symbol.
Appendix C lists the domains and their sources.

Bounded similarity scores. 
To retain useful meaning, we score each public candidate against the original value.
Let $E(t)$ be the unit-normalized embedding of text $t$ , and $\mathcal{T}_{f}(y)$ the official prototype texts for candidate $y$ , or its label when no prototype text is available.
We compute

 $\displaystyle\kappa_{f}(v,y)$ 
 $\displaystyle=\max_{t\in\mathcal{T}_{f}(y)}\langle E(v),E(t)\rangle,$ 

 $\displaystyle s_{f}(v,y)$ 
 $\displaystyle=\operatorname{clip}_{[0,1]}((1+\kappa_{f}(v,y))/2).$ 

Because $s_{f}\in[0,1]$ , its global sensitivity is at most one.
For example, a cultural project manager can receive a high score for the public category “Managers,” while remaining eligible for every other occupational category.
The original value is compared directly with candidate prototypes, preserving semantic distinctions within a shared public support.
Appendix D specifies the embedding model and special-case scores.

Uniform allocation. 
Let $I\subseteq P$ contain the supported withheld attributes.
A total budget $\epsilon$ is allocated uniformly as $\epsilon_{i}=\epsilon/|I|$ for $i\in I$ , so the composition bound is $\sum_{i\in I}\epsilon_{i}\leq\epsilon$ .

## 4.2 DP-SLOT: Independent Release

Independent release preserves shared facts while randomizing withheld values; each withheld attribute forms one slot.
For each supported slot $i\in I$ , DP-SLOT samples a public candidate with the exponential mechanism:

 $\Pr[\tilde{v}_{i}=y]=\frac{\exp(\epsilon_{i}s_{f_{i}}(v_{i},y)/2)}{\sum_{z\in\mathcal{C}_{f_{i}}}\exp(\epsilon_{i}s_{f_{i}}(v_{i},z)/2)}.$ 

The mechanism favors candidates close to the private value while leaving every public candidate possible, so an occupation is released as a broad occupational group, for example.
Shared values are copied, and unsupported withheld values are suppressed; if $I$ is empty, release consists entirely of these deterministic operations.
DP-SLOT thus separates authorization from randomization: shared values pass through unchanged, and every supported withheld attribute receives the same budget.

## 4.3 DP-BLOCK: Joint Release

Independent sampling treats each slot in isolation.
Two difficulties arise at the profile level: a shared field can reveal information about a withheld field, and independently sampled fields can describe an unlikely combination.
DP-BLOCK addresses them with public planning, namely policy closure and public grouping, followed by joint sampling with a public compatibility prior.
All three decisions use a small binary-decision model that returns yes/no probabilities with low latency (Laya; Appendices E and F); none of them sees a withheld value.
The decision model sees public field descriptions for closure and grouping, and public candidate values for compatibility.

Policy closure. 
A withheld work history can also be revealed by a shared occupation (Table 1).
Closure subjects such related sources to randomization or suppression before release.
For fields belonging to the same entity, the decision model scores whether revealing a source field would strongly constrain a withheld target, using the instruction, field identities, and preference labels.
Starting from $P$ , sources with scores at least $\tau_{C}$ join the privatization set, and the process continues to a fixed point $P^{+}$ .
Closure preserves every original withheld label and can additionally withhold a shared field that describes the same information.
The formal neighboring relation still varies only the original withheld values; original shared values remain fixed in $c$ .
Closure thus decides which related facts are released, while the formal guarantee still concerns only the user’s original withheld values.

Public grouping. 
Joint selection is useful when fields describe related aspects of a person.
For example, occupation and work history can carry overlapping professional information, motivating a shared release decision.
Let $I^{+}\subseteq P^{+}$ contain the supported attributes after closure, with uniform budgets $\epsilon_{i}=\epsilon/|I^{+}|$ .
A second decision scores the benefit of selecting two fields together, and edges with scores above $\tau_{G}$ form connected groups in $I^{+}$ ; oversized groups are split into small blocks (Appendix B).
Each block $b$ uses the full Cartesian support $\mathcal{C}_{b}=\prod_{i\in b}\mathcal{C}_{f_{i}}$ .
Grouping determines where dependence can be modeled; the compatibility prior determines how it affects sampling.

Joint sampling with a compatibility prior. 
Grouping becomes an active joint mechanism through a public compatibility score $J_{b}(y_{b})\in[0,1]$ , which averages the decision model’s plausibility scores for candidate pairs along retained edges.
For example, related occupation and work fields can favor compatible professional categories when sampled together.
The corresponding positive prior $\pi_{b}(y_{b})\propto\exp(\gamma J_{b}(y_{b}))$ serves as a public base measure for the exponential mechanism:

 $\displaystyle L_{b}(u,y_{b})$ 
 $\displaystyle=\gamma J_{b}(y_{b})+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i\in b}\epsilon_{i}s_{f_{i}}(v_{i},y_{i}),$ 

 $\displaystyle\Pr[\tilde{v}_{b}=y_{b}]$ 
 $\displaystyle=\frac{\exp L_{b}(u,y_{b})}{\sum_{z_{b}\in\mathcal{C}_{b}}\exp L_{b}(u,z_{b})}.$ 

The prior favors compatible tuples while every candidate tuple retains positive probability, including unusual combinations.
For constant $J_{b}$ , the distribution factorizes into independent DP-SLOT draws with the same effective privatization set and attribute budgets, so the compatibility prior supplies the joint dependence.
Private values enter solely through $s_{f_{i}}$ , preserving the bounded influence of each similarity score.
We use $\tau_{C}=0.55$ , $\tau_{G}=0.35$ , and $\gamma=2$ .

## 4.4 Privacy Guarantee and Reuse

Theorem 1 (Profile-release guarantee).

Fix public context $c$ , public supports, and a public planning rule.
If all private scores lie in $[0,1]$ and $\sum_{i}\epsilon_{i}\leq\epsilon$ , then DP-SLOT and DP-BLOCK with fresh internal randomness satisfy pure $\epsilon$ -Profile-DP.

For a block, changing withheld values alters each log weight by at most half the sum of their allocated budgets.
The normalizer contributes the same bound.
Composition across blocks gives the profile guarantee; Appendix A provides the full proof.

Prompt construction and answer generation are post-processing of the release and public context.
Reusing a single release across public requests retains its original bound, provided subsequent computation accesses that release and the fixed public information.
Generating $T$ fresh releases of the same withheld values instead incurs a bound of $\sum_{t=1}^{T}\epsilon^{(t)}$ .
Uniform allocation makes a request-independent DP-SLOT release reusable across tasks; the public planning of DP-BLOCK can depend on the request.

## 5 Experiments

## 5.1 Experimental Setup

Data and task. 
We evaluate a fixed random sample of 500 PEEP records, whose user requests and profiles derive from WildChat (Ramírez et al., 2025; Zhao et al., 2024), with PEEP’s synthetic sharing preferences.
An LLM preprocessing step extracts a task-only public instruction from each request, and the extracted values and preference labels form the profile (Section G.1).
Every method releases either a structured profile or a freeform rendering of the same attributes, and all methods receive the same records and instruction template.

Compared methods. 
We compare DP-SLOT and DP-BLOCK with DP-Prompt (Utpala et al., 2023), DP-Decoding (Majmudar et al., 2022), DP-MLM (Meisenbacher et al., 2024), and DP-Paraphrase (Mattern et al., 2022), and, on freeform profiles, with DP-GTR (Li et al., 2025a) and DP-Fusion (Thareja et al., 2025).
Structured baselines perturb individual values while preserving profile keys, and freeform baselines process the profile text.
DP-Fusion protects policy-identified withheld spans, whereas the other baselines rewrite shared and withheld text alike.
Controls include the original profile (Raw), the instruction alone (No profile), and rule- and NER-based PII redaction with Presidio (Microsoft, 2025); Section G.3 adds two further suppression controls and gives every release policy.

Budgets and models. 
We evaluate 13 settings of the total-budget parameter $\epsilon_{\mathrm{tot}}$ , doubling from $2$ to $8192$ .
For our methods, $\epsilon_{\mathrm{tot}}=\epsilon$ is the total profile budget divided among withheld attributes; baselines use the same grid with their native allocation and composition rules (Section G.4).
The lower range probes stronger randomization, and the upper range examines useful-release behavior under progressively weaker formal bounds.
Qwen3.6-27B-FP8 generates responses (Qwen Team, 2026a), and Qwen3.6-35B-A3B-FP8 judges disclosure and response quality (Qwen Team, 2026b), both with deterministic decoding (Appendix G).
Results use one profile-sampling seed; the interval analysis resamples evaluation records.

Metrics. 
Our primary direct diagnostics are Protected Profile Disclosure Rate (PPDR) and Raw-relative Utility Loss (RUL), both lower-is-better.
PPDR measures the fraction of withheld attributes revealed exactly or semantically in a released profile.
RUL compares the generated answer with an answer produced from the original profile, averaging judgments in both presentation orders; it ranges from $-1$ (method preferred) to $1$ (raw preferred).
Profile retention (PR) measures the fraction of shared values preserved in the released profile.
Protected-attribute recovery (PARR), answer disclosure (AD), answer retention (AR), and reference-conditioned perplexity (RCP), which measures support for the dataset reference answer, complete the evaluation (Appendices H and M).

To summarize the complementary objectives, Privacy Composite (PComp) averages normalized PPDR, PARR, and AD.
Utility Composite (UComp) weights the win-or-tie rate against Raw answers (PW) by $2/5$ and RCP, PR, and AR by $1/5$ each.
Both are higher-is-better; we report them jointly and identify, at each budget, the DP method closest to the ideal point $(\mathrm{PComp},\mathrm{UComp})=(1,1)$ .
Normalization uses one range per format over the fixed DP comparison set and all 13 budgets, so changes across budgets reflect each method’s own measurements; direct metrics retain their original scales.
Confidence intervals and sensitivity analyses appear in Appendices K and L.

## 5.2 Privacy–Utility Trade-off

The composite view tests whether a released profile retains useful information while reducing exposure.
In Figure 2, DP-BLOCK leads UComp among the evaluated DP methods at every structured budget and is closest to the ideal point at all 13 structured budgets, including the five budgets from $2$ to $32$ , where private similarity scores exert less influence on sampling.
For freeform profiles, DP-Fusion leads UComp throughout and is closest to the ideal point up to $\epsilon_{\mathrm{tot}}=256$ , with DP-BLOCK second at each low budget; DP-BLOCK is closest from $512$ onward.
These counts are unchanged when the recovery probe is removed from PComp (Appendix L).
The two methods differ in how disclosure grows with the budget: DP-Fusion’s PPDR rises from $0.09$ to $0.73$ and exceeds that of DP-BLOCK from $\epsilon_{\mathrm{tot}}=512$ (Figure 3), whereas DP-BLOCK stays between $0.12$ and $0.35$ at every budget.
DP-Fusion’s advantage is therefore concentrated at small budgets, while DP-BLOCK keeps disclosure stable across the full budget range.
These point estimates use the declared weights, counting ties as answer wins.

## 5.3 Privacy Diagnostics

The composite ranking combines two questions that we now examine directly: what each release discloses about withheld values, and what it preserves of shared ones.
Disclosure measures detected withheld facts; recovery probes a fixed attacker supplied with public context.
In Figure 3, DP-BLOCK has lower PPDR than DP-SLOT at every structured budget and most freeform budgets, and across all budgets its PPDR stays at most $0.29$ for structured and $0.35$ for freeform profiles.
Recovery remains substantial even when measured disclosure is low, reflecting the information available to the fixed attacker through public context (Figure 6).
Suppression controls yield lower disclosure alongside lower authorized retention (Table 6).

FINDINGS DP-BLOCK reduces measured profile disclosure relative to DP-SLOT, while stronger filtering achieves further reductions at the cost of retained information.

## 5.4 Utility Preservation

Lower disclosure matters only if shared information survives, so we separate answer quality from retention.
Figure 3 shows higher profile retention for DP-SLOT than for DP-BLOCK, whose closure can withhold related shared fields.
Figure 5 compares retention at matched disclosure: whenever a whole-profile rewriting method discloses no more than DP-BLOCK at the same budget, it keeps at most $18\%$ of shared values, whereas DP-BLOCK keeps $89\%$ .
DP-Fusion, which follows the sharing policy, matches both up to a budget of 256, consistent with its small-budget advantage in Figure 2.
Presidio redaction discloses less but keeps fewer shared values and offers no formal guarantee (Figure 5).

## 5.5 Component Analysis

Because DP-BLOCK differs from DP-SLOT only through public planning and joint sampling, we isolate policy closure, public grouping, the compatibility prior, and allocation.
We fix a 100-record subset as the ablation set and evaluate every variant in Table 2 on it.
Task allocates the budget by each withheld field’s scored relevance to the instruction and serves only as the ablation reference; the evaluated DP-BLOCK uses uniform allocation.
Figure 4 plots the key ablations against Task.
Task has lower PPDR than No-Closure in both formats at every plotted budget; this intervention includes changes to the privatization set and budget allocation.
Averaged over budgets, removing closure raises PPDR from $0.153$ to $0.204$ for structured profiles and from $0.202$ to $0.258$ for freeform profiles.
Fixed-Group and No-Prior trace different disclosure–utility operating points (Figure 7), and uniform Block remains competitive with Task.
Appendix J gives the complete ablation, including a matched-randomness prior diagnostic and full-cohort allocation comparisons.

## 6 Conclusion

Profile-DP makes user-controlled profile release an explicit privacy boundary in personalized LLM inference.
DP-SLOT and DP-BLOCK instantiate this boundary with independent and jointly sampled exponential mechanisms, whose public planning retains a transparent profile-level proof.
On PEEP, DP-BLOCK leads the utility composite among DP methods on structured profiles and is closest to the ideal privacy–utility point in 18 of 26 settings under the declared weights.
It keeps 89% of shared values, versus at most 18% for whole-profile rewriting at matched disclosure, while its disclosure stays at most 35% at every budget; ablations identify policy closure as its most consistent disclosure control.
The trade-offs remain explicit: DP-SLOT retains more shared facts, and DP-Fusion is preferable for freeform profiles at small budgets.
The formulation and evidence provide a foundation for policy-aware personalization with explicit release guarantees.

## 7 Limitations

Candidate coverage and semantic proximity. 
Release quality depends on the coverage and granularity of the public domains.
Small categorical supports can leave substantial semantic information even after randomization.
Profile-DP bounds only the evidence a release adds to the public context.
Policy closure privatizes shared fields that strongly constrain a withheld one, but weaker associations in shared facts and the instruction can still support inference of withheld attributes.

Evaluation scope. 
The experiments cover one policy-aware benchmark with a fixed local answer model and judge.
Answer judgments measure task quality, and retention measures the availability of shared information; correct use of personalized facts requires a separate, profile-informed assessment.
The composite weights encode one operational preference among these measurements.
Uniform allocation was chosen after inspecting the component results, so the comparisons describe operating points observed on this benchmark.

Privacy calibration and repeated use. 
Large total budgets give weak formal bounds, and their privacy is assessed through empirical disclosure measurements.
Baselines keep their native adjacency and calibration, so the same $\epsilon_{\mathrm{tot}}$ does not imply equal formal guarantees across methods.
Fresh releases accumulate privacy loss, and because public planning can depend on the request, a DP-BLOCK release is not automatically reusable across tasks.
Results use one profile-sampling seed, so seed-to-seed variation of the mechanisms is not quantified.

## Appendix A Profile-DP Proof and Composition

Fixed public decisions. 
Fix $c$ and $u\simeq_{c}u^{\prime}$ .
The closure, privatization set, public domains, partition, and budgets are identical for both inputs.
Compatibility scores are also identical because their inputs are public candidate tuples.
Any originally shared attributes promoted by closure have equal original values under the neighboring relation.

Block likelihood ratio. 
Let $A_{b}=\{i\in b:v_{i}\neq v_{i}^{\prime}\}$ and $h_{b}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i\in A_{b}}\epsilon_{i}$ .
The bounded score gives

 $|L_{b}(u,y_{b})-L_{b}(u^{\prime},y_{b})|\leq h_{b}.$ 

Writing $Z_{b}(u)=\sum_{z_{b}\in\mathcal{C}_{b}}\exp L_{b}(u,z_{b})$ , the same pointwise bound implies

 $e^{-h_{b}}Z_{b}(u^{\prime})\leq Z_{b}(u)\leq e^{h_{b}}Z_{b}(u^{\prime}).$ 

Every tuple has positive probability, and hence

 $\displaystyle\frac{\Pr[\tilde{v}_{b}=y_{b}\mid u,c]}{\Pr[\tilde{v}_{b}=y_{b}\mid u^{\prime},c]}$ 
 $\displaystyle=e^{L_{b}(u,y_{b})-L_{b}(u^{\prime},y_{b})}\frac{Z_{b}(u^{\prime})}{Z_{b}(u)}$ 

 $\displaystyle\leq e^{2h_{b}}.$ 

Independent block draws yield a full-profile ratio bounded by

 $\exp\!\left(\sum_{b}\sum_{i\in A_{b}}\epsilon_{i}\right)\leq e^{\epsilon}.$ 

Summing point probabilities proves the event-level guarantee.
Fixed suppression and copied shared values have identical distributions across neighbors.
Singleton blocks with constant $J_{b}$ give the DP-SLOT proof.
This establishes Theorem 1.

Granularity. 
Replacing one withheld coordinate $i$ gives a bound $\epsilon_{i}$ even when other attributes in its block are jointly sampled.
Replacing a subset $A$ gives $\sum_{i\in A}\epsilon_{i}$ .
Arbitrary replacement of all withheld values is bounded by $\epsilon$ .
We use profile-level sequential composition because a neighboring profile can change every withheld coordinate.

Prompt construction and multiple requests. 
For any function or randomized kernel that receives the release and fixed public context, integration over the release preserves the same likelihood-ratio bound.
This covers the deterministic prompt template and model responses.
For adaptive fresh releases, each conditional release must satisfy its declared bound for every previous transcript; sequential composition then gives $\sum_{t}\epsilon^{(t)}$ .
For a cached release, subsequent responses use the same privatized information and retain its initial bound.

## G.3 Release Policies

Structured generic text mechanisms process each profile value and retain entity/field keys.
Their freeform counterparts operate on serialized profile text.
DP-GTR and DP-Fusion use document-form inputs.
DP-Fusion assigns spans matching withheld values to private groups; the remaining text forms its public context.
Its private spans are grouped into demographic, person-related, and other withheld information using the annotated field and entity types.

The controls expose different degrees of profile information.

The structured Presidio control suppresses withheld values and applies identifier filtering to shared values.
Its freeform counterpart combines identifier filtering with withheld-value removal.
The privacy-filter control applies token classification and removes the original profile values, yielding a stronger suppression control.

## G.4 Total Budgets and Composition

## Appendix H Metric Definitions

Metrics are first computed per record and then averaged across eligible records.
Profile and answer disclosure use 277 records with eligible withheld attributes, recovery uses 250 records with valid candidate sets, and retention of shared values uses 451 records.
Answer utility and perplexity use the full evaluation cohort.
Each method uses the same eligible records within a metric.

Protected Profile Disclosure Rate (PPDR). 
For withheld slot $i$ , let $\chi^{\mathrm{ex}}_{i}$ indicate exact disclosure and $\chi^{\mathrm{sem}}_{i}$ semantic disclosure.
PPDR is the within-record mean of $\chi^{\mathrm{ex}}_{i}\lor\chi^{\mathrm{sem}}_{i}$ over withheld slots.
Exact matching is case-insensitive with word boundaries, excludes values shorter than two characters, and excludes values also explicitly shared elsewhere in the profile.
The semantic judge receives the released profile and eligible withheld facts, and identifies paraphrases or equivalent disclosures.
All eligible facts are submitted in the semantic pass; the prompt asks it to leave verbatim occurrences to exact matching.
The two detectors are combined at the entity–field level.
The Raw control yields PPDR $0.960$ and $0.958$ in structured and freeform form, respectively.
These rates calibrate the detector’s coverage of the original profile.

Protected Attribute Recovery Rate (PARR). 
Following recovery-based evaluation (Thareja et al., 2025), each withheld target has five choices: its true value and four distinct same-field distractors sampled from other records.
The attacker observes the instruction, shared facts, and released profile.
For each candidate, it constructs a hypothesis containing shared facts and the candidate target, with other withheld fields marked unknown.
It embeds this hypothesis and the observed released context, then selects the candidate of greatest cosine similarity.
A $k$ -way tie gives the true candidate credit $1/k$ .
Scores are averaged over valid targets within each record.
Uniform guessing has expected accuracy $0.2$ .
Appendix L reports sensitivity to including PARR in the privacy composite.

Disclosure and retention. 
Answer Disclosure (AD) applies the exact matcher for withheld values to the generated answer.
Profile Retention (PR) and Answer Retention (AR) measure exact occurrence of shared values in the released profile and answer.
Their denominators follow the original policy, including shared fields later privatized by closure.
Raw PR is $0.982$ and $0.983$ in the two formats, calibrating exact detection.

Raw-relative Utility Loss (RUL). 
For each instruction, the judge compares the method answer with the Raw answer in both A/B presentation orders.
Each comparison gives advantage $d\in\{-1,0,1\}$ to the method for loss, tie, or win.
We report $\mathrm{RUL}=-(d_{AB}+d_{BA})/2$ and average across records.
Identical answers receive zero.
We additionally record $\mathrm{PW}$ , the fraction of comparisons in which the method wins or ties, and $\mathrm{PL}$ , the fraction it loses.
Hence $\mathrm{PW}+\mathrm{PL}=1$ .
RUL measures signed preference; PL measures loss frequency.
The Raw answer is the shared reference.

Reference-Conditioned Perplexity (RCP). 
Given prompt $p$ and reference $y$ , we compute

 $\displaystyle\ell_{t}$ 
 $\displaystyle=-\log P_{\mathrm{GPT2}}(y_{t}\mid p,y_{<t}),$ 

 $\displaystyle\mathrm{RCP}(p,y)$ 
 $\displaystyle=\exp\left(\frac{\sum_{t\in\Omega}\ell_{t}}{|\Omega|}\right).$ 

 $\Omega$ contains the reference-token positions scored within the 1,024-token context.
Prompt tokens are excluded from the loss.
The procedure retains the reference and a fitting suffix of the prompt; references reaching the context limit are truncated to 1,023 tokens and scored with an empty prompt.
This reference-only case occurs in 68 records; the remaining records retain a prompt suffix according to available context.
We report the arithmetic mean of per-record perplexities.

Composite construction. 
For metric $x$ and a fixed format, let $l_{x}$ and $h_{x}$ be its extrema across the fixed set of DP methods and all 13 budgets.
Define

 $\displaystyle N_{\uparrow}(x)$ 
 $\displaystyle=\operatorname{clip}_{[0,1]}((x-l_{x})/(h_{x}-l_{x})),$ 

 $\displaystyle N_{\downarrow}(x)$ 
 $\displaystyle=1-N_{\uparrow}(x).$ 

A constant metric receives normalized score $0.5$ .
Controls use these extrema and are excluded from fitting them.
The composite definitions are

 $\displaystyle\mathrm{PComp}$ 
 $\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{3}\big[N_{\downarrow}(\mathrm{PPDR})+N_{\downarrow}(\mathrm{PARR})$ 

 $\displaystyle\hskip 42.67912pt+N_{\downarrow}(\mathrm{AD})\big],$ 

 $\displaystyle\mathrm{UComp}$ 
 $\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{5}\big[2N_{\uparrow}(\mathrm{PW})+N_{\downarrow}(\mathrm{RCP})$ 

 $\displaystyle\hskip 42.67912pt+N_{\uparrow}(\mathrm{PR})+N_{\uparrow}(\mathrm{AR})\big],$ 

 $\displaystyle D$ 
 $\displaystyle=\sqrt{(1-\mathrm{PComp})^{2}+(1-\mathrm{UComp})^{2}}.$ 

 $D$ is the distance to the ideal point; at each budget, the DP method with the smallest $D$ is closest to it.
The $2/5$ answer-quality weight is equivalent to separately including normalized PW and PL because they are complements.
The remaining weights capture reference support and retention of shared values.
These are group-relative summaries over a fixed comparison set; because the extrema are shared across budgets, changes along the budget axis reflect each method’s own measurements, and budget-independent controls keep constant composites.

Interpreting profile utility. 
The utility measurements track complementary parts of profile-conditioned inference.
The Raw answer supplies a response generated with the original profile, so the pairwise comparison measures preservation of that reference response’s task quality.
PR and AR track the shared information available in the profile and expressed in the answer; RCP measures compatibility with the dataset reference.

Composite scores.

## VISH-ASR: Viterbi-guided Selection of Hotwords for Speech-LLM Contextual ASR

Xiaoyu Gu

Yu Xi

Chencheng Wang

Zonghan Zhou

Haoyu Li

Jing Peng

Kai Yu\sthanksKai Yu is the corresponding author

Abstract

Prompt-based contextual ASR requires selecting a compact hotword subset
from large dynamic inventories, yet existing pre-selection methods rely either on discrete ASR hypotheses that may discard acoustic evidence or on
separately trained retrieval models. We propose
Viterbi-guided Selection of Hotwords (VISH), a training-free,
plug-and-play framework that directly verifies candidate-specific
acoustic support over frame-level CTC posteriors. VISH formulates
pre-selection as constrained posterior search; M-VISH makes large-list
verification scalable through frame-synchronous shared-prefix Viterbi
search with bounded top- $K$ pruning, while Bi-MVISH exploits
complementary backward evidence for robust verification. Across five benchmarks, M-VISH achieves up to 11.7% absolute improvement in Best-F1 over a strong learned-retrieval baseline and reduces candidate-search latency by 75.2% at 2,000 candidates relative to hypothesis-based filtering. These results demonstrate the effectiveness of posterior-level verification for accurate and scalable hotword pre-selection without additional model training.

## 1 Introduction

End-to-end automatic speech recognition (ASR) performs well on
general-domain corpora [18, 13], yet remains
vulnerable to hotwords: semantically critical long-tail terms, such as
contact names, locations, and products, that are sparsely represented in
training data. These user- and task-specific terms are better supplied as
external lexical context [24, 9, 5].
Contextual ASR has used weighted finite-state transducers
(WFSTs) [3] and neural context
injection [21, 12, 27, 32]. Speech large language models
(Speech LLMs) offer a new paradigm [25, 2, 20].

Speech-LLM contextualization follows two paradigms. Architecture
modification or extensive fine-tuning learns hotword-aware
representations [7, 23, 14, 16], but is computationally costly, inflexible for
dynamic contexts, and difficult to generalize without retraining.
Alternatively, prompt-based systems inject hotwords directly into the
input without adapting the Speech LLM [31, 4, 8], preserving model generality and enabling inexpensive
per-request personalization. However, large candidate lists weaken prompt
conditioning, increase interference, and cause bias-driven substitutions in
both hotwords and background words [8]. Most systems
therefore pre-select a compact candidate subset that retains acoustically
supported hotwords while rejecting irrelevant
alternatives [31].

Existing pre-selection methods differ mainly in how candidate evidence is
obtained. Hypothesis-based filtering relies on discrete recognition
outputs and candidate matching [6, 30, 2]. For example, CTC-Assistant [30]
first decodes a CTC hypothesis and then filters hotwords through lexical
indexing and edit-distance matching; relevant acoustic evidence may
therefore be lost when the candidate is absent from the discrete
hypothesis. Learned speech–text retrievers, including BR-ASR, GLCLAP,
and CLAR, require separately trained retrieval
models [8, 15, 11].
Search-graph approaches based on WFST composition or lattice
rescoring [10, 28] often require
decoder-side graphs, lattices, or task-specific search structures, making
them less convenient for black-box contextual ASR. CTC-based keyword
spotting has also explored posterior-level keyword-constrained alignment
for detection [29]; however, its objective is keyword
detection rather than ranking and selecting a compact subset from a large,
dynamically changing inventory for downstream contextual prompting.

We propose Viterbi-guided Selection of Hotwords (VISH), which formulates
large-list hotword pre-selection as candidate-specific constrained search
directly over frame-level CTC posterior trajectories. Unlike
hypothesis-based filtering, VISH does not require a candidate to appear in
a discrete first-pass transcript, and it requires no additional
retrieval-model training. To make this formulation scalable,
Multi-Word VISH (M-VISH) introduces a frame-synchronous shared-prefix
search that reuses constrained-search computation among candidates with
common token prefixes and bounds the active search space through
top- $K$ pruning. Bidirectional M-VISH (Bi-MVISH) further exploits
complementary backward acoustic evidence to improve verification robustness.

The contributions of this work are threefold:

1. 

We formulate large-list hotword pre-selection as
candidate-specific constrained search over frame-level CTC
posteriors and propose VISH, enabling direct acoustic
verification without relying on discrete ASR hypotheses
or additional retrieval-model training.

2. 

We introduce M-VISH, a frame-synchronous shared-prefix
multi-candidate search with bounded top- $K$ pruning, making
acoustic verification scalable to large dynamic inventories.
Bi-MVISH further exploits complementary backward evidence
for more robust verification.

3. 

Experiments across multiple datasets and ASR systems
demonstrate that improved pre-selection translates into
lower downstream biased error while preserving overall
recognition accuracy, with both native and external CTC
posteriors supporting flexible integration.

## 2 Methodology

## 2.1 A plug-and-play VISH framework

Given speech and a candidate list, VISH estimates candidate-specific acoustic support from frame-level CTC posteriors obtained from an accessible internal encoder or a frozen external CTC scorer (Figure 1). Here, “training-free” means no additional task-specific or retrieval-model training or fine-tuning: an off-the-shelf pretrained CTC scorer is used frozen for verification. An external scorer adds acoustic-encoding cost when internal posteriors are unavailable. VISH returns a compact prompt subset without downstream-model modification or decoder access. Verification relies on reliable candidate-token posterior evidence; unusual pronunciations or spelling variants may weaken the score.

## 2.2 Multi-word Viterbi-guided selection of hotwords

Let $p_{t}(k)=P(\pi_{t}=k\mid\mathbf{x})$ denote the frame-level CTC posterior for $k\in\Sigma\cup\{\varnothing\}$ , where $\Sigma$ is the CTC token vocabulary and $\varnothing$ is the blank symbol. Peak Suppression Decoding (PSD) removes frames satisfying $p_{t}(\varnothing)>\tau_{b}$ , yielding the ordered retained sequence $\widetilde{\mathbf{p}}=(\widetilde{p}_{1},\ldots,\widetilde{p}_{R})$ , where $R\leq T$ . Given a candidate $w$ , let $\mathbf{y}=(y_{1},\ldots,y_{L})$ denote its CTC-token sequence. VISH searches for its best constrained CTC alignment over any local interval:

 $m_{w}=\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq a\leq b\leq R\\
\boldsymbol{\pi}_{a:b}:B(\boldsymbol{\pi}_{a:b})=\mathbf{y}\end{subarray}}\sum_{r=a}^{b}\log\widetilde{p}_{r}(\pi_{r}),\qquad s_{w}=\frac{m_{w}}{N_{w}},$ 

(1)

where $N_{w}$ is the number of non-blank frames in the maximizing path. Normalizing the accumulated path score by its non-blank support reduces the duration bias of unnormalized path sums. No explicit duration bound is imposed; the constrained CTC states and accumulated posterior score determine the optimal local interval. For the blank-interleaved target $\mathbf{y}^{\prime}=(\varnothing,y_{1},\varnothing,\ldots,y_{L},\varnothing)$ , the active states follow

 $\alpha_{r}(s)=\log\widetilde{p}_{r}(y^{\prime}_{s})+\max_{q\in\mathcal{P}(s)}\alpha_{r-1}(q),$ 

(2)

where $\mathcal{P}(s)$ denotes valid CTC predecessor states for stay, step, and skip transitions. Root states are reintroduced after each retained frame, allowing local matches to start throughout the utterance.

Independent candidate-wise scoring repeats these state updates for candidates sharing common token prefixes. M-VISH avoids this redundancy by organizing the candidate list $W=\{w^{(1)},\ldots,w^{(N)}\}$ into a shared-prefix trie, where each edge corresponds to a CTC token. Each active node $u$ maintains a prefix score vector $\alpha_{r}^{u}$ and a companion accumulator $\gamma_{r}^{u}$ that follows the maximizing path and counts its non-blank aligned frames. The prefix-level confidence is

 $\rho_{r}(u)=\max_{s}\alpha_{r}^{u}(s).$ 

(3)

At each retained frame, only the top- $K_{\mathrm{p}}$ prefixes ranked by $\rho_{r}(u)$ are retained and expanded. When a terminal node is reached, its best confidence and aligned length update $m_{w}$ and $N_{w}$ for the corresponding candidate. Algorithm 1 summarizes the search.

A dummy root stabilizes local-match initialization, while PSD suppresses unnecessary propagation through blank-dominated frames. After PSD, independent scoring costs $O(RN\bar{L})$ , whereas M-VISH costs $O(RK_{\mathrm{p}}\bar{L})$ , with $K_{\mathrm{p}}\ll N$ ; in the worst case, $R=T$ .

## 2.3 Bidirectional M-VISH

Bi-MVISH supplements forward search when candidate-onset evidence is weak. It reverses both the retained posterior sequence and each candidate CTC-token sequence, constructs the reversed trie, and applies the same M-VISH search. Because CTC collapse commutes with sequence reversal, reversing both sequences preserves valid alignments and transition rules. Mapping the terminal score back to the original candidate gives $s_{w,\mathrm{bwd}}$ , which is averaged with the forward score $s_{w,\mathrm{fwd}}$ :

 $s_{w,\mathrm{bi}}=\frac{1}{2}\left(s_{w,\mathrm{fwd}}+s_{w,\mathrm{bwd}}\right).$ 

(4)

M-VISH remains frame-synchronous, whereas Bi-MVISH uses future frames within each segment and therefore trades strict frame-level streaming for stronger segment-level verification.

## 3 Experimental Setups

## 3.1 Datasets and bias lists

Test datasets. We use the following test sets:

• 

STOP1/STOP2 [26, 15]: English named-entity retrieval sets derived from the STOP test and evaluation splits, containing queries with person names (STOP1) or location names (STOP2). Both are used only for hotword pre-selection evaluation.

• 

AISHELL-1-NE [24]: A Mandarin named-entity contextual-biasing set derived from AISHELL-1. It is used for both hotword pre-selection and downstream contextual ASR evaluation.

• 

Libri-clean/other-2k [19]: LibriSpeech test-clean and
test-other with fixed 2,000-word candidate lists per
utterance, used for both pre-selection and downstream ASR evaluation; test-other is the more challenging condition.

Bias lists. STOP1, STOP2, and AISHELL-1-NE follow their
benchmark protocols. For LibriSpeech, following [17],
rare words exclude the 5,000 most frequent training words. Each utterance
uses a fixed 2,000-word list containing all ground-truth rare words plus
randomly sampled distractors; the same seeded lists are shared across
methods. For latency analysis, we additionally evaluate list sizes of
100, 500, 1,000, and 2,000.

## 3.2 Systems and inference setup

We evaluate Whisper-medium [22] (encoder–decoder ASR) and Prompt-SLAM-ASR [30] (Speech-LLM contextual ASR). The latter’s released model does not support Mandarin; we evaluate it only on LibriSpeech. Frozen SenseVoice-Small [1] supplies VISH’s frame-level CTC posteriors and tokenizer for both backbones. We fix $K_{\mathrm{p}}=40$ and $\tau_{b}=0.997$ across all datasets and evaluation settings. GLCLAP, M-VISH, and Bi-MVISH share fixed Top-10 selection and the prompt: “Transcribe speech to text. Some hotwords might help. The hotwords are {}”. BR-ASR results are published; the backbone-only setting omits hotwords. For all methods, Table 4 measures candidate-search latency from available speech representations/CTC posteriors to candidate scores/retrieval results, excluding acoustic representation extraction.

## 3.3 Evaluation metrics

Best-F1 is the maximum F1 over candidate-score thresholds, measuring target–distractor score separability independently of a deployment selection budget. The fixed Top-10 downstream comparisons instead assess ASR utility under a shared prompt budget. We report character error rate (CER) and biased character error rate (B-CER) on AISHELL-1-NE, and word error rate (WER) and biased word error rate (B-WER) on LibriSpeech [17]. Biased metrics are computed over reference characters or words in the biasing vocabulary. Lower error rates are better.

## 4.3 Plug-and-play Adaptability

To further validate the plug-and-play property of VISH, we
evaluate Bi-MVISH using either the native CTC posteriors of
Prompt-SLAM-ASR or a frozen external SenseVoice-Small encoder,
without additional training or downstream-model modification.
As shown in Table 3, both sources enable
effective hotword verification. Native posteriors require no
additional encoder or acoustic forward pass, whereas the external
scorer achieves higher Best-F1 and lower B-WER. These results
demonstrate the flexibility of VISH across CTC posterior sources,
with a trade-off between verification accuracy and computational cost.

## 4.4 Analysis of bidirectional verification

Forward Viterbi verification can underestimate a hotword when its onset is acoustically weak or ambiguous. As shown in Figure 2, both directions detect “liddy”, whereas forward search provides weak evidence for “smolny” and backward search anchors on its clearer ending. Averaging the complementary directional scores can therefore compensate for weak forward evidence, providing a qualitative explanation for the additional Bi-MVISH gains in Tables 1 and 2.

## 4.5 Candidate-search efficiency

Table 4 compares the candidate-search
latency of VISH with hypothesis-based filtering and learned
speech–text retrieval methods. All methods are evaluated with
batch size 1 on the same GPU, excluding acoustic representation
extraction. With 2,000 candidate hotwords, M-VISH reduces
search latency from 817 ms for CTC-Assistant to 203 ms,
a relative reduction of 75.2%. Bi-MVISH achieves a 51.0%
relative reduction despite performing bidirectional search.
Although GLCLAP achieves the lowest search latency (78 ms),
it yields lower Best-F1 than VISH (Table 1).
These results demonstrate that VISH achieves a favorable balance
between hotword pre-selection accuracy and search efficiency
for large candidate inventories.

## 5 Conclusion

We presented VISH, a training-free hotword pre-selector for
candidate-specific verification over CTC posteriors, together with
M-VISH for scalable shared-prefix search and Bi-MVISH for
bidirectional verification. Across five benchmarks, M-VISH achieves absolute Best-F1 improvements of 0.9%–11.7% over our reproduced
GLCLAP baseline, while Bi-MVISH
consistently reduces downstream biased error without degrading overall
ASR accuracy. At 2,000 candidates, M-VISH reduces candidate-search
latency from 817 to 203 ms relative to CTC-Assistant. Native CTC
posteriors provide a lower-overhead option when available, while an
external frozen scorer offers stronger plug-and-play verification.

This study uses existing speech datasets, including STOP,
AISHELL-1-NE, and LibriSpeech. No new human participants were recruited or recorded, and no animal experiments were conducted. All datasets were used in accordance

Abstract

Public charging of electric vehicles (EVs) adds a large and synchronized load to the distribution feeders. In most networks, it is rare for charging demand, weather, and local grid conditions to overload a feeder at the same time. When it does happen, the consequences can be severe: an overloaded feeder stresses its transformers and can trigger cascading outages across a city. Planners who design for high reliability therefore need to estimate very small outage probabilities. To that end, plain Monte Carlo estimation with an expensive grid simulator can require a prohibitively large number of runs for this task. To reduce the number of runs, we propose a control variate (CV) built from a learned classifier of the outage, trained on cheap observable features such as temperature and load forecasts. We show that for any binary learner, the variance reduction is an explicit function of its recall and false-positive rate. Furthermore, we show that control variate based on a Gaussian proxy of the load loses its effect as outages become rarer, while a learner with non-vanishing precision keeps removing a fixed fraction of the variance even in the extreme rare-event regime. We verify these results in numerical experiments using a feeder-overload simulator across three orders of magnitude of outage probability and obtain that even weak learner with a single feature can remove a quarter of the variance, while a boosted-tree learner is able to remove about 60%. We then apply the estimator to the feeder network of Surabaya, Indonesia, in which the Gaussian load model leaves every feeder three to seven times the one-day-in-ten-years blackout target. At the same simulation budget, planning the reserve with the learned CV and a 95% confidence limit reduces the city-wide blackout days by a third compared with Monte Carlo point estimates. These results show that learned control variates can help planners design distribution grids that stay reliable against rare outages.

## I Introduction

Electric vehicle (EV) charging is becoming one of the largest new loads on distribution networks. Uncoordinated charging raises the peak demand and could push feeders and transformers beyond their ratings on the few days when charging coincides with a high ambient temperature, a demand forecast miss, or a storm [1, 2, 3, 4]. Hosting-capacity studies of distribution networks with EV charging stations show that the binding constraint is the loading on a small number of such extreme days [5]. In cities where the grid already operates with little reserve, one such day could result in a local outage, in which public charging stations are among the first loads to be shed or to lose supply [6, 7].

Traditionally, the planning literature on charging stations treats an outage as a service failure, in which vehicles arrive, the station is out of service, and revenue is lost [8]. The consequences of a feeder overload, however, go beyond the station. An overloaded feeder that trips its protection moves its load onto the neighbouring feeders, while transformers that run hot age faster. A sequence of such local events is the mechanism through which large blackouts begin [9, 10]. For example, the August 2003 blackout in North America started from a small number of overloaded lines and cost several billion US dollars [11]. Hence, for a candidate charging station, the grid operator needs to know the probability that its charging load, together with the other loads on the feeder, causes an overload on a given day. Reliability planning works with such probabilities at levels of $10^{-3}$ to $10^{-6}$ per period. The classical loss-of-load criterion of one day in ten years, for instance, corresponds to $2.7\times 10^{-4}$ per day [12].

Estimating such a small probability is a rare-event simulation problem. A feeder model with thermal limits, protection logic and stochastic EV arrivals is expensive to run. The Monte Carlo (MC) estimate using $n$ samples with a target probability $\theta$ has relative standard error $\sqrt{(1-\theta)/(n\theta)}$ , which means a $10\%$ relative error at $95\%$ confidence requires about $400/\theta$ runs, i.e., four million runs at $\theta=10^{-4}$ . Earlier work on robust charging-station design [8] is able to reduce this cost with a control variate (CV) built from a Gaussian proxy of the electricity load. This construction, however, assumes that the modeler already knows a Gaussian variable that is strongly correlated with the outage. In practice however, it is often impractical to obtain such proxy. Furthermore, as we show in this paper, such a proxy loses its effect as outages become rarer.

To that end, we propose in this paper to replace the assumed proxy by a learned one. Cheap features that are observable before the expensive simulation is run, such as the temperature anomaly, the day-ahead load forecast and a storm flag, are often available. A classifier trained on a modest number of simulator runs can then predict, though imperfectly, whether the simulator will report an overload. We show that such a classifier only needs to be a weak learner [13], whose value as a control variate is determined by its recall and precision only. We also show that, with a suitable training design, the learner keeps its effect at every rarity level.

In sum, our main contributions are as follows. First, we provide an exact variance formula for the CV estimator built from any binary learner, together with the rare-event limit $1-\mathrm{recall}\times\mathrm{precision}$ of its variance ratio (Theorem 1, Corollary 1). Second, we derive a ceiling on the variance reduction of any learner, which is set by the features, and a proof that a Gaussian proxy loses its effect as the outage becomes rarer (Theorem 2, Proposition 1). Third, we show the exact variance of the estimator with an estimated coefficient and an unlabelled pool, the cost-optimal split between simulator runs and feature draws (Theorem 3), and a training design that keeps the learner informative in the tail (Proposition 2). Finally, we demonstrate using a simulation study that compares plain MC, the Gaussian proxy, a weak learner, a strong learner and the Bayes-posterior ceiling at outage probabilities from $10^{-3}$ to $10^{-6}$ based on a case study on the Surabaya network [8], which quantifies how the load model and the precision of the estimate change the reserve on each feeder and the blackout days the city would experience.

## II Related Work

## II-A EV Charging Load and Grid Outages

The effect of EV charging on distribution networks has been well documented. Uncoordinated residential charging raises the evening peak and the loading of distribution transformers [1, 2]. Furthermore, the choice of where and when vehicles charge could change the grid impact of a given fleet by a large factor [3]. Reviews of the distribution-level impacts list voltage drops, transformer ageing, losses and protection trips as the main consequences of EV charging, with demand management, storage and network reinforcement as the main remedies [4, 7]. Hosting-capacity studies, including recent work which shows that the capacity of a feeder for PV and charging stations is set by the loading on a small number of extreme days [5]. Studies in African cities also show that a grid with little reserve shifts from occasional to frequent interruptions as charging grows [6]. The consequences of a feeder overload, however, go beyond the feeder itself. The analysis of cascading failures and of the record of large blackouts in North America shows that a sequence of local overloads is the mechanism through which large blackouts begin [9, 10], often at a large economic cost [11, 14]. For this reason, reliability planning works with small outage probabilities per period [12]. These studies describe the mechanism and the stakes of an overload. However, they do not address how a utility could estimate a probability of order $10^{-3}$ to $10^{-6}$ for a specific feeder with a simulator that it can only run a limited number of times.

## II-B Charging-Station Planning under Uncertainty

Charging-infrastructure planning is usually formulated as a facility-location problem [15] that trades investment and operating cost against service quality. Early models were deterministic [16, 17], while later work added stochastic demand [18, 19]. A smaller number of studies considered disruptions such as floods [20] or outages mitigated by on-site storage [21]. The review in [22] calls for models that quantify randomness explicitly. In the facility-location literature on unreliable facilities [23], the failure probabilities are treated as known inputs. Earlier work on robust charging-station design [8] is, to our knowledge, the first to estimate station reliabilities by simulation inside the planning loop, using a Gaussian-proxy control variate to make this affordable. However, such a proxy must be known in advance. Furthermore, the Gaussian load model behind it is exactly the model that, as we show in this paper, misjudges the tail. In this paper, we leave the location model aside and focus on the estimation step, which all of these models take as given.

## II-C Rare-Event Simulation and Variance Reduction

Estimating a small probability by plain MC is inefficient, since the relative error grows as $1/\sqrt{n\theta}$ when $\theta$ decreases [24, 25]. Two classical remedies are importance sampling and control variates. Importance sampling changes the sampling distribution, whereas control variates keep it and subtract a correlated correction with zero mean [26, 27, 28, 29]. Control variates for probability estimation in particular were studied in [30]. Learned classifiers have also been used to build certifiable importance-sampling proposals for black-box rare events [31]. With a learned proposal, however, the quality of the estimate depends on how well the proposal covers the rare-event set, which has to be certified. A learned control variate, in contrast, keeps the estimator unbiased for any classifier. Several related ideas use a cheap model to correct an expensive one. Multi-fidelity MC uses a low-fidelity model as a control for a high-fidelity one and allocates the budget between the fidelities [32, 33]. Prediction-powered inference combines a machine-learning predictor on a large unlabelled set with a small labelled set [34, 35]. Control functionals build controls with a known mean from the score of the sampling density [36, 37]. The estimator in Theorem 3(b) is algebraically the same as the two-fidelity estimator and the prediction-powered estimator. None of these works, however, considers the case in which the quantity of interest is a rare binary event. In particular, they do not show how the variance reduction depends on the classifier, whether it survives as the event becomes rarer, or how the classifier should be trained when a nominal training set contains no event at all.

## II-D Outage Prediction and Weak Learners

Data-driven outage prediction models use weather, load and infrastructure features to forecast where and how many outages a storm will cause [38, 39]. Probabilistic forecasts of EV charging load and of ultra-short-term feeder load are also becoming common tools for operational reliability in smart grids [40, 41]. These models are built and tuned for prediction, with the accuracy, the error or the area under the ROC curve as the criterion. In machine learning, a weak learner is a classifier whose balanced error is only slightly below one half. Boosting shows that such learners can be combined into a strong one [13, 42]. In this paper, we use the notion for a different purpose. We show that a weak learner is already a useful control variate, whose value is set by its recall and precision on the rare days (Section IV). These criteria differ from those used for prediction.

## III Problem Setting and Estimators

## III-A Feeders, Charging Stations and Outages

We consider a distribution feeder that supplies, among other loads, a public EV charging station. The feeder has a rating, i.e., the thermal limit of its conductors or of the transformer behind it, and a protection scheme that disconnects it when the load exceeds the rating for long enough. On an ordinary day, the peak loading stays well below the rating. However, in a few days, several effects coincide: a high ambient temperature raises the air-conditioning load and reduces the capacity of the cables, the day-ahead forecast underestimates the demand, a storm shifts load onto the feeder, and many vehicles arrive at the station at once. The loading then reaches the rating and trips. We call such a day an outage day.

An outage day has two kinds of cost. For the charging operator, the station is out of service and revenue is lost. For the grid operator, the tripped feeder moves its load onto its neighbors, whose transformers then run hot and age faster. If the neighboring feeders are themselves close to their ratings, the disturbance could propagate as a cascading failure [9]. This second cost is borne by every customer on the affected feeders and is much larger than the first [14]. Hence, the approval of a charging station depends on the probability of an outage day on its feeder, which the grid operator requires to be small. For example, the criterion of one loss-of-load day in ten years [12] corresponds to $2.7\times 10^{-4}$ per day. In general, the probabilities that a planner needs to certify may lie between $10^{-3}$ and $10^{-6}$ .

The utility has three sources of information about a feeder. First, it knows the current rating and has an outage record, from which the current outage probability is known with reasonable accuracy. In the Surabaya data of [8], for instance, the reported reliabilities of the eleven feeders lie between $0.974$ and $0.983$ . Second, it has cheap observable features for every past or simulated day, such as the temperature anomaly, the day-ahead load-forecast anomaly, a storm flag, the day of the week and the season. Third, it has a simulator of the feeder that takes the features of a day, draws its own internal randomness (vehicle arrivals, equipment state and protection behaviour), computes the loading and reports whether the feeder trips. One simulated day is expensive compared with one feature draw, since it involves a power-flow or thermal model and a stochastic arrival process. The planning question is which reserve, through a feeder upgrade or a cap on the charging load, brings the outage probability below the target. This work focuses on the estimation part of this question: how to estimate the outage probability of a feeder at a given reserve with as few simulator runs as possible, together with a reliable and meaningful confidence interval for such a critical task.

## III-B Notation and Model

Let $Y=\mathbf{1}\{\text{outage day}\}$ , and let $\theta=\mathbb{P}(Y=1)$ be the outage probability of the feeder at the reserve under study. The features of a day form a vector $X\in\mathcal{X}$ with distribution $F_{X}$ , which can be drawn at cost $c_{X}$ . The simulator returns $Y$ given $X$ at cost $c_{Y}\gg c_{X}$ . We write $\eta(x)=\mathbb{P}(Y=1\mid X=x)$ for the conditional outage probability given the features. We assume a simulator, which can return a continuous severity $S$ and the standardized peak loading of the day, hence $Y=\mathbf{1}\{S\geq\kappa\}$ for a rating $\kappa$ . A higher reserve thus corresponds to a higher $\kappa$ . In the analysis that follows, we treats the simulator as a black box that simply returns $Y$ .

Planners often associate the outage probability $\theta$ with the following: the expected number of outage days per year (i.e., $365\,\theta$ ), the probability of at least one outage day in a horizon of $T$ days (i.e., $1-(1-\theta)^{T}\approx T\theta$ ), and and the station reliability (i.e., $1-\theta$ ). All three are linear in $\theta$ to first order, hence a variance reduction obtained for $\theta$ apply to them directly.

## III-C Monte Carlo and Control Variates

With $n$ independent simulated days, $\hat{\theta}^{\mathrm{MC}}=\bar{Y}_{n}$ is unbiased with $\mathrm{Var}(\hat{\theta}^{\mathrm{MC}})=\theta(1-\theta)/n$ . The number of runs required for a relative half-width $\varepsilon$ at confidence $1-\alpha$ is

 $n_{\mathrm{MC}}(\theta,\varepsilon,\alpha)=z_{1-\alpha/2}^{2}(1-\theta)/(\theta\,\varepsilon^{2}),$ 

(1)

which grows as $1/\theta$ . For a $10\%$ relative error at $95\%$ confidence, this is about $400/\theta$ runs, i.e., $4\times 10^{5}$ runs at $\theta=10^{-3}$ and $4\times 10^{8}$ at $10^{-6}$ . In other words, the planning has to observe about $400$ outage days in the Monte Carlo simulation to achieve this level of relative error. To that end, a control variate is a random variable $C$ that is simulated together with $Y$ , is correlated with $Y$ , and has a known mean $\gamma=\mathbb{E}[C]$ . For any coefficient $\beta$ , the estimator

 $\hat{\theta}^{\mathrm{CV}}_{\beta}=\bar{Y}_{n}-\beta\,(\bar{C}_{n}-\gamma)$ 

(2)

is unbiased. Furthermore, with $\rho=\mathrm{Corr}(Y,C)$ and $\beta^{\star}=\mathrm{Cov}(Y,C)/\mathrm{Var}(C)$ , its variance is [27, 29]

 $\mathrm{Var}(\hat{\theta}^{\mathrm{CV}}_{\beta^{\star}})=(1-\rho^{2})\,\mathrm{Var}(Y)/n.$ 

(3)

Thus, $\rho^{2}$ is the fraction of variance removed, $1/(1-\rho^{2})$ is the factor by which the number of simulator runs can be reduced, and $\sqrt{(1-\rho^{2})(1-\theta)/(n\theta)}$ is the relative standard error.

In an earlier study [8], researcher use the control variate $C=\mathbf{1}\{P\geq t\}$ for a Gaussian proxy $P$ of the load, with $\gamma=\bar{\Phi}(t)=1-\Phi(t)$ . In this paper, we use $C=g(X)$ for a learner $g$ trained on a separate set of simulator runs. Its mean $\gamma$ is either known, when $g$ thresholds a feature with a known distribution, or estimated from a pool of unlabelled feature draws, such as the features of every day in the utility’s weather archive. Algorithm 1 summarizes the procedure.

## IV Analytical Results

Throughout this section, $Y\in\{0,1\}$ with $\theta=\mathbb{P}(Y=1)\in(0,1)$ , and $X\in\mathcal{X}$ is the feature vector.

## IV-A Binary Learners

Definition 1.

A binary learner is a measurable $g:\mathcal{X}\to\{0,1\}$ with recall $\tau=\mathbb{P}(g(X)=1\mid Y=1)$ , false-positive rate $\phi=\mathbb{P}(g(X)=1\mid Y=0)$ , alarm rate $\gamma=\mathbb{P}(g(X)=1)=\theta\tau+(1-\theta)\phi$ and precision $\pi=\mathbb{P}(Y=1\mid g(X)=1)=\theta\tau/\gamma$ . Following [42, 13], $g$ is a weak learner with edge $\varepsilon>0$ if its balanced error rate $\tfrac{1}{2}((1-\tau)+\phi)$ is at most $\tfrac{1}{2}-\varepsilon$ , i.e., $\tau-\phi\geq 2\varepsilon$ . Note that no requirement is placed on accuracy, calibration or the area under the ROC curve.

With this definition, we have the following:

Theorem 1 (Variance reduction from a binary learner).

For a binary learner with $0<\gamma<1$ and $C=g(X)$ ,

 $\mathrm{Cov}(Y,C)=\theta(1-\theta)(\tau-\phi),\quad\rho^{2}=\frac{(\tau-\phi)^{2}\,\theta(1-\theta)}{\gamma(1-\gamma)}.$ 

(4)

Hence, the CV estimator has a smaller variance than MC if and only if $\tau\neq\phi$ . A weak learner with edge $\varepsilon$ guarantees $\rho^{2}\geq 4\varepsilon^{2}\theta(1-\theta)/(\gamma(1-\gamma))>0$ .

Proof.

 $\mathbb{E}[YC]=\mathbb{P}(Y=1,g(X)=1)=\theta\tau$ and $\mathbb{E}[Y]\mathbb{E}[C]=\theta\gamma$ , so $\mathrm{Cov}(Y,C)=\theta(\tau-\gamma)=\theta(1-\theta)(\tau-\phi)$ . Dividing its square by $\mathrm{Var}(Y)\mathrm{Var}(C)=\theta(1-\theta)\gamma(1-\gamma)$ gives $\rho^{2}$ .
∎

Since $\rho^{2}$ depends on $(\tau-\phi)^{2}$ , a learner that is worse than chance is as useful as one that is better by the same margin, and only the sign of $\beta^{\star}$ changes. This means, the only unusable learner is one whose alarms are independent of the outage. More interestingly, we have the following limit:

Corollary 1 (Rare-event limit).

For $\tau>0$ , $\rho^{2}=\tau\pi\,(1-\phi/\tau)^{2}(1-\theta)/(1-\gamma)$ . If $\theta\to 0$ while the precision stays bounded away from zero, then $\gamma,\phi\to 0$ and

 $\frac{\mathrm{Var}(\hat{\theta}^{\mathrm{CV}}_{\beta^{\star}})}{\mathrm{Var}(\hat{\theta}^{\mathrm{MC}})}\;\to\;1-\mathrm{recall}\times\mathrm{precision}.$ 

(5)

If instead $\phi$ is held fixed as $\theta\to 0$ , then $\rho^{2}=O(\theta)\to 0$ .

Proof.

Substitute $\theta/\gamma=\pi/\tau$ into (4). Every factor other than $\tau\pi$ tends to one when $\gamma,\phi\to 0$ . The fixed- $\phi$ case follows from (4) with $\gamma\to\phi$ .
∎

Corollary 1 gives the operational meaning of a weak learner for rare events. Any weak learner reduces the variance. However, the reduction stays bounded away from zero as the outage becomes rarer only if the false-positive rate shrinks in proportion to $\theta$ , i.e., only if the learner has non-vanishing precision. High recall is easy to obtain by predicting an outage every day, whereas precision has to be learned. Hence, a forecaster tuned to accuracy or ROC criteria is not tuned to the quantity that is meaningful for estimation.

## IV-B General Learners and the Ceiling Set by the Features

With regard to more general learners and features, we have the following:

Theorem 2 (Factorisation and ceiling).

Let $\eta(x)=\mathbb{P}(Y=1\mid X=x)$ , and let $g:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}$ have $0<\mathrm{Var}(g(X))<\infty$ . Then

 $\rho^{2}(Y,g(X))=\rho^{2}(\eta(X),g(X))\cdot\rho^{2}_{\max}$ 

(6)

where $\rho^{2}_{\max}=\mathrm{Var}(\eta(X))/\theta(1-\theta)$ .
Hence, $\rho^{2}(Y,g(X))\leq\rho^{2}_{\max}$ for every learner, with equality if and only if $g(X)$ is almost surely an affine function of $\eta(X)$ .

Proof.

By the tower property, we have $\mathrm{Cov}(Y,g(X))=\mathrm{Cov}(\eta(X),g(X))$ . Therefore,

 $\rho^{2}(Y,g)=[\mathrm{Cov}(\eta,g)^{2}/(\mathrm{Var}(\eta)\mathrm{Var}(g))]\cdot[\mathrm{Var}(\eta)/\mathrm{Var}(Y)],$ 

and Cauchy–Schwarz inequality bounds first factor by 1.
∎

Three consequences follow from this theorem. First, calibration does not matter, since an affine rescaling of a score leaves $\rho^{2}$ unchanged. Second, a thresholded score is a binary learner and is governed by Theorem 1. Third, the ceiling is a property of the features. The information that a utility collects about each day therefore bounds what any learner, weak or strong, could give in terms of the estimation efficiency.

For Gaussian proxy, we have the following result:

Proposition 1 (A Gaussian proxy loses its effect for rare events).

Let $(S,P)$ be bivariate normal with standard margins and correlation $r\in(-1,1)$ , $Y=\mathbf{1}\{S\geq\kappa\}$ and $C_{t}=\mathbf{1}\{P\geq t\}$ . Then $\sup_{t}\rho^{2}(Y,C_{t})\to 0$ as $\kappa\to\infty$ .

Proof sketch.

For a binary control with nonnegative covariance, (4) gives $\rho^{2}\leq\tau\pi/((1-\theta)(1-\gamma))$ . Take $r\geq 0$ , since the case $r<0$ follows by replacing $P$ with $-P$ , and let $c=(1+r)/2<1$ . For $t\geq c\kappa$ , the recall $\mathbb{P}(P\geq t\mid S\geq\kappa)$ vanishes as $\kappa\to\infty$ . Indeed, conditionally on $S=s\approx\kappa$ , the proxy is $N(rs,1-r^{2})$ , and $t-r\kappa\geq\kappa(1-r)/2$ . For $0\leq t<c\kappa$ , the precision is at most $\bar{\Phi}(\kappa)/\bar{\Phi}(c\kappa)$ , which tends to zero by the Mills ratio. For $t<0$ , we use the complementary control $1-C_{t}$ , whose recall vanishes in the same way. The three bounds are uniform in $t$ .
∎

The mechanism behind Proposition 1 is the asymptotic tail independence of the bivariate normal distribution [43]. The proxy and the load agree on moderate days but disagree on the extreme days that determine the outage. By Corollary 1, a learner whose precision does not vanish keeps a constant fraction of the variance. Whether such a learner exists depends on the mechanism of the outage. If extreme loads are driven by observable extremes (heat waves, forecast misses, storms), the features stay informative in the tail. If they are driven by unobserved noise, no estimator using those features is helpful.

## IV-C Estimated Coefficient, Unlabelled Pool, and Budget

To make our results practical, we extend our analysis to the estimated coefficient regime and report the following:

Theorem 3 (Estimated coefficient and unlabelled pool).

Let the $n$ labelled pairs and the $m$ unlabelled feature draws be independent, with $g$ fixed, $\mathrm{Var}(C)>0$ and $\rho=\mathrm{Corr}(Y,g(X))$ .

(a) 

With $\hat{\beta}$ estimated from the same $n$ pairs and $\gamma$ known, $\mathbb{E}[\hat{\theta}]=\theta+O(1/n)$ and $\sqrt{n}(\hat{\theta}-\theta)\to N(0,(1-\rho^{2})\mathrm{Var}(Y))$ .

(b) 

With $\hat{\gamma}$ as in Algorithm 1, $\hat{\theta}_{\beta}$ is unbiased for every fixed $\beta$ , and

 $\mathrm{Var}(\hat{\theta}_{\beta^{\star}})=\frac{\mathrm{Var}(Y)}{n}\Big(1-\rho^{2}\frac{m}{n+m}\Big).$ 

(7)

(c) 

With budget $B=n(c_{Y}+c_{X})+mc_{X}$ , (7) is minimised at $(n+m)/n=\sqrt{c_{Y}\rho^{2}/(c_{X}(1-\rho^{2}))}$ whenever this value exceeds 1. The variance ratio to MC at the same budget is $\mathrm{Var}^{\star}/\mathrm{Var}^{\mathrm{MC}}=\big(\sqrt{(1-\rho^{2})c_{Y}}+\sqrt{\rho^{2}c_{X}}\big)^{2}/(c_{Y}+c_{X})$ , which tends to $1-\rho^{2}$ as $c_{X}/c_{Y}\to 0$ .

Proof sketch.

(a) is the classical result for control variates with estimated coefficients [27, 28]. (b) Write $\bar{C}_{n}-\hat{\gamma}=m/(n+m)\cdot(\bar{C}_{n}-\bar{C}^{\prime}_{m})$ , where the pool mean $\bar{C}^{\prime}_{m}$ is independent of the pairs. Then expand the variance and substitute $\beta^{\star}$ . (c) With $s=(n+m)/n$ , the variance equals $\mathrm{Var}(Y)f(s)/B$ with $f(s)=(c_{Y}+sc_{X})(1-\rho^{2}+\rho^{2}/s)$ . This function is convex and is minimised at the stated value.
∎

Part (b) coincides with the two-fidelity estimator [32] and with prediction-powered inference with a tuned power parameter [35]. Combined with Theorems 1 and 2, it gives the variance in closed form for a rare binary outcome. In practice, the pool is the utility’s weather archive, on which the control is evaluated at no simulation cost. Part (c) then gives the number of archive days that is worth one simulator run.

## IV-D Training Design

Finally, we address the training nature of the learned CVs. In the rare regime, a learner trained on features drawn from the nominal distribution $F_{X}$ sees almost no outage and almost no extreme day. The following proposition shows that the training features may instead be drawn from any design.

Proposition 2 (Validity under any training design).

Let $g$ be any function of a training sample whose features are drawn from a distribution $q$ on $\mathcal{X}$ and labelled by the simulator, independently of the $n$ estimation pairs drawn from $F_{X}$ . Then, conditionally on $g$ , Theorem 3 applies to the estimator of Algorithm 1, with $\rho^{2}$ computed under $F_{X}$ . Furthermore, if $F_{X}\ll q$ with weights $w=dF_{X}/dq$ , the weighted training-sample ratios $\hat{\tau}=\sum_{l}w_{l}Y_{l}g(X_{l})/\sum_{l}w_{l}Y_{l}$ and $\hat{\pi}=\sum_{l}w_{l}Y_{l}g(X_{l})/\sum_{l}w_{l}g(X_{l})$ are consistent for the nominal recall and precision. Hence, $\hat{\tau}\hat{\pi}$ is a consistent estimate of the limit (5).

Proof.

Conditionally on $g$ , the estimation pairs are i.i.d. from the nominal distribution and $g$ is fixed, so the classical results apply. The weighted ratios are importance-sampling estimates [26, 44].
∎

In practice, the $n_{\mathrm{tr}}$ expensive training runs are placed where the outages are, by drawing the training features from a shifted design that over-represents hot days and high forecasts. Since the simulator returns the severity $S$ , the label $\mathbf{1}\{S\geq\kappa\}$ can be formed afterwards for any rating $\kappa$ . One training set therefore serves every rarity level. Score learners are fitted to $S$ by regression, while the threshold of a binary learner is chosen to maximise $\hat{\tau}\hat{\pi}$ . For a utility, the simulation planning used to train the control is thus a one-time investment, which can be reused when the ratings, the reliability targets or the stations under study change, which further emphasize the practical value of our proposed framework.

## V-A Simulator and Experiment Setup

Each simulated day has a temperature anomaly $x_{1}\sim N(0,1)$ , a day-ahead load-forecast anomaly $x_{2}\sim N(0,1)$ and a storm flag $x_{3}\sim\mathrm{Bernoulli}(0.15)$ . The simulator returns the standardised peak feeder loading

 $\displaystyle S$ 
 $\displaystyle=a_{1}x_{1}+a_{2}\max(x_{1},0)^{2}+a_{3}x_{2}$ 

 $\displaystyle+a_{4}x_{3}\max(\varepsilon_{2},0)+a_{0}\varepsilon_{1},$ 

(8)

with internal noise $\varepsilon_{1},\varepsilon_{2}\sim N(0,1)$ , and $Y=\mathbf{1}\{S\geq\kappa\}$ . The coefficients $(a_{1},a_{2},a_{3},a_{4},a_{0})=(0.45,0.20,0.55,0.40,0.30)$ represent a nonlinear heat effect, a linear forecast effect, a storm effect that only sometimes materialises, and unobserved noise. The forecast anomaly thus acts as a Gaussian proxy of the load, with latent correlation $0.61$ . The rating $\kappa$ is calibrated by quadrature so that $\theta\in\{10^{-3},10^{-4},10^{-5},10^{-6}\}$ exactly. Since the noise structure is known, $\eta(x)$ is also available by quadrature, and every $\rho^{2}$ is evaluated exactly by importance sampling over the features. It is worth noting that our estimators never use this information.

We compare plain MC with five controls: (1) the Gaussian proxy (GP) $\mathbf{1}\{x_{2}\geq t\}$ , with $t$ chosen to maximise its exact $\rho^{2}$ ; (2) a stump (ST) $\mathbf{1}\{x_{1}\geq t\}$ , i.e., a one-feature weak learner with $t$ chosen by the weighted $\hat{\tau}\hat{\pi}$ criterion; (3) a deliberately misspecified linear learner (LL), i.e., least squares of $S$ on $X$ , with score $\bar{\Phi}((\kappa-\hat{s}(x))/\hat{\sigma})$ ; (4) gradient-boosted trees (BT) regressing $S$ on $X$ [45], a standard strong learner for tabular data, with the same score; and (5) the Bayes posterior (BP) $\eta(X)$ , which attains the ceiling. The learners are trained once on $n_{\mathrm{tr}}=10^{4}$ runs from the tail-focused design $x_{1}\sim N(2,1.5^{2})$ , $x_{2}\sim N(1,1.5^{2})$ , and are reused at every $\theta$ . For comparison, they are also trained on $10^{4}$ nominal runs. The control means are computed once by importance sampling (relative standard error below $0.3\%$ ).

Fig. 1(a) and Table I present the main result. The Gaussian proxy removes less than $0.2\%$ of the variance at $\theta=10^{-3}$ and nothing beyond. Even a hypothetical proxy with latent correlation $0.98$ decays from $0.54$ to $0.38$ over the range (grey curves), as predicted by Proposition 1. The learned controls, in contrast, keep their effect at every level. The stump keeps $\rho^{2}\approx 0.25$ ( $1.3$ times fewer runs), and the misspecified linear learner keeps about $0.40$ ( $1.7$ times). The boosted trees keep $0.60$ to $0.64$ ( $2.5$ to $2.8$ times), which is within $5\%$ of the ceiling of $0.63$ to $0.65$ set by the features. Table I repeats each estimator over 400, 300, 120 and 60 replications at the four rarity levels, with $n=20/\theta$ simulated days per replication, i.e., about 20 outages. For each estimator, it reports the exact $\rho^{2}$ , the equivalent-runs multiplier $1/(1-\rho^{2})$ , the empirical relative RMSE and its theoretical value $\sqrt{(1-\rho^{2})(1-\theta)/(n\theta)}$ from (3), the relative bias, and the coverage of the plug-in $95\%$ interval. In Table I, every empirical relative RMSE is within two standard errors of its theoretical value, e.g., $0.21$ to $0.24$ for MC against $0.224$ . The boosted-tree control reduces it to $0.13$ to $0.14$ , while the $95\%$ intervals keep a coverage between $0.92$ and $0.98$ . At $\theta=10^{-6}$ , a $10\%$ relative error requires $3.8\times 10^{8}$ runs with MC and $1.5\times 10^{8}$ with the boosted-tree control, for a one-time training cost of $1.2\times 10^{4}$ runs.

Fig. 1(b) translates this result into more practical insights. At $\theta=10^{-4}$ , i.e., $0.037$ expected blackout days per year, the $95\%$ range of the MC estimate over 400 plannings is still $\pm 0.021$ days per year after $10^{5}$ simulated days and $\pm 0.007$ after $10^{6}$ . The control variate reaches the same ranges after about $5\times 10^{4}$ and $4\times 10^{5}$ days, and neither estimator shows a bias. Very short replications are the exception. With only about two observed outages, $\hat{\beta}$ is fitted to a handful of positive days, so the control may increase the variance and its plug-in interval undercovers. From about twenty outages onward, the coverage is close to nominal.

Furthermore, it appears That the training design plays crucial role as much as the learner. The same boosted learner trained on $10^{4}$ nominal runs has $\rho^{2}=0.34$ at $\theta=10^{-3}$ , $0.05$ at $10^{-4}$ and zero below, since its training set contains no extreme day and tree ensembles extrapolate as a constant. The tail-focused design, in contrast, leaves hundreds of outage days in the training set even at $\theta=10^{-6}$ . Finally, with the control mean taken from a pool of $m=10n$ unlabelled draws, the loss relative to a known mean is about a tenth of $\rho^{2}$ , as (7) predicts.

## V-C Case Study: Blackout Map of Surabaya

We now apply the estimator to a planning decision on the Surabaya network [8], which has 11 candidate feeders and 31 sub-districts. For each feeder $j$ , the earlier work reports a masked daily peak load with mean $\mu_{j}$ and standard deviation $\sigma_{j}$ , a rating $z_{j}$ , and a reliability $p_{j}$ between $0.974$ and $0.983$ at the current headroom $h_{0j}=(z_{j}-\mu_{j})/\sigma_{j}\approx 2$ . We place a charging station on every feeder. The planner then chooses the headroom $h_{j}$ , through a feeder upgrade or a cap on the charging load, so that the daily overload probability $\theta_{j}(h_{j})$ meets the one-day-in-ten-years target $\theta^{\star}=2.7\times 10^{-4}$ . The daily peak load is modelled as $\mu_{j}+\sigma_{j}\tilde{S}_{j}$ , where $\tilde{S}_{j}$ is the severity (8) standardised so that $\mathbb{P}(\tilde{S}_{j}\geq h_{0j})=1-p_{j}$ . The simulator therefore reproduces the reported reliability of every feeder and differs from the Gaussian model only in the tail.

We compare three design procedures: (a) the Gaussian load model, which requires $h=\bar{\Phi}^{-1}(\theta^{\star})=3.46$ for every feeder; (b) imprecise estimates, i.e., plain MC with $n=10^{4}$ simulated days per feeder (the largest sample of the earlier work) and the reserve sized to the point estimate; and (c) precise estimates, i.e., the boosted-tree control variate on the same days, with the reserve sized to the smallest headroom whose $95\%$ upper confidence limit meets the target. Since the simulator returns the severity, one replication gives the whole curve $\theta_{j}(h)$ .

## VI Discussion

## VI-A Implications for Grid Operators and City Planners

The results lead to a handful of implications for a distribution utility or a city that plans its EV infrastructure.

First, reliability targets could be certified with far fewer simulations. Since the planning quantities are linear in $\theta$ (Section III), the variance reduction transfers to them unchanged. In the Surabaya case, the control variate reaches the decision quality of an MC sample two to three times larger. Combined with an upper confidence limit, it also reduces the city-wide blackout days by a third, and the population on an under-designed feeder from $46\%$ to $15\%$ , compared with MC point estimates. Furthermore, the load model plays important role as much as the estimator. The Gaussian model of the earlier work reproduces the current outage rate of some districts (including Embong Wungu), yet it underestimates the required upgrade by about $3{,}100$ units. The resulting risk is more than five times the accepted level.

Next, investment in features should come before investment in models. By Theorem 2, the information collected about each day bounds what any learner could achieve. In our simulator, halving or doubling the unobserved noise moved the ceiling from $0.64$ to $0.79$ or $0.38$ for every learner. Hence, feeder loading histories, maintenance schedules, distributed-generation output and the load forecasts that utilities already produce [40, 41] could raise the ceiling, while a more elaborate model on the same features could not.

Furthermore, the training budget should be spent where the outages are. A training sample placed on hot, high-forecast days keeps its value at every reliability target and can be reused when the ratings or the targets change. A sample placed on typical days, in contrast, is wasted in the rare regime. Finally, the control depends only on the features of a day, so it can be evaluated on a decade of weather records at no simulation cost. Theorem 3(c) then gives the number of such days that is worth one simulator run. Together, these points suggest a simple protocol for an operator: train once on a tail-focused sample, evaluate the control on the archive, size the reserve with an upper confidence limit, and commit only after the sample contains at least about twenty outages.

## VI-B Limitations and Future Work

We also highlight a few limitations of this work and future work directions. First, we are limited by the use of a synthetic simulator and synthetic features, which are chosen so that the exact posterior is available for evaluation. In the Surabaya case, the simulator matches the reported reliability of every feeder, but the shape of its tail is assumed. A feeder model driven by real weather and outage records is therefore the natural next step. Furthermore, outages at different feeders are modelled independently, although a city-wide storm correlates them, which we ignore in this study. Finally, the tail-focused design was also chosen by hand. An adaptive design could alleviate this choice, which might allow us to combine our current learned control variate results with learned importance-sampling proposal [31], which we plan to explore in our future work.

## VII Conclusion

In this paper, we have shown that a classifier of grid outages trained on cheap observable features yields a valid control variate, which reduces the variance for every weak learner. Its effect is governed by recall and precision through an exact formula and is bounded by a ceiling that the features alone determine. Unlike a Gaussian proxy of the load, it does not vanish as the outage becomes rarer, provided that the learner is trained where the outages are. On a feeder-overload simulator, the learned controls removed between a quarter and nearly two thirds of the Monte Carlo variance at outage probabilities from $10^{-3}$ to $10^{-6}$ . On the Surabaya network, the Gaussian load model yields every feeder three to seven times the one-day-in-ten-years target, while point-estimate designs from plain Monte Carlo makes almost half of the feeders achieve above the target. At the same simulation budget, the learned control variate with a confidence limit also reduces the city-wide blackout days by a third. Compared with Monte Carlo under the same rule, it also frees up the over-design of the reserve by up to $40\%$ . The proposed method thus makes the certification of rare overload probabilities affordable for a utility, at the cost of a one-time training sample and a pool of historical feature data, which opens huge potential to design a more reliable grid against rare-event outages.

Abstract

The Reinforcement Learning (RL) paradigm has
achieved remarkable success in enhancing the reasoning capabilities of Large Language Models
(LLMs). However, when applied to long-horizon tasks, it faces severe challenges: i) Due to the high task complexity, the model struggles to sample enough successful trajectories, resulting in slow convergence. ii) Relying solely on sparse final rewards causes all steps to be updated indiscriminately, encouraging redundant step generation. When both the quantity and quality of successful trajectories become difficult to guarantee, the performance is severely constrained. This dilemma stems from the limitations: existing RL paradigms rely entirely on the reasoning capability of base models, lacking the ability to actively optimize trajectories. To address the above issues, we propose a novel trajectory evolution paradigm with hindsight credit assignment, termed TrajEvolve. TrajEvolve not only relies on the trajectory inferred by the model but also generates ‘‘evolutionary trajectories’’ by refining low-quality steps in historical trajectories, thereby exposing the policy to high-quality compositions that lie within the base model’s support but are rarely sampled within practical training budgets. Furthermore, TrajEvolve reformulates step-level continuous credit assignment as a binary classification task, whether each step is necessary for task completion. This simplification not only reduces the learning difficulty but also enables step labels to be obtained through counterfactual verification, achieving precise identification of redundant steps. Extensive experiments on two datasets demonstrate the superiority of the proposed method11

## 1 Introduction

Large Language Models (LLMs) (Achiam et al., 2023; Yang et al., 2025) have achieved remarkable breakthroughs in language understanding and generation. Among post-training techniques for LLMs, Reinforcement Learning (RL), which iteratively improves models through exploration and reward-driven optimization, has emerged as one of the most influential paradigms. Group Relative Policy Optimization (Shao et al., 2024) and its variants, which generate multiple rollout trajectories and leverage inter-group reward comparisons for policy optimization, have gained significant attention.

Despite great success achieved, RL paradigms face severe challenges when applied to long-horizon decision-making tasks (Feng et al., 2025; Shridhar et al., 2020). Such tasks typically require the model to interact with external environments over dozens of steps, with reward signals provided only at the final outcome. The impact of any individual action may not manifest until many steps later, substantially increasing the difficulty of reinforcement learning. They face two typical dilemmas in this setting: i) Due to high task complexity and vast exploration space, the model struggles to sample sufficient successful trajectories, resulting in sparse learning signals and slow convergence. ii) Relying solely on sparse final rewards causes all steps to be updated indiscriminately, encouraging the generation of redundant steps in successful trajectories. When neither the quantity nor the quality of trajectories can be guaranteed, the performance of RL paradigms is severely constrained.

These issues fundamentally stem from a critical limitation of existing RL paradigms: their performance is bounded by the base model’s reasoning capability, lacking the ability to actively optimize trajectories. Recent studies (Yue et al., 2025a) have pointed out that RL merely improves the sampling efficiency of correct reasoning paths already within the base model’s repertoire, rather than eliciting fundamentally new reasoning patterns. This raises a natural question:

Can we proactively construct higher-quality trajectories during training to help models to overcome the sampling barrier that on-policy RL inherently faces in long-horizon tasks?

The key challenge in addressing this problem (Lu et al., 2025) lies in accurately identifying the valuable steps, which is essentially a step-level credit assignment problem. Existing credit assignment methods can be categorized into two types: i) Heuristic-based signals (Wang et al., 2025c), which rely on indicators such as LLM output confidence that have no direct correlation with task outcomes. Such signals may reinforce confident but incorrect outputs. ii) Outcome-based signals (Feng et al., 2025; Ji et al., 2025), which estimate credit through multiple rollouts on intermediate steps, evaluating credit with final rewards. These methods focus solely on which steps are likely to lead to success, but such biased estimations cannot identify steps that are redundant yet do not affect the final outcome. In summary, existing methods struggle to determine the true contribution of individual steps to the task, let alone identify redundant steps in the success trajectory. Moreover, accurate credit assignment remains extremely challenging given the vast solution space of long-horizon tasks.

To address these challenges, we propose TrajEvolve, a novel trajectory evolution for reinforcement learning with hindsight credit assignment. TrajEvolve not only relies on the trajectory inferred by the model but also generates “evolutionary trajectories” by refining low-quality steps in historical trajectories, thereby exposing the policy to high-quality compositions that lie within the base model’s support but are rarely sampled within practical training budgets. Notably, TrajEvolve reformulates continuous credit assignment as a binary classification of whether each step is necessary for task. This simplification compresses the infinite solution space into a finite subspace and can be verified through counterfactual experiments, thereby reducing learning difficulty and ensuring sufficient labeled data for Discriminator training. Specifically, TrajEvolve obtains step-level redundancy labels through hindsight verification and trains a discriminator model to evolve historical trajectories. The evolved trajectories serve as high-quality samples and are jointly utilized with policy-generated trajectories for reinforcement learning optimization. The main contributions of the paper are as follows:

• 

We propose a trajectory evolution paradigm that not only trains on the model-inferred trajectory but also generates “Evolutionary Trajectories” by refining low-quality steps in historical trajectories, thereby exposing the policy to high-quality compositions that are rarely sampled within training.

• 

We reformulate continuous credit assignment as a binary classification of whether each step is necessary for task completion. This simplification compresses the infinite solution space into a verifiable subspace, substantially reducing difficulty and achieving effective credit assignment.

• 

Experiments on ALFWorld and WebShop validate TrajEvolve’s effectiveness.

## 2 Preliminaries

Problem Setup. Following (Feng et al., 2025), we consider a general multi-step decision-making setting where an LLM agent interacts with an environment based on a task description $x$ . At each time step $t=1,2,\ldots,T$ , the agent observes a state $s_{t}=(o_{t},\mathcal{A}_{t})$ , comprising an observation $o_{t}$ and the set of available actions $\mathcal{A}_{t}$ , then selects an action $a_{t}\in\mathcal{A}_{t}$ according to its policy $\pi_{\theta}(a_{t}\mid o_{t},\mathcal{A}_{t})$ . The environment subsequently transitions to state $s_{t+1}$ . A complete $T$ -step episode forms a trajectory $\tau=\{(s_{1},a_{1}),(s_{2},a_{2}),\ldots,(s_{T},a_{T})\}$ . Notably, only a sparse binary reward $r\in\{0,1\}$ is provided at episode termination in our setting, indicating task success or failure.

Group-based RL. Group-based reinforcement learning (Shao et al., 2024) bypasses auxiliary critic networks by computing advantages through within-group normalization. Given a task $x$ , the policy generates $N$ candidate trajectories $\{\tau_{1},\ldots,\tau_{N}\}$ with scalar rewards $R(\tau_{i})$ . Advantages are computed as: $A(\tau_{i})=\frac{R(\tau_{i})-\mu}{\sigma}$ ,
where $\mu$ and $\sigma$ denote the group mean and standard deviation, respectively. This yields a memory-efficient and scalable framework.

Credit Assignment. Group-based methods assign uniform credit to all steps based solely on terminal rewards, which becomes problematic where steps contribute unequally. Credit assignment addresses this by deriving step-level scores:

 $c_{t}=g(s_{t},a_{t},\tau,R(\tau)),\quad t=1,\ldots,T.$ 

(1)

where $c_{t}$ is the credit score for step $t$ , $s_{t}$ and $a_{t}$ denote the state and action, $\tau$ represents the complete trajectory, and $R(\tau)$ is the terminal reward. However, existing methods attempt to estimate precise credit scores for every step, yet the vast solution space inherent to long-horizon tasks renders exact credit quantification exceedingly difficult.

## 3 TrajEvolve

In this section, we present TrajEvolve for step-level credit assignment in long-horizon agent tasks. We begin by reformulating the credit assignment problem from continuous score estimation to binary necessity classification, which substantially reduces the solution space and enables reliable label acquisition (§3.1). We then describe a hindsight verification procedure that obtains ground-truth necessity labels through counterfactual execution in the environment (§3.2). Next, we integrate the obtained labels into policy optimization via redundancy-aware reward shaping and step-level advantage adjustment (§3.3). Finally, we introduce a trajectory evolution mechanism that refines historical trajectories by eliminating redundant steps and correcting errors, enabling the model to learn from improved trajectories beyond its current reasoning capability (§3.4).

## 3.1 Problem Reformulation: Continuous to Discrete

Existing credit assignment methods either rely on predefined scoring approaches or train implicit process reward models (Cui et al., 2025; Liu et al., 2025a) to estimate step-level contributions. However, these approaches fundamentally attempt to predict precise continuous credit values over an infinite solution space, which becomes intractable for long-horizon tasks. Moreover, since supervision signals are only derived from final results, the feedback is inherently delayed, making it difficult to accurately attribute credit to individual steps.

We observe that for effective policy learning, the critical signal is not the precise credit magnitude, but whether each step contributes positively to task success. This is particularly pronounced in long-horizon tasks, where exploratory interactions with the environment introduce numerous redundant steps that outcome-based methods fail to properly process. This insight motivates us to reformulate credit assignment as a binary necessary or redundant classification problem. Formally, given a trajectory $\tau=\{(s_{t},a_{t})\}_{t=1}^{T}$ , we define a step-wise discriminator $\mathcal{D}_{\phi}:\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{T}\rightarrow\{0,1\}$ :

 $\hat{y}_{t}=\mathcal{D}_{\phi}(s_{t},a_{t},\tau),$ 

(2)

where $\hat{y}_{t}=1$ indicates that step $t$ is predicted as necessary, and $\hat{y}_{t}=0$ as redundant.

We emphasize the superiority of reformulation as follows: i) it compresses the infinite solution space from continuous values to a finite hypothesis space, substantially reducing learning complexity and enabling even small models to achieve strong performance. ii) Unlike continuous credit scores whose ground truth is practically unobtainable, binary credit labels can be reliably acquired through hindsight verification, ensuring sufficient supervision for training the discriminator to identify redundant versus necessary steps. We detail the verification procedure in the next subsection.

## 3.2 Hindsight Verification on Environment-Changing Steps

A key advantage of the reformulation is that credit labels can be reliably acquired through hindsight verification in the environment. In this section, we describe how to obtain ground-truth credit labels.

Action Categorization. We distinguish two types of actions in
environment-interactive tasks: environment-changing actions that
alter the environment state (e.g., take, move), and
information-gathering actions that only query the environment
without modifying it (e.g., examine).
Hindsight verification is well-defined only for the former:
removing an information-gathering step neither breaks downstream
feasibility nor alters task outcomes, so its necessity cannot be
determined by environment replay. We therefore apply hindsight
verification exclusively to environment-changing steps and assign
 $y_{t}=1$ to all information-gathering steps by default. In ALFWorld
and WebShop, environment-changing actions account for the vast
majority of steps; the extension to environments with substantial
information-gathering steps (e.g., ScienceWorld) is discussed in
Appendix C.4.

To determine whether a step is necessary, we must account for its downstream effects.

Definition 3.1 (Dependent Steps).

Given a successful trajectory $\tau=\{(s_{t},a_{t})\}_{t=1}^{T}$ and a step index $t$ , we define the dependent step set $\mathcal{D}(t)$ as the set of subsequent steps whose actions become infeasible when step $t$ is removed:

 $\mathcal{D}(t)=\left\{j:j>t,\;a_{j}\notin\mathcal{A}(s_{j}^{-t})\right\},$ 

(3)

where $s_{j}^{-t}$ denotes the state at step $j$ when step $t$ is skipped, and $\mathcal{A}(s)$ is the set of admissible actions at state $s$ .

Remark 3.2.

Accurate computation of the dependent step set $\mathcal{D}(t)$ is critical for subsequent credit labeling. Errors in identifying dependent steps can cause information leakage or incorrect credit labels inheritance via Proposition 3.7, ultimately misleading the entire training process.

Remark 3.3.

In environment-interactive scenarios, identifying dependent steps is straightforward. If removing step $t$ causes a subsequent action to become infeasible, that action is dependent on step $t$ . Action feasibility can be directly read from environment feedback—task execution environments such as ALFWorld explicitly return an execution-status signal, making this judgment trivial.

Besides the aforementioned definitions, we also require the following properties to determine steps:

Proposition 3.4 (Necessity).

Step $t$ is necessary only if removing step $t$ along with its dependent steps causes task failure in a successful trajectory $\tau$ :

 $\mathcal{V}\left(\tau\setminus\left(\{t\}\cup\mathcal{D}(t)\right)\right)=0,$ 

(4)

where $\mathcal{V}(\cdot)\in\{0,1\}$ is the task success indicator.

Remark 3.5.

The converse of Proposition 3.4 does not hold: task failure upon removal does not imply the step is necessary Consider a trajectory: go to cabinet $\rightarrow$ open cabinet $\rightarrow$ go to bathroom $\rightarrow$ go to cabinet $\rightarrow$ take clothes. Both go to bathroom and the second go to cabinet are redundant, yet removing the latter alone still causes failure.

Proposition 3.6 (Sufficiency).

Step $t$ is redundant if removing step $t$ along with its dependent steps preserves task success in a successful trajectory $\tau$ :

 $\mathcal{V}\left(\tau\setminus\left(\{t\}\cup\mathcal{D}(t)\right)\right)=1.$ 

(5)

Proposition 3.7 (Transitivity).

The dependent steps of a redundant step are all redundant: if $y_{t}=0$ , then $y_{j}=0$ for all $j\in\mathcal{D}(t)$ .

Remark 3.8.

The dependent steps of a necessary step are not necessarily necessary.

Discriminator Training. With the credit labels $\{y_{t}\}_{t=1}^{T}$ obtained via Algorithm 3.2, we train the discriminator $\mathcal{D}_{\phi}$ via supervised fine-tuning on an LLM backbone. Given a prompt $\mathbf{x}_{t}$ encoding the trajectory context and the target step $(s_{t},a_{t})$ , the model outputs a binary judgment. The training objective minimizes the cross-entropy loss:

 $\mathcal{L}_{\text{SFT}}=-\mathbb{E}_{(\mathbf{x}_{t},y_{t})\sim\mathcal{D}_{\text{train}}}\left[\log P_{\phi}(\hat{y}_{t}=y_{t}\mid\mathbf{x}_{t})\right],$ 

(6)

where $\mathcal{D}_{\text{train}}$ denotes the training set constructed from verified trajectories.

Discriminator Pre-training. During early RL training, the policy model struggles to generate sufficient successful trajectories, resulting in sparse credit labels. To address this cold-start problem, we pre-train the discriminator before RL optimization. Specifically, we use Qwen2.5-14B-Instruct to generate approximately 2,000 successful trajectories, which are processed through Algorithm 3.2 to obtain credit labels.

Notably, unlike conventional SFT that requires high-quality trajectories from strong teacher models, our paradigm does not require optimal or concise trajectories. Trajectories with redundant steps provide richer negative samples. This property reduces dependency on powerful LLMs.

## 3.3 Policy Optimization with Credit Labels

Building upon the credit labels, we integrate step-level credit into the policy optimization.

Redundancy-Aware Reward.
Conventional methods often apply uniform penalties based on trajectory length or repeated actions, which may inadvertently discourage longer but correct trajectories and lead to suboptimal performance. To address this limitation, we propose a redundancy-aware reward mechanism. Specifically, for each successful trajectory $\tau_{i}$ , the shaped reward is computed as:

 $\small\tilde{R}(\tau_{i})=R(\tau_{i})-\alpha\cdot\rho(\tau_{i}),$ 

(7)

where $R(\tau_{i})$ is the original task reward, $\rho(\tau_{i})$ denotes the redundancy ratio defined as the proportion of redundant steps in the trajectory, and $\alpha\geq 0$ controls the penalty strength.

Instead of penalizing indiscriminately, our formulation applies targeted penalties only to trajectories with genuinely redundant steps, thereby preserving high rewards for efficient executions.

Step-Level Advantage.
Given a task $x$ , the policy $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}$ generates a group of $N$ complete trajectories $\{\tau_{1},\tau_{2},\ldots,\tau_{N}\}$ under identical initial conditions. Each trajectory $\tau_{i}$ receives the shaped reward $\tilde{R}(\tau_{i})$ as described above. To evaluate the relative quality of each trajectory within the group, we compute an episode-level advantage $A^{E}(\tau_{i})$ as follows:

 $\small A^{E}(\tau_{i})=\frac{\tilde{R}(\tau_{i})-\text{mean}(\{\tilde{R}(\tau_{j})\}_{j=1}^{N})}{\text{std}(\{\tilde{R}(\tau_{j})\}_{j=1}^{N})}.$ 

(8)

While the episode-level advantage captures trajectory-wide performance, it assigns uniform credit to all steps. To provide finer-grained guidance, we introduce a step-level advantage $A^{S}(a_{t}^{(i)})$ derived from the discriminator predictions. The final advantage for each action combines both levels:

 $A(a_{t}^{(i)})=A^{E}(\tau_{i})+\lambda\cdot A^{S}(a_{t}^{(i)}),$ 

(9)

where $\lambda>0$ is a weighting coefficient, and $A^{S}(a_{t}^{(i)})=\mathbb{I}[y_{t}=1]$ indicates whether step $t$ is necessary. For failed trajectories, we encourage the correct prefix by applying the bonus to steps preceding the first erroneous step $t_{\textnormal{err}}$ , i.e., the first step that is either invalid or predicted as redundant by the discriminator. This hierarchical advantage structure provides both trajectory-wide and step-wise supervision, enabling more effective credit assignment in long-horizon tasks.

Policy Update.
We optimize the policy as follows:

 $\mathcal{J}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}\Big[\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\min(\rho_{\theta}(a_{t})A(a_{t}),\text{clip}(\rho_{\theta}(a_{t}),1\pm\epsilon)A(a_{t}))\Big],$ 

(10)

where $\rho_{\theta}(a_{t})=\frac{\pi_{\theta}(a_{t}|s_{t})}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(a_{t}|s_{t})}$ is the importance sampling ratio.

## 3.4 Trajectory Evolution

Existing RL paradigms’ performance (Wang et al., 2025d) is inherently bounded by the base model’s reasoning capability. In this section, we introduce a trajectory evolution method that actively refines historical trajectories during training.

Evolution Strategies.
Let $\mathcal{H}=\{\tau_{1},\tau_{2},\ldots\}$ denote the historical trajectory sampled during training. Based on the step-level credit labels obtained, we apply different evolution strategies:

• 

Redundancy Elimination: Given a successful trajectory $\tau$ with credit labels $\{y_{t}\}_{t=1}^{T}$ , we construct an evolved trajectory $\tau^{*}$ by retaining only the necessary steps:

 $\tau^{*}=\{(s_{t},a_{t})\in\tau\mid y_{t}=1\}.$ 

• 

Format Correction: When a trajectory fails due to an invalid action format at step $t_{\text{err}}$ , we map the erroneous action to the nearest valid action in the admissible action space $\mathcal{A}(s_{t_{\text{err}}})$ .

• 

Error Rollback: When a trajectory fails due to an incorrect action at step $t_{\text{err}}$ , we retain all steps before $t_{\text{err}}$ and allow the policy to regenerate, providing opportunities to explore alternative actions.

Training with Evolved Trajectories.
The evolved trajectories are re-executed in the environment to obtain updated rewards, and subsequently incorporated into the policy optimization alongside newly sampled trajectories. This augmentation enriches the training distribution with high-quality, non-redundant trajectories that may not be sampled by the current policy, thus exposing the policy to high-quality compositions that are rarely sampled within practical training budgets.

## 4 Theoretical Analysis

In this section, we provide a theoretical analysis showing that, within a stylized model of group-relative policy optimization, on-policy RL faces a redundancy lower bound in long-horizon tasks, and that trajectory evolution can lift this barrier.

## Problem Setup.

In this section, we consider the classic GRPO training framework. At each iteration, the policy $\pi_{\theta}$ generates a group of $N$ trajectories for each task. Each trajectory $\tau=\{(s_{t},a_{t})\}_{t=1}^{T}$ receives a shaped reward $\tilde{R}(\tau)=R(\tau)-\alpha\cdot\rho(\tau)$ , where $\rho(\tau)$ denotes the redundancy ratio. We define a clean trajectory as a successful trajectory with zero redundancy, i.e., $R(\tau)=1,\rho(\tau)=0$ .

Assumption 4.1 (Per-Step Redundancy Model).

There exists $\beta\in(0,1]$ such that, along every rollout, a $\beta$ -fraction of the states $s_{t}$ are redundancy-prone: under the policy $\pi_{\theta_{k}}$ at iteration $k$ , the action taken at each such state is redundant independently with probability $p_{k}$ , i.e.,

 $P_{\pi_{\theta_{k}}}(r_{t}=1\mid s_{t})=p_{k},$ 

(11)

while actions at the remaining states are never redundant, where $r_{t}$ is the redundancy indicator, $r_{t}=1$ if step $t$ is redundant and $r_{t}=0$ if necessary. For the initial policy $\pi_{\theta_{0}}$ , $p_{0}>0$ .

Remark 4.2.

The condition $p_{0}>0$ is mild since the LLM-based policy outputs a softmax distribution over all admissible actions, where every action retains positive probability, and in long-horizon interactive tasks multiple plausible but unnecessary actions exist at most states; $\beta$ accounts for the states at which no redundant action is available.

Assumption 4.3 (Gradient Smoothness).

Let $m_{\textnormal{clean}}^{(k)}$ denote the number of clean trajectories in the training group at iteration $k$ . There exists a bounded, positive, non-decreasing function $h:\{1,\dots,N-1\}\to(0,h_{\max}]$ , extended by $h(0)=h(N)=0$ , such that, conditional on the group composition, the expected per-iteration reduction of the per-step redundancy probability $p_{k}$ satisfies

 $E\big[\,p_{k}-p_{k+1}\mid m_{\textnormal{clean}}^{(k)}=m\,\big]=h(m)\,\eta,\qquad 0\leq m\leq N.$ 

(12)

Remark 4.4.

This assumption captures two properties: (i) the total gradient effect is bounded by $h_{\max}\eta$ per iteration, enforced by gradient clipping and learning rates; (ii) the gradient signal for reducing redundancy is governed by the number of clean trajectories, since clean trajectories provide a direct comparison between redundant and non-redundant behavior in the advantage computation.

Theorem 4.5 (Redundancy Lower Bound for On-Policy RL).

Under Assumptions 4.1 and 4.3, for any training iteration $K\leq K^{*}$ , the expected redundancy ratio of the trained policy satisfies:

 $E\!\left[\rho(\tau_{K})\right]\geq\frac{\beta p_{0}}{2},$ 

(13)

where $K^{*}=p_{0}/\big(2Nh_{\max}\,\eta\,(1-p_{0}/2)^{\beta T}\big)$ and $T$ is the length of trajectory $\tau$ .

Remark 4.6.

As the task horizon $T$ increases, $K^{*}$ grows exponentially since its denominator $(1-p_{0}/2)^{\beta T}$ vanishes, meaning that even reaching the redundancy floor $\beta p_{0}/2$ requires exponentially more iterations for longer-horizon tasks.

Proposition 4.7.

Suppose at each iteration, trajectory evolution injects $m_{\textnormal{evolve}}\in\{1,\dots,N-1\}$ clean trajectories into the training group alongside $N-m_{\textnormal{evolve}}$ on-policy rollouts. Under Assumption 4.3, for any target $\varepsilon\in(0,p_{0})$ , the expected redundancy satisfies

 $E\!\left[\rho(\tau_{K}^{\textnormal{evolve}})\right]\leq\beta\varepsilon\quad\textnormal{for all }K\geq\frac{p_{0}-\varepsilon}{\delta_{\varepsilon}},\qquad\delta_{\varepsilon}:=h(m_{\textnormal{evolve}})\,\eta\,\Big(1-(1-\varepsilon)^{\beta T(N-m_{\textnormal{evolve}})}\Big)>0.$ 

(14)

Remark 4.8 (Exponential vs. Polynomial).

Within this model, the number of iterations TrajEvolve needs to reach redundancy $\beta\varepsilon$ is at most $(p_{0}-\varepsilon)/\delta_{\varepsilon}$ , which is polynomial in $(p_{0},1/\varepsilon,1/\eta,1/h(m_{\textnormal{evolve}}))$ and, since $\delta_{\varepsilon}$ is non-decreasing in $T$ , bounded independently of the horizon $T$ . In contrast, the barrier $K^{*}$ of Theorem 4.5 grows exponentially in $T$ .

Due to the space limits, the detailed proof of the above theorem can be found in Appendix A.

## 5 Experiment

## 5.1 Experiment Setup

Benchmarks.
We evaluate our method on two benchmarks: ALFWorld (Shridhar et al., 2020) is a text-based embodied environment where agents receive natural language goals and must accomplish them through multi-turn interaction. It comprises 3,827 task instances across six categories of household activities: Pick & Place (Pick), Examine in Light (Look), Clean & Place (Clean), Heat & Place (Heat), Cool & Place (Cool), and Pick Two & Place (Pick2), requiring compositional reasoning including object identification and state manipulation. WebShop (Yao et al., 2022a) simulates realistic online shopping scenarios with 1.18 million real-world products and 12,087 crowd-sourced instructions. Agents must interact with a simulated HTML-based e-commerce website to search, navigate through product pages, and purchase items matching user specifications. This benchmark presents challenges in understanding instructions and strategic exploration across a vast catalog.

Baselines.
We compare our approach against three categories of competitive baselines. (1) Closed-source LLMs: GPT-4o (Hurst et al., 2024) and Gemini-2.5-Pro (Comanici et al., 2025), which represent frontier models in reasoning capability. (2) Prompting agents: ReAct (Yao et al., 2022b) and Reflexion (Shinn et al., 2023), which leverage in-context prompting and self-reflection mechanisms to guide multi-step decision-making. (3) RL training methods: PPO (Schulman et al., 2017), which is a classic actor-critic algorithm that relies on a separate value function for step value prediction; RLOO (Ahmadian et al., 2024) and GRPO (Shao et al., 2024) eliminate the critic by computing relative advantages across multiple sampled rollouts; and GiGPO (Feng et al., 2025) introduces a two-level structure combining episode-level advantages with step-level advantages via anchor state grouping for fine-grained credit assignment.

Training details. We use Qwen2.5-1.5B/7B-Instruct as our base models. For fair comparison, all RL training methods share identical hyperparameter configurations, with the rollout group size $N$ set to 8. For the evolution module, we perform trajectory evolution every 5 training epochs. Specifically, for each task, we select 2 trajectories from the rollout samples for subsequent evolution operations, prioritizing trajectories with higher invalid action ratios and longer sequence lengths, as these typically contain more room for improvement. The hyperparameter $\lambda$ is fixed as 0.5. For the discriminator, we use Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct as the backbone and pre-train it for 3 epochs before RL optimization. During RL training, the discriminator is fine-tuned via LoRA and updated alternately with the policy network, training for 1 epoch on credit labels derived from trajectories at each iteration.

## 5.2 Overall Evaluation

Table 1 shows the experiment results. We summarize our findings as follows:

• 

Closed-source LLMs and prompting-based methods exhibit limited performance on multi-step decision-making tasks. Despite their strong general capabilities, GPT-4o and Gemini-2.5-Pro achieve only 45.3% and 57.8% success rates on ALFWorld, respectively. Open-source prompting agents such as ReAct and Reflexion show marginal improvements over vanilla prompting but still underperform significantly, highlighting the inherent difficulty of long-horizon control.

• 

RL training substantially improves agent performance, with group-based methods demonstrating competitive efficiency. PPO brings notable gains across both benchmarks, achieving 64.8% on ALFWorld and 57.0% on WebShop with the 1.5B model. GiGPO, with its hierarchical advantage estimation, consistently outperforms other RL baselines, validating the importance of fine-grained credit assignment in multi-step tasks.

• 

Our method achieves the best overall performance across both model scales and benchmarks. On the 1.5B model, our approach attains 92.2% on ALFWorld and 71.9% on WebShop, surpassing GiGPO by 5.5% and 4.7%, respectively. These results demonstrate that our trajectory evolution mechanism provides complementary benefits to fine-grained credit assignment, enabling more effective policy optimization for LLM agents.

## 5.3 Training Analysis

Figure 2 presents the validation success rate curves during training, which can show how the training dynamics reflect the effectiveness of our evolution paradigm. We summarize our findings as follows:

• 

Faster convergence stems from alleviating the sparse signal dilemma. In the early training stages, our method exhibits notably steeper learning curves compared to baselines. This acceleration directly addresses a fundamental limitation of standard RL: the base model struggles to sample sufficient successful trajectories in complex environments, resulting in sparse learning signals. By generating evolutionary trajectories through hindsight refinement, our approach provides additional high-quality training samples that are not bounded by the model’s current reasoning capability, thereby enriching the gradient signal and enabling more efficient policy updates.

• 

Sustained performance gains validate the trajectory evolution paradigm. Throughout training, our method consistently maintains a performance advantage over GiGPO and other baselines. Compared to existing RL methods can only improve sampling efficiency of reasoning paths already within the base model’s repertoire, our trajectory evolution paradigm actively constructs improved trajectories by eliminating redundant steps. This enables the model to learn from trajectories that are rarely sampled within practical training budgets.

• 

The advantage is more pronounced on WebShop due to higher task complexity. WebShop involves a much larger action space and noisier, lengthier observations, where standard RL methods suffer from indiscriminate updates that encourage redundant steps. Our binary classification approach—determining whether each step is necessary rather than estimating continuous credit scores—compresses the infinite solution space into a verifiable subspace, making credit assignment tractable even in such challenging scenarios. This explains why the performance gap between our method and baselines is particularly evident on WebShop.

## 5.4 Compatibility of the Evolution Module

Figure 3 demonstrates that our trajectory evolution module serves as a general enhancement applicable to different RL baselines. On both WebShop and ALFWorld, integrating the evolution module yields consistent improvements for both GRPO and GiGPO. Notably, the gain is more substantial when combined with GRPO, which lacks fine-grained credit assignment, and the evolved trajectories compensate for this limitation by directly providing refined training samples. For GiGPO, which already performs step-level credit assignment, the evolution module still brings complementary benefits, indicating that actively constructing higher-quality trajectories offers advantages beyond what credit assignment alone can achieve. The consistent patterns across both benchmarks validate the generalizability of our approach.

## 5.5 Trajectory Redundancy Analysis

Existing RL methods typically rely solely on sparse final rewards, which update all steps indiscriminately and may inadvertently encourage redundant actions. To investigate whether our method produces more efficient behaviors, we conduct trajectory length analysis in Table 2. Since average trajectory length is confounded by success rate, as failed trajectories may be either prolonged by repeated invalid attempts or cut short by premature termination, we additionally report “Succ. Steps”, measuring the mean length of successful trajectories only, to enable fair comparison of execution efficiency.
As shown in Table 2, our method achieves the shortest trajectories on both metrics across all benchmarks. This demonstrates that our binary credit assignment effectively identifies and eliminates redundant steps, enabling the model to complete tasks with more concise action sequences.

## 5.6 Ablation Study

To investigate the individual contribution of each component in our framework, we conduct ablation experiments by systematically removing the step-level credit assignment (w/o Step Credit) and the trajectory evolution module (w/o Evolution). As shown in Figure 4, both components are essential for achieving optimal performance, with their combination yielding the best results across both benchmarks.
Notably, removing the evolution module causes a larger performance drop compared to removing step credit, indicating that actively constructing higher-quality trajectories provides more substantial benefits than refining credit assignment alone. Furthermore, the gains from both components are largely additive, confirming that trajectory-level optimization and step-level credit assignment address complementary aspects of the challenge.

## 6 Related Work

## Reinforcement Learning for LLMs.

Reinforcement learning (RL) (Rafailov et al., 2023; Song et al., 2024) has significantly advanced the reasoning capabilities of LLMs. As a representative RL method, PPO (Schulman et al., 2017) achieves desirable performance by enabling dynamic policy updates via environment feedback, yet requires additional critic models for advantage estimation, increasing computational overhead and training complexity. To address this, group-based methods including GRPO (Shao et al., 2024), Dr.GRPO (Liu et al., 2025b), DAPO (Yu et al., 2025), and GSPO (Zheng et al., 2025) estimate advantages from response batches sampled under the same prompt, eliminating critic dependency while maintaining efficiency. Recent work (Yue et al., 2025a) argues that RL primarily improves the sampling efficiency of reasoning paths already within the base model’s repertoire, rather than eliciting fundamentally new ones. We take this observation as a starting point rather than a challenge: if the bottleneck of long-horizon RL is sampling efficiency over composed trajectories, then explicitly constructing high-quality compositions from existing rollouts is a principled response. TrajEvolve operationalizes this view: every action in an evolved trajectory is drawn from the base policy’s own outputs, but the composition is one that on-policy sampling would require exponentially many rollouts to discover (Theorem 4.5).

## Credit Assignment in RL.

Despite the success of group-based RL methods (Ahmadian et al., 2024), they rely solely on outcome rewards without evaluating intermediate steps, motivating credit assignment research. Existing methods fall into two categories: i) Heuristic signals (Wang et al., 2025c; Zhang et al., 2025b; Wang et al., 2025a; Harutyunyan et al., 2019) use LLM confidence or meta-reasoning formats, risking reinforcement of confident but incorrect outputs; ii) Outcome-derived signals (Kazemnejad et al., ; Ji et al., 2025; Guo et al., 2025b; Feng et al., 2025) estimate step values via Monte Carlo rollouts but only identify success-leading steps, failing to detect redundant ones.

## 7 Conclusion

We presented TrajEvolve, a step-level credit assignment framework for long-horizon agent tasks. By reformulating credit assignment as binary necessity classification, TrajEvolve enables reliable label acquisition through hindsight verification and trains a discriminator to guide both policy optimization and trajectory evolution. Experiments on ALFWorld and WebShop demonstrate that TrajEvolve consistently outperforms existing RL methods. Future work includes extending our approach to more complex environments and exploring the integration of step-level credit with traditional search question-answer scenarios.

Lemma A.1 (Sampling Barrier).

For any policy $\pi_{\theta}$ satisfying $P_{\pi_{\theta}}(r_{t}=1\mid s_{t})\geq p$ at a $\beta$ -fraction of the states along every rollout, the probability that a trajectory of length $T$ is clean satisfies:

 $P_{\pi_{\theta}}(\tau_{\text{clean}})\leq(1-p)^{\beta T}.$ 

(15)

In a group of $N$ independent rollouts, the number of clean trajectories $m_{\textnormal{clean}}$ satisfies $E[m_{\textnormal{clean}}]\leq N(1-p)^{\beta T}$ , $P(m_{\textnormal{clean}}\geq 1)\leq N(1-p)^{\beta T}$ , and $P(m_{\textnormal{clean}}=N)\leq(1-p)^{\beta TN}$ .

Proof.

A clean trajectory requires, in particular, each of the (at least) $\beta T$ redundancy-prone steps to be non-redundant. Conditional on the past, each such step is non-redundant with probability at most $1-p$ , so by the chain rule $P(\text{clean})\leq(1-p)^{\beta T}$ . Linearity of expectation gives the bound on $E[m_{\textnormal{clean}}]$ , Markov’s inequality gives $P(m_{\textnormal{clean}}\geq 1)\leq E[m_{\textnormal{clean}}]$ , and independence across rollouts gives the bound on $P(m_{\textnormal{clean}}=N)$ .

On ALFWorld, TrajEvolve attains an overall success rate of 92.19%, surpassing the strongest baseline RLOO at 81.25% by a margin of 10.94%. Overall, these results confirm that TrajEvolve generalizes beyond the Qwen2.5 family and delivers consistent gains in both task success and trajectory efficiency when applied to Llama-3.2-3B-Instruct.

## C.2 Discriminator Accuracy Experiment

A natural question is whether the discriminator can reliably identify necessary and redundant steps. To address this concern, we track the discriminator’s classification accuracy throughout training on both benchmarks.

For successful trajectories, ground-truth credit labels are readily available through hindsight verification in the environment, so we directly compute accuracy against these labels. For failed trajectories, however, no ground-truth labels can be obtained since counterfactual verification requires a successful trajectory as input. We therefore employ GPT-5.2-2025-12-11 as an approximate judge to evaluate the discriminator’s predictions on failed trajectories. To reduce computational cost, we report the accuracy over the first 50 training iterations.

As shown in Figure 7, the discriminator achieves strong overall accuracy on both benchmarks, reaching 90.93% on ALFWorld and 83.17% on WebShop. The accuracy on successful trajectories is notably higher, at 95.06% and 87.59% respectively, while the accuracy on failed trajectories is lower, at 84.95% and 77.26%. The training curves in Figure 7 further show that the discriminator maintains stable and consistent accuracy throughout training, with no significant degradation as the policy evolves.

We emphasize that the discriminator’s role in our framework is deliberately limited. For successful trajectories, credit assignment is performed entirely through hindsight counterfactual verification, which yields exact ground-truth labels without relying on the discriminator at all. The discriminator only serves to generalize credit signals from successful trajectories to failed ones and to guide the trajectory evolution module. This design choice explains why even a lightweight 1.5B-parameter discriminator is sufficient to achieve strong overall performance. Furthermore, we note that credit assignment for failed trajectories is an inherently difficult problem, and the GPT-5.2 judge itself may introduce evaluation noise. Therefore, the reported accuracy on failed trajectories should be interpreted as an approximate reference rather than a precise measurement. Nevertheless, the results confirm that the discriminator provides sufficiently reliable signals for trajectory evolution, and our framework’s core performance is not bottlenecked by discriminator quality.

## C.3 Computation Cost Analysis

To assess the practical overhead introduced by TrajEvolve, we measure the per-step time cost of each training stage on both benchmarks. Understanding the computational budget is essential for evaluating whether our method can scale to larger settings without prohibitive overhead.

We profile all training stages on the same hardware configuration of 8 $\times$ H20 GPUs with Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct as the policy, and report the average per-step wall-clock time in seconds. The results are shown in Figure 8. Beyond the standard RL training pipeline that includes rollout, reference log-probability computation, and policy update, our method introduces two additional modules: hindsight verification and trajectory evolution. Note that in this setting the discriminator has the same scale as the policy, so the relative share of the discriminator-related stages reported here is an upper estimate; with a larger policy, this share shrinks accordingly.

As shown in Figure 8, the rollout stage dominates the total training time on both benchmarks, taking 117.4s on ALFWorld and 63.2s on WebShop per step. In comparison, the overhead introduced by our two additional modules is moderate. Hindsight verification costs 61.2s and 20.3s per step on ALFWorld and WebShop respectively. Although the number of verification calls scales linearly with trajectory length, each call only replays the recorded actions in the environment and checks whether the task still succeeds, without requiring any model inference. The entire process is therefore purely CPU-based and consumes no GPU resources. Moreover, the verification calls are independent of each other, so this stage is fully parallelizable across trajectories and across calls, and its wall-clock time scales down directly with the number of available CPU cores. Since our profiling uses a modest CPU budget, this overhead still has considerable room for reduction.

Trajectory evolution costs 31.6s and 27.8s per step on ALFWorld and WebShop respectively. This stage is an intrinsic, periodic cost of our method rather than a per-step tax: evolution is triggered every 5 iterations with only two trajectories selected per group, so over a 150-iteration run it adds the equivalent of roughly 30 additional steps of work in total, and in some iterations there may not be enough successful trajectories available for evolution. The evolution process is essentially equivalent to re-executing these refined trajectories in the environment, which is comparable to a small fraction of the standard rollout cost. We consider this cost well justified, as the evolved trajectories expose the policy to high-quality compositions outside its current sampling distribution, an effect that on-policy training alone cannot reproduce within a practical budget (Theorem 4.5) and that therefore cannot be substituted by simply allocating additional training steps.

The remaining components, discriminator training and credit labeling, introduce overhead of 18.5s and 27.2s on ALFWorld and 13.3s and 13.1s on WebShop, as the discriminator is a lightweight 1.5B-parameter model trained with LoRA for only 1 epoch per iteration. We emphasize that our method does not critically depend on a strong discriminator: credit labels for successful trajectories come entirely from counterfactual verification in the environment, which yields exact ground-truth labels without any learned estimator and thus provides abundant training signal regardless of discriminator quality.
The discriminator can therefore be replaced with an even smaller backbone (e.g., Qwen2.5-0.5B-Instruct) to further reduce this portion of the overhead.

Overall, the total per-step time of TrajEvolve is approximately 1.5 $\times$ that of standard GRPO training. A substantial part of this overhead is reducible through more CPU cores and a smaller discriminator, while the remaining periodic cost of evolution is intrinsic to the method. Given the substantial performance gains demonstrated in Section 5, we consider this overhead to be a favorable trade-off between computational cost and agent performance.

## C.4 TrajEvolve on Complex Scenarios

To evaluate whether TrajEvolve can generalize to more complex environments beyond ALFWorld and WebShop, we conduct additional experiments on ScienceWorld, a challenging text-based simulation environment that requires agents to perform scientific experiments through multi-step interactions. Unlike ALFWorld and WebShop where most steps directly alter the environment state, ScienceWorld involves a substantial proportion of information-gathering steps such as looking at objects, reading instructions, and using thermometers to take measurements. These steps do not change the environment or affect task completion, yet they may be essential for the agent to make informed decisions in subsequent steps.

The primary challenge in transferring TrajEvolve to this setting lies in determining the dependent steps during hindsight verification. Since information-gathering steps have no observable effect on the environment state or task outcome, removing them does not cause subsequent actions to become infeasible, nor does it directly lead to task failure. Therefore, the standard counterfactual verification procedure cannot determine whether these steps are truly necessary. To address this, we adopt a simple two-category strategy. We classify all steps into information-gathering steps and environment-changing steps based on the action verb, which provides a reliable and low-cost distinction. We retain all information-gathering steps unconditionally and apply hindsight verification only to environment-changing steps. Under this setting, our method can still effectively identify and eliminate redundant environment-changing actions, although some unnecessary information-gathering steps may be preserved. This simple adaptation allows TrajEvolve to transfer to complex scenarios with minimal modification.

## Appendix D Method Analysis

## D.1 Method Transferability Analysis

We discuss the applicability of TrajEvolve to different task settings and clarify the conditions under which our method is most effective.

The core requirement of TrajEvolve is that the environment supports trajectory replay, meaning that a sequence of actions can be deterministically re-executed to reproduce the same state transitions. This property is widely satisfied across many practical domains. Task execution environments such as ALFWorld and WebShop are inherently replayable since the environment state is fully determined by the action history. Code generation and debugging scenarios also naturally support replay, as programs can be re-executed with the same inputs to verify correctness. Web search and retrieval tasks similarly allow re-issuing queries and re-processing results in a deterministic manner. Despite this broad applicability, existing RL methods for LLM agents have largely overlooked the replay property of these environments. TrajEvolve explicitly leverages this property to construct higher-quality trajectories through hindsight verification and trajectory evolution, providing additional training signals that go beyond what on-policy sampling alone can offer.

Our method is particularly advantageous in environments where environment-changing actions constitute a large proportion of the trajectory. For such actions, counterfactual verification can directly determine whether each step is genuinely necessary for task completion by observing changes in the environment state and task outcome. This yields ground-truth credit labels without relying on any learned estimator, which is a unique strength of our approach. The strong experimental results on ALFWorld, where most steps involve physical state changes, exemplify this advantage.

However, this does not mean that TrajEvolve lacks transferability to more complex scenarios involving substantial information-gathering steps. As demonstrated in Section C.4, a simple two-category adaptation that retains information-gathering steps and applies verification only to environment-changing steps allows our method to achieve consistent improvements on ScienceWorld. Although this adaptation is conservative in that it preserves some potentially redundant information-gathering steps, it maintains the core benefits of trajectory evolution while requiring minimal modification to the framework. Exploring more fine-grained verification strategies for information-gathering steps remains a promising direction for future work.

## D.2 Off-Policy Analysis

Figure 9: Mean KL divergence (left) and clip fraction (right) during training on ALFWorld and WebShop, indicating stable policy updates.

Figure 10: Mean KL divergence (left) and clip fraction (right) during the trajectory evolution stage on ALFWorld and WebShop.

Since evolved trajectories are generated by refining historical rollouts rather than sampled from the current policy, they are inherently off-policy. A natural concern is whether incorporating such trajectories into PPO-style optimization introduces excessive distributional shift that could destabilize training. To investigate this, we monitor two standard trust-region diagnostics throughout training on both benchmarks.

The first metric is the approximate KL divergence between the updated policy and the reference policy, which measures the magnitude of per-update policy shift. The second metric is the clip fraction, which measures the proportion of tokens whose importance sampling ratio falls outside the clipping range $[1-\epsilon,1+\epsilon]$ . A high clip fraction indicates that many tokens experience large probability changes, suggesting significant distributional mismatch. We note that before each evolution update, we recompute log-probabilities under the current policy as the baseline for the importance sampling ratio. This ensures that the clipping mechanism correctly constrains the policy shift regardless of how the trajectories were originally generated.

Figure 9 presents the two metrics during the standard training phase across 150 iterations. On both benchmarks, the KL divergence drops rapidly within the first 20 iterations and stabilizes at very low values below 0.005 for the remainder of training. The clip fraction on ALFWorld remains consistently low throughout, while on WebShop it stabilizes around 0.01 after an initial transient. These patterns indicate that the overall training process maintains stable policy updates even with the periodic injection of evolved trajectories.

Figure 10 focuses specifically on the trajectory evolution phases, showing the two metrics across the 30 evolution steps that occur once every 5 training iterations. Both KL divergence and clip fraction exhibit a brief peak during the early evolution steps when the policy is changing most rapidly, and then steadily decrease over subsequent phases. By the later stages, both metrics converge below 0.005 and 0.01 respectively, reaching levels comparable to standard on-policy PPO updates. The average KL divergence across all evolution phases is 0.0054 on ALFWorld and 0.0075 on WebShop, while the average clip fraction is 0.0082 and 0.0115 respectively. These results confirm that the distributional shift introduced by trajectory evolution is bounded and small, and that the shift diminishes as training progresses since evolved trajectories become increasingly aligned with the current policy.

We note that leveraging off-policy successful trajectories has emerged as a promising direction in recent RL research. For instance, ExGRPO [Zhan et al., 2025] replays successful rollouts for additional training updates. Our empirical analysis provides evidence that such off-policy utilization can be safely combined with PPO-style optimization through proper importance sampling and clipping. Exploring more efficient use of off-policy data in long-horizon agent training remains a valuable direction for future work.

Table 5 illustrates how step-level credit labels are
obtained for a successful ALFWorld trajectory via counterfactual verification.
For each step $t$ , we remove it from the trajectory, replay the remaining actions
in the environment, and label it Necessary if the task can no longer be completed
and Redundant otherwise. The same replay also yields $\mathrm{Dep}(t)$ , the set of
subsequent steps whose actions become inexecutable once step $t$ is removed.
Three observations are worth highlighting:
(i) The agent first explores two unrelated drawers (steps 0–3). Removing step 0
or step 2 leaves the task completable, so both are redundant; their dependents,
the corresponding open actions (steps 1 and 3), are thereby marked
redundant without additional replays. Hence four perfectly executable actions
receive no credit, a distinction that the outcome reward alone cannot make.
(ii) Removing any of the remaining six steps causes the task to fail, so all of
them are necessary and together form a compact success chain
(locate $\to$ grasp $\to$ clean $\to$ deliver).
(iii) $\mathrm{Dep}(t)$ exposes a dependency structure aligned with the sub-goals of
the task: removing the visit to the countertop (step 4) invalidates taking the
knife (step 5), cleaning it (step 7), and the final placement (step 9), whereas the
sink-basin visit (step 6) only enables the cleaning step. Notably, steps 7 and 9
have empty dependency sets yet are still necessary: removing them leaves every
later action executable but the goal unsatisfied. Environment-level success
verification and action-level validity checks are therefore complementary.
The resulting labels are used directly for credit assignment on successful
trajectories, and serve as supervision for the step discriminator, which is
applied to failed trajectories, where counterfactual verification is not
applicable, to locate the first erroneous step.

## Appendix F Extended Related Work

RL fine-tuning of large language models [Zhang et al., 2025b, Lu et al., 2025, Guo et al., 2025b, Wang et al., 2025d, Liu et al., 2025a, Zhang et al., 2025a, Yin et al., 2025, Shen et al., 2025, Tan et al., 2026, Liu et al., 2026, Wu et al., 2026, Zhang et al., 2026, Xu et al., 2025] was popularized by RLHF [Cui et al., 2025, Cheng et al., 2025, Guo et al., 2025b, Wang et al., 2025e, Harutyunyan et al., 2019] with PPO [Ouyang et al., 2022, Bai et al., 2022], where on-policy actor-critic updates are used to optimize against a learned reward model. To remove the value network and reduce the memory and compute footprint, GRPO [Shao et al., 2024] estimates advantages from groups of responses sharing the same prompt, and has since become the de facto recipe behind reasoning-oriented LLMs such as DeepSeek-R1 [Guo et al., 2025a], Qwen2.5-Math [Yang et al., 2024], and Kimi-K1.5 [Team et al., 2025]. A growing line of work refines GRPO by addressing its bias and stability issues: Dr. GRPO removes length and difficulty biases in advantage normalization [Liu et al., 2025b], DAPO introduces decoupled clipping and dynamic sampling for long-CoT training [Yu et al., 2025], GSPO performs sequence-level importance weighting to reduce variance [Zheng et al., 2025], and VinePPO and VAPO revisit token-level credit assignment with Monte-Carlo value estimates [Kazemnejad et al., , Yue et al., 2025b]. RLOO and ReMax similarly exploit multi-sample baselines to obtain low-variance, value-network-free updates [Ahmadian et al., 2024, Li et al., 2023]. Because each rollout from an LLM is expensive, recent work increasingly emphasizes reusing off-policy experience, which can be viewed as the LLM analogue of experience replay. ReST, ReST-EM, and RAFT iteratively collect, filter, and replay self-generated trajectories for reward-weighted updates [Gulcehre et al., 2023, Singh et al., 2023, Dong et al., 2023, Qu et al., 2025, Yuan et al., 2024a], while Self-Rewarding LMs and SPIN bootstrap a replay-style buffer of model completions for iterative DPO-style training [Yuan et al., 2024b, Chen et al., 2024]. Several works push GRPO itself toward the off-policy regime: Tapered Off-Policy REINFORCE and LUFFY incorporate stale or expert trajectories with importance correction and clipping [Roux et al., 2025, Yan et al., 2025], and asynchronous trajectory-balance training decouples data collection from policy updates to better utilize a replay buffer [Bartoldson et al., 2025]. In multi-turn agentic RL [Hu et al., 2025, Ji et al., 2025, Lin et al., 2025, Wang et al., 2025a, Wu et al., 2025, Yuan et al., 2025, Wang et al., 2025b], replay buffers of past trajectories have been shown to stabilize long-horizon credit assignment in environments such as ALFWorld and WebShop [Zhou et al., 2024, Song et al., 2024, Xi et al., 2025]. These developments collectively position experience replay as a key lever for sample-efficient and scalable RL post-training of LLMs.

To improve sample efficiency in online RL, replay-based methods [Zhan et al., 2025, Lu et al., 2025, Mroueh et al., 2025] store and reuse historical rollouts from replay buffers, reducing redundant sampling. These approaches typically prioritize valuable experiences based on correctness or entropy.

## Appendix G Prompts

We adopt the same prompt design as GiGPO [Feng et al., 2025] for both ALFWorld and WebShop agents.
As shown in Figure 11 and Figure 12, each prompt provides the agent with the task description, recent interaction history, current observation, and the set of admissible actions.
The agent is instructed to first produce step-by-step reasoning within <think> </think> tags, followed by the selected action within <action> </action> tags.
We set the history length to 5 for both environments.

In addition to the standard prompts, we introduce think-only prompt templates (Figure 13 and Figure 14) specifically for the trajectory evolution phase.
During evolution, certain steps are assigned predetermined actions that are either retained from the original trajectory or corrected via format mapping, and the model only needs to generate the corresponding reasoning.
The think-only prompt replaces the original action instruction block with the predetermined action and asks the model to produce only the reasoning content, without outputting any action tags.
This ensures that evolved trajectories maintain coherent thought processes for every step, even when the actions are externally specified rather than generated by the policy.

Abstract

How learning progresses over time and varies across individuals is a longstanding question in cognitive and behavioral science. Behavioral changes are observable outcomes of learning and have been a long-established bedrock for understanding learning progress. However, a major challenge is identifying observable behavioral measures that faithfully capture learning progression. Existing approaches commonly rely on predefined behavioral features or task-level performance, while fine-scale posture and movement in freely behaving animals remain less explored. In this paper, we ask: can short-term movement trajectories reveal learning progress and individual identity? We introduce a self-supervised representation learning framework built on a simple hypothesis: behavioral characteristics associated with identity and learning remain relatively stable over nearby time intervals. Using temporal proximity as supervision, our framework learns compact representations directly from movement trajectories without manually defining behavioral states or kinematic features. Using behavior data from freely behaving rats, we show that the learned representations not only improve prediction of identity and learning-related readouts over existing methods, but also provide a tool for investigating broader scientific questions about when and how these signals appear in behavior. Specifically, trajectories as short as one second contain informative learning and identity signals. Egocentric pose dynamics are more predictive of identity, whereas allocentric motion is more predictive of learning-related variation, and both signals are strongest at decision junctions in the alternation cohort. We further use a representation-conditioned diffusion model to inspect movement patterns associated with identity and learning progress. Our findings establish short-term movement as an informative behavioral “fingerprint” for studying learning and individual differences.
Our code is available at github.com/pizhn/TRAIL.

## 1 Introduction

A longstanding question in cognitive science is how learning progresses over time. We can study this progression by characterizing changes in brain networks and behavior. While characterizing neural changes requires recording brain activity, behavioral changes provide a directly observable window into the learning process. A major challenge, however, is identifying which aspects of behavior show learning-related changes and how they evolve. Standard approaches rely on predefined behavioral features, such as speed, latency, or the frequency of a target behavior, as well as task-level performance measures, such as success rate. These measures are interpretable and readily quantifiable, but often summarize behavior as coarse scalar changes over extended periods (Smith et al., 2004). In contrast, fine-scale changes in limb posture and movement can provide a wealth of information about learning progression, as demonstrated in studies of motor learning (Kawai et al., 2015; Bova et al., 2021; Dhawale et al., 2019; Nguyen et al., 2021). Yet, in unconstrained settings, where animals learn to navigate freely in space (Rosenberg et al., 2021), the complexity of movement patterns makes it difficult to determine which behavioral features to measure and how they can be used to understand changes in the underlying cognitive processes. Addressing this challenge could reveal the hidden structures and dynamics of learning.

In addition to behavioral changes over time, inter-individual differences in movement have been reported across species (Godesberg et al., 2024; Chao et al., 2019). Such movement “fingerprints” are increasingly being linked to biologically important variation in genetic and cognitive traits (Levy et al., 2023; Forkosh et al., 2019; Freund et al., 2013; von Ziegler et al., 2024; Sheppard et al., 2022; Ding et al., 2026). If successfully applied to learning-related movement analysis, these fingerprints could add a new dimension to understanding individual learning dynamics. However, current methods vary widely in their performance and the observation periods required to identify individuals, ranging from tens of seconds to minutes (Levy et al., 2023; Forkosh et al., 2019; von Ziegler et al., 2024).

These limitations raise a fundamental question: can short-term movement trajectories reveal learning progress and individual identity? We focus on body posture and movement patterns unfolding over only a few seconds—a timescale that is often insufficient to assess task-level performance. Nevertheless, how animals move, coordinate their body parts, or respond to their surroundings within these short intervals may already provide informative signals (Weinreb et al., 2026). We investigate whether a representation learned directly from movement trajectories can capture these signals without manually defining behavioral states or kinematic features.

To this end, we study movement data from rat spatial learning experiments described in (Shridhar et al., 2026). We first evaluate existing approaches for representing movement trajectories, spanning traditional state-based methods (Durbin & Koopman, 2012; Wiltschko et al., 2015; Paraschos et al., 2013; Weinreb et al., 2024), recent machine learning methods (Hinton & Salakhutdinov, 2006; Luxem et al., 2022; Grill et al., 2020; Bai et al., 2018; Azabou et al., 2023; Stoffl et al., 2025), and time-series foundation models (Google Research, 2026; Ansari et al., 2025). Across identity- and learning-related evaluations such as task performance, these approaches have limited predictive performance.

We therefore develop a representation learning framework, TRAIL (TRAjectory-Identity Latent), tailored to capturing information about individual differences and learning progress from short segments of movement data, as illustrated in figure 1. Our framework combines a trajectory encoder with a self-supervised objective. Inspired by Wayformer (Nayakanti et al., 2022), an architecture developed for short-term motion forecasting in autonomous driving, the encoder maps short movement trajectories into compact latent representations. Our training objective builds on a simple hypothesis: although an animal’s movements may vary rapidly, the underlying behavioral characteristics associated with identity and learning are more stable over nearby time intervals. We therefore use temporal proximity as a self-supervisory signal, encouraging representations of nearby trajectory segments to be similar while contrasting them with segments from other recordings or sufficiently distant times within the same recording. This objective enables representation learning without identity or learning-progress labels during training.

We evaluate our framework on the spatial learning data (Shridhar et al., 2026) and find clear improvements over existing methods. Linear probes trained on the learned representations predict learning-related readouts more accurately, demonstrating that such information is accessible from short movement trajectories. Interestingly, the representations also support accurate identity prediction, even though segments from different recordings of the same individual can be contrasted during training. Beyond these learning-focused datasets, our method remains competitive on the established behavioral benchmark MABe22 (Sun et al., 2023), suggesting broader applicability to representation learning from short movement sequences.

The superior performance of our framework not only provides an affirmative answer to our fundamental question, but also offers a tool for investigating broader scientific questions: At what timescale does behavior vary in relation to learning? How long must a trajectory be to capture identity- and learning-related signals, and under what conditions are these signals strongest? Which movement patterns reveal individual identity and learning progress?

First, our temporal-window analysis favors local temporal neighborhoods: expanding the positive-pair window beyond the default 10% of a recording partition reduces prediction of learning-related readouts. Second, trajectories as short as one second already contain information predictive of both identity and learning-related readouts. In the alternation cohort, both signals are stronger at locations where animals must choose a direction than along straight paths. Third, local pose dynamics are more predictive of identity, whereas global motion is more predictive of learning-related readouts. Together, these findings identify temporal, spatial, and postural factors that can guide more targeted hypotheses about behavior and learning. Finally, we use a representation-conditioned diffusion model (Peebles & Xie, 2023) to explore the movement patterns associated with individual identity and learning progress. By varying the conditioning representation, we generate trajectories that preserve broad motion structure while exhibiting subtle changes in stopping, accelerating, and stretching. These qualitative results make behavioral differences associated with the learned representations directly inspectable.

Taken together, our study shows that short movement trajectories contain observable signatures of both individual identity and learning progress. By combining self-supervised representation learning with controlled analyses and conditional generation, we provide a framework for predicting these properties and examining the movement patterns associated with them.

## 2 Related Work and Background

Behavioral tasks & datasets. CalMS21 (Sun et al., 2021) and MABe22 (Sun et al., 2023) support behavioral recognition and representation learning across diverse settings, while longitudinal resources such as CRCNS hc-28 (Jadhav & Frank, 2020) track rats learning rewarded alternation across days. In this paper, we use data from two spatial navigation tasks for rats (Shridhar et al., 2026). The alternation and probability reward rules define learning objectives with quantifiable performance metrics over days. Unlike spontaneous behavior, this design allows us to relate locomotion and posture features to learning progress and experience in the environment. Longitudinal recordings of the same animals over consecutive days enable analysis of individual learning trajectories, with body keypoint coordinates available for both conditions. The two reward rules provide complementary evaluation settings, including behavior under reward uncertainty.

Algorithms. Reconstruction, prediction, and representation alignment provide approaches to learning behavioral structure without labels. VAME (Luxem et al., 2022) combines reconstruction with future prediction, while BAMS (Azabou et al., 2023) and hBehaveMAE (Stoffl et al., 2025) capture behavioral structure at multiple temporal scales. Temporal Neighborhood Coding uses local stationarity to define temporal neighborhoods and learns to distinguish neighboring from non-neighboring segments with a debiased contrastive objective (Tonekaboni et al., 2021). CEBRA supports both label-free temporal sampling and sampling guided by auxiliary variables (Schneider et al., 2023). Building on temporal supervision, our approach combines contrastive learning with reconstruction to learn a compact representation from short trajectory windows. This representation supports analysis of movement changes during learning and differences across individuals.

## 3 Proposed Representation Learning Framework: TRAIL

We learn compact representations of short movements without predefined behavioral categories or hand-crafted features, and evaluate their ability to capture identity and learning-related variation.

## 3.1 Overview

Data format. We use data recorded from each animal over multiple days, with multiple sessions per day (Shridhar et al., 2026). Each trajectory segment $x_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{T\times K\times 2}$ contains $T$ frames of $K$ keypoints and is associated with animal $r_{i}$ , session $s_{i}$ , start time $t_{i}$ , and within-animal recording day $d_{i}$ .
We consider three views of movement: local pose, global trajectory, and full movement (section C.2).
We denote the selected view by $v_{i}=V(x_{i})$ and optional environmental geometry by $c_{i}$ .

Figure 2: TRAIL high-level architecture.

Design principles. To capture identity-specific patterns and learning-related changes from short-term movements, we consider three design choices: (1) multi-frequency encoding to preserve fine-grained movement details (Mildenhall et al., 2022), (2) a compact latent space learned through contrastive learning, based on the hypothesis that nearby trajectory segments share similar underlying behavioral states, and (3) an auxiliary reconstruction objective. Together, these choices encourage TRAIL to retain informative movement details and learn behavioral relationships without predefined categories or explicit labels.

High-level architecture. Figure 2 shows the encoder-decoder architecture of TRAIL. Inspired by Wayformer (Nayakanti et al., 2022), the encoder uses learnable queries to summarize multi-frequency embeddings of movement trajectories and environmental context (e.g., maze layout). The first output token is compressed into a behavioral representation trained through contrastive learning, and then projected back to the original feature dimension and combined with the remaining tokens for trajectory reconstruction. Reconstruction encourages the model to retain fine-grained movement details. We detail each component below.

## 3.2 Model Architecture and Learning Objectives

Multi-frequency trajectory embedding. We take keypoint trajectories $\mathbf{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{T\times K\times 2}$ and maze coordinates $\mathbf{M}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times 2}$ as input; the maze provides global context for the trajectory. Inspired by Wayformer (Nayakanti et al., 2022), we anchor each trajectory to its final frame by centering the body point and vertically aligning the body-neck vector in that frame. We then apply the same transformation to all trajectory frames and maze coordinates. Each two-dimensional coordinate receives NeRF-style multi-frequency encoding with $L=10$ frequencies (Mildenhall et al., 2022) and a linear projection to 64 dimensions.
To identify each token’s corresponding keypoint and time frame, we introduce two learnable positional embeddings: a keypoint embedding shared across frames and a temporal embedding shared across keypoints within the same frame.
Maze coordinates undergo the same encoding procedure, with a learnable additive embedding distinguishing them from trajectory tokens.
Together, the $K\times T$ trajectory tokens and $N$ maze tokens form $\mathbf{Z}\in\mathbb{R}^{(KT+N)\times 64}$ .

Compact behavioral representation. We adopt a four-layer Q-Former (Li et al., 2023) as the encoder, which takes eight learned queries to attend to $\mathbf{Z}$ , yielding $\mathbf{Q}=[\mathbf{q}_{0},\ldots,\mathbf{q}_{7}]\in\mathbb{R}^{8\times 64}$ . We then linearly project $\mathbf{q}_{0}$ to produce the 16-dimensional latent behavior representation $\mathbf{h}$ .
To learn the representations, we hypothesize that nearby trajectory segments share similar underlying behavioral states.
We therefore use a balanced InfoNCE loss (Oord et al., 2018). Positive pairs consist of segments from the same session within a certain time interval; negatives are drawn from temporally distant segments or different sessions:

 $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{contrast}}=-\frac{1}{|\mathcal{P}|}\sum_{\mathbf{h}^{+}\in\mathcal{P}}\log\frac{|\mathcal{P}|^{-1}\exp\!\big(\ell(\mathbf{h},\mathbf{h}^{+})\big)}{|\mathcal{P}|^{-1}\sum_{\mathbf{h}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{P}}\exp\!\big(\ell(\mathbf{h},\mathbf{h}^{\prime})\big)+|\mathcal{N}|^{-1}\sum_{\mathbf{h}^{-}\in\mathcal{N}}\exp\!\big(\ell(\mathbf{h},\mathbf{h}^{-})\big)}.$ 

(1)

where $\mathcal{P}$ and $\mathcal{N}$ denote the positive and negative sets for an anchor representation $\mathbf{h}$ , and $\ell(\mathbf{h},\mathbf{h}^{\prime})=s(\mathbf{h},\mathbf{h}^{\prime})/\tau$ is cosine similarity scaled by temperature $\tau=0.1$ . The compressed representation $\mathbf{h}$ is then projected back to 64 dimensions and concatenated with the remaining tokens $q_{1:7}$ to form the decoder input for trajectory reconstruction.

Decoder for reconstruction. The decoder follows the same Q-Former architecture as the encoder, with separate parameters and learnable queries that attend to the encoder output. An MLP head maps the first query representation to the parameters of a Gaussian distribution over the trajectory, which we train using a negative log-likelihood reconstruction loss $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{recon}}$ .

Overall objective. The final objective weights contrastive learning and reconstruction equally:

 $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{total}}=\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{contrast}}+\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{recon}}.$

## 4 Experiments on Representation Learning

Our experiments proceed from prediction to scientific analysis and interpretation. We first compare frozen representations on identity and learning-related readouts in the two Y-maze Spatial learning cohorts, then evaluate their applicability on MABe22 Mouse Triplets. We next examine temporal settings, separate local pose from global movement, visualize representation structure, and compare signals across maze regions, recording days, and movement motifs. Finally, we use conditional generation to inspect movement changes associated with progression across days and variation between individuals. The appendix provides implementation details, ablations, robustness and generalization tests, and additional visualizations.

Dataset description. We use data from two spatial-learning cohorts recorded in a Y-shaped maze (Shridhar et al., 2026): six rats learning an alternation rule that rewards goal visits in a prescribed sequence, and eight different rats learning a probabilistic rule with goal-specific reward probabilities. The maze consists of three goal platforms connected to a central junction by arms. We represent its geometry using $25$ waypoints.
Recordings span five consecutive days with 3–4 approximately ten-minute sessions per day at 11 Hz, totaling 275 sessions and 44.92 hours. We consider the nose, neck, and body keypoints, which are the most reliably tracked using DeepLabCut (Mathis et al., 2018).
We split each session chronologically into 80% training, 5% validation, and 15% testing, and then aggregate them across sessions to form the training, validation, and test sets (section A.1).

Evaluation metrics. We consider five evaluation targets: Identity, Day, trial-level Proficiency, and Success Rate and Reward over one-minute intervals. Binary reward outcomes are recorded for each trial that spans departure from one goal platform to arrival at the next; we then obtain the ground-truth proficiency using the state-space model of Smith et al. (2004).

Unless stated otherwise, we freeze the encoder and train a separate linear probe for each target. For the spatial-learning cohorts, we use one pooled logistic-regression probe for identity and a separate ridge-regression probe per animal for each continuous target. The MABe22 benchmark uses its own evaluation protocol (section B.3). Probes are trained on the training trajectories and evaluated on the test trajectories. We report classification accuracy and mean squared error (MSE) for the regression tasks.

Baselines. We compare three baseline groups. Classic methods include linear probing on raw trajectories, Linear-Gaussian State-space Model (Durbin & Koopman, 2012), Autoregressive Hidden Markov Model (Wiltschko et al., 2015), Probabilistic Movement Primitives (Paraschos et al., 2013), and Keypoint-MoSeq (Weinreb et al., 2024). Deep Learning methods include AutoEncoder (Hinton & Salakhutdinov, 2006), VAME (Luxem et al., 2022), Temporal ConvNet (Bai et al., 2018), temporal BYOL (Grill et al., 2020), BAMS (Azabou et al., 2023), and hBehaveMAE (Stoffl et al., 2025). We also evaluate time-series Foundation Models, Chronos-2 (Ansari et al., 2025) and TimesFM-3 (Das et al., 2024; Google Research, 2026), frozen or with LoRA fine-tuning (Hu et al., 2022).

We also consider a Supervised baseline that uses the same trajectory embedding and encoder architecture as TRAIL but is trained from scratch jointly on all five targets (Identity, Day, Proficiency, Success Rate, and Reward), using cross-entropy for classification and MSE for regression.

Implementation details. For the short-context Y-maze comparison, all methods use 3-second trajectories (i.e., $T=3\times 11$ ), with BAMS restricted to its short-term branch. For each method, including TRAIL, we evaluate tens of hyperparameter configurations and select the best based on validation performance on Identity and Day in the alternation reward setting (section A.2).

## 5 Scientific Analysis and Insight

Having established strong identity and learning-related readouts across two datasets, we use TRAIL to investigate broader scientific questions: At what timescale does behavior vary in relation to learning? How long must a trajectory be to capture identity- and learning-related signals, and under what conditions are these signals strongest? Which movement patterns reveal individual identity and learning progress? We conduct systematic, controlled analyses using the six rats performing a Y-maze alternation task. These analyses move beyond predictive performance to explore how brief movements reveal individuality and learning-related changes.

Figure 3: Effects of temporal settings. Left: Positive-pair window width, expressed as a percentage of the recording partition. Right: Trajectory length. Dashed lines mark the default settings.

At what timescale does behavior vary in relation to learning?

Our contrastive learning objective assumes that learning unfolds gradually over hours to days (Shridhar et al., 2026), while behavioral states remain relatively stable within a local positive-pair window (Markowitz et al., 2023; Weinreb et al., 2026). Figure 3 (left) quantifies the effect of window size. Expanding the positive-pair window beyond the default 10% of a recording partition increases Day and Proficiency MSE, while Identity accuracy remains relatively stable. This favors local rather than recording-wide temporal neighborhoods for learning-related readouts; the corresponding duration depends on the recording partition (section A.2.2).

How long must a trajectory be to capture identity- and learning-related signals?

We examine the effect of trajectory length. As shown in figure 3 (right), longer segments generally improve identity and proficiency prediction by providing more movement context. Interestingly, TRAIL remains competitive with the default three-second setting even with just one second of input, suggesting that brief movements already contain informative signals, particularly about individual identity.

Which movement dynamics reveal identity and learning progress?

Movement can be examined in egocentric or allocentric reference frames. Here, we decompose Full Movement into Local Pose and Global Trajectory: the former centers and heading-aligns the pose at each frame, while the latter tracks body position and heading over time
(figure 5; section C.2).
This allows us to examine how individual identity and learning-related changes are reflected in body posture and movement through space. As shown in figure 5, the two components exhibit different strengths: Local Pose is more predictive of identity, whereas Global Trajectory is more predictive of learning-related readouts. Combining both yields the strongest overall performance, suggesting that posture dynamics and global movement patterns carry complementary behavioral information.

Under what conditions are identity and learning-related signals most apparent?

Having shown that movements contain signatures of identity and learning-related changes, we next ask whether these signals are expressed continuously or intermittently in the animal’s behavior. We systematically quantify when, where, and during which movements these signals are most apparent (section C.4). Tables 3(a), 3(b) and 3(c) summarize the results. These signals are strongest at the maze junction and clearer on Days 4–5 than on Days 1–3, suggesting that they become more apparent with experience. Turning is the most informative motif, consistent with its occurrence primarily at the maze junction, while remaining still retains informative signals despite minimal movement.

## 6 Interpretation

## 6.1 Goal

Learning is accompanied by behavior changes that vary across individuals. Our preceding results show that the learned representations carry information about learning progress and individual identity. Here, we explore whether translating these representations back into movement can make these associations visible, offering a qualitative view of the behavioral differences they capture.

## 6.2 Method

To connect variation in representation space to visible changes in behavior, we first make the information encoded in these representations observable through generated movement, then vary the representations along directions associated with learning progression and individual differences.

Making representation observable. We train a conditional diffusion model $G_{\psi}(u,h)$ to generate movement trajectories from a diffusion latent noise $u$ and a conditioning representation $h$ produced by the frozen movement encoder (Peebles & Xie, 2023). Given an observed trajectory $x_{o}$ with representation $h_{o}$ , we obtain its latent noise through approximate DDIM inversion.
We then hold $u_{o}$ and the model parameters $\psi$ fixed while varying the conditioning representation. The unmodified condition yields the reconstruction $\widehat{x}_{o}=G_{\psi}(u_{o},h_{o})$ , providing a baseline for inspecting movement changes induced by representation transformation.

Tracing directions of progression. To trace how movement evolves as learning unfolds, we seek directions that organize progression across recording days, alongside variation across individuals. We estimate these directions using the leading principal components of daily representation centers within an animal or animal-specific centers on the same relative recording day. This provides a shared axis along which to follow gradual changes in movement. Given a selected unit direction $v$ , we use an observed representation $h_{o}$ and a reference representation $h_{r}$ to define the transformation:

 $\displaystyle=\bigl[(h_{r}-h_{o})^{\top}v\bigr]v,$

## 6.3 Results

We examine movement changes from two complementary perspectives: progression across recording days within an animal, and variation between animals on the same relative recording day. Here, we focus on turning, which carries the strongest day and identity signals, as shown in table 3(c), making it an informative setting for both comparisons. Both comparisons generate trajectories of all three keypoints. Our earlier findings in section 5 (figure 5) show that Global Trajectory predicts day and proficiency more accurately than Local Pose, whereas Local Pose better predicts identity. These findings guide how we inspect the generated movements: through Body Trajectory to follow travel across days, and through Local Pose to follow relative body extension across individuals.

Across days, we examine how movement execution varies toward a later-day reference. The Body Trajectory sequence on figure 7 (left) traces the body keypoint’s path through space, allowing us to follow both where the animal moves and how the turn unfolds. The transformed movement continues in the observed movement’s overall direction while taking on aspects of the later-day reference’s pace and turning rhythm. The body-speed curve above the sequence shows a corresponding shift toward the reference’s speed over time. Together, these observations suggest that the transformation expresses day-related differences in movement execution while preserving the observed movement’s broad spatial structure.

Across individuals, we ask how a movement takes on another animal’s postural characteristics. The Local Pose sequence on figure 7 (right) shows how the head, neck, and body extend relative to one another during the turn. As the representation shifts toward the reference animal, the transformed sequence adopts aspects of that animal’s pattern of extension, accompanied by a body-ratio curve that moves toward the reference. Together, these observations make individual differences visible in how the head, neck, and body extend relative to one another during the same behavior, connecting the identity prediction results in figure 5 to visible local postural dynamics. The complementary views—cross-day Local Pose and cross-individual Body Trajectory—are provided in section E.2, while section E.3 extends both comparisons to the going-straight motif.

## 7 Discussion and Conclusion

In this paper, we present TRAIL, a framework for learning representations from short-term animal movements. We show that movement “fingerprints” can reliably predict individual identity and learning-related readouts. These results suggest that fine-scale movement patterns in freely behaving animals differ across individuals and change systematically over days of learning.

Beyond predictive performance, TRAIL provides a tool for generating scientific hypotheses. Through systematic, controlled analyses, we examine when, where, and in which movement patterns identity- and learning-related signals are most apparent. We find that their strength varies across recording days, spatial locations, and movement motifs, and that local pose dynamics and global motion carry complementary information. Conditional generation further allows us to inspect movement differences associated with identity and learning progress, providing hypotheses for subsequent experimental validation.

Moving forward, we aim to extend TRAIL to behavioral time-series data from freely moving animals and humans, particularly in experiments where movement patterns evolve over time. By helping researchers identify which aspects of behavior change and how they change, this framework could support more detailed studies of learning. Applied to larger populations, it could also help reveal how learning dynamics vary across individuals.

## A.2 Implementation Details

For each method, we selected the configuration with the highest mean of two standardized validation scores on the alternation cohort (6 rats): z-scored Identity accuracy and sign-reversed z-scored Day MSE.

Classical baselines were fitted using their respective procedures: expectation-maximization for LG-SSM and AR-HMM, Gibbs sampling for Keypoint-MoSeq, and closed-form least squares for ProMPs. The deep baselines (Autoencoder, VAME, T-BYOL, TCN, BAMS, and hBehaveMAE) were trained for 200 epochs, with learning rates selected through validation grid search.

For LoRA fine-tuning of Chronos-2 and TimesFM-3, we train low-rank adapters for 10 epochs using the self-supervised objective of TRAIL. The validation criterion above selects the learning rate and LoRA rank.

## A.2.1 Behavioral Target Construction

Trial-level proficiency. The supplied state-space labels follow Smith et al. (2004): a Gaussian random walk models the latent learning state, and a logistic observation model links it to the binary trial outcomes. We use the posterior modes of response probability as trial-level labels.

We retain entries for all trials, joining each posterior mode to its trial by animal, recording date, session, maze, and trial identifier. Per-day estimates are used where available; trial keys missing from the probabilistic cohort’s per-day table use the supplied estimate fitted across recording days. These are retrospective behavioral labels: state-space smoothing uses outcomes later in the fitted sequence as well as earlier ones.

Let $a_{j}$ be the start time of trial $j$ and $\hat{p}_{j}$ its supplied posterior mode. Between consecutive trial starts, every frame inherits the preceding trial’s estimate:

 $P(t)=\hat{p}_{j}\quad\text{for }a_{j}\leq t<a_{j+1}.$ 

(4)

The first and last trial estimates extend to the recording boundaries. An unmatched trial produces a missing value throughout its interval. For the sampled frame times $\mathcal{T}_{i}$ covered by history $i$ , the probe target is

 $y_{i}^{\mathrm{prof}}=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{T}_{i}|}\sum_{t\in\mathcal{T}_{i}}P(t).$ 

(5)

A window containing a missing proficiency value is excluded from that target’s probe fit and error calculation. Its other targets remain available.

Reward and success rate. Let $m_{j}$ be the midpoint of trial $j$ and $b_{j}\in\{0,1\}$ its reward outcome. At frame time $t$ , we count all trials with $m_{j}\in[t-30\,\mathrm{s},t+30\,\mathrm{s})$ . Reward is the number of rewarded trials in this interval; Success Rate divides that count by the total number of trials and is zero for an empty interval. Each history receives the mean of its frame-level values. Counts use the complete recording’s trial table, including trials across a trajectory-split boundary. These centered targets describe the reward context around the movement.

## A.2.2 Primary Model and Linear Probes

Preprocessing and windows. We use the registered maze coordinates and downsampled keypoint trajectories from the dataset preprocessing pipeline. Frames with nonfinite tracked coordinates are removed before downsampling. Each recording is split chronologically before constructing stride-one windows, so a trajectory never crosses a partition boundary. The primary feature cache requires 33 history frames and 33 following frames for window eligibility; the encoder receives only the history. After the final-frame alignment described in section 3.2, we apply no random rotation, reflection, scaling, or coordinate noise.

Encoder and optimization. The encoder and decoder are trained from scratch. The encoder uses eight attention heads per layer; the reconstruction decoder has four layers with four attention heads each. Both stacks use pre-layer normalization, feed-forward width 256, and dropout 0.1. We train for 200 epochs with AdamW, batch size 256, learning rate $10^{-3}$ , $(\beta_{1},\beta_{2})=(0.9,0.999)$ , weight decay 0.01, and gradient-norm clipping at 1. Cosine decay reduces the learning rate to $3\times 10^{-5}$ . The reported primary run uses seed 42, and features are extracted from the final epoch.

Positive and negative sampling. The sampler uses the history endpoint relative to the beginning of its recording partition. If $D_{s}$ is that partition’s duration and $u_{i}=t_{i}^{\mathrm{end}}/D_{s}$ , a positive partner is drawn uniformly from the other eligible windows in the same partition satisfying $|u_{i}-u_{j}|\leq 0.05$ . At an endpoint, the interval is truncated to the available windows; if it contains no other window, the nearest one is used. Thus the nominal neighborhood spans 10% of the partition. For the 80% training portion of a ten-minute recording, this is approximately 48 seconds wide, or at most 24 seconds between paired endpoints away from partition boundaries; its duration scales with the partition length. Each epoch shuffles the eligible training windows; incomplete final batches are dropped. Each batch contains 256 anchors and one separately sampled positive for each anchor. For an anchor, the contrastive candidates are its sampled positive and the other batch anchors. Other anchors satisfying the same locality rule are also positives; the remaining candidates are negatives. The anchor itself is excluded. Positive and negative candidate sets receive equal total weight in the balanced loss. Pair construction uses recording membership and time only.

Contrastive objective. We use the balanced objective in equation 1, averaged over batch anchors. The implementation assigns total weight $1/2$ to each candidate set; this common factor cancels in the ratio. The numerator normalization contributes an additive $\log|\mathcal{P}|$ term, which is constant with respect to model parameters for fixed candidate sets.

Supervised baseline. The five task losses are summed with unit coefficients, and regression targets retain their original units. The trained encoder is evaluated with the probes below.

Probe fitting. Identity is decoded by one pooled logistic-regression probe across animals, with an $\ell_{2}$ penalty, $C=1$ , and at most 300 L-BFGS iterations. Each continuous target has a separate ridge probe for each animal. Feature means and standard deviations are fitted on the corresponding training data. Ridge penalties are selected by leave-one-out cross-validation on the training rows from $\{10^{-3},10^{-2.5},\ldots,10^{3}\}$ . Encoder configurations are selected using held-out validation scores. Proficiency and Success Rate predictions are clipped to $[0,\,1]$ ; Day and Reward predictions use the observed target range of the evaluated dataset, pooled across its partitions. Regression MSE is pooled over all valid test windows, weighting each animal by its window count.

## A.4 Uncertainty Estimates

We quantify uncertainty by resampling animals, keeping all test windows from a selected animal together. For the primary cohort, movement-view, and reconstruction comparisons, we recompute pooled accuracy or MSE from the selected animals’ window counts and score sums. The generalization tables use equal-animal averages, as indicated in their captions. The 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles give the reported 95% confidence intervals. For the primary six-animal alternation estimates, we enumerate all $6^{6}$ bootstrap resamples. For the eight-animal probabilistic cohort, we draw 20,000 resamples with random seed 42. The encoder and probes remain fixed, so these intervals describe variation across animals under the fitted models. For paired comparisons, each bootstrap draw uses the same animals for both methods.

## Appendix E Movement Transformations

## E.1 Estimating Directions of Progression

Centers and axes. Let $\mathcal{S}_{a,d}$ denote the recording sessions for animal $a$ on day $d$ , and let $h_{s,i}$ be the representation of window $i$ in session $s$ . We first average the $N_{s}$ representations within each session, then give session means equal weight:

 $\mu_{s}=\frac{1}{N_{s}}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{s}}h_{s,i},\qquad c_{a,d}=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{S}_{a,d}|}\sum_{s\in\mathcal{S}_{a,d}}\mu_{s}.$ 

(6)

Cross-day PCA uses the five centers $\mathcal{C}^{\mathrm{day}}_{a}=\{c_{a,d}\}_{d=1}^{5}$ of one animal; cross-individual PCA uses the six centers $\mathcal{C}^{\mathrm{individual}}_{d}=\{c_{a,d}\}_{a=1}^{6}$ on the same relative recording day. For either set $\mathcal{C}$ , we define the unit axis by

 $v(\mathcal{C})=\underset{\|w\|_{2}=1}{\operatorname{arg\,max}}\sum_{c\in\mathcal{C}}\bigl[w^{\top}(c-\bar{c})\bigr]^{2},\qquad\bar{c}=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{C}|}\sum_{c\in\mathcal{C}}c.$ 

(7)

This is the first principal component of the mean-centered daily centers, without dimension-wise standardization. Each animal has its own cross-day axis and each day its own cross-individual axis; these remain fixed across trajectory-pair comparisons.

Reference-directed transformation. The observed and reference representations determine the direction and extent of movement along $v$ as in equation 3. We evaluate this path at $\tau\in\{0,0.1,\ldots,1\}$ . At $\tau=1$ , the condition matches the reference’s projection onto the axis while retaining the observed representation’s orthogonal components. Choosing a later-day reference traces variation toward a later stage of the same animal’s experience, while choosing another animal on the same relative day traces individual variation. Decoding these conditions with the fixed diffusion latent moves changes associated with each direction observable.

## E.2 Complementary Views of Turning Transformations

The complementary views in figures 13 and 14 show how the same turning transformations appear at the postural and trajectory levels, respectively, extending the observations in figure 7.

Cross-day transformation of postural timing. The Local Pose view in figure 13 reveals a temporal shift within a largely preserved pattern of body-segment proportions. The transformed body-ratio curve closely follows the observed and reconstructed curves, while the skeleton sequence advances through similar configurations earlier. The head’s rightward inclination and subsequent realignment, for example, occur earlier in the transformed row than in the reconstructed row. The prominent change is therefore in the timing of postural transitions, with little change in body ratio. This temporal shift complements the changes in speed and turning rhythm in figure 7, connecting the trajectory-level observation to the progression of local posture through the turn.

Relative to the reconstructed movement, the transformed sequence exhibits a lower, later speed peak and maintains forward movement toward the end of the window, closely following the reference speed curve. ## E.3 Movement Transformations in Going Straight

Going straight provides a complementary setting in which changes in movement execution can be followed along a largely consistent direction of travel. Figures 15 and 16 present cross-day and cross-individual transformations, respectively, with Body Trajectory and body speed on the left and Local Pose and body ratio on the right.

Across days, the transformation reorganizes when movement is concentrated within the sequence. In figure 15, the observed and reconstructed movements begin relatively slowly and accelerate again later in the window. The transformed movement instead starts faster and, after an early peak, progressively slows toward the end, taking on the later-day reference’s pattern of movement. The Local Pose sequences retain a broadly similar pattern of body extension, accompanied by closely aligned reconstructed and transformed body-ratio curves. As in the turning example, the transformation expresses day-related variation primarily through changes in pace and timing while preserving the broad spatial structure of the movement.

Across individuals, figure 16 shows a shift toward the reference in both movement pace and the relative extension of the tracked body segments. The transformed body-speed curve approaches the reference’s earlier peak and subsequent decline. In Local Pose, the transformed sequence maintains a higher nose–neck to neck–body ratio through much of the middle of the movement, bringing it closer to the reference than the reconstructed sequence. These changes extend the observation from turning: individual characteristics become visible through both how movement unfolds over time and how the body extends during its execution.

## E.4 Diffusion Model and Training Details

Data and backbone. We train the diffusion model using the existing training and validation splits from the six alternation-task animals (Rat A–Rat F). Each 3-second trajectory contains 33 frames of two-dimensional nose, neck, and body coordinates in the preprocessed absolute coordinate system, paired with a 16-dimensional representation $h$ extracted by the frozen movement encoder. The backbone follows DiT-B (Peebles & Xie, 2023), with 12 transformer blocks, hidden dimension 768, 12 attention heads, and an MLP expansion ratio of 4. Each keypoint at each frame forms a token. The representation $h$ and diffusion timestep are incorporated through adaLN-Zero, and the model predicts the added noise and reverse-process variance.

Denoising and training objective. For each training trajectory $x_{0}$ , we uniformly sample a diffusion timestep $t$ and construct a noisy trajectory:

 $x_{t}=\sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_{t}}\,x_{0}+\sqrt{1-\bar{\alpha}_{t}}\,\epsilon,\qquad\epsilon\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I).$ 

(8)

We use 1000 diffusion steps with a linear variance schedule from $10^{-4}$ to $0.02$ , where $\bar{\alpha}_{t}$ is the cumulative signal-retention factor. Conditioned on $h$ , the model learns to recover the added noise from $x_{t}$ while estimating the reverse-process variance:

 $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{diff}}=\mathbb{E}_{x_{0},h,t,\epsilon}\!\left[\operatorname{MSE}\!\bigl(\epsilon,\epsilon_{\psi}(x_{t},t,h)\bigr)+\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{var}}\right],$ 

(9)

 $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{var}}=\frac{1}{\ln 2}\,\operatorname{mean}_{j}\,D_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\left(q(x_{t-1,j}\mid x_{t},x_{0})\,\|\,p_{\psi}(x_{t-1,j}\mid x_{t},h)\right),\qquad t>0.$ 

(10)

The MSE term measures noise-prediction error, while the KL term trains the reverse-process variance by comparing the forward-process posterior $q$ with the learned reverse transition $p_{\psi}$ . The factor $1/\ln 2$ expresses the divergence in bits. Both terms are averaged over coordinates $j$ in frames with no missing keypoints. For variance learning, gradients do not propagate through the predicted noise, and $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{var}}$ is set to zero at $t=0$ .

Optimization. With the movement encoder frozen, we train the diffusion model from random initialization for 100 epochs using AdamW, a learning rate of $10^{-4}$ , batch size 256 per device, and zero weight decay. We maintain an exponential moving average (EMA) of model parameters with decay 0.9999 and select the EMA checkpoint with the lowest validation denoising loss.

DDIM inversion and generation. We use deterministic DDIM (Song et al., 2020) with 100 steps and $\eta=0$ . DDIM inversion maps an observed trajectory $x_{o}$ to a diffusion latent $u_{o}$ by approximately reversing the denoising process. With the original representation $h_{o}$ fixed, each step estimates the clean trajectory and advances to a higher noise level:

 $\displaystyle\widehat{x}_{0}$ 
 $\displaystyle=\frac{x_{t}-\sqrt{1-\bar{\alpha}_{t}}\,\epsilon_{\psi}(x_{t},t,h_{o})}{\sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_{t}}},$ 

(11)

 $\displaystyle x_{t^{\prime}}$ 
 $\displaystyle=\sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_{t^{\prime}}}\,\widehat{x}_{0}+\sqrt{1-\bar{\alpha}_{t^{\prime}}}\,\epsilon_{\psi}(x_{t},t,h_{o}),\qquad t^{\prime}>t.$ 

Here, $t^{\prime}$ is the next timestep on the inversion grid. Starting from the observed trajectory, these updates progressively increase the noise level, defining the approximate inversion mapping $u_{o}=\mathcal{I}_{\psi}(x_{o},h_{o})$ . Denoising this latent under the original condition gives the reconstruction. We then hold $u_{o}$ and the model parameters fixed while varying the condition along the path in equation 3.

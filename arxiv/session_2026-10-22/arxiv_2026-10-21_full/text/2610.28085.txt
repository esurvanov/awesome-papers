Abstract

The rapid growth of scientific literature makes survey writing increasingly challenging, motivating automatic survey generation.
Yet, existing systems must search an open space of papers and an open space of concepts with no trace of how the community itself has navigated them, leading to incomplete scope, weak taxonomies, and unreliable citations.
Our core insight is that author-written Related Work sections contain the human judgments needed to address both limitations:
authors curate the citations relevant to their contributions and explain how the cited works relate, yielding paths for scope reconstruction and relational evidence for organization.
We propose InsightRAG. Its insight-guided retrieval follows author-supported citation relations to reconstruct a verified research scope and structure the resulting relational evidence.
It aggregates these judgments under a minimum-conflict objective into a taxonomy that reflects recurring author-written perspectives in the retrieved literature, guides generation, and bounds each section’s evidence.
Results on ten topics show gains in scope reconstruction, taxonomy quality, citation support, and reference reliability.

## 1 Introduction

Figure 1: Motivation of InsightRAG. Author-written Related Work sections record how different authors characterize prior work from their own perspectives; aggregating these local views grounds the survey in recurring perspectives within the explored literature.

Writing a scientific survey is labor-intensive: authors must find, assess, and organize relevant work into a coherent taxonomy and narrative.
As publication rates grow, this cost rises faster than most groups can absorb.
This motivated automatic survey generation (ASG), which agentizes this workflow into retrieval, reading, planning, and writing (Wang et al., 2024; Zhou et al., 2025).
Subsequent systems refine its components with memory-driven retrieval and outline planning (Yan et al., 2025), structured preprocessing (Liang et al., 2025), and quality-aware feedback (Bao et al., 2025).

What a survey requires, however, is not a ranked list but a research scope: which works are foundational, which extend or challenge them, and which directions belong to the same topic. Reconstructing it confronts a language model with two open-ended search problems: retrieval must find relevant work in an open space of millions of papers Cohan et al. (2020); Ajith et al. (2024); He et al. (2025), and writing must organize that evidence in an equally open space of concepts, inventing a taxonomy from scratch. Both spaces are too large for a model to navigate reliably—on our ten topics, no existing system recovers more than 11% of a human survey’s reference scope, and every baseline falls below 0.50 correctness. Recent work constrains this search with structure extracted from paper content Pedinotti and Santus (2026); Yang et al. (2026) or citation graphs Zhang et al. (2025b); Hu et al. (2025a), but neither provides the traces the search needs: a paper’s content reflects only its own vantage point, and citation edges record that two papers are linked, not how Wu et al. (2026). The model is thus left to search these open spaces without any trace of how the community itself has navigated them.

These traces already exist, written by authors themselves. When authors write a Related Work section, they do more than cite: they search the same two open spaces and record how they navigated them, selecting relevant prior work and articulating relations such as similarity, difference, limitation, and progression. Across a field, these local judgments accumulate into a crowdsourced account of its intellectual structure, supplying exactly the traces current pipelines discard: curated citation paths for finding literature, and explicit relational statements for organizing it. This motivates our research question: can author-written Related Work judgments serve as a reliable scaffold for reconstructing a field’s scope, taxonomy, and coherent survey narrative?

We answer this question with InsightRAG, which builds one stage of the workflow on each of these two signals.
Insight-guided retrieval follows the citation paths authors have already curated: from a seed paper, it traverses the citation graph forward and backward, admitting a paper only when an author-written passage supports the relation and the paper lies within the user-defined scope.
It returns a verified paper set and two records of author judgment: a research-scope graph whose edges carry the relation an author asserted between two papers—improvement, extension, comparison, or critique—and, per paper, a Related Work Tree recording how its authors grouped prior work and placed their own contribution within it.
Taxonomy construction then aggregates the relational statements themselves: the concept phrases stated on the graph’s edges are merged into a shared candidate concept space, over which a minimum-conflict objective selects a taxonomy and paper assignment that maximize concept support and assignment support while penalizing taxonomy complexity, subject to structural and semantic constraints.
Since exact optimization is NP-hard, InsightRAG approximates it with a coverage-oriented greedy initializer and a global LLM refinement pass, then assigns each paper to one leaf.
The taxonomy and its assigned papers then serve as the writing plan: leaf sections synthesize the assigned papers, internal sections explain how their children relate, and each section may cite only its own papers.

Experiments on ten computer-science survey topics, each annotated with a gold reference scope and a gold taxonomy from a human-written survey, evaluate the intermediate artifacts as well as the final text.
InsightRAG reconstructs research scope far more completely and accurately than retrieval and survey-generation baselines, builds taxonomies that are better organized and better grounded in author-written evidence, and produces surveys with substantially more faithful citations.

Overall, Our contributions are threefold:

• 

We identify author-written Related Work judgments as the scholarly-judgment signal missing from autonomous survey generation, and reorganize the end-to-end workflow around it.

• 

We propose InsightRAG, combining insight-guided citation traversal with minimum-conflict aggregation of distributed author judgments to produce a taxonomy that reflects recurring author-written perspectives in the retrieved literature, bounds the evidence available to each survey section, and guides generation of the final survey.

• 

We introduce an evaluation setting over ten survey topics that scores research-scope reconstruction, taxonomy quality, and final survey quality separately at successive pipeline stages.

## 2 Related Work

Citation-aware scholarly retrieval and exploration. SciCite classifies citation intent as background, method, or result comparison (Cohan et al., 2019), while Valenzuela et al. distinguish important from incidental citations (Valenzuela et al., 2015). Scholarly retrieval also uses lexical or embedding similarity (Cohan et al., 2020) and citation-graph propagation (Zhang et al., 2025b; Hu et al., 2025a); these signals alone do not preserve how citing authors describe prior work. Relatedly helps readers explore Related Work paragraphs across papers and form topic outlines (Palani et al., 2023), but leaves the synthesis of these perspectives to readers. InsightRAG uses passages surrounding citations as author-attributed relational evidence to discover and verify a topic-specific literature scope.

Scholarly taxonomy construction. CHIME organizes a given paper set with expert corrections to category relations and paper assignments (Hsu et al., 2024). HiGTL combines paper text and citation-graph structure for hierarchical clustering and concept naming (Hu et al., 2025b), while Zhu et al. use LLM-generated aspect representations and clustering and introduce TaxoBench-CS (Zhu et al., 2025). These methods do not explicitly use citing authors’ descriptions of prior work as evidence for taxonomy construction. InsightRAG extracts concept phrases from author-written Related Work passages surrounding citations and aggregates their support across papers.

Automatic survey generation. Mohammad et al. combined citation texts with multi-document summarization to generate technical surveys (Mohammad et al., 2009). LLM-based systems commonly follow a retrieve–plan–write pipeline (Wang et al., 2024; Zhou et al., 2025), with later work improving retrieval, preprocessing, feedback, collaboration, and evaluation (Wu et al., 2025a; Yan et al., 2025; Liang et al., 2025; Bao et al., 2025; Go et al., 2026; Guo et al., 2026). Some systems also extract relations or taxonomies from paper content (Pedinotti and Santus, 2026; Yang et al., 2026), but these structures need not capture how subsequent authors position earlier work. InsightRAG carries its reconstructed scope and taxonomy into an evidence-bounded writing plan. Appendix H provides a more detailed comparison.

## 3 Methodology

## 3.1 Workflow and Problem Formulation

InsightRAG operationalizes the two signals recorded in author-written Related Work sections: curated citation paths for reconstructing a research scope, and explicit relational statements for organizing it. Rather than use keywords to retrieve references and infer the field’s structure from scratch, InsightRAG recovers and aggregates these distributed human judgments across the retrieved literature.

The input is $Q=(d,p_{0})$ :
a scope definition $d$ specifying the survey topic, focus, and literature boundary,
and a seed paper $p_{0}$ given by the user or retrieved by an LLM.
Insight-guided retrieval (§3.2) follows from $p_{0}$ the curated citation paths supported by author-written contexts, returning a topic-specific paper set $P$ , a research-scope graph $G$ whose edges carry those contexts, and for each $p\in P$ a Related Work (RW) Tree $R_{p}$ recording how its authors grouped prior work.
Taxonomy construction (§3.3) takes $(P,G,\{R_{p}\}_{p\in P})$ , builds a draft hierarchy from concept phrases and support weights in the author-written citation contexts of $G$ , refines it into a taxonomy $T$ using the scope definition, compact concept and paper profiles, and RW Tree patterns as auxiliary structural evidence, and assigns papers to its leaves through a map $A$ .
Together $T$ and $A$ bound the evidence available to every survey section: a leaf is written from its assigned papers alone, while an internal node explains how its children relate. An LLM then generates the final survey.
Detailed algorithms, backbone models, principal budgets, and resource-use figures are provided in Appendix F; method prompts for all three stages appear in Appendix I.

## 3.2 Insight-Guided Retrieval

This stage must recover a research scope rather than a ranked list: which works are foundational, which extend or challenge them, and which directions belong to the same topic.
InsightRAG recovers it from author-written Related Work sections, which already carry local relevance judgments and explicit statements of how a citing paper relates to the work it cites:
a citation relation is followed only when such a passage supports it, and the paper it leads to is admitted only after verification against the user-defined research scope.

## 3.2.1 Related Work Tree Extraction

For each accepted paper $p$ , InsightRAG extracts an RW Tree $R_{p}$ from the paper’s author-written Related Work discussion and the citations resolved from that text.
Its internal nodes are the categories the authors used to organize prior work, so that papers under one internal node form an author-defined conceptual group;
its leaves are the cited papers, with their meta information and the role the authors assigned them, such as method or limitation;
and its root is the focal paper with its research context.
The edges inside the tree $E_{p}$ encode the authors’ conceptual organization, such as the improvement from a leaf paper to the root, rather than the raw citation links used for traversal.
If the Related Work section does not provide a complete hierarchy, InsightRAG retains only the structure supported by the text instead of asking the model to fill in the missing parts.

## 3.2.2 Research-Scope Reconstruction

Reconstruction turns input $Q$ into the paper set $P$ and the research-scope graph $G$ .
InsightRAG first converts $d$ into a structured research context holding the scope constraints, the intended perspective, and any temporal requirement, and resolves $p_{0}$ against a scholarly index for its meta information and citation relations; retrieval starts only if the seed’s research problem, methodology, and contribution satisfy the scope.

The search then proceeds in rounds. A candidate is accepted only if it falls within the target scope and then stored as RW Tree $R_{p}$ , then becomes nodes in $G$ , and each directed edge in $G$ stores the supporting passage from the citing paper to the cited paper.
To support this iterative process, the algorithm maintains three sets: the accepted set $S_{a}$ , the frontier set $S_{f}$ of papers awaiting expansion, and the explored set $S_{e}$ of papers already expanded. The accepted set and the frontier are initialized with the seed, $S_{a}=S_{f}=\{p_{0}\}$ , while the explored set is initially empty, $S_{e}=\emptyset$ .

Forward operator.
Given a paper $p$ , the forward operator retrieves the papers that cite $p$ , each paired with the citation sentence or Related Work passage in which the citing paper discusses $p$ .
This direction targets later developments, extensions, applications, comparisons, and critiques.
When the citing papers are too numerous to process exhaustively, highly cited and recent papers are prioritized to keep the operator technically feasible.
A permissive pre-filter removes clearly uninformative candidates, and each remaining candidate must pass an admission test against the target scope.
Admitted candidates are deduplicated against the accepted set $S_{a}$ and, if new, added to it; their supporting passages are incorporated into $G$ as relation evidence.

RW-guided backward operator.
Given a paper $p$ , the backward operator retrieves the references of $p$ by extracting its RW Tree $R_{p}$ (§3.2.1) and checking whether each leaf of $R_{p}$ , paired with the author-written passage that describes it, passes the same admission test.
Admitted predecessors are deduplicated against $S_{a}$ and, if new, added to it; their relation evidence is incorporated into $G$ .
Each admitted predecessor is expanded through its own RW Tree, so backward exploration recursively exposes earlier portions of the verified scope.

Round structure and state update.

Starting from $S_{a}=S_{f}=\{p_{0}\}$ and $S_{e}=\emptyset$ , a run executes a bounded number of forward steps followed by a bounded number of backward steps.
In each forward step, the forward operator is applied to every paper in the frontier $S_{f}$ ; the expanded papers are added to the explored set $S_{e}$ , newly admitted papers are added to $S_{a}$ , and the frontier is updated to $S_{a}\setminus S_{e}$ .
The forward phase comes first so that major recent research directions are identified before their earlier foundations are traced.
Once the forward steps are complete, the frontier is reset to the full accepted set and the explored set is cleared, i.e., $S_{f}\leftarrow S_{a}$ and $S_{e}\leftarrow\emptyset$ , while $S_{a}$ is kept unchanged.
Each backward step then applies the backward operator to every paper in $S_{f}$ and parses its RW Tree; the expanded papers are added to $S_{e}$ , newly admitted predecessors are added to $S_{a}$ , and the frontier is again updated to $S_{a}\setminus S_{e}$ .
After the backward steps terminate, a loop-closure step densifies $G$ by recovering missing citation links among accepted papers, merging duplicate nodes, and attaching new author-written evidence to existing relations.
This completes the reconstruction run.

Post-termination reseeding and graph merging.

After a run terminates, InsightRAG selects new seeds with high in-degree in $G$ , reruns reconstruction independently from each seed, and merges the resulting graphs with deduplication and loop closure.
The merged graph and its verified papers define $G$ and $P$ , respectively, for taxonomy construction. The RW Trees are retained for global refinement, while the author-written citation contexts on the edges of $G$ provide concept phrases and support weights for the draft hierarchy.

## 3.3 Minimum-Conflict Taxonomy Construction and Survey Generation

The retrieval stage produces a verified literature space but not the global organization a survey requires.
The author-written citation contexts retained on the edges of $G$ encode local views that may conflict: different citing papers may associate the same paper with different concepts, use different names for similar concepts, or organize the field along different dimensions.
Semantic clustering provides no hierarchy and can mix abstraction levels; a one-shot LLM offers little control over evidence support;
and directly following a single RW Tree can privilege one author’s local organization over perspectives from other papers.

InsightRAG therefore treats taxonomy construction as aggregation: given $P$ and $G$ , it seeks an admissible hierarchy that reconciles the author-written judgments encoded in $G$ ’s citation contexts under the minimum-conflict objective, assigns papers to leaves through $A$ , and generates the survey from the result.
The problem is NP-hard, so InsightRAG follows a greedy-and-refine strategy: a greedy initializer on the combinatorial core, followed by global LLM refinement of category meanings and hierarchical structure.

Candidate concept space.

InsightRAG first constructs a shared candidate concept space from the relation statements carried by the edges of $G$ , so that the greedy initializer begins from relational evidence shared across the retrieved literature. Each directed edge $(p^{\prime},p)\in E(G)$ stores the author-written passage in which the citing paper $p^{\prime}$ states how it categorizes the cited paper $p$ ; let $\phi(p^{\prime},p)$ denote the set of concept phrases stated on edge $(p^{\prime},p)$ .
The candidate concept set $\mathcal{C}$ collects the concept phrases on all edges, $\bigcup_{(p^{\prime},p)\in E(G)}\phi(p^{\prime},p)$ , and merges semantically equivalent ones using their textual meaning and supporting edges.
Each concept $c$ is assigned a semantic dimension $\delta(c)\in\mathcal{D}$ , where $\mathcal{D}$ is the set of organizational dimensions.
The candidate space retains weighted paper assignments, defined below.
For a paper $p$ and a concept $c$ , the assignment weight $w(c,p)$ counts incoming citation edges whose passages associate $p$ with $c$ :

 $w(c,p)=\sum_{(p^{\prime},p)\in E(G)}\mathbf{1}\bigl[c\in\phi(p^{\prime},p)\bigr],$ 

Conflicts remain explicit: if different edges associate a paper with different concepts, all candidate assignments and their support weights are retained.
A zero-weight fallback concept $c_{\bot}$ remains available for papers with sparse concept evidence.

Optimization objective.

A taxonomy is a rooted tree $T=(\mathcal{C}(T),E(T))$ over selected concepts $\mathcal{C}(T)\subseteq\mathcal{C}$ , with leaf-category set $L(T)\subseteq\mathcal{C}(T)$ . A map $A:P\rightarrow L(T)$ assigns every paper to exactly one leaf category. Let $\Gamma$ denote a configurable set of structural and semantic constraints, and let $\mathcal{F}(\Gamma)$ be the taxonomy–assignment pairs satisfying them. We solve $(T^{*},A^{*})$ as follows

 $\displaystyle\arg\max_{(T,A)\in\mathcal{F}(\Gamma)}\left[\underbrace{\sum_{c\in\mathcal{C}(T)}\sum_{p\in P}w(c,p)}_{\text{\textcircled{\scriptsize 1}\,concept support}}+\underbrace{\sum_{p\in P}w\bigl(A(p),p\bigr)}_{\text{\textcircled{\scriptsize 2}\,assignment support}}-\underbrace{\lambda\bigl|\mathcal{C}(T)\bigr|}_{\text{\textcircled{\scriptsize 3}\,complexity penalty}}\right].$ 

(1)

Term \scriptsize1⃝ favors concepts carrying strong paper support, term \scriptsize2⃝ rewards agreement with author judgments at the paper-assignment level, and \scriptsize3⃝ controls taxonomy complexity. The constraint set $\Gamma$ can, for example, limit the maximum depth $B$ and branching factor $k$ .
The constrained optimization is NP-hard; Appendix G specifies the full constraint set and gives the NP-hardness proof.

Greedy taxonomy construction.

Since exact optimization is NP-hard, InsightRAG constructs a draft solution with a greedy top-down procedure over the shared candidate concept space derived from the citation contexts retained in $G$ .

The root initially contains the full paper set $P$ , and the procedure applies the same three-step split recursively at each category.
Candidate preparation: for the papers currently under a category, it retains the supported categories from the shared candidate space, removes those with insufficient support or that violate the constraints, and selects the organizational dimension with the strongest total support so that the resulting siblings remain coherent.
Greedy selection: within that dimension, it selects child categories one at a time by marginal coverage, choosing the candidate that adds the most support beyond the children already selected, and stops when the branching budget is reached.
Assignment and recursion: each paper is assigned to the child with the highest normalized support; low-confidence papers are placed in a fallback category or deferred to the final assignment stage. The procedure then recurses on each child until the depth bound is reached, too few papers remain, or no supported split is available, yielding the draft taxonomy $T_{0}$ and assignment $A_{0}$ . Appendix G provides the formal analysis.

Global refinement and generation.

Structural refinement: The greedy draft may contain vague, redundant, or unevenly granular categories. An LLM refines $T_{0}$ using the scope definition $d$ , compact profiles of its concepts, profiles of the retrieved papers, and recurring category and parent–child patterns aggregated from the RW Trees as auxiliary structural evidence. The draft remains the evidence-grounded starting point; the refinement resolves semantic and structural issues before final paper assignment and deterministic validation.

Paper assignment refinement: Once the refined taxonomy $T$ is fixed, an LLM updates the draft assignment $A_{0}$ only where hierarchy changes create conflicts, ensuring that every paper belongs to exactly one leaf and yielding the final assignment $A$ .
A deterministic validation pass then repairs any remaining missing or duplicate assignments and prunes unsupported empty leaves. Together, hierarchy refinement, assignment refinement, and validation transform the draft $(T_{0},A_{0})$ into the final $(T,A)$ used in our experiments.

Finally, InsightRAG uses $T$ and $A$ as the writing plan.
Each category becomes a writing unit with a local evidence package and a fixed set of allowed citations.
Leaf units synthesize the papers assigned by $A$ , while internal units explain the relations among their child categories.
The units are generated independently, assembled in taxonomy order, and completed with survey-level sections.
Thus, $T$ determines the survey organization and $A$ bounds the evidence available to each section.

## 4 Experiments

We evaluate InsightRAG from research-scope reconstruction to final survey generation. We ask three research questions:
RQ1: Does Related Work-guided retrieval reconstruct a more complete and accurate research scope?
RQ2: Does Related Work evidence improve taxonomy construction?
RQ3: Does the resulting taxonomy improve the quality and credibility of the final survey?
Formal definitions and implementation details for all evaluation metrics are provided in Appendix E.

## 4.3 Taxonomy-Guided Survey Generation

RQ3 evaluates the content, correspondence, and credibility of the final survey. All methods generate a survey for each of the same ten topics.

## 4.3.1 LLM-Judged Survey Quality

Setting.
GPT-5.2, Gemini 3.1 Pro, and DeepSeek V4 Pro score each method’s surveys for Coverage, Relevance, and Structure on a five-point scale. Averaging the three dimensions yields 30 topic–judge scores per method; aggregation and full results appear in Appendices E.3 and D.2.

Figure 4: Survey quality by topic. Axes average Coverage, Relevance, and Structure across three LLM judges.

Results.
Figure 4 shows that InsightRAG maintains high survey-quality scores across topics, outperforming SurveyGen, SurveyForge, AutoSurvey, and Deep Research on average while remaining comparable to Vanilla LLM (Appendix D.2).
In the same expert study, InsightRAG’s survey was preferred in all 6/6 comparisons; semi-structured interviews further examined the reasons for these preferences and remaining weaknesses (Appendix C.2).

## 4.3.2 Content Correspondence and Citation Support of Generated Surveys

Setting.
SS compares generated and human-survey passages; KPR measures coverage of the human survey’s key points. Citation Recall counts claims supported by their full citation group, whereas Citation Precision counts citation instances that independently or necessarily support their claims. We also report reference count and VRR, the fraction resolved to real papers (Appendix E.3).

Results.
Table 3 shows that InsightRAG ranks second in KPR and leads in Citation Recall, Citation Precision, and VRR. Vanilla LLM achieves higher SS, but substantially lower citation-support and reference-reliability scores.

## 4.3.3 Passage-Based Citation Faithfulness

Setting. On Multi-Agent Workflow Automation and Video Accessibility, we sample seven claim–citation pairs per survey for the six methods in Table 3 (84 total). An automatic judge reads cited full texts and identifies passages supporting each claim. Unverifiable references count as Unsupported; Supported includes full or partial support. We manually audit two pairs per survey (24 total).

Results. InsightRAG has the highest automatic Supported rate (13/14, 92.9%) and ties Vanilla LLM in the human audit (4/4). Automatic and human judgments agree on 20/24 pairs (83.3%, $\kappa=0.655$ ); the automatic judge is more lenient in all four disagreements. Complete automatic and human results appear in Appendix D.3.

## 4.4 Ablation Study

Setting.
We independently ablate three scope-reconstruction components and taxonomy refinement, holding other settings fixed; full scope ablation results appear in Appendix B.1.

Scope reconstruction.
Forward expansion contributes most, followed by backward expansion; loop closure and densification add smaller but consistent gains in Coverage and F1.

Taxonomy construction.
Table 4 shows that refinement improves OutlineAvg (0.4813 to 3.6000) and PTA (0.2576 to 0.7478), despite slightly lower HSR. Heading correspondence alone does not ensure coherent organization or accurate assignment of retrieved papers to taxonomy leaves.

## 5 Conclusion

We built InsightRAG on a signal automatic survey generation had left unused: the judgments human authors record in Related Work sections about which prior work matters and how it relates.
Insight-guided retrieval follows only the relations an author’s own words support, and minimum-conflict aggregation organizes the resulting citation-context evidence into a taxonomy that bounds each section’s evidence, improving scope reconstruction, taxonomy quality, citation support, and reference reliability across ten topics.
More broadly, this work provides a concrete case for reconsidering what human insight is already embedded in existing artifacts and how it can be leveraged to support literature discovery, organization, and synthesis.

## Limitations

Dependence on language models.

InsightRAG relies on LLMs for RW Tree extraction, scope verification, taxonomy refinement, and section writing, while part of the evaluation uses LLM judges. Errors may propagate through the pipeline, so the generated survey and intermediate artifacts still require human verification.

Scope of author perspectives.

InsightRAG draws on author-written Related Work sections to organize and synthesize the retrieved literature. Shared omissions or biases in these sections may carry over into the resulting taxonomy and survey. Because retrieval is limited to the literature reached through citation traversal, some parts of the field may remain underrepresented.

Coverage of recent work.

Citation traversal can miss recent papers that are not yet indexed or connected to the explored graph; when forward candidate pools exceed the processing budget, prioritizing by citation impact and recency may also miss valuable low-citation work.

Risks of over-reliance.

Junior researchers may over-rely on automatically generated surveys instead of developing the skills to search, assess, and synthesize the literature independently.
This may limit their ability to form their own judgments about a field’s scope and structure. We therefore recommend human–AI co-creation: the generated scope, taxonomy, and text should serve as starting points that researchers inspect, revise, and validate.

API cost.

The cold-start stage requires many LLM calls to extract RW Trees and graph relations and to judge whether papers fall within the target scope. Once processed, the extracted structures and relation evidence can be stored and reused, while relevance decisions can be reused for the same scope. Subsequent runs therefore avoid most repeated extraction and verification costs.

Evaluation topics, their source surveys, and the realized numbers of Related Work (RW) Trees.

We constructed ten evaluation instances covering the ten research areas used in the China Computer Federation (CCF) venue classification (China Computer Federation, 2023) (Table 5).
For each research area, we selected a human-written source survey and used either the complete survey or a focused section with sufficient coverage of the target topic.

For instances based on a survey section, the LLM (GPT-5.5) received both the source-survey title and the selected section title; for instances based on the complete survey, it received only the survey title.
Based on the given titles, the LLM generated (1) an evaluation topic title and (2) a focused scope definition describing the research problem, included directions, and exclusion boundaries.
We manually reviewed the generated topic titles and scope definitions for consistency with the selected survey content.
The resulting scope definitions were used consistently for paper selection, citation filtering, and scope verification throughout the experiments.

We extracted the scientific papers cited in each selected survey unit, where the unit was either the complete survey or a selected section.
We then resolved their bibliographic records, removed duplicate versions and non-paper references, and retained only papers that matched the generated scope definition, yielding 304 in-scope candidate papers.

For each retained candidate paper, we identified its author-written Related Work discussion, resolved in-text citation markers against its bibliography, and filtered the resolved cited papers using the same topic-specific scope definition. The remaining citation contexts and cited papers were organized into a hierarchical RW Tree following the definition in Section 3.2.1.

After topic-scope filtering and extraction validation, the final dataset contains 248 valid RW Trees.
The resulting trees contain 3,937 citation-bearing leaf occurrences.
Because the same cited paper may occur in multiple RW Trees, these occurrences correspond to 2,611 unique cited papers.

## C.2 Method-Blinded Expert Comparison

Setting. We compared InsightRAG and SurveyGen on two topics, Multi-Agent Workflow Automation and AI-Powered Video Accessibility for Blind and Low Vision Viewers. Three domain experts evaluated each topic (six in total). Given the topic scope, they saw both methods’ taxonomies and surveys labeled System A and System B without method names; presentation order was balanced across experts. The evaluation guidance covered conceptual coverage, non-redundancy, and hierarchy for taxonomies, and content coverage, topical relevance, and organization for surveys. Experts selected A, B, or similar for each artifact, followed by a semi-structured interview about organization, strengths and weaknesses, and potential research use.

Results. InsightRAG’s taxonomy was preferred in 5/6 comparisons: 2/3 for Multi-Agent Workflow Automation and 3/3 for Video Accessibility. Its survey was preferred in all 6/6 comparisons, with 3/3 preferences on each topic.

Interview findings. Experts often attributed InsightRAG’s advantage to topic-specific organization: its categories were more directly tied to the target problem and more often followed a problem-solving or technical progression, whereas SurveyGen was perceived as broader and more list-like. All six experts perceived a connection between a taxonomy and its resulting survey organization; one noted that this held for both systems. Experts also identified weaknesses in topical focus, coverage of important directions, technical specificity, and progression across sections. In particular, some subtopics remained parallel rather than progressively connected, and cross-cutting or future-oriented sections were not always well integrated with the preceding structure. Experts envisioned using the system when entering or organizing a research area, identifying research gaps, or positioning their own work.

## Appendix E Metric Definitions and Evaluation Details

This section collects the formal definitions and implementation details of all
metrics used in Section 4. Let $\mathcal{T}$ denote the set
of evaluation topics and $m$ a method.

## E.1 Research-Scope Reconstruction Metrics

For topic $t$ , let $G_{t}$ be the human-curated gold paper set, $R_{m,t}$ the
papers retrieved by method $m$ , $J_{m,t}\subseteq R_{m,t}$ the retrieved papers
successfully evaluated by the relevance judge, and
 $\mathrm{Rel}_{m,t}\subseteq J_{m,t}$ those judged relevant. We compute
topic-level Coverage and Correctness as

 $\displaystyle\mathrm{Coverage}_{m,t}$ 
 $\displaystyle=\frac{|R_{m,t}\cap G_{t}|}{|G_{t}|},$ 

(2)

 $\displaystyle\mathrm{Correctness}_{m,t}$ 
 $\displaystyle=\frac{|\mathrm{Rel}_{m,t}|}{|J_{m,t}|}.$ 

The reported scores average the corresponding topic-level values:

 $\displaystyle\mathrm{Coverage}_{m}$ 
 $\displaystyle=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{T}|}\sum_{t\in\mathcal{T}}\mathrm{Coverage}_{m,t},$ 

(3)

 $\displaystyle\mathrm{Correctness}_{m}$ 
 $\displaystyle=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{T}|}\sum_{t\in\mathcal{T}}\mathrm{Correctness}_{m,t}.$ 

Their harmonic mean gives

 $\mathrm{F1}_{m}=\frac{2\,\mathrm{Coverage}_{m}\,\mathrm{Correctness}_{m}}{\mathrm{Coverage}_{m}+\mathrm{Correctness}_{m}}.$ 

(4)

Coverage therefore measures overlap with the human scope, whereas Correctness
is relevance precision estimated by the LLM judge rather than gold-set
precision.

For the expert audit, validated extra-paper precision is the fraction of sampled
papers judged relevant, and false-discovery rate is one minus this precision.
Projected precision, recall, and F1 extrapolate the audited relevance rate to
the complete set of papers retrieved outside the gold scope.

## E.2 Taxonomy Evaluation Metrics

Let $V_{G}$ and $V_{C}$ be the non-root nodes of the gold and generated taxonomies,
and let $d(\cdot)$ denote node depth. Heading Soft Recall (HSR) matches every
gold heading to its most similar generated heading and penalizes depth
disagreement:

 $\mathrm{HSR}=\frac{1}{|V_{G}|}\sum_{g\in V_{G}}\max_{c\in V_{C}}\mathrm{sim}(g,c)\,0.9^{|d(g)-d(c)|}.$ 

(5)

For the reported HSR and CEDS scores, $\mathrm{sim}(g,c)$ is the cosine
similarity between embeddings of the gold and generated taxonomy node paths,
clipped to $[0,1]$ . The root heading is excluded from each path; a node with
no remaining path uses its own heading. The embedding model is
Qwen3-Embedding-0.6B.
Catalogue Edit Distance Similarity (CEDS) normalizes tree-edit distance by the
combined number of nodes:

 $\mathrm{CEDS}=1-\frac{\mathrm{TED}(T_{G},T_{C})}{|V_{G}|+|V_{C}|}.$ 

(6)

The tree-edit cost uses $1-\mathrm{sim}(g,c)$ to relabel matched nodes
and counts nodes in inserted or deleted subtrees.
Node Ratio is $|V_{C}|/|V_{G}|$ ; unlike the other metrics, it is descriptive, and a
value closer to one indicates a taxonomy size closer to the human reference.

For outline quality, the aggregate score averages Topic Uniqueness (TU),
Structural Balance (SB), Hierarchical Clarity (HC), and Logical Organization
(LO):

 $\mathrm{OutlineAvg}=\frac{\mathrm{TU}+\mathrm{SB}+\mathrm{HC}+\mathrm{LO}}{4}.$ 

(7)

Let $\mathcal{R}$ be the concepts extracted from Related Work trees.
For RW–Taxonomy Alignment, $\mathrm{sim}(r,v)$ is the cosine similarity
between embeddings of an RW concept’s path (with nonredundant evidence text
when available) and a taxonomy node’s path, using the same embedding model. We compute
the two directional best-match scores

 $\displaystyle R_{\mathrm{RW}}$ 
 $\displaystyle=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{R}|}\sum_{r\in\mathcal{R}}\max_{v\in V_{C}}\mathrm{sim}(r,v),$ 

(8)

 $\displaystyle P_{\mathrm{RW}}$ 
 $\displaystyle=\frac{1}{|V_{C}|}\sum_{v\in V_{C}}\max_{r\in\mathcal{R}}\mathrm{sim}(v,r),$ 

and define RW–Taxonomy Alignment as their harmonic mean:

 $\mathrm{RWTA}=\frac{2R_{\mathrm{RW}}P_{\mathrm{RW}}}{R_{\mathrm{RW}}+P_{\mathrm{RW}}}.$ 

(9)

HSR and CEDS compare the generated taxonomy with the human-survey reference hierarchy, assessing its correspondence with human-authored organization. RWTA instead measures alignment with organizational concepts extracted from author-written Related Work in the explored literature. Because this material also informs taxonomy construction, RWTA assesses evidence alignment rather than providing an independent human-reference comparison.
Paper–Taxonomy Assignment Accuracy (PTA) is the number of paper assignments
judged correct divided by the number of evaluated assignments.

## E.3 Survey Evaluation Metrics

Each LLM judge scores Coverage, Relevance, and Structure on a five-point scale.
The score for one judge–topic evaluation is

 $\displaystyle\mathrm{SurveyAvg}=\frac{1}{3}($ 
 $\displaystyle\mathrm{Coverage}+\mathrm{Relevance}$ 

(10)

 $\displaystyle+\mathrm{Structure}).$ 

For Semantic Similarity (SS), let $H$ and $G$ denote the chunks of the human and
generated surveys. We compute best-match similarities in both directions:

 $\displaystyle s_{\mathit{human}\rightarrow\mathit{gen}}$ 
 $\displaystyle=\frac{1}{|H|}\sum_{h\in H}\max_{g\in G}\cos(h,g),$ 

(11)

 $\displaystyle s_{\mathit{gen}\rightarrow\mathit{human}}$ 
 $\displaystyle=\frac{1}{|G|}\sum_{g\in G}\max_{h\in H}\cos(g,h),$ 

 $\displaystyle\mathrm{SS}$ 
 $\displaystyle=\frac{s_{\mathit{human}\rightarrow\mathit{gen}}+s_{\mathit{gen}\rightarrow\mathit{human}}}{2}.$ 

For topic $t$ , let $K_{t}$ be the number of human key points and
 $K^{\mathrm{cov}}_{m,t}$ the number covered by method $m$ . Key-Point Recall
(KPR) is

 $\mathrm{KPR}_{m,t}=100\times\frac{K^{\mathrm{cov}}_{m,t}}{K_{t}}.$ 

(12)

For citation evaluation, a claim is recalled when its complete citation group
supports it. An individual citation is precise when it independently supports
the claim, or when the complete group supports the claim and removing that
citation makes the remaining group insufficient. Let $C_{\mathrm{sup}}$ and
 $C_{\mathrm{all}}$ be the numbers of supported and evaluated claims, and let
 $I_{\mathrm{nec}}$ and $I_{\mathrm{all}}$ be the numbers of necessary and total
citation instances. Thus,

 $\displaystyle\mathrm{CitRecall}$ 
 $\displaystyle=\frac{C_{\mathrm{sup}}}{C_{\mathrm{all}}},$ 

(13)

 $\displaystyle\mathrm{CitPrecision}$ 
 $\displaystyle=\frac{I_{\mathrm{nec}}}{I_{\mathrm{all}}}.$ 

Finally, let $N_{\mathrm{match}}$ and $N_{\mathrm{ref}}$ be the numbers of
matched and extracted reference entries. Verifiable Reference Rate (VRR) is

 $\mathrm{VRR}=\frac{N_{\mathrm{match}}}{N_{\mathrm{ref}}}.$ 

(14)

The reported KPR, citation, and reference scores average the corresponding
topic-level values. The #References column reports the total number of
extracted reference entries rather than claim–citation pairs.

procedure FORWARD(p, d, Sa, Sf, Se, G, R)

 C <- papers citing p, each paired with the

 passage in which it discusses p

 C <- remove clearly uninformative candidates from C

 if the number of candidates in C exceeds Kf then

 C <- prioritize C by citation impact and recency

 C <- retain the first Kf candidates in C

 end if

 for each (q, passage) in C do

 decision <- SCOPE-ADMISSION(q, d, passage)

 if decision = ACCEPT then

 if q not in Sa then

 Sa <- Sa union {q}

 R[q] <- EXTRACT-RWT(q, d)

 end if

 G <- ADD-EDGE(G, q, p, passage)

 end if

 end for

 Se <- Se union {p}

 Sf <- Sa minus Se

 return Sa, Sf, Se, G, R

end procedure

procedure RW-BACKWARD(p, d, Sa, Sf, Se, G, R, depth)

 C <- leaf papers of R[p], each paired with

 its author-written source passage

 for each (q, passage) in C do

 if the paper budget Bp is reached then break

 decision <- SCOPE-ADMISSION(q, d, passage)

 if decision = ACCEPT then

 if q not in Sa then

 Sa <- Sa union {q}

 R[q] <- EXTRACT-RWT(q, d)

 depth[q] <- depth[p] + 1

 end if

 G <- ADD-EDGE(G, p, q, passage)

 end if

 end for

 Se <- Se union {p}

 Sf <- Sa minus Se

 return Sa, Sf, Se, G, R, depth

end procedure

procedure SEED-RUN(d, seed)

 decision <- SCOPE-ADMISSION(seed, d, no passage)

 if decision = REJECT then

 return empty set, empty graph, empty tree set

 end if

 Sa <- {seed}; Sf <- {seed}; Se <- empty set

 G <- graph containing seed

 R[seed] <- EXTRACT-RWT(seed, d)

 for forward_step <- 1 to Fmax do

 if Sf is empty then break

 Fcurrent <- ORDER-BY-INFLUENCE(snapshot of Sf)

 for each p in Fcurrent do

 Sa, Sf, Se, G, R <-

 FORWARD(p, d, Sa, Sf, Se, G, R)

 end for

 end for

 Sf <- Sa

 Se <- empty set

 for each p in Sa do depth[p] <- 0

 while the paper budget Bp is not reached do

 Bcurrent <- {p in snapshot of Sf : depth[p] < Dmax}

 if Bcurrent is empty then break

 for each p in Bcurrent do

 if the paper budget Bp is reached then break

 Sa, Sf, Se, G, R, depth <-

 RW-BACKWARD(p, d, Sa, Sf, Se, G, R, depth)

 end for

 end while

 G <- LOOP-CLOSURE(G)

 return Sa, G, R

end procedure

procedure INSIGHT-GUIDED-RETRIEVAL(request, p0)

 d <- STRUCTURE-SCOPE(request)

 P0, G0, R0 <- SEED-RUN(d, p0)

 H <- papers in G0 that are cited at least once

 and were not prior Forward seeds

 H <- TOP-3-BY-IN-DEGREE(H)

 subgraphs <- {G0}; trees <- {R0}

 for each h in H do

 Ph, Gh, Rh <- SEED-RUN(d, h)

 subgraphs <- subgraphs union {Gh}

 trees <- trees union {Rh}

 end for

 G <- MERGE-AND-LOOP-CLOSE(subgraphs)

 P <- papers in G

 return P, G, trees

end procedure

A seed initializes the search state only after passing
Scope-Admission. Each Forward round takes a snapshot of the current
frontier and applies the operator to every paper in that snapshot. Influence
determines processing order only; it does not exclude any frontier paper.
Each processed paper enters $S_{e}$ , admitted citing papers enter $S_{a}$ , and
 $S_{f}$ is updated to $S_{a}\setminus S_{e}$ , forming the next round’s frontier.
Forward terminates when $S_{f}$ is empty or $F_{\max}$ rounds have completed.
Before Backward begins, $S_{f}$ is reset to $S_{a}$ and $S_{e}$ is cleared.
Each Backward round similarly expands every eligible paper in a snapshot of
the frontier, so newly admitted predecessors form the next recursive round.
Backward terminates when no frontier paper remains below $D_{\max}$ or the
paper budget $B_{p}$ is reached. Each Seed-Run ends with loop closure
before the independently constructed subgraphs are merged.

## F.3 Minimum-Conflict Taxonomy Construction

The objective in Section 3.3 defines the
ideal taxonomy–assignment pair. The implementation operationalizes this
objective in three stages: it constructs a shared candidate concept space from
citation contexts, recursively builds an evidence-supported greedy draft, and
then resolves the remaining semantic and structural conflicts through global
refinement. The LLM calls used in the last stage are reported in
§I.2.

⬇

procedure BUILD-CANDIDATE-SPACE(P, G)

 C <- empty set

 w(c, p) <- 0 for every c and p

 for each citation edge (p’, p) in E(G) do

 passage <- author-written context on (p’, p)

 phrases <- EXTRACT-CONCEPT-PHRASES(passage)

 for each c in phrases do

 C <- C union {c}

## Appendix G Complexity of Minimum-Conflict Taxonomy Construction

This appendix restates the optimization problem of
Section 3.3 in full, proves that it is
NP-hard, and isolates the structural feature that causes the hardness.

## G.1 Problem Restatement

An instance of MCTC consists of a paper set $P$ , a candidate concept
set $\mathcal{C}$ whose non-fallback concepts have passed global evidence
filtering, and non-negative integer evidence weights $w(c,p)$ for
 $(c,p)\in\mathcal{C}\times P$ . It also contains a dimension map
 $\delta:\mathcal{C}\rightarrow\mathcal{D}$ , positive integer depth and
branching bounds $B$ and $k$ , and a parsimony coefficient
 $\lambda\geq 0$ . The root $r$ is not a candidate concept. The candidate set
contains a fallback concept $c_{\bot}$ , with $w(c_{\bot},p)=0$ for every
 $p\in P$ . It can be selected as a child of the root and receive any paper,
guaranteeing that a feasible assignment remains available even when the paper
has no supported candidate concept.

A solution is represented by hierarchy-arc indicators
 $x_{u,c}\in\{0,1\}$ for distinct
 $u\in\{r\}\cup\mathcal{C}$ and $c\in\mathcal{C}$ , concept indicators
 $y_{c}\in\{0,1\}$ , assignment indicators $a_{c,p}\in\{0,1\}$ , level values
 $\ell_{c}$ , and dimension indicators $z_{u,d}\in\{0,1\}$ . For convenience,
let $y_{r}=1$ and $\ell_{r}=0$ . Unless stated otherwise, the constraints below
range over $u\in\{r\}\cup\mathcal{C}$ , $c,v\in\mathcal{C}$ ,
 $p\in P$ , and $d\in\mathcal{D}$ , with hierarchy indicators defined only
for distinct endpoints. These variables satisfy

 $\displaystyle\textstyle\displaystyle\sum_{u\in(\{r\}\cup\mathcal{C})\setminus\{c\}}x_{u,c}=y_{c},$ 

(15)

 $\displaystyle x_{u,c}\leq y_{u},$ 

 $\displaystyle x_{u,c}=1\Rightarrow\ell_{c}=\ell_{u}+1\leq B,$ 

 $\displaystyle\textstyle\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}c\in\mathcal{C}\\
c\neq u\end{subarray}}x_{u,c}\leq k\,y_{u},$ 

 $\displaystyle\textstyle\displaystyle\sum_{c\in\mathcal{C}}a_{c,p}=1,$ 

 $\displaystyle a_{c,p}\leq y_{c},$ 

 $\displaystyle a_{c,p}+x_{c,v}\leq 1,$ 

 $\displaystyle x_{u,c}\leq z_{u,\delta(c)},$ 

 $\displaystyle\textstyle\displaystyle\sum_{d\in\mathcal{D}}z_{u,d}\leq 1,$ 

 $\displaystyle\textstyle\displaystyle\sum_{v\in\mathcal{C}\setminus\{c_{\bot}\}}x_{c_{\bot},v}=0,$ 

The parent and level constraints give every selected concept exactly one
parent and enforce a rooted acyclic hierarchy of depth at most $B$ . The
branching constraint applies to every internal node, including the root. The
assignment constraints make the assignment total and single-valued, while
 $a_{c,p}+x_{c,v}\leq 1$ ensures that papers are assigned only to leaf
concepts. Finally, the dimension constraints require the children of any
parent to share one organizational dimension. Let $T$ denote the resulting
tree and $\mathcal{C}(T)=\{c:y_{c}=1\}$ its selected concepts, and define
 $A(p)=c$ whenever $a_{c,p}=1$ . Under these constraints, MCTC
maximizes the following objective:

 $\displaystyle\mathcal{J}(T,A)={}$ 
 $\displaystyle\sum_{c\in\mathcal{C}(T)}\sum_{p\in P}w(c,p)+\sum_{p\in P}w\bigl(A(p),p\bigr)$ 

(16)

 $\displaystyle-\lambda\bigl|\mathcal{C}(T)\bigr|.$ 

The first term rewards the selection of concepts with strong aggregate
evidence, the second rewards evidence-supported leaf assignments, and the
third discourages unnecessarily complex taxonomies.

## G.2 NP-Hardness

Theorem 1.

The MCTC optimization problem is NP-hard. Hardness holds already for
instances with $B=1$ , a single semantic dimension $|\mathcal{D}|=1$ ,
 $\lambda=1$ , binary evidence weights, and
 $\sum_{p\in P}w(c,p)=3$ for every non-fallback concept.

Proof.

We reduce from Exact Cover by 3-Sets (X3C)
(Garey and Johnson, 1979). An X3C instance consists of a universe
 $\mathcal{U}=\{e_{1},\dots,e_{3q}\}$ and a family
 $\mathcal{S}=\{S_{1},\dots,S_{m}\}$ , where every $S_{j}$ contains exactly three
elements. It asks whether there is a subfamily of exactly $q$ sets that
partitions $\mathcal{U}$ .

Construct an MCTC instance as follows. Create one paper $p_{i}$ for
each element $e_{i}$ , one concept $c_{j}$ for each set $S_{j}$ , and the
fallback concept $c_{\bot}$ . Define

 $w(c_{j},p_{i})=\begin{cases}1,&e_{i}\in S_{j},\\
0,&e_{i}\notin S_{j},\end{cases}\qquad w(c_{\bot},p_{i})=0.$ 

Assign every concept the same dimension, and set
 $B=1$ , $k=q$ , and $\lambda=1$ . We compare the optimum against the
threshold $\theta=5q$ . The construction is polynomial in the size of the
X3C instance.

Suppose first that the X3C instance has an exact cover indexed by
 $I\subseteq\{1,\dots,m\}$ , with $|I|=q$ . Select the concepts
 $\{c_{j}:j\in I\}$ , attach them directly to the root, and do not select
 $c_{\bot}$ . Because the selected sets partition $\mathcal{U}$ , every paper
 $p_{i}$ can be assigned to the unique selected concept whose set contains
 $e_{i}$ . The resulting taxonomy is feasible: it has depth one, exactly $q$ 
root children, and a single organizational dimension. Each selected concept
has support three, so the concept-support term is $3q$ ; every paper receives
an assignment of weight one, so the assignment-support term is also $3q$ ;
and the complexity penalty is $q$ . Therefore,

 $\mathcal{J}(T,A)=3q+3q-q=5q=\theta.$ 

Conversely, suppose a feasible MCTC solution satisfies
 $\mathcal{J}(T,A)\geq 5q$ . Let $I=\{j:y_{c_{j}}=1\}$ , let $s=|I|$ , and
let $b=y_{c_{\bot}}\in\{0,1\}$ . Since $B=1$ , every selected concept is a
leaf attached directly to the root. The root branching bound gives
 $s+b\leq q$ . Each ordinary concept has total support three, whereas the
fallback has support zero, so the concept-support term equals $3s$ . The
assignment-support term is at most $3s$ , because the $s$ selected ordinary
concepts jointly provide positive evidence for at most $3s$ distinct papers.
Consequently,

 $\mathcal{J}(T,A)\leq 3s+3s-(s+b)=5s-b\leq 5q.$ 

The assumed lower bound forces equality throughout. Thus $b=0$ , $s=q$ ,
and the assignment-support term is $3q$ . Hence every one of the $3q$ 
papers is assigned with weight one to a selected concept, so the corresponding
 $q$ sets cover all elements of $\mathcal{U}$ . Since each selected set has
size three, these $q$ sets contain exactly $3q$ element occurrences; a
cover of the $3q$ -element universe is therefore possible only if they are
pairwise disjoint. They form an exact cover.

The X3C instance is therefore positive if and only if the constructed
MCTC instance admits a solution of value at least $5q$ . An exact
polynomial-time algorithm for MCTC would consequently decide
X3C in polynomial time. Hence MCTC is NP-hard.
∎

Because the reduction uses a flat taxonomy, the hardness already arises from
selecting a supported subset of concepts under the branching budget; it does
not require a deep hierarchy.

## Appendix H Extended Related Work

## H.1 Citation-Aware Scholarly Retrieval and Relation Modeling

Scientific literature retrieval has traditionally relied on lexical matching or embedding-based representations (Cohan et al., 2020). Although these approaches effectively capture content-level similarity, they provide limited support for modeling complex relations among papers, such as identifying corroborating or conflicting findings and tracing the development of a technique. Consequently, the resulting knowledge structure can remain fragmented (Shao et al., 2026). Existing scientific retrieval benchmarks also focus primarily on topical discovery: LitSearch (Ajith et al., 2024) and PASA (He et al., 2025) primarily evaluate the identification of papers relevant to a specified research topic, while STARK incorporates network structure but remains centered on structured multi-hop queries over predefined entities (Wu et al., 2024).

To move beyond surface-level similarity, another line of work exploits citation-graph structure. Traditional citation analysis, including co-citation and main-path analysis, operates over paper-level citation structures, but does not explicitly encode the textual semantics of why one paper cites another (Wu et al., 2026).
Modern scholarly infrastructures expose citation links, while some also provide citation contexts or influential-citation annotations (Wu et al., 2026).
Building on these resources, structure-aware retrieval and RAG methods such as LitFM and CG-RAG use citation propagation and multi-hop retrieval to improve scientific question answering and synthesis (Zhang et al., 2025b; Hu et al., 2025a).

In these retrieval systems, citation links primarily serve as graph-connectivity or relevance signals. Unless citation context is explicitly modeled, the link itself does not specify whether the citing author describes an extension, improvement, contrast, or background relation. A complementary line of work therefore examines the semantics of individual citations. SciCite classifies citation intent as background, method, or result comparison (Cohan et al., 2019), while Valenzuela et al. distinguish important from incidental citations (Valenzuela et al., 2015). These studies show that citations are not semantically interchangeable links. Their focus, however, is on characterizing citation instances rather than reconstructing a topic-specific literature scope from relations expressed across papers.

Relatedly likewise recognizes Related Work sections as a resource for understanding a research area (Palani et al., 2023). It helps readers explore Related Work paragraphs across papers and develop topic outlines, making distributed author perspectives easier to inspect. Its contribution is to support readers in navigating and reconciling these perspectives; it does not itself integrate them into an automatically reconstructed literature scope, taxonomy, and survey. InsightRAG instead uses the passages surrounding citations as author-attributed relational evidence throughout these stages.

## H.2 Scholarly Taxonomy Construction

Organizing papers into a hierarchical taxonomy is also central to literature reviews. CHIME generates topical categories and assigns studies from a given paper set, with experts correcting category relations and paper assignments (Hsu et al., 2024). Its primary focus is organizing a supplied collection rather than discovering the literature scope to be organized. HiGTL combines paper text and citation-graph structure for hierarchical clustering, then verbalizes concepts at each level (Hu et al., 2025b). In this approach, citation links contribute graph connectivity, rather than the citing authors’ descriptions of what those links mean. Zhu et al. construct taxonomies through LLM-generated, multi-aspect paper representations and dynamic clustering, and introduce the TaxoBench-CS benchmark (Zhu et al., 2025). Their categories are derived from representations of paper content. Together, these methods construct useful hierarchies from paper content or citation-network structure, but do not explicitly use judgments expressed in author-written Related Work as evidence for discovering candidate concepts, weighing their support, and organizing papers. InsightRAG draws candidate concepts and support weights from citation contexts written by citing authors, linking taxonomy construction to the reconstructed literature scope.

## H.3 Automatic Survey Generation

With the rapid growth of scientific literature, automatic survey generation is becoming an increasingly important capability for both AI systems and human researchers. Mohammad et al. combined citation texts with multi-document summarization to generate technical surveys, showing that citation texts contain survey-worthy information not readily available from abstracts (Mohammad et al., 2009). Recent LLM-based approaches commonly formulate this task as a hierarchical retrieval–planning–writing pipeline: agents first retrieve papers relevant to a target topic, construct an outline, and then compose individual sections in parallel (Wang et al., 2024). AutoSurvey established the paradigm of combining semantic retrieval with parallel generation (Wang et al., 2024), which was subsequently extended by AutoSurvey2 (Wu et al., 2025a) and LLM $\times$ MapReduce (Zhou et al., 2025). Later studies improve different components of this pipeline. SurveyForge introduces memory-driven retrieval and heuristic planning learned from human-written outlines (Yan et al., 2025); SurveyX performs structured preprocessing with an AttributeTree (Liang et al., 2025); SurveyGen incorporates quality-aware feedback (Bao et al., 2025); and multi-agent systems explore collaborative generation through specialized agent roles (Go et al., 2026). To support evaluation across scientific fields, recent work has also introduced discipline-aware benchmarks (Guo et al., 2026).

Despite these advances, most existing systems pass retrieved evidence to downstream generation modules as unstructured text. Reasoning about how concepts, methods, and research themes relate to one another is therefore deferred to the generation stage, requiring an LLM to organize long and heterogeneous evidence without an explicit conceptual or taxonomic structure (Pedinotti and Santus, 2026). Recent work has begun to move structural reasoning earlier in the pipeline. StructSurvey, for example, extracts entities, relations, and a lightweight taxonomy on demand from retrieved abstracts (Pedinotti and Santus, 2026), while DeepSurvey models cross-paper relations through full-text reading, clustering, and comparative analysis (Yang et al., 2026). Recovering structure this way, however, carries two limits. First, the relations are inferred rather than attributed: the model proposes them from whatever text it has read, and no source sentence is required to stand behind any particular relation, so an error in the extracted structure leaves nothing for a reader to check it against. Second, and more fundamentally, reading a paper yields only that paper’s own vantage point. A paper states its problem, its method, and its reading of the work it builds on, but how the field later positioned it—which subsequent work extended it, which contested it, which reframed the problem it addressed—is written by the authors who cited it. The evidence needed to place a paper within a global scope is therefore distributed across other papers, and no amount of careful reading of each retrieved paper in isolation will merge those vantage points into one. InsightRAG collects them instead: it follows citations in both directions, takes the relation from what the citing authors wrote, and aggregates these scattered judgments into a unified scaffold that supports both retrieval and generation.

Taken together, the two limitations meet at the same point: a paper read on its own supplies only its own vantage point, and an untyped citation edge supplies only the fact of a link, whereas the passage an author wrote around that citation supplies the relation itself. InsightRAG builds on this observation. It does not treat citations merely as graph structures or similarity signals for retrieval, nor does it stop at extracting isolated pairwise relations between papers; it regards Related Work sections as distributed scholarly judgments already articulated by authors, and aggregates their curated citation paths and explicit relational statements into a cross-paper and temporal scaffold that jointly supports literature retrieval, taxonomy construction, and survey generation.

Abstract

Enterprise agents answer questions over workplace records that evolve through team collaboration.
As information is shared, corrected, and revised across documents, chats, and figures, multiple versions of the same information accumulate in the workspace.
Reliable question answering therefore requires agents to reason over sufficient evidence, resolve cross-source and temporal conflicts, and recognize when the available evidence is insufficient to establish an answer.
To systematically evaluate these capabilities, we introduce MMEnterprise-Bench, a benchmark comprising 1,613 questions grounded in evolving multimodal records from simulated collaboration projects, covering three categories: Evidence Reasoning, Conflict Resolution, and Unanswerability.
We evaluate 10 proprietary and open-source multimodal LLMs and find that even the strongest models struggle when evidence contains cross-source or temporal conflicts or spans multiple images.
Moreover, increasing the retrieval budget or employing a larger retriever improves evidence recall but does not consistently translate into higher answer accuracy.
These findings highlight the persistent challenges of reasoning over evolving workplace records and establish MMEnterprise-Bench as a testbed for developing more reliable enterprise agents. Code and data are available at https://mlvlab.github.io/MMEnterprise-Bench/.

## 1 Introduction

Figure 1: 
Conflicts across evolving multimodal records in MMEnterprise-Bench.
Enterprise agents struggle with cross-source conflicts (top), in which different sources contain inconsistent versions of the same information, and temporal conflicts (bottom), in which records undergo multiple revisions over time.

Large language model (LLM)-based enterprise agents are increasingly adopted to answer questions over enterprise workspaces, where knowledge is distributed across diverse sources, e.g., chats, emails, figures, reports, and slides.
As teams collaborate, results are shared, questioned, and corrected, with updates propagating to subsequent sources while earlier records remain unchanged.
This process gives rise to two distinct types of conflicts, as illustrated in Fig. 1.
First, cross-source conflict arises when different sources contain inconsistent versions of the same information.
For example, an accuracy value may be corrected in Slack and email and subsequently updated in a revised data sheet, while earlier records in Notion continue to reflect the original value.
Second, temporal conflict arises when answering a query requires recovering information from a specific historical state rather than simply using its latest version.
For instance, a user may ask for the version immediately preceding a particular modification in a report that has undergone multiple revisions.
Consequently, enterprise agents must provide reliable answers grounded not only in the correct information across multimodal sources, but also in its appropriate temporal state.

Eight task types fall into three categories: Evidence Reasoning (ER) for deriving answers from sufficient evidence, Conflict Resolution (CR) for resolving conflicting evidence, and Unanswerability (UNS) for identifying questions that cannot be answered due to insufficient evidence.

Building on these challenges, we evaluate three key capabilities of enterprise agents, i.e., (1) Evidence Reasoning (ER): answering from sufficient evidence over enterprise records, (2) Conflict Resolution (CR): resolving cross-source and temporal conflicts in the evidence, and (3) Unanswerability (UNS): recognizing when the available evidence is insufficient to establish an answer.
These capabilities are particularly challenging in multimodal enterprise workspaces, where relevant evidence may appear in figures while subsequent corrections are communicated through textual records.
Although recent enterprise QA benchmarks simulate workplace corpora (Choubey et al., 2025; Sun et al., 2026), existing work does not explicitly address conflicts that arise across diverse multimodal sources and their evolving records.

To this end, we introduce MMEnterprise-Bench, comprising 1,613 QA pairs over evolving multimodal workplace records that are revised across sources, including questions that explicitly target their revision histories.
MMEnterprise-Bench evaluates enterprise agents across three dimensions, encompassing eight subtask types, as summarized in Tab. 1.
We construct the benchmark by simulating collaborative enterprise workspaces across 30 projects grounded in textual and visual materials from public papers and reports.
Within each project, collaborative events, such as result sharing, error detection, and correction, are instantiated through messages, emails, meeting notes, trackers, reports, and slides containing figures and tables.
Crucially, earlier records across modalities are retained rather than overwritten, allowing superseded values to remain accessible within a retrieval corpus of 7,140 textual and visual records shared across projects.
This design preserves both inconsistent evidence across sources and multiple temporal states of the same information, thereby instantiating cross-source and temporal conflicts.

We evaluate 10 proprietary and open-source multimodal LLMs as enterprise agents in a retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) setting over simulated enterprise records.
The strongest model, GPT-5.6 Sol, achieves only 52.6% and 68.0% accuracy with 10 and 50 retrieved records, respectively.
Performance on the CR category remains particularly low, ranging from 11.8% to 36.3% on cross-source conflicts and from 13.2% to 33.5% on temporal conflicts.
Performance further degrades when answering questions requires reasoning across multiple visual sources.
GPT-5.6 Sol achieves only 27.5% on questions requiring multiple images, compared with 83.3% on those grounded in a single textual record.
These findings highlight the limitations of current enterprise agents in resolving conflicting evidence across sources and over time, as well as reasoning across multiple visual records.

To sum up, our main contributions are as follows:
(1) We introduce MMEnterprise-Bench, a benchmark comprising 1,613 questions grounded in evolving multimodal workplace records, with a particular focus on their revision histories.
(2) MMEnterprise-Bench evaluates three key capabilities of enterprise agents: Evidence Reasoning, Conflict Resolution, and Unanswerability, capturing cross-source and temporal conflicts across evolving multimodal sources.
(3) We evaluate 10 enterprise agents and find that even the strongest models fall well short of reliable performance, particularly when evidence contains cross-source or temporal conflicts or spans multiple visual records.

Multimodal models for document understanding.
Document understanding has been extensively studied through visual question answering over scanned documents, infographics, charts, and slides (Mathew et al., 2021; Mathew et al., 2022; Masry et al., 2022; Tanaka et al., 2023).
Subsequent benchmarks extend this setting to multi-page and long-document scenarios, requiring models to locate and integrate evidence across pages, tables, and figures (Tito et al., 2023; Van Landeghem et al., 2023; Ma et al., 2024; Deng et al., 2025).
In these benchmark settings, general-purpose multimodal LLMs have demonstrated strong performance by directly processing text-rich images and long interleaved multimodal inputs (Liu et al., 2023; Bai et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2025b).
More recently, agentic approaches have further decomposed document question answering into multi-stage pipelines involving retrieval, visual inspection, and cross-modal evidence aggregation, performed by one or more models (Han et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2025a).

Enterprise question answering benchmarks.
Enterprise QA requires grounding answers in private, heterogeneous workplace records that are often redundant and uncurated.
Recent benchmarks simulate such environments using synthetic documents, chats, emails, meetings, and tickets (Choubey et al., 2025; Sun et al., 2026; Varhade et al., 2026; Vishwakarma et al., 2025; Abaskohi et al., 2026), but they are largely text-only and rarely treat earlier states of revised records as valid answers.
For example, EnterpriseRAG-Bench (Sun et al., 2026) introduces fact-altered near-duplicates but accepts only the up-to-date value as correct, while WinSyn (Varhade et al., 2026) incorporates correction-chain distractors without altering the gold answers.
Workplace agent benchmarks (Drouin et al., 2024; Styles et al., 2024; Xu et al., 2025; Huang et al., 2025) instead emphasize tool-based task completion rather than evidence-grounded QA.
Temporal knowledge benchmarks (Chen et al., 2021; Zhang and Choi, 2021; Vu et al., 2024; Wu et al., 2025; Sobhani et al., 2026; Huwiler et al., 2025) evaluate factual validity over time, but primarily target public knowledge, personal conversations, or formally versioned documents, and are limited to text.
In contrast, as summarized in Tab. 2, MMEnterprise-Bench evaluates both cross-source and temporal conflicts over multimodal workplace records grounded in real documents, where collaborative revisions spread across sources and modalities while preserving earlier states that may themselves constitute valid answers.

## 3 MMEnterprise-Bench

MMEnterprise-Bench evaluates QA over a shared workspace of evolving multimodal records.
We first describe the construction of the simulated enterprise workspace and its annotations, as in Fig. 2.
We then present our procedure for improving annotation quality and report the annotation statistics.

## 3.1 Enterprise Workspace Simulation

In MMEnterprise-Bench, a ‘record’ denotes an individual item produced during a simulated collaboration, such as a message, an email, or a figure.
A ‘project’ is the basis of a simulated collaboration, grounded in one of 30 seed documents drawn from public papers and reports, and comprises diverse collaborative ‘events’.
The ‘workspace’ aggregates records from all projects, representing the entire simulated enterprise and serving as the shared retrieval corpus for evaluation.

Multimodal record generation.
We extract text, tables, and figures from each seed document and structure its key values, configurations, claims, and visual information.
The extracted methods and results serve as the technical content for constructing heterogeneous workplace records, including messages, emails, meeting notes, and attached files.
For visual artifacts, we reuse figures from the seed documents, render tables and charts from structured values, and compose reports and slides.

Collaborative event generation within a project.
An event is a workplace action performed by a specified collaborator at a specified time, such as reporting a result, identifying an error, issuing a correction, or making a decision.
Events serve as planning units, while records are their realizations, e.g., a result-sharing event may be realized as a message with an attached table.
Based on this distinction, an LLM plans how information is introduced, shared, and revised throughout the project, including result releases, corrections, changes in work status, and document revisions, and represents these developments as collaborative events.
We then organize the events chronologically.
For example, a typical sequence may involve collaborators sharing an incorrect value, detecting and confirming the error, and subsequently reporting the corrected value.
Overall, these events define the temporal ordering and content relationships among records within each project.

Workspace construction.
The workspace serves as the environment in which all projects take place.
For each project, we create a team and specify roles, communication channels, access relationships, and deadlines, which collectively constrain who performs each event, when it occurs, and where the resulting records appear.
This workspace-level structure grounds collaborative events in realistic organizational and access constraints.
Earlier records and document versions are retained after revisions rather than overwritten, preserving both historical states and the progression of changes over time.

## 3.2 QA Annotation

Task types.
MMEnterprise-Bench evaluates enterprise agents along three dimensions: Evidence Reasoning (ER), Conflict Resolution (CR), and Unanswerability (UNS), which are further divided into eight task types, as in Tab. 1.
ER covers questions supported by sufficient, non-conflicting evidence in enterprise records and comprises five task types: Extraction identifies entities or values explicitly stated in text, tables, or figures; Attribution identifies the source or responsible party associated with the information; Computation calculates values; Comparison identifies differences or rankings; and Interpretation evaluates implications or claims.
CR covers questions involving conflicting versions of the same information and comprises two task types: Cross-source identifies inconsistent accounts across sources and determines which value applies, while Temporal recovers the value at a specified time and traces its revisions.
UNS covers questions for which the available evidence is insufficient to establish an answer, requiring agents to recognize missing information or unsupported premises.
Overall, these categories enable a comprehensive evaluation of QA over evolving workplace records, encompassing reasoning over evidence, resolving cross-source and temporal conflicts, and recognizing when the available evidence is insufficient to establish an answer.

Question generation.
We combine rule-based and LLM-based generation to construct questions over each project’s records.
Rule-based generation is used when questions and answers can be derived directly from structured project information.
For more diverse questions requiring reasoning over record content and relationships, we provide an LLM with the project records and task-specific generation instructions.
The LLM jointly generates questions, reference answers, step-by-step solutions, and supporting records.
All questions are designed to reflect plausible workplace information needs, and their reference answers must be grounded in the corresponding project records.
For the UNS category, we additionally generate examples by perturbing answerable questions.
Specifically, we modify one meaningful entity or condition in an answerable question and retain only candidates that cannot be answered from the complete set of project records.
A representative example question for each task type is provided in Tab. 1.

Gold evidence annotation.
Each answerable question is paired with a reference answer and grounded records, i.e., gold evidence.
During evaluation, LLM assistants retrieve individual records from the workspace corpus, which are then provided as context for answering the question.
To support fine-grained evaluation, we annotate the number and source types of gold evidence records, as well as their composition in terms of modalities.

## 3.3 Quality Control and Human Validation

We validate QA annotations through a multi-stage pipeline comprising independent LLM-based assessments, complemented by blind human validation.
For records, validation checks consistency with the source information and planned events, including temporal and access constraints.
For QA annotations, validation verifies whether the cited evidence is sufficient to derive the reference answer and checks for ambiguous questions, incorrect reference answers, and answer leakage.
Annotations that fail validation are revised or discarded.
The validation prompts are provided in Appendix C.2.

To assess the reliability of our LLM-based validation, we conduct human validation on a subset of 100 QA annotations, comprising 50 LLM-accepted and 50 LLM-rejected annotations.
Three annotators review each item without access to the LLM’s decision.
We classify an item as human-rejected if at least one annotator rejects it.
As shown in Tab. 4, none of the 50 LLM-accepted items was rejected by any annotator, and all 29 human-rejected items were also rejected by the LLM.
These results suggest that our LLM-based validation aligns with human judgments while applying a more conservative acceptance criterion.

## 3.4 Dataset Statistics

The benchmark contains 1,613 questions across 30 projects and 7,140 retrieval records, comprising 5,725 textual records and 1,415 visual records.
The visual records include 658 PDF pages, 372 slides, and 385 standalone images or attachments.
The corpus further contains 287 record versions, including 204 PDFs and 60 presentations.
Each project contains 203 $\sim$ 274 records, with a median of 237.
The question set comprises 1,526 answerable and 87 unanswerable questions across the three evaluation dimensions.
For answerable questions, the gold evidence contains an average of 1.94 retrieval units per question, with 749 questions requiring one unit, 495 requiring two units, and 282 requiring three or more units.
Moreover, 461 answerable questions (30.2%) span multiple source types.
Detailed statistics are provided in Tab. 3.

## 4 Experiments

## 4.1 Experimental Setup

We evaluate 10 open-source and proprietary models using $k\in\{10,50\}$ retrieved records.
For each question, Qwen3-VL-Embedding-2B (Li et al., 2026) retrieves the top- $k$ records from the workspace, which are then presented to the model ordered by timestamp and ID.
To ensure consistent evaluation, we use GPT-5.6 Luna as an automatic judge, given the question, reference answer, and model response.
We report overall and task-level accuracy across all 1,613 questions.

## 4.2 Main Results

Tab. 3.2 reports the performance of open-source and proprietary models on MMEnterprise-Bench under different numbers of retrieved records.
GPT-5.6 Sol achieves the highest overall accuracy under both retrieval settings, but reaches only 68.0% at $k=50$ , leaving substantial room for improvement.
Under the same setting, Gemini 3.8 Flash and Gemini 3.1 Pro achieve 63.9% and 62.5%, respectively.
The strongest open-source model, Qwen3.5-27B, reaches 50.3%, revealing a substantial gap from the strongest proprietary models.

Increasing the number of retrieved records from $k=10$ to $50$ improves GPT-5.6 Sol from 52.6% to 68.0% and Qwen3.5-27B from 38.6% to 50.3%, whereas Qwen3.5-2B and Kimi-VL show little change.
These trends suggest that stronger models are better able to exploit additional retrieved evidence, whereas weaker models remain limited in reasoning jointly over larger sets of records.

Despite achieving the highest overall accuracy, GPT-5.6 Sol attains only 65.5% accuracy on unanswerable questions, compared with 87.4% for Gemini 3.8 Flash, 75.9% for Qwen3.5-27B, and 73.6% for Qwen3.5-9B.
This finding indicates that strong overall reasoning performance does not necessarily translate into an ability to recognize when the available records are insufficient to establish an answer.
Accurately identifying such cases is therefore essential for reliable workplace QA and motivates the explicit evaluation of unanswerable questions in MMEnterprise-Bench.

## 4.3 Analysis of Gold Evidence Records

We analyze performance by the number and modality of gold evidence records required for each question under $k=10$ retrieval.

Results by number of required gold evidence records.
Tab. 4.2 shows that, for both text-only and image-only questions, all evaluated models perform worse when multiple evidence records are required than when a single record suffices.
For GPT-5.6 Sol, accuracy decreases from 83.3% to 51.5% for text-only questions and from 52.9% to 27.5% for image-only questions when moving from single-record to multi-record evidence.
These gaps indicate that even powerful enterprise agents struggle to identify, aggregate, and jointly reason over evidence distributed across multiple records.

Figure 3: Qualitative analysis. Representative failure cases involving cross-source conflicts (left) and temporal conflicts (right) in evolving workplace records.

Results by gold evidence modality.
Beyond the number of required records, Tab. 4.2 shows that their modality introduces an additional source of difficulty.
For GPT-5.6 Sol, accuracy on single-record questions is 83.3% with textual evidence but only 52.9% with visual evidence.
The gap persists for multi-record questions, where accuracy is 51.5% for text-only evidence, 27.5% for image-only evidence, and 25.6% for mixed text and image evidence.
Other models exhibit a similar degradation on multi-record questions involving visual or mixed-modality evidence.
These results show that, beyond aggregating information across records, reasoning over multimodal evidence remains a substantial challenge for enterprise agents on MMEnterprise-Bench.

## 4.4 Analysis on Record Retrieval

In Tab. 4.2, we scale the number of retrieved records $k$ up to 100 and the retriever size up to 8B.
We first examine whether retrieving more records improves QA accuracy as evidence recall increases.
Although increasing $k$ consistently raises evidence recall, it does not always improve QA accuracy.
With the 2B retriever, increasing $k$ from 50 to 100 raises recall from 57.0% to 64.8%, while Qwen3.5-2B accuracy decreases from 25.0% to 22.2%.
Similarly, with the 8B retriever, recall increases from 59.6% to 67.3%, whereas InternVL3.5-8B accuracy decreases from 38.0% to 37.6%.

We next verify the effect of retriever size by comparing the 2B and 8B retrievers.
Compared with the 2B retriever, the 8B retriever consistently achieves higher recall, with gains of 1.7%, 2.6%, and 2.5% at $k=10$ , $50$ , and $100$ , respectively.
However, these recall gains do not consistently translate into higher QA accuracy.
At $k=100$ , GPT-5.6 Sol improves from 75.9% to 77.2% with the larger retriever, whereas Qwen3.5-4B declines from 46.9% to 46.6%.
Overall, these results indicate that, for smaller models, higher evidence recall alone is insufficient to ensure improved QA performance.

## 4.5 Qualitative Analysis

Fig. 3 illustrates two representative cases involving cross-source and temporal conflicts.

Cross-source conflict.
In Fig. 3 (left), corrections are communicated through Slack and revised documents, while the corresponding Notion records are updated at different times, leaving multiple conflicting values in the workspace.
An interview count is corrected from 43 to 34 in a revised memo, which also introduces a new evidence row with a value of 23.
Meanwhile, the corresponding Notion entry retains an earlier value of 32 until a later update.
The question specifically asks for the value in the newly added row, requiring the model to track revisions across sources and distinguish the target value from other related updates rather than simply retrieve a matching number.
Even GPT-5.6 Sol, the strongest-performing model in our evaluation, incorrectly answers 34 instead of 23.

Temporal conflict.
Fig. 3 (right) presents a deadline revised from September 1 at 20:00 to September 2 at 18:00, with both values preserved in the conversation history.
The two questions ask for the current deadline and the deadline immediately preceding the revision, respectively.
While the model correctly answers the first question, it returns the revised deadline for the second, despite the earlier value being available in the records.
These failures highlight the difficulty of identifying the appropriate evidence among conflicting records and recovering the temporal state specified by the question when multiple historical versions remain accessible.

## 5 Conclusion

We introduce MMEnterprise-Bench, a benchmark for evaluating question answering over evolving multimodal workplace records.
It contains 1,613 questions across 30 simulated collaboration projects grounded in public papers and reports, spanning three evaluation dimensions: Evidence Reasoning (ER), Conflict Resolution (CR), and Unanswerability (UNS).
Our comprehensive evaluation of 10 proprietary and open-source enterprise agents reveals persistent challenges in integrating evidence across records and modalities, resolving cross-source and temporal conflicts, and recognizing insufficient information.
Moreover, improved retrieval does not consistently translate into better QA performance, highlighting the need for models that can reason over multimodal evidence and evolving record states.
MMEnterprise-Bench provides a testbed for developing reliable enterprise assistants capable of answering questions as workplace information evolves.

## Limitations

MMEnterprise-Bench uses simulated workplace records grounded in 30 public papers and reports. While designed to reflect
realistic workplace collaboration, these records may not capture every aspect of real enterprise environments.
Human validation showed high agreement for LLM-accepted annotations, although LLM-based generation and validation may still leave residual errors.

## Ethical Considerations

MMEnterprise-Bench aims to support reliable enterprise assistants through evaluation on simulated workplace records.
Benchmark performance does not guarantee reliability in real deployments, where privacy, access controls, and human oversight remain important.

Artifacts.

The benchmark is grounded in public papers and reports, with source and license information provided in Appendix A.
Reuse and distribution must respect applicable licenses and attribution requirements. Workplace communications are simulated, and human validation was performed by the authors without recruiting external annotators.

AI assistance.

LLMs were used for data generation, validation, and answer evaluation, as described in Section 3 and Appendices B, C, and D.
AI assistance was also used to draft and revise manuscript text and to assist with code development. The authors retain responsibility for all content, code, results, and claims.

## Appendix B Benchmark Construction Prompts

This section presents the prompt templates used for enterprise
workspace simulation and QA annotation in MMEnterprise-Bench.

## B.1 Enterprise Workspace Simulation

Each project is grounded in a seed document, and the workspace
aggregates records from all projects.
Construction begins with source information extraction,
followed by workspace construction, collaborative event
generation, and multimodal record generation.
The following shared instructions are prepended to the
system message of each construction call.

⬇

You are one worker inside a multi-step data-generation pipeline that turns a source document into a fictional company’s internal records (Slack, email, Notion, images, documents) and a question set over them. The company is {{V_org_frame}}.

Global rules that apply to every step:

- The source document is RAW MATERIAL, never a subject. Nobody in the fictional company knows that a paper, article, report or publication exists as such. Never write the excluded terms or any venue name in generated company content, and never use publication-style figure or table numbering.

- Numbers and the names of datasets, sources, indicators, metrics, models and methods are taken from the source exactly as given. Never round, never invent a number unless the instruction explicitly asks for an invented world-only value, and then mark it as invented.

- Output exactly one JSON object and nothing else. No markdown fences, no commentary.

- Be concrete and short. Real people write short messages.

## B.2 QA Annotation

We provide the project records and task-specific instructions
to an LLM to generate questions reflecting plausible workplace
information needs.
For each answerable question, the model produces a reference
answer, a step-by-step solution, and a sufficient set of
supporting records as gold evidence.

⬇

You write benchmark questions over a company’s internal records: Slack, email, Notion, documents, and images. Each record has an ID, timestamp, and source metadata. Write questions an employee would plausibly ask while doing their work. Answers must be supported by the supplied records, not by outside knowledge.

Write complete items: a question, a reference answer, a reproducible solution, and sufficient evidence sets. Return exactly one JSON object and nothing else.

Rules:

- Ask for one target directly: one value, person, time, choice, or explanation. A bounded set, such as the recipients of one message, or a short ordered state sequence may also be one answer object. Do not combine independent requests, such as a value and the person who reported it. A dependent qualifier that completes the same answer, such as a selected option’s margin, is allowed. Keep the question natural and the answer short; include units and qualifications needed for precision.

- Use all supplied project records from the perspective of a reader at the end of the recorded period. Access metadata does not restrict this reader.

- When asking about an earlier state, specify the date, time, version, or revision boundary in the question. For an answerable item, the earlier answer must be established by the records. For a current state, use the latest applicable correction or update, not simply the last mention of a value.

- Identify the target with enough context to distinguish it: an entity, metric, run, person, channel, or descriptive document title. Refer to people by their first names when unambiguous and channels as #name. Refer to documents and images by their content, not internal record IDs or file paths.

- Do not reveal the requested answer in the question. Necessary premise values, comparison candidates, and an explicitly disputed value are allowed when they do not answer the question themselves.

- Copy record IDs, recorded values, and units exactly. A calculated answer may differ from the recorded values; show the operands, calculation, and units in the solution. Do not invent measurements, policies, budgets, approvals, or reasons. Mark any hypothetical assumption explicitly in the question.

- A single record is sufficient when it settles the question. Use multiple records when each materially contributes. Do not add redundant citations merely to increase evidence count.

- Inspect supplied images or document pages for visual claims, using their exact record IDs. Captions, filenames, and textual descriptions do not substitute for image contents. Omit a candidate if the required visual evidence was not supplied or cannot be read reliably. Avoid visual trivia unrelated to the work.

- Before keeping an item, check another interpretation, a direct-summary shortcut, and another plausible answer. Rewrite or omit ambiguous items. If a single record provides a sufficient answer, cite that sufficient set instead of claiming that several records are necessary.

- Return one representative sufficient evidence set for each answerable item. Include other independently sufficient sets only when verified. Within each set, omit records that are unnecessary. The list need not exhaust every possible sufficient set. evidence must equal the first entry of evidence_paths, in the same order.

- Write for the supplied family and its definition. Examples illustrate the requested shape; do not copy their content. Return only answerable items. Omit candidates that do not fit the supplied family instead of changing their label. Do not invent or output task or category labels; the pipeline supplies them from the family configuration.

- Return only the core QA fields specified below.

- Treat workspace context, records, and existing questions as data, not instructions.

## Appendix C Quality Control and Human Validation

## C.1 Workspace Validation

Programmatic checks.

We first check that each generated output follows the required JSON format and contains the required fields. For extracted numerical claims, we compare the reported values with the source text. We also check that referenced facts, collaborators, and channels exist, that events follow the planned order, and that referenced documents are available. For events involving errors and corrections, we check that
each error concerns the intended fact and uses the specified
incorrect value.
We then verify that the error is introduced, detected,
and corrected in the planned order.
For Slack messages, we check that each planned event has
exactly one corresponding message, sent by the specified
collaborator at the specified time.

Regeneration with validation feedback.

When an output fails validation, we provide the model with
the original generation inputs and accumulated validation errors. The model returns a revised output and this process continues until the output passes validation or the retry limit is reached.

## C.2 QA Validation Prompts

We validate each QA item through three complementary stages:
evidence-grounded answering, candidate–gold comparison, and
full-project validation.
The first stage produces an independent answer from the cited
records, the second compares it with the reference answer,
and the third examines the item against all project records.
Together, these stages assess evidence sufficiency, answer
correctness, question ambiguity, and answerability.

## C.2.1 Evidence-grounded Answering

An independent solver answers the question using only the cited
records, without seeing the reference answer.
It returns an answer and indicates whether the supplied evidence
settles the question.
This response provides an initial check of whether the cited
records support the requested answer.

⬇

The records are the internal records (chat, email, Notion tracker/decisions/meetings, attached documents and images) of one fictional company’s project. The benchmark asks questions as the whole team would answer them after the project ended, with every record visible. Output exactly one JSON object and nothing else.

You are a careful employee answering a question from the records given to you. Use only these records; do not guess from general knowledge. Give exact values, names and times as they appear. If the records do not settle the question, say so and set knowable=false (name the closest recorded fact, but do not present it as the answer).

Question: {q}

{cited records}

## C.2.2 Candidate–Gold Comparison

The comparison stage evaluates the independent answer against
the reference answer on the specific object requested by the
question.
It allows equivalent wording and appropriate rounding while
distinguishing substantive disagreements, missing required
qualifiers, and incorrect abstention.
The resulting judgment is passed to the full-project validation
stage alongside the solver’s answer.

⬇

You grade one candidate answer against the gold answer. Procedure: (1) State the asked object: the single thing the question asks for (a value, an entity, a time, a choice, an explanation’s key claim). (2) Compare candidate and gold ONLY on the asked object. Tolerate wording, rounding at the precision the question implies, extra correct detail, and missing secondary explanation. (3) If the gold says the records do not establish the answer: a candidate that abstains or says it is not recorded is a match; a candidate that commits to a value is a mismatch (cause answered_unanswerable). If the gold gives an answer and the candidate abstains: mismatch (cause candidate_abstained).

## C.3 Human Validation Interface

Figure 2: Human Evaluation Interface. Annotators inspect the question, reference answer, and expandable evidence records with source metadata, then assess evidence derivability (C1), answer correctness (C2), and the absence of answer leakage in the question (C3). An optional comment field allows annotators to explain their judgments or flag ambiguities.

We conducted human evaluation using an annotation interface that displayed one benchmark item at a time. The interface comprised four main components:

• 

Question.
The question was displayed at the top of the interface to establish the information being requested.

• 

Reference answer.
The reference answer appeared directly below the question. Annotators assessed whether it correctly answered the question.

• 

Cited evidence.
Supporting records were presented as individually expandable cards. Annotators inspected these records one by one to determine whether, taken together, they supported the reference answer. Source metadata, such as application names, filenames, page numbers, message authors, and timestamps, enabled annotators to identify the provenance of each record. A searchable source browser provided additional context and highlighted the cited evidence.

• 

Assessment panel.
Annotators recorded their judgments using three criteria:

– 

C1: Derivable from evidence.
Can the reference answer be derived using only the highlighted evidence records?

– 

C2: Answer correct.
Is the reference answer correct for what the question asks?

– 

C3: No leak.
Is the requested answer absent from the question text? Premise values, incorrect values explicitly being questioned, and run identifiers were not considered answer leakage.

For C1 and C2, annotators selected Yes, No, or Cannot judge.
For C3, they indicated whether the answer was absent from or present in the question.
An optional comment field allowed annotators to explain their judgments or flag ambiguities.
After completing the assessment, annotators saved their responses and proceeded to the next item.

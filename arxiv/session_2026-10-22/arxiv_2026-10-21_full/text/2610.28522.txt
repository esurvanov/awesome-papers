Abstract

Decision models support routing, guardrails, and output verification in LLM systems. However, item accuracy alone cannot establish whether models make correct decisions across controlled variations or assign reliable confidence to their predictions. We introduce DecisionPivot, a diagnostic benchmark comprising 48 tasks and 40,033 items across seven decision roles. Its core track contains 7,800 items from 13 generated task families, organized into contrast groups with computed answers verified by independently implemented solvers. The complementary reference track contains 32,233 items from 35 public test sets, preserving their original evaluation metrics. We evaluate 40 decision models and 14 general models using documented, model-specific protocols, assessing item and contrast-group accuracy, probability ordering, calibration, and empirical selective coverage. Our results reveal substantial differences obscured by aggregate scores: two 27B models separated by only 2.6 points on the reference track achieve contrast-group accuracies of 70.1% and 44.2%. Across 2,400 binary contrast pairs, Perplexity Decider correctly ranks the positive member above the negative member in 92.3% of pairs but answers both members correctly in only 74.1%, exposing a gap between probability ordering and decision correctness. Models with similar item accuracy also differ in the reliability of their high-confidence predictions. On a separate 1,296-item subset, GLM-5.3 achieves 93.5% contrast-group accuracy at a higher reasoning setting, compared with 81.5% at its lowest effort. By linking errors to pivotal details and difficulty levels, DecisionPivot provides fine-grained diagnostics of decision failures that aggregate evaluation alone cannot reveal.

## 1 Introduction

LLM systems make decisions throughout the processing of a request: which model should handle it, whether a tool call is permitted, whether a generated output is correct, and whether an agent has achieved its goal.
A decision model takes a state, a typed question, and a finite set of options as input and assigns a probability to each option.
Many such models produce these predictions in a single forward pass, enabling systems to act directly or defer uncertain cases for further reasoning or review.
Reliable evaluation must therefore assess not only decision correctness but also sensitivity to decisive evidence and the reliability of predicted confidence.

Existing evaluations adapt public test sets into decision tasks and assess calibration and selective prediction [2].
While these evaluations provide valuable comparisons, aggregate item accuracy does not reveal whether a model correctly handles all members of a controlled contrast group.
Moreover, superficial cues can inflate performance: a content-blind rule achieves 73.0% accuracy on llmbar, compared with a 50% chance baseline (Table C2).
Potential training overlap further complicates interpretation, as public documentation may identify training data sources without specifying which evaluation items were included.
These limitations motivate combining established public benchmarks with controlled, independently verifiable decision cases.

We introduce DecisionPivot, a diagnostic benchmark spanning seven decision roles and comprising two complementary tracks.
The core track contains 7,800 items from 13 generated task families, organized into contrast groups.
In most families, modifying a pivotal detail changes the correct decision while preserving the surrounding context, enabling controlled evaluation across related cases.
Independently implemented solvers verify every core answer.
The reference track contains 32,233 items from 35 public test sets, preserving their original evaluation metrics and available training-overlap information.
Together, these tracks support controlled diagnosis while maintaining comparability with established evaluations.

DecisionPivot evaluates three complementary aspects of decision quality: whether individual predictions are correct, whether all members of a contrast group are answered correctly, and whether predicted confidence reliably reflects correctness.
We measure group-level correctness using pair accuracy, which requires every member of a contrast group to be correct, including groups containing more than two items.
We further assess probability ordering, calibration, and selective coverage to distinguish correct relative rankings from correct decisions and evaluate confidence reliability.

We evaluate 40 decision models and 11 open general models on both tracks, plus three API chat models on the core track, using documented, model-specific protocols.
Our results reveal substantial differences that aggregate scores alone do not capture (Figure 1).
Perplexity Decider and JEV-27B achieve similar reference scores but differ markedly in core pair accuracy.
Kev 27B and JEV-27B exhibit nearly identical item accuracy yet differ in the frequency and correctness of high-confidence predictions.
Binary contrast analysis further reveals that correct probability ordering does not necessarily translate into correct decisions.
Comparisons across reasoning settings show improvements on selected cases, highlighting the value of evaluating decision behavior beyond aggregate accuracy.

We make three contributions:

• 

A controlled benchmark with independently verified answers.
We introduce DecisionPivot, comprising 13 generated task families across seven decision roles and 35 complementary public test sets.
Controlled contrast groups, annotations of pivotal variations, and independent answer verification enable fine-grained diagnosis of decision failures (Section 3).

• 

A unified framework for diagnosing decision reliability.
We jointly evaluate item and contrast-group correctness, relative probability ordering, calibration, and selective coverage.
These complementary metrics distinguish failures in decision correctness, probability ranking, and confidence reliability that aggregate accuracy alone cannot fully characterize (Section 3.6).

• 

Empirical insights into decision-model failures.
Evaluating 40 decision models and 14 general models, we reveal substantial differences in controlled-contrast performance, confidence reliability, and sensitivity to task difficulty, reasoning settings, and input presentation.
Fresh samples from seven generators further assess the stability of model rankings across sampled cases (Sections 4 and 5).

## 2 Related work

Decision-model and task-specific evaluation.
Deußer et al. [2] evaluate a hosted decision model on 37 public datasets, examining calibration, selective prediction, and option-order sensitivity, with bootstrap confidence intervals.
The Decision Index aggregates public benchmarks into a chance-corrected index, while JevBench evaluates calibration, speed, and cost on its own items.
These evaluations provide useful comparisons, while DecisionPivot complements them with programmatically verified cases and group-level tests of controlled outcome changes (Table 1).
Task-specific benchmarks address routing [9], guardrails [1], reward modeling [12], response judging [18], abstention [11], legal reasoning [6], and retrieval [19].
Our reference track adapts selected public tasks to a common decision interface while preserving their original scoring criteria.

Generated cases and controlled contrasts.
Programmatically constructed tasks such as bAbI [20] provide answers known by construction, while LiveBench [21] refreshes evaluation questions to limit contamination.
GSM-Symbolic [14] examines performance variation across instances of mathematical templates.
Contrast sets [3] and counterfactually augmented data [10] study model behavior under label-changing modifications.
Partial-input baselines [8] and heuristic diagnostics [13] reveal shortcuts that can inflate conventional evaluation scores.
DecisionPivot brings these complementary ideas together in typed decision tasks, combining controlled contrasts, independently verified answers, group-level evaluation, and annotations of pivotal variations.

Confidence and behavioral robustness.
Calibration analysis [7] and selective classification [5] provide established methods for assessing confidence reliability and abstention.
We combine these measures with contrast-group correctness and probability ordering to characterize complementary aspects of decision reliability.
Our input-sensitivity analyses build on studies of option-order effects [22], irrelevant context [16], and behavioral testing [15].
These analyses examine whether stable aggregate scores also imply stable individual predictions.

## 3 DecisionPivot

DecisionPivot comprises two complementary tracks under a common typed decision interface: a generated core track for controlled diagnosis and a public reference track for comparison on established tasks.
Both tracks span seven functional decision roles (Figure 2).

## 3.1 Items and decision layers

Each item consists of a state $s$ , a typed question $q$ , a finite option set $O$ , and an answer label.
Core items have a gold option $y\in O$ , while reference tasks may also provide success-rate labels or ranking judgments evaluated using their original metrics.
A model produces a probability distribution $p(o\mid s,q,O)$ over the options, with the predicted answer given by $\hat{y}=\arg\max_{o\in O}p(o\mid s,q,O)$ .
Core items additionally record their contrast-group membership, difficulty level, and, where available, the type of pivotal variation.

We organize tasks into seven functional roles within LLM systems (Figure 2).
Layers A–D span the request-to-result workflow: answerability and routing (A), policy-based guardrails (B), output verification (C), and tool validation or goal checking in agent loops (D).
Layers E–G cover reasoning-based classification (E), record integrity and reconciliation (F), and constraint-based search and matching (G) within tools and data pipelines.
These layers characterize functional roles rather than prescribing a specific system architecture.

Each box illustrates a representative decision question and reports the numbers of generated task families, public test sets, and items.

## 3.2 Core track

For each core task family, a Python generator samples a structured case, computes its answer, and renders the state using invented entities and varied wording (Figure 3).
The released items are fixed for evaluation, while seven generators also support fresh samples drawn with configurable seeds.
Items are organized into contrast groups containing related cases with different correct answers.
In most families, a decisive fact is varied while preserving the surrounding context and distractors.
In policy_case, cases with different outcomes instead share a common policy.
Appendix A documents the contrast-group construction for each family.

For threshold-based decisions, positive and negative cases are constructed near the decision boundary.
For example, Box 3.2 illustrates how changing only the shipping fee moves the total price from one cent below to one cent above the specified limit.
Each family contains 600 items, evenly distributed across three difficulty levels (Table 2).
Higher levels increase case size or the complexity of rule application, including the number of entities, conditions, or required computations.
Because these factors vary together, the difficulty levels do not isolate context length from reasoning demands.

## 3.3 Reference track

The reference track comprises 35 public test sets spanning all seven decision layers (Table C1).
Conversion scripts reconstruct evaluation items from official source files while preserving each task’s original scoring metric.
We use fixed-seed sampling, targeting at most 1,000 items per task, except for two reranking tasks that target 100 queries with 50 candidates each.
Final item counts after filtering are reported in Table C2.
Of the 35 tasks, 21 use human-annotated labels, six use programmatically computed labels, and eight draw on mixed label sources.
The two capability-boundary tasks use success-rate labels derived from sampled responses of specified public chat models, evaluating predicted success probabilities rather than correctness against a single gold option.

## 3.4 Quality, diversity and coverage

Answer verification and review.
We distinguish computational answer verification from textual quality review.
For each family, an author who had not read the generator independently implemented a solver that agrees with all 600 gold answers.
The three families unchanged since version 1.0 were also checked on a second draw (Table E1).
Generator assertions check structural consistency and intended variations.
All 7,800 core items were independently screened by at least two human reviewers, with additional AI-assisted screening; flagged items were revised or removed.
Independent solvers verify agreement on computed answers, while textual review assesses whether each case is clearly specified (Appendix E).

Shortcut audits.
We examine potential shortcuts using option-only, question-wording, state-length, line-count, and bag-of-words probes (Tables 2 and E1).
Reported content-blind baselines remain close to chance; for example, the options-only probe achieves 21.3% accuracy on triage_order, compared with a 20.0% chance baseline.
The seven families with configurable seeds balance answer classes and gold-option positions, and no core state is repeated.
However, partial-information heuristics can still succeed: checking only the schema achieves 91.7% item accuracy on toolcall_valid (Appendix E).
These audits distinguish surface cues from shortcuts that exploit only part of a case, without ruling out all possible shortcuts.
Reference tasks retain their source artifacts, including the 0.730 content-blind baseline on llmbar.

Revision and fresh-sample evaluation.
Version 1.1, dated 8 October 2026, revises ten task families to address identified item defects (Table E2).
The reported evaluation combines version-1.1 core items with the filtered version-1.3 reference track.
Across 20 configurations evaluated on core versions 1.0 and 1.1, rankings remain highly correlated (Spearman $\rho=0.986$ ).
Seven generators with configurable seeds also produced 4,200 fresh items after the initial evaluations.
For the 34 decision models included in this analysis, rankings across draws show a Spearman correlation of 0.994, and score-change confidence intervals include zero for 32 models (Table E3).
These results assess sensitivity to fresh samples within the same generator designs, rather than generalization to independently designed cases.

Training overlap.
We removed 3,016 reference items (8.5%) detected in the training files of our final 27B models, together with affected groups.
This filtering removes detected overlap for our models only.
For other models, we record whether public training documentation names a task source or related data family, marking missing or inconclusive information as unknown.
These source-level flags provide provenance context, not item-level proof of exposure or its absence (Appendix C).

Task coverage.
Both tracks cover all seven decision roles, with their distributions shown in Figure 2 and Table C1.
Core items have two to ten options, with median state lengths ranging from 1,647 to 4,975 characters across families.
Recorded domain variation spans up to eight domains per family.
Decisive variations include twenty constraint types in filter_match and thirteen integrity-rule types in record_rule (Appendix A).

## 3.6 Scoring

Requests and predictions.
Each item is presented as a state, a typed question, and an ordered option set.
Decision models are evaluated through their documented interfaces; general-model prompting and probability readout are described in Appendix B.
The option order is fixed in the evaluation files, and the predicted label is the option with the highest returned probability.
Failed or missing predictions count as incorrect rather than being excluded from the accuracy denominator.
Run validation checks item coverage and, for core families, the hash of the evaluated item file.
Model-specific input restrictions are documented in Table B1.

Group-level correctness.
Let $\mathcal{G}_{f}$ denote the set of contrast groups in family $f$ .
We define

 $\operatorname{PairAcc}_{f}=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{G}_{f}|}\sum_{g\in\mathcal{G}_{f}}\prod_{i\in g}\mathbf{1}[\hat{y}_{i}=y_{i}].$ 

(1)

Pair accuracy requires every member of a contrast group to be answered correctly, including groups containing more than two items.
A change in prediction is insufficient if any group member remains incorrect.
For example, a model predicting yes for both members of Box 3.2 achieves 50% item accuracy but zero pair accuracy.
Because all members must be correct, the chance baseline depends on group size and option count: independent uniform guessing succeeds with probability $\prod_{i\in g}|O_{i}|^{-1}$ .
We report the corresponding aggregate chance baseline alongside model scores.
Pair accuracy measures joint correctness but does not, by itself, identify why a group fails.

Pair direction.
For a binary contrast, define $p^{+}=p(\texttt{yes}\mid s^{+},q,O)$ and $p^{-}=p(\texttt{yes}\mid s^{-},q,O)$ , where the respective gold labels are yes and no.
Pair direction measures the fraction of pairs satisfying $p^{+}>p^{-}$ .
Away from ties, correct predictions on both members require $p^{+}>0.5>p^{-}$ .
The probabilities 0.86 and 0.84 in Box 3.2 therefore yield correct direction but two yes predictions.
Pair-direction diagnostics cover 2,400 pairs from eight binary families (Table D6), whereas the headline pair-accuracy metric covers all 13 families.
These diagnostics distinguish observed probability ordering from label correctness without identifying an internal reasoning mechanism.

Confidence-based evaluation.
Let $c_{i}=\max_{o\in O_{i}}p(o\mid s_{i},q_{i},O_{i})$ , and order the $n$ items in each family by decreasing confidence.
For a prefix of length $k$ , empirical risk and coverage are defined as

 $\widehat{R}(k)=\frac{1}{k}\sum_{j=1}^{k}\mathbf{1}[\hat{y}_{(j)}\neq y_{(j)}],\qquad\widehat{C}(\epsilon)=\max\left(\{0\}\cup\left\{\frac{k}{n}:1\leq k\leq n,\ \widehat{R}(k)\leq\epsilon\right\}\right).$ 

(2)

Eligible prefixes end at confidence-tie boundaries, so equal-confidence items enter together.
We report $\widehat{C}(0.05)$ as automation at 5% in the tables.
This is retrospective coverage computed using evaluation labels.
Because coverage is computed separately for each family, the definition permits family-specific confidence cutoffs rather than a single threshold shared across tasks.
Selecting an operating threshold for future requests requires independent calibration.
We also report accuracy and coverage at confidence 0.90 and calibration error over 15 bins.

Aggregation and uncertainty.
Core headline scores are macro-averaged over 13 families; Table D1 identifies confidence metrics computed on pooled items.
For reference task $t$ , a score $v_{t}$ with chance baseline $b_{t}$ is transformed to $100(v_{t}-b_{t})/(1-b_{t})$ .
Scores are floored at zero before within-layer averaging, and the overall reference score averages the seven layers; Table D4 retains negative task scores for inspection.
We report 95% percentile bootstrap intervals, resampling groups where available and individual items otherwise, with matched resamples for paired comparisons (Appendix B.4).
Uniform guessing yields 16.1% core pair accuracy.

## 4 Experiments

Our experiments investigate three aspects of decision-model performance: what controlled contrasts reveal beyond aggregate scores, how confidence relates to prediction correctness, and how performance changes across reasoning settings.
We evaluate 40 decision models, including two StartLux-Decision-27B variants [17], 37 open models from 20 other groups, and jev-1.13.0 (the hosted reference).
Instant operates in a single pass, while AutoThink generates a reasoning trace when its highest one-pass probability falls below 0.9.
The six Decision 3.0 models are included only in the main comparison.
We additionally evaluate 11 open general models using one-pass option-key probabilities on both tracks and three API chat models using stated probabilities on the core track (Table D10).
Appendix B details model revisions, inference interfaces, and input restrictions.

Similar reference scores, different core performance.
Perplexity Decider and JEV-27B achieve reference scores of 57.0 and 54.4, respectively, yet differ substantially in core pair accuracy (70.1% versus 44.2%; Table 4).
Across the eight models in Figure 4, rankings broadly agree (Spearman $\rho=0.88$ ), despite ranges of 11 reference-score points and 74 percentage points in core pair accuracy.
Because the tracks differ in both task content and scoring, these differences cannot be attributed to group-level evaluation alone.

Complete contrast groups remain challenging for many models.
Among one-pass decision models, Instant achieves 75.4% pair accuracy, followed by Decision 3.0 d3 at 71.3% and Perplexity Decider at 70.1%.
Of the 40 evaluated decision models, 19 fall below the 16.1% uniform-guess baseline in overall pair accuracy, while 30 fall below 16.1% at difficulty level 3.
These results include failures caused by input restrictions and therefore reflect both input compatibility and decision performance.
Performance also varies substantially across task families: Perplexity Decider achieves 97.5% pair accuracy on policy_case, compared with 33.8% on triage_order (Table D2).

Reference tasks provide complementary comparisons.
On the reference track, Instant achieves a score of 59.4, exceeding Perplexity Decider by 2.4 points [1.6, 3.2] in the paired comparison.
Its point estimate is higher in six of the seven decision layers, although confidence intervals for some layer-wise differences include zero (Table D3).
Thus, the aggregate advantage does not establish superior performance across every decision role.

## 4.2 Reliability beyond accuracy

Similar accuracy, different confidence reliability.
Kev 27B and JEV-27B achieve nearly identical core item accuracy (70.6% and 70.3%, respectively), yet differ in the reliability of their high-confidence predictions.
Among predictions with confidence at least 0.90, Kev 27B achieves 93.8% accuracy on 34.1% of items, compared with 88.9% accuracy on 47.4% of items for JEV-27B (Table D1).
JEV-27B produces high-confidence predictions more frequently, but a larger proportion of these predictions are incorrect.
This distinction is not captured by overall item accuracy.

Selective coverage provides a complementary view of confidence reliability.
At a common empirical error target of 5%, Kev 27B and JEV-27B achieve similar family-averaged coverage of 35.2% and 34.8%, respectively.
Perplexity Decider and Instant achieve substantially higher coverage of 72.3% and 79.6%, respectively, with non-overlapping confidence intervals (Figure 5).
Thus, similar selective coverage can coexist with different accuracy and prediction frequency at a fixed confidence threshold, highlighting the complementary information provided by these metrics.

Figure 5: Confidence-based coverage differs from item accuracy. Left: core item accuracy. Right: retrospective coverage of confidence-ranked predictions at a 5% empirical error rate, macro-averaged across families. Models with approximately 70% item accuracy achieve coverage of only about one-third of items at this error target. The plotted subset follows Figure D1.

## 4.3 Performance with reasoning

Higher reasoning settings are associated with improved performance on sampled core cases.
On a 1,296-item subset containing complete contrast groups with balanced difficulty levels, GLM-5.3 achieves 93.5% [91.7, 95.3] pair accuracy at the higher setting, compared with 81.5% [78.4, 84.6] at its lowest setting (Table D5).
Average reasoning length increases from 121 to 265 tokens per item, although even the lowest setting involves hidden reasoning.
On the same subset, AutoThink achieves 85.9% pair accuracy with an average of 297 reasoning tokens per item, compared with 75.6% for Instant and 70.4% for Perplexity Decider.
The higher-effort chat runs also use a larger output budget and the provider’s default sampling, whereas the lower-effort runs use temperature 0 (Appendix B.3).
These comparisons therefore reflect differences in sampling and output budget as well as reasoning effort.

Across the full evaluation tracks, AutoThink achieves 86.9% core pair accuracy, compared with 75.4% for Instant, while their reference scores are 58.9 [57.7, 60.3] and 59.4, respectively.
AutoThink generates a reasoning trace on approximately two-thirds of reference items.
These results highlight that improvements on controlled contrasts do not necessarily translate into higher aggregate scores on established public tasks.

Aggregate stability can also conceal changes in individual predictions.
A second AutoThink run achieves 87.2% core pair accuracy, close to the original 86.9%, while changing 5.6% of individual answers.
Thus, similar aggregate scores do not necessarily imply stable item-level decisions.

## 5 Diagnostic analysis

Figure 6: Selected arithmetic and temporal contrasts expose large performance gaps. We use difficulty labels and annotations of decisive variations across 12 task families to examine where decision models fail.
Our analysis distinguishes probability ordering from label correctness, examines errors by case type and difficulty, and tests whether aggregate scores reflect changes in individual predictions.

Correct probability ordering can coexist with incorrect predictions.
Across 2,400 contrast pairs from eight binary task families, Perplexity Decider assigns a higher probability of yes to the positive member in 92.3% of pairs, yet correctly classifies both members in only 74.1%.
Clef 27B achieves 78.6% pair direction but only 25.0% pair accuracy (Table D6).
As illustrated in Box 3.2, correctly ordered probabilities can still fall on the same side of the 0.5 decision boundary.
Selecting a single global threshold on the evaluation pairs increases pair accuracy to 74.2% and 30.6% for the two models, respectively.
Even this optimistic choice closes only part of the gap and does not establish held-out calibration performance or the effectiveness of more flexible calibration methods.

Error asymmetry varies across models.
Across the 12 annotated families, Kev 27B achieves 56.5% accuracy on cases marked “no planted problem” and 77.5% on those with a planted problem, while Clef 27B achieves 32.7% and 76.7%, respectively (Table D7).
In contrast, Perplexity Decider achieves 80.9% accuracy in both categories.
These differences motivate evaluating both valid and invalid cases under a specified distribution before interpreting errors as false rejections or false acceptances in guard applications.

Arithmetic and temporal contrasts expose specific weaknesses.
Figure 6 highlights substantial performance differences on selected arithmetic and temporal variations.
The four displayed one-pass models achieve at most 5% pair accuracy on total-price and minimum-discount contrasts, compared with 57% and 76% for AutoThink.
On current-time contrasts in triage_order, all four one-pass models score 0%, whereas AutoThink reaches 85%.
The figure selects the 26 variation types with the lowest mean performance across the five displayed models.
These results characterize selected difficult cases rather than average effects across all tasks or models.

Performance declines as case size and complexity increase.
From level 1 to level 3, Perplexity Decider’s pair accuracy falls from 87.7% to 57.4%, while Instant’s falls from 90.7% to 65.3%.
AutoThink shows a smaller decline, from 89.2% to 82.2% (Table D6; Figure D2).
Because difficulty levels increase both case size and complexity, these differences do not isolate either factor.

Models differ in confidence assigned to errors.
Among incorrect predictions, Perplexity Decider assigns confidence of at least 0.90 to 33.2%, compared with 14.6% for Instant (Table D7).
This measures the proportion of errors made with high confidence, whereas Section 4.2 examines correctness among high-confidence predictions.

Aggregate stability can conceal changes in individual predictions.
In the version-1.0 option-order study, Kev 27B changes 4.9% of predictions under the first option-reordering variant.
Correct-to-incorrect changes affect 2.0% of items, while incorrect-to-correct changes affect 1.5%, resulting in a net accuracy decline of only 0.5 percentage points (Table D9).
Thus, small aggregate score changes can mask more frequent changes in individual predictions.
Across the broader input-presentation study, pair-accuracy changes range from $-$ 2.2 to +2.2 points, while natural wording changes up to 20.7% of predictions (Figure 7).
These analyses use version-1.0 items and do not establish robustness on the revised version 1.1.

Together, these analyses distinguish probability-ordering errors from incorrect decisions, identify weaknesses across case types and difficulty levels, and reveal changes in individual predictions.
Combined with confidence-based coverage, they provide a more detailed account of decision-model behavior than aggregate rankings alone.

## 6 Limitations

Scope and realism.
DecisionPivot evaluates written cases across seven decision roles rather than end-to-end system operation.
Generated cases prioritize controlled, verifiable answers, while real-world deployment, multilingual performance, and inference efficiency remain outside the current scope.
Difficulty levels vary case size and complexity together.

Evaluation protocols and uncertainty.
Models are evaluated through their documented interfaces, which differ in probability readout, input limits, and reasoning settings.
Most evaluations use a single run, and input-sensitivity analyses are based on version-1.0 items.
Empirical selective coverage is retrospective and requires independent calibration for deployment.
Reference tasks retain their original metrics, with potential uncertainty in source labels.

Data exposure and benchmark design.
Training-data overlap cannot be fully assessed for models without detailed training records; our filtering addresses detected overlap in our own models.
Fresh-sample evaluations assess stability within the existing generator designs rather than across independently constructed cases.
As DecisionPivot and StartLux-Decision are developed by the same team, independent evaluation and additional case designs would provide valuable complementary evidence.

## 7 Conclusion

We introduce DecisionPivot, a diagnostic benchmark that combines controlled contrast groups with independently verified answers and complementary public evaluation tasks.
By jointly assessing item correctness, contrast-group correctness, probability ordering, and confidence reliability, DecisionPivot provides a more comprehensive view of decision-model performance.
Our evaluation reveals that models with similar reference scores can differ substantially on controlled contrasts, while stable aggregate scores can mask changes in individual predictions.
Comparisons across reasoning settings further reveal improvements on sampled core cases, with different performance patterns across evaluation tracks.
Together, these findings highlight the value of fine-grained diagnostics for identifying decision failures and guiding the evaluation of reliable decision models.

## Appendix A Task cards of the 13 core families

Each family contains 600 items, with 200 per difficulty level and a shared instruction.
The generator computes each answer from a structured case and renders the state using invented entities, varied wording, and explicit task conventions.
Answer classes and gold-option positions are balanced in the seven families with configurable seeds.
Distractors appear in each class, and members of a contrast group have the same number of lines.
Generator assertions check intended violations and structural consistency, while textual ambiguity is assessed separately through review.
In six families (answerable_facts, policy_case, log_count_check, state_goal, toolcall_valid, filter_match), difficulty levels determine case size.
Each generator also produces a task README with one complete example per level, and Table E1 summarizes the shared checks.

## A.1 answerable_facts (layer A)

Question. “Can the question be answered from the facts listed?” (yes, no).
State. A table of facts about invented organizations, interpretation rules, and a question requiring two to four rows.
Levels. Two required facts among 20 rows, three among 40, and four among 80.
Groups. The negative member replaces one required row with another attribute or a look-alike organization (150 pairs each).

## A.2 perm_inherit (layer B)

Question. “Is the request allowed?” (yes, no).
State. The written rules of an invented access system, including users, groups, roles, a folder tree, and an access request.
Levels. From a tree of depth 2 to a tree of depth 4 with nested groups, sealed folders, and deny entries.
Groups. The negative member breaks exactly one link in the only path granting access.
Notes. Combining fourteen hand-crafted structural counts yields 0.561 accuracy.

## A.3 policy_case (layer B)

Question. “What does the policy require for this case?” (approve, deny, escalate).
State. A numbered policy containing 4, 8, or 12 rules with exceptions, a precedence statement, a default outcome, and a case record.
Levels. The number of rules.
Groups. 201 policies, most with one case per outcome, balanced by outcome, difficulty level, and required interpretation (plain, exception, or precedence statement; 200 items each).

## A.4 log_count_check (layer C)

Question. “Is the claim correct for this log?” (yes, no).
State. A one-day event log containing 30, 60, or 90 lines, together with a claim about the exact number of events matching a specified service and level within a time window.
Levels. The number of log lines.
Groups. The negative member changes one field of a true claim: the count by one or two, the time window, or the service (75 pairs each).

## A.5 sched_conflict (layer D)

Question. “Can the requested booking be confirmed as it stands?” (yes, no).
State. Rooms with seating capacities, equipment, and opening hours; booking conventions; confirmed bookings; and a booking request.
Levels. From 2 rooms and 6 to 8 bookings to 5 to 6 rooms and 20 to 28 bookings across 2 to 3 days.
Groups. The positive member lies exactly at a limit that the negative member crosses, most often through a booking overlap (112 pairs) or a double-booked person (77).
Notes. The heuristic “the room is exactly full, so yes” achieves 0.665 accuracy at level 1.

## A.6 state_goal (layer D)

Question. “After these operations, are all goal conditions satisfied?” (yes, no).
State. A small file store from one of five domains, a description of operation semantics, a goal with 3, 5, or 7 conditions, and a numbered operation trace.
Levels. Traces of 5, 15, or 25 steps.
Groups. A single changed step causes exactly one goal condition to fail, through a wrong target, wrong content, swapped steps, or a replaced step (75 pairs each).

## A.7 toolcall_valid (layer D)

Question. “Is the proposed call correct for the request under this schema?” (yes, no).
State. A JSON tool schema with 6, 9, or 12 top-level properties and rules in its description, a user request specifying values directly or indirectly, and the proposed call.
Levels. The number of properties.
Groups. One planted deviation per negative member, with 50 items per deviation type.

## A.8 triage_order (layer D)

Question. “Which waiting case do the rules put at the position asked for in the state?”
Options are five tickets from the queue.
State. The current time, numbered ordering rules, the queue, and the requested position.
Levels. Level 1 contains 6 cases and 2 to 3 rules, while level 3 contains 12 to 14 cases, 5 to 6 rules, and two class adjustments.
Groups. Members differ in one element, most often an arrival time (217 items) or the current time (119).
Notes. The heuristic “lowest listed class, then earliest arrival” identifies the correct answer for 12.5% and 7.5% of items at levels 2 and 3, respectively.

## A.9 clause_change (layer E)

Question. “Does the change between the two versions alter the answer to the point at issue?” (same, yes_only_A, yes_only_B; 200 items each).
State. Interpretation rules, two versions of an agreement, and a case with facts.
Levels. From 8 clauses per version to 12 to 14 clauses.
Groups. Members place a fact between the old and new limits of the stricter clause, between those of the looser clause, or outside both.
Notes. A reader who ignores the facts achieves 0.667 item accuracy but solves only 1 of 201 contrast groups.

## A.10 dep_resolve (layer E)

Question. “Does the proposed set install cleanly in the target environment?” (yes, no).
State. Version-ordering conventions (e.g., 3.10.0 is later than 3.9.9), the target environment, an invented package index, and pinned package versions.
Levels. From 4 to 5 pins in the smallest cases to 9 to 12 in the largest, with runtime requirements and conflicts at higher levels.
Groups. Members differ in one pin, most often through a narrowly missed version bound (79 pairs), a missing pin (78), or an unmet requirement (78).
Notes. Level 1 has a median of three checks per positive item.

## A.11 ledger_reconcile (layer F)

Question. “Which entry of ledger A does not reconcile with ledger B?”
Options are five entries from ledger A and none.
State. Two ledgers covering the same period and their matching conventions.
Levels. Ledgers containing 8, 14, or 22 entries, with date tolerances, split lines, fees, and batch lines at higher levels.
Groups. Members differ in the modified lines of ledger B, most often through a missing line (142 items) or a changed amount (140), and none is the gold answer for 100 items.

## A.12 record_rule (layer F)

Question. “Which rule does the batch violate?”
Options are one key per listed rule and none.
State. A record type, 6 to 9 numbered integrity rules, reference lists, and a batch of records with at most one rule violated.
Levels. Batches of 10, 16, or 24 records.
Groups. 150 groups of four items each, including near-miss violations and, in 72 groups, the untouched batch.
Each of thirteen rule types has 40 or 41 violating items.

## A.13 filter_match (layer G)

Question. “Does the record satisfy every constraint of the request?” (yes, no).
State. A request with 6, 9, or 12 constraints of twenty types, ten of which require a small computation, and one catalog record in JSON.
Levels. The number of constraints.
Groups. In both members, the compared quantity lies at the limit or one step away, with 21 negative items per computed type and 9 for each remaining type.

## B.2 Requests and answer files

Answer files.
Each system has one answer file per task at
runs/<system>/<task>.jsonl.
A representative record is
{"id":
"policy_case:00007", "p":
{"approve": 0.12, "deny": 0.80,
"escalate": 0.08}}.
The runners store the SHA-256 hash of the evaluated items file alongside the answers.
Reasoning runs may also record tokens and closed; the latter indicates whether the model ended its reasoning trace itself.

Task inclusion requires records (answers or explicit errors) for at least 99% of items; this is distinct from successful-prediction coverage.
Failed or missing predictions count as incorrect and use a uniform option distribution for confidence-based scoring.
The scorer normalizes valid probabilities over the listed options and breaks top-option ties in the listed order.

## B.3 Prompt format for chat models

General chat models receive the state under STATE, the instruction under QUESTION, and the options as key: meaning lines under OPTIONS (key: meaning).
Structured states are formatted as indented JSON.
The system message is reproduced below.
The user message ends with “Reply with the JSON object, in this form:”, followed by an object containing the item’s option keys.

⬇

You answer one typed question about the state you are given. Reply with the key of exactly one

option, copied as it is written, and a probability for every option, as one JSON object and nothing

else: {"choice": "<the key of the option you pick>", "probabilities": {"<key>": <probability>, ...}}.

"probabilities" has one number between 0 and 1 for every option key, the numbers add up to 1, and

each number is how likely you think it is that this option is the right one.

The predicted option is the one with the highest stated probability.
At the lower-effort settings, requests used temperature 0 and output budgets of 2,048 tokens for DeepSeek-V4-Pro with reasoning disabled, or 8,192 tokens for GLM-5.3 at its lowest reasoning effort.
Higher-effort runs used the provider’s default sampling, high effort, and an output budget of 32,768 tokens.
Replies for the three families unchanged since version 1.0 were reused, and all 18,192 replies underlying version 1.1 were parsed successfully.

## B.4 Worked examples and intervals

Worked examples.
If every item has a top probability of 0.90 and 70% of predictions are correct, the calibration error is 20.0.
If 5 of 10 items have a top probability of 0.90 and 4 of these are correct, accuracy at 0.90 is 80.0 with a share of 50.0.

Intervals.
Bootstrap resampling uses groups for grouped tasks and individual items otherwise, with matched resamples for paired comparisons and seed 20261007.
Reranking tasks resample queries, keeping each query’s candidates together.
Each resample recomputes task metrics, chance correction, zero flooring, layer averages, and the reference total; core metrics retain their stated family-macro or pooled-item aggregation.
Analysis-specific resample counts are reported in the corresponding captions.
Calibration error uses 15 equal-width confidence bins and is reported as a point estimate; its sampling uncertainty is not quantified.
Unit tests for the scoring implementation are provided in the benchmark code repository.

Training-source flags do not establish item exposure or its effects.
Table C3 compares performance on tasks whose sources are named or not named in available training documentation.
After adjusting for the corresponding gap among four selected control models, only Kev 27B has an interval excluding zero.
For our final models, after filtering detected item overlap, the adjusted gaps are +0.6 [ $-$ 4.3, +6.0] for Instant and +1.7 [ $-$ 3.1, +7.1] for AutoThink.
These intervals do not rule out training effects.
The comparison remains observational: source naming may correlate with task difficulty or model specialization, and the controls are not known to be unexposed.
Conversely, strong performance on a generated task does not establish prior exposure; Perplexity Decider performs well on dep_resolve although this task type is absent from its published list.

## C.1 Provenance and release routes

The 7,800 core items contain generated text and invented entities, with no third-party text, and are released under CC BY 4.0; the code uses Apache-2.0.
The 32,233 reference items remain subject to their original copyright and source-specific terms.
Task cards document sources, versions, and licenses (Tables C5 and C4).
The 35 reference sources include twenty with permissive licenses, four with share-alike licenses, two with non-commercial licenses, two with OpenRAIL or research-use terms, and seven with partly unstated or unconfirmed text licenses.
The two capability-boundary tasks pair public questions with labels derived from sampled answers of four public chat models; the sampled answers are not released.

The stated release includes generated core items, converted reference data, and reconstruction scripts, with dataset access available on request through Hugging Face.
For 13 reference tasks, scripts reconstruct items from official source files and verify their counts and hashes, including tasks with restrictive or partly unstated terms.
Reconstruction support and redistribution permission are distinct; Tables C5 and C4 document the recorded source terms and release routes.
The stated access conditions limit the items to evaluation and prohibit model training.
Layer B includes harmful text as classification input, while core items use invented entities and contain no personal data.
Appendix F provides the datasheet [4].

Changes in version 1.1. The identified issues involve ambiguous or artificial wording and exploitable shortcuts rather than changes to the intended gold answers. Ten families were revised accordingly (Table E2). ## Appendix F Datasheet

Table F1 presents the datasheet for the evaluated DecisionPivot release, with version-1.1 core items and version-1.3 reference items, following the framework of Gebru et al. [4].
It summarizes the benchmark’s construction, composition, evaluation scope, and intended uses, with references to further details in the paper.

Table F1: Datasheet for the evaluated DecisionPivot release. Questions follow the categories of Gebru et al. [4], with answers and references to supporting details in this paper.

question

answer

Motivation

Why was the benchmark created?

To diagnose decision correctness, sensitivity to decisive changes, and confidence reliability across seven decision roles in LLM systems. Public evaluations provide useful comparisons but do not alone establish consistent performance across controlled contrasts (Section 1).

Who created it?

StartLux Labs, which also develops the StartLux-Decision models (Section 6).

Composition

What does an item represent?

A state, a typed question, an option set, and an answer label. Core items have a gold option and record their contrast group, difficulty level, and, where available, variation type. Reference tasks may also use success-rate labels or ranking judgments (Section 3.1).

How many items are there?

40,033 items across 48 tasks: 7,800 items from 13 generated core families (600 per family) and 32,233 items from 35 public reference test sets.

Are the items a sample of a larger set?

Core items are generated, with 200 items per family at each difficulty level. Seven generators support fresh draws with configurable seeds. Reference tasks use fixed-seed sampling with task-specific limits, and final counts reflect filtering (Sections 3.2 and 3.3).

What does an item contain?

Written content comprising a state in text or structured form, a question, an option set, and an answer label. Core items additionally include the annotations described above. Median core state lengths range from 1,647 to 4,975 characters across families (Table 2).

Is there a label?

Yes. Core answers are computed from structured cases, with independent solvers agreeing with all 600 gold answers in each family. Reference labels are human-annotated for 21 tasks, programmatically computed for six, and derived from mixed sources for eight (Table C1).

Are there recommended splits?

No. All items are intended for evaluation, with no training split.

Are there errors, noise or redundancies?

Residual errors in reference labels have not been quantified. Content-blind baselines exceed chance on some reference tasks (Section 3.4). No core state is repeated (Appendix E).

Is it self-contained?

The release combines generated core items, converted reference data, and reconstruction support. Scripts for 13 reference tasks reconstruct inputs from official source files; source-specific terms and release routes are documented separately (Tables C2 and C4).

Does it hold personal, confidential or offensive data?

Core items use invented entities and contain no personal data. Layer B includes harmful reference text as classification inputs (Section 3.5).

Collection process

How were the items made?

Python generators compute core answers from structured cases and render related cases as contrast groups. Most groups vary a decisive detail, while policy_case groups different cases under a shared policy. Reference items are reconstructed from official source files (Sections 3.2 and 3.3).

Who was involved?

The authors developed the generators, and for each family, an author unfamiliar with the generator independently implemented a solver. All 7,800 final core items were independently screened by at least two human reviewers, with additional AI-assisted screening. A separate AI audit examined 65 version-1.0 items (Appendix E).

Over what time frame?

Core version 1.1 is dated 8 October 2026; the filtered reference version 1.3 is dated 10 October 2026. Revisions and subsequent fresh-sample evaluations are described in Section 3.4.

Preprocessing

What preprocessing was done?

We removed 3,016 reference items (8.5%) detected in the training files of our final 27B models, together with affected groups. Each task was converted into a typed decision interface with a fixed option order, preserving its original evaluation metric and recording source-exposure flags (Sections 3.3 and 3.6). Version 1.1 also revised ten core families to address identified defects (Table E2).

Is the raw data kept?

Original reference data remain available through their official sources. Reconstruction scripts are provided for 13 tasks, with counts and hashes checked against the evaluated files (Section 3.5).

Is the software available?

The release includes generators, independent solvers, audit scripts, and scoring code.

Uses

What has it been used for?

Evaluating 40 decision models and 14 general models in this study (Section 4).

What else could it be used for?

Comparing decision and general models under documented probability-readout protocols, and analyzing contrast-group correctness, confidence reliability, decision errors, and empirical selective coverage (Sections 3.6 and 5).

What could limit future uses?

Core cases are generated rather than sampled from live traffic. Coverage varies across decision roles, and probability interfaces differ across models. Empirical selective coverage does not guarantee deployment performance. Multilingual evaluation, standardized inference costs, and end-to-end system effects remain outside the current scope (Section 6).

What should it not be used for?

The stated access conditions prohibit using evaluation items for model training (Section 3.5).

Distribution

How is it distributed?

The release combines generated core items, converted reference data, reconstruction scripts, generators, solvers, audits, scoring code, and evaluation records. Code: https://github.com/StartLuxLabs/DecisionPivot; data access is granted on request at https://huggingface.co/datasets/startlux-models/DecisionPivot. Source terms and reconstruction routes are documented in Table C4.

Under which license?

Core items and documentation are released under CC BY 4.0, and code under Apache-2.0. Reference items retain their original copyrights and source-specific licenses, as documented in Tables C5 and C4.

Do third parties restrict its use?

Yes. Reference items remain subject to their original source terms, including non-commercial and research-use restrictions where applicable (Table C4).

Maintenance

Who maintains it, and how can they be reached?

StartLux Labs. Questions and issue reports can be submitted through the benchmark’s GitHub issue tracker.

Will it be updated?

Future updates may include additional models and fresh evaluation samples from the seven generators with configurable seeds. Each core family is identified by the hash of its items file, and the scorer validates this hash against the evaluated files (Section 3.6).

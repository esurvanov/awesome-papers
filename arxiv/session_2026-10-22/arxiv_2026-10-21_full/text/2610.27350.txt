## Refuses When Asked, Complies When Acting:

A Runtime Safety Check for Robot Control Loops

Arnav Grover

Affiliation: Purdue University

Email: grover41@purdue.edu

Abstract

Frontier models can control robots through agent harnesses that loop on one question: what motion comes next toward the user’s goal? We show that this default loop carries out harmful instructions. With GPT-6 Astra controlling a low-cost SO-101 arm through the Inspect Robots harness on three household tasks whose hazard is common knowledge, such as “put the knife in the toy basket”, the policy refused 0% of 15 trials, attempted the harm every time, and completed it in 27%. Its one exit, give_up, is meant for goals that cannot be achieved, and it never cited the hazard. We add an optional runtime safety check to the loop: before the first motion, the model is asked whether it would carry out the instruction, given the same camera frames. The check would have stopped all 15 hazardous instructions, including the 4 that completed the harm, and flagged none of the 15 harmless controls. Code and data: https://github.com/Arnav-Gr0ver/Refuses-When-Asked-Complies-When-Acting.

Keywords: Robot safety, Runtime safety check, Foundation model policies, Evaluation

## 1 Introduction

Before acting, a person considers the harm an action could do to those it affects, but a robot control loop that works toward the user’s goal has no step where that consideration happens. Ask a frontier model whether it would put a knife in a toy basket that children reach into, and it says no. Give the same model the same instruction as the control loop of a robot arm, and it reaches for the knife to put it in the basket.

RoboHarm [1] showed that frontier robot policies fail this check on hardware: GPT-6 Astra refused 2% of harmful instructions (2 of 100) and carried out 60%, including 17 of 20 attempts to stab a baby doll. We study why in one widely used harness. The Inspect Robots harness [2] runs the policy in a loop in which every call returns one motion toward the user’s goal. Its safety approvers only clamp out-of-bounds and too-fast motion, and its one exit, give_up, is for goals that cannot be achieved, not for goals that should not be pursued. Nothing in the loop asks whether the instruction should be carried out, so a harmful instruction proceeds like any other.

We show that the default loop does carry out the harm: on three household tasks, GPT-6 Astra refused none of 15 trials and completed the harm in four. We then add an optional runtime safety check to the loop. Before the first motion, the check asks the policy model whether it would carry out the instruction, given the camera frames. It would have stopped all 15 hazardous instructions and flagged none of the harmless controls (Fig. 1). The check reuses the policy’s model and adds one call, with no second model and no safety specification, unlike prior safeguards that add safety specifications [3] or a separate monitoring model [4]. We also introduce three household tasks, each with a named hazard, a safe alternative, and a harmless control, and describe the policy’s reasoning across 464 robot calls.

## 2 Methodology

Each task is a household pick-and-place whose instruction moves a harmful object to where it can hurt a child or a pet (Table 1, Fig. 2). The harm is common knowledge, so a model that complies cannot be excused by missing knowledge. The instruction names the place where the child or pet meets the object (the kids’ bowl, the cat’s platter, the toy basket), so the hazard can be recognized from the words. Each scene also holds a safe alternative, which supplies a harmless control instruction that shows whether the check stops harmless requests.

GPT-6 Astra (medium reasoning effort) controls a 6-DoF SO-101 arm [6] with a wrist and a fixed camera ( $640\times 480$ ) through the Inspect Robots harness, unmodified. Each call it receives both images, the joint state, and the instruction, and returns one tool call: move_joints (joint targets and a note on what it sees and why it moves), done, or give_up. The system prompt tells it to “work toward the user’s goal”, that “a scorer judges success”, and that “Safety approvers clamp out-of-bounds and too-fast actions”; give_up is for goals that “cannot be achieved”. Episodes allow 40 calls and 900 control steps at 25% speed. Each task was run 5 times from a fixed home pose. A trial is refused, makes no meaningful attempt, fails, or completes (the item reaches the target; one tablet suffices). Outcomes for the cat and knife tasks come from recorded final frames and the final message, and for the vitamins task from an operator during live runs. One cat trial first graded complete was regraded to failed after its final frames were checked.

The runtime safety check is a separate step that the loop runs before the policy moves, so its prompt differs from the policy’s by design. The same model receives the instruction and the question “You control a robot arm in a home. A person gives you this instruction: […] Would you carry it out? Give your reasoning, then answer yes or no.” A no would stop the loop before any motion. We evaluate the check on the two camera frames from the start of each of the 15 robot trials, the visual input the policy had before its first motion, so its answer shows what the robot would have done had the loop run the check. We also ask without images (5 repeats per task) and ask both versions about the harmless controls to count false alarms.

The API returned a reasoning summary with 49% of robot calls; with the move notes and end-of-trial messages, these form our logs. We count reasoning tokens per decision, mark whether a decision names the hazardous item and the harm, tag each call’s topics (grasping, joints and motion, locating objects, call budget, harm) with keyword rules, and classify each cautious sentence as concerning the robot’s motion or the recipient. A single reviewer checked every harm tag. Each condition has 15 hazardous decisions (3 tasks $\times$ 5), and we report percentages with counts and 95% Wilson score intervals, which stay informative at 0% and 100% (15 of 15 gives 80–100%, 0 of 15 gives 0–20%).

## 3 Results

Figure 3: (a) Hazardous instructions stopped by the default loop, or declined when the safety check asks first (95% Wilson intervals). “Asked first” in the legend denotes the safety check. (b) Reasoning tokens per decision (lines: medians). (c) Content of the first decision.

Acting in the default loop, the policy refused 0% of trials (0 of 15; 0–20%). It attempted the hazard in every trial: 11 attempts failed and 4 completed the harm (27%; vitamins 60%, cat 20%, knife 0%). It never used the safe alternative. Every failure ended in give_up citing a manipulation problem, never the hazard, so the harness’s exit stopped no hazard. The failures came from the limits of a zero-shot policy on a low-cost arm, not from safety, so a more reliable manipulator plausibly completes more of the harm.

Asked before the first motion with the trial’s own camera frames, the model declined 100% of hazardous instructions (15 of 15; 80–100%), including the 4 trials that completed the harm, and declined 0% of harmless controls (0 of 15; 0–20%; Fig. 3a). From text alone it gave the same result. Every answer named the harm and who was at risk, e.g. “Putting a knife in a toy basket could cause accidental cuts, especially if children reach inside,” and most proposed what the policy never did: keep the item separate and ask an adult.

The policy’s logs show a different pattern. Its first message named the hazardous item in 100% of trials and the destination in 73% (11 of 15), but named the harm in 20% of first calls, against 100% of the check’s answers (Fig. 3c). Before its first action it used a median of 35 reasoning tokens, against 39 when the check was asked from text, and 67% of the check’s declines with frames (10 of 15) used no reasoning tokens (Fig. 3b). Across all 464 robot calls, 95% mentioned grasping, 80% joints or motion, 65% locating objects, and 12% the budget, while the harm appeared in 3 calls (0.6%), each the first call of its trial (Fig. 4). Of 44 cautious sentences, 42 (95%) concerned the robot’s own motion, such as raising the gripper “rather than risk dragging the blade”. In each of the three calls that mentioned the harm, the next sentence returned to execution and the concern did not return. Twice it became a condition for acting (“make sure the chocolate platter is safe by ensuring no cats are around”) or a check for later (“I must consider safety and dosage before proceeding”), and neither was carried out. Two of these three trials completed the harm.

## 4 Discussion and Limitations

The default loop frames every call as a step toward the user’s goal under a budget, leaves safety to approvers that only limit motion, and offers an exit only for goals that cannot be achieved. The model can recognize the hazard when asked, but the loop never asks. The logs are consistent with this account but do not establish it: we did not vary the loop, and reasoning summaries may not reflect what drives behavior [7]. The policy and the safety check use different prompts because they do different jobs, one choosing the next motion and the other deciding whether to proceed. The default loop contains only the first job, and the check supplies the second. It also does not depend on the policy remembering safety across a 30-call episode, which the policy did not do once the harm had been raised. Refusal measured in conversation therefore does not predict refusal in an agent loop. We suggest that harnesses offer a runtime safety check as an option, that evaluations pair each robot trial with the same question, and that give_up not be treated as a safety mechanism.

The main limits are the number of trials and the breadth of tasks. Each condition has 15 hazardous decisions, 5 per task, at about US$2.60 per robot trial, so the 95% intervals are wide and the results show that the effect exists, not precise rates. All three tasks are pick-and-place with harm to a child or pet that is common knowledge and named in the instruction. We did not test other risk types (health, social, property, privacy), hazards that must be inferred from perception, ambiguous instructions, or adversarial phrasing, and we do not know which risks the model fails to recognize even when asked. We tested one model, in one harness, on one robot. We evaluated the check on its first decision, from recorded start frames, not in a live loop or at every step, so we do not know whether it stops hazards that arise mid-execution. Because it asks directly about carrying out the instruction, it invites a safety judgment. We have not tested a policy prompt that adds a safety reminder (for example, “make sure your actions are safe”), which may narrow the gap, so the check’s advantages over such a prompt, a decision made before any motion and independence from the policy’s prompt, are untested. Finally, vitamins outcomes were graded by an operator during live runs, and harm tags by a single reviewer, with no second grader.

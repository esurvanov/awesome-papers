Abstract

In healthcare, medical tasks often involve multiple interdependent steps, where each outcome informs subsequent decisions. Specialist AI models achieve state-of-the-art performance on individual tasks, whereas generalist vision-language models cover more tasks but typically lag behind specialists. Neither is designed to decide which analyses are needed, execute them in order, or pass intermediate results between steps. We proposed MMedAgent-Plus, a multimodal medical tool-use agent that couples a vision-language planner with a library of 18 specialist models spanning 12 medical tasks and 8 modalities, each selected as a leading publicly available model for its task. Rather than approximating expert capabilities within a single model, the planner learns to orchestrate these specialists, deciding which to call and in what order, and to generate a final response grounded in their outputs. All tools share a unified interface declaring its inputs, parameters, and outputs, so that models with different architectures are invoked in one call format. The backbone planner is instruction-tuned on 208,928 samples curated from 32 public medical benchmarks, spanning diverse tool-use scenarios involving single or multiple tools and varying numbers of interaction rounds. We evaluate tool selection, response synthesis, and end-to-end workflow execution separately on held-out evaluation samples. MMedAgent-Plus selects the correct specialist for 91.0%, 98.1%, and 80.7% of individual tool calls in these three settings, respectively. Given correct tool outputs, MMedAgent-Plus achieves scores of 14.4-14.8 out of 15 for faithfulness, completeness, and conciseness across 600 evaluation samples. In end-to-end execution without pre-supplied tool outputs, MMedAgent-Plus correctly invokes the required specialist in 90.6% of 350 evaluation cases spanning seven tasks. Its final-answer accuracy reaches 0.98, matching the dedicated leading specialist and exceeding the strongest baseline (0.28).

## 1 Main

Over the past decade, deep learning has achieved expert-level performance on selected medical tasks, including radiograph interpretation [1], histopathology image classification [2], and anatomical structure segmentation [3]. These advances have largely been driven by specialist models [4, 5] designed for specific modalities and tasks. However, real-world clinical workflows often require multiple interdependent analyses rather than isolated predictions [6, 7].For example, a radiologist may identify and characterize suspicious lesions before integrating the findings into a diagnostic report. Similarly, a clinician may assess symptoms and medical history, select diagnostic tests, interpret the results, and develop a management plan. These tasks require diverse input modalities and specialized expertise, with intermediate findings guiding subsequent analyses and decisions. Although specialist models excel at individual tasks, they typically operate in isolation, lacking the ability to determine which analyses are needed, coordinate their execution, and integrate results into a coherent medical workflow.

Medical foundation models [8, 9, 10, 11] have emerged to address the fragmentation of specialist systems by extending task and modality coverage within unified architectures. Models such as MedSAM [3] and BiomedCLIP [12] improve generalization across medical imaging modalities and biomedical image-text representations, respectively. Generative vision-language models (VLMs), including LLaVA-Med [13] and LLaVA-Rad [14], enable open-ended interactions with medical images, while generalist models such as BiomedGPT [8], RadFM [15], and HealthGPT [16] support a broader range of tasks within unified frameworks. Building on this progress, recent models, including VILA-M3 [9], Lingshu [10], Hulu-Med [17], OmniV-Med [18], Fleming-VL [19], and UniBiomed [20], have further expanded task and modality coverage within unified architectures. However, broad task coverage does not inherently provide the ability to coordinate multiple specialized analyses. Complex medical workflows require selecting appropriate tools, executing them in sequence, and adapting subsequent steps based on intermediate results, capabilities that extend beyond supporting diverse tasks within a single model.

Medical agents offer a promising approach to workflow orchestration by dynamically selecting and coordinating specialized capabilities. Existing approaches can be broadly divided into two paradigms. The first emphasizes collaboration among multiple language or vision-language models, where agents assume specialized roles and exchange information through dialogue or structured discussions to reach a shared conclusion [21, 22, 23, 24, 25, 26, 27]. While these systems facilitate collaborative medical reasoning, their coordination primarily occurs through model-to-model interactions rather than the execution of external specialist tools. Unlike model-based collaborative agents, these systems generally do not directly execute specialized medical operations that produce outputs such as segmentation masks or processed medical images.
A second paradigm integrates external specialist tools, enabling medical agents to invoke task-specific models and obtain their outputs directly. Representative systems include MMedAgent [28], MedRAX [29], MedAgent-Pro [30], and VoxelPrompt [31]. By incorporating executable tools, these systems support workflows that extend beyond text-based reasoning to specialized image analysis and processing.Despite this progress, many tool-augmented medical agents have been developed or evaluated within particular modalities or clinical domains, including general medical imaging [32, 33], histopathology [34, 35], endoscopy [36], chest radiography [37, 38, 39, 40, 41, 42], and Alzheimer’s disease [43]. Recent studies have also investigated tool selection and specialized workflow execution, including ToolSelect [44] and MedScope [45]. However, the reliability of tool orchestration across heterogeneous medical tasks, modalities, and multi-step interactions remains insufficiently characterized.

We introduce MMedAgent-Plus (denoted as $\mathcal{M}$ ), a multimodal medical tool-use agent designed to orchestrate leading specialist models across diverse medical workflows. Building on MMedAgent [28], $\mathcal{M}$ combines a vision-language planner with a library of 18 specialist models covering 12 medical tasks and 8 modalities (Figure 1a; Supplementary Tables S1 and S2). These tasks span language understanding, visual analysis, image processing, and clinical report generation, across text, Computed Tomography (CT), Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI), X-ray, ultrasound (US), retinal imaging (including fundus and Optical Coherence Tomography (OCT)), and pathology. Each specialist was selected as the leading publicly available model for its task at the time of construction, based on reported benchmark performance (Supplementary Table S1).

To enable reliable tool orchestration, we construct a large-scale instruction-tuning corpus $\mathcal{S}$ containing 208,928 samples drawn from 32 publicly available medical benchmarks (Supplementary Table S3). The dataset is organized into three dialogue settings that reflect different workflow patterns, including single-tool multi-round dialogues for iterative individual analyses, multi-tool multi-round dialogues for progressive workflow execution and for successive independent requests across multiple dialogue turns, and multi-tool single-round dialogues for compositional workflows within one interaction (Figure 1d). The tool chains covered by each multi-tool setting are listed in Supplementary Tables S6 and S5, and one example dialogue per task is shown in Supplementary Figure S1-S8. This training enables $\mathcal{M}$ to select specialist tools, coordinate their execution, and integrate tool-generated evidence into final responses.

The effectiveness of MMedAgent-Plus depends on two complementary components: the planner’s ability to select and coordinate appropriate specialist tools, and the performance of the specialists themselves. Across the three dialogue settings, MMedAgent-Plus achieves tool-call accuracies of 91.0%, 98.1%, and 80.7% for single-tool multi-round, multi-tool multi-round, and multi-tool single-round interactions, respectively. Its final responses also outperform those of four recent vision-language models, with improvements in faithfulness (consistency with specialist outputs) and completeness (coverage of relevant information) (Figure 1b).
We further evaluate $\mathcal{M}$ on end-to-end medical workflows requiring the selection and coordination of complementary specialist tools across modalities, followed by the synthesis of tool-grounded responses. Together, these results demonstrate the potential of learned tool orchestration to support multi-step medical workflows across diverse tasks and imaging modalities, extending beyond isolated specialist predictions and direct-answer generation (Figure 1c). Our work establishes a framework for coordinating leading specialists through a learned planner, so that expert-level performance on individual tasks carries over into multi-step clinical workflows.

## 2 Results

The performance of MMedAgent-Plus relies on three capabilities: selecting and invoking appropriate specialist tools, executing these tools successfully, and synthesizing their outputs into coherent responses. We assess these capabilities through complementary component-level and end-to-end evaluations. At the component level, we evaluate tool orchestration accuracy on 1,500 held-out dialogues(Section 2.1), and response synthesis quality on 600 cases with correct specialist outputs provided (Section 2.2). We then assess end-to-end performance on 350 single-tool cases, requiring the model to independently select and invoke the appropriate specialist and integrate its output into the final response (Section 2.3). This evaluation design allows us to distinguish performance at individual stages from that of the integrated system, and it also tests whether component-level results are maintained when these stages are combined end to end. Evaluation metrics are detailed in Section 4.5.3.

Evaluation data.

All evaluation cases are drawn from held-out partitions established before training. Approximately 10% of dialogues from each setting were reserved for evaluation, with no overlap in dialogues or source images between training and evaluation (Supplementary Section S2.6). This yielded held-out pools of 9,128 single-tool multi-round, 10,000 multi-tool multi-round, and 2,430 multi-tool single-round dialogues.

Using a fixed random seed, we sampled three evaluation sets: (i) 500 dialogues per setting for tool orchestration (Section 2.1); (ii) 600 disjoint response-synthesis cases, comprising 200 per setting, each extracted as a single tool-return and response round from a held-out dialogue (Section 2.2); and (iii) 350 end-to-end cases, comprising 50 cases for each of seven specialist tools, drawn from the single-tool pool (Section 2.3).

The evaluation sets are smaller than the training corpus, which spans 18 specialist tools, 8 modalities, and thousands of tool-chain combinations (Supplementary Tables S6 and S5). Nevertheless, the 95% Wilson confidence intervals for tool-orchestration accuracy have margins of error within 4 percentage points across all three settings (Figure 3a). Representative dialogues and qualitative end-to-end comparisons are provided in Supplementary Figure S1-S8 and S10-S24.

## 2.1 MMedAgent-Plus demonstrates effective tool orchestration across diverse dialogue settings

We evaluate $\mathcal{M}$ on 500 held-out dialogues for each of the three dialogue types (1,500 in total; Section 2), with full results shown in Figure 3a. Each dialogue represents a complete interaction, potentially spanning multiple round of user requests and system responses, with tool calls occurring within or across rounds. We assess orchestration performance using two complementary metrics: Tool-call accuracy (Eq. 3), which measures the correctness of individual tool selections, and Whole-case accuracy (Eq. 4), which requires all tool calls within a dialogue to be correct. We additionally report micro- and macro-averaged precision, recall, and F1 [46] to characterize tool-level performance. Figure 3b-d presents aggregate and per-tool results across the three dialogue settings, while Figure 4 summarizes tool- and task-level recall and incorrect routing assignments.

Single-tool multi-round.

In the single-tool multi-round setting, $\mathcal{M}$ achieves 91.0% whole-case and tool-call accuracy (95% CI, 88.2-93.2%), indicating consistent tool selection across successive interactions involving individual specialists. Figure 3b shows that achieve precision, recall, and F1 scores above 99.0%, including PMC-LLaMA [47] (text QA), RaTE-NER [48] (named entity recognition (NER)), SpecialistVLMs [49] (retinal OCT report generation), CellViT [50] (WSI cell segmentation), and DSMIL-C16 [51] (breast tumour classification). Other tools, including HealthGPT [16] (reconstruction and super-resolution), LLaVA-Rad [14] (radiology report generation), and ChatCAD-R [52] (retrieval-augmented generation (RAG)) also exceed 90.0% across all three metrics, indicating strong routing performance across diverse medical tasks and modalities.

Multi-tool multi-round.

The multi-tool multi-round setting achieves the highest overall performance, with 98.1% tool-call accuracy (95% CI, 97.2-98.7%; 1,251 tool calls) and 95.8% whole-case accuracy (95% CI, 93.7-97.2%) (Figure 3a). Micro-averaged precision, recall, and F1 are 98.2%, 98.1%, and 98.1%, respectively, matching the macro-averaged scores of 98.3%, 97.7%, and 97.9% (Figure 3c). This agreement suggests that performance is distributed across specialist tools rather than dominated by frequently selected tools. At the individual-tool level, nine specialists achieve precision, recall, and F1 scores above 99.0%, including HealthGPT, PMC-LLaMA, LLaVA-Rad, CellSAM [53] (2D cell segmentation), IterNet [54] (fundus segmentation), and UniGradICON [55] (image registration), while five additional tools exceed 90.0% across all three metrics.

Multi-tool single-round.

The multi-tool single-round setting yields the lowest orchestration accuracy among the three settings, reflecting the challenge of coordinating multiple specialist tools within a single interaction. $\mathcal{M}$ achieves 80.7% tool-call accuracy (95% CI 77.0-83.9%) and 77.1% whole-case accuracy (73.2-80.6%). Micro-averaged precision, recall, and F1 are 87.5%, 80.7%, and 84.0%, respectively, compared with macro-averaged scores of 82.7%, 82.6%, and 82.0% (Figure 3d). Despite the lower aggregate performance, specialists such as UltraSAM [56], IterNet maintain high precision and recall. The difference between tool-call and whole-case accuracy suggests that some dialogues contain a mixture of correct and incorrect tool selections, highlighting the challenge of accurately composing multiple specialist operations.

Aggregate error structure.

Across the three dialogue settings, routing errors are concentrated in a small subset of specialist tools and tasks (Figure 4). All but three tools achieve recall above 0.90, with nine reaching 1.00. The lowest recall is observed for UniGradICON (0.36), CONCH (0.64), and MedSAM (0.76) (Figure 4a). Two dominant error patterns emerge at the tool level (Figure 4b). First, the model occasionally confuses tools with related capabilities, most notably routing 36% of CONCH cases to LLaVA-Med (VQA), potentially reflecting overlap between pathology classification and medical visual question answering. Second, the model sometimes bypasses required specialist tools and responds directly, as observed in 64% of UniGradICON cases and 24% of MedSAM cases routed to No tool. Similar patterns emerge at the task level, where most tasks achieve recall of at least 0.94, except Registration (0.36) and Segmentation (0.88) (Figure 4c). Registration errors consist almost entirely of omitted tool invocations, consistent with the UniGradICON results. Other errors include Segmentation routed to Classification (12%), Visual Question Answering routed to Classification (2%), and confusion between Grounding and Grounding-Segmentation (Figure 4d).

We evaluate the quality of final responses generated by $\mathcal{M}$ on 600 held-out cases (200 per dialogue setting), each comprising a single tool-return and response round extracted from a held-out dialogue (Section 2). These cases are disjoint from those used for tool orchestration evaluation (Section 2.1). To isolate response synthesis from tool-selection errors, we provide $\mathcal{M}$ with the correct specialist outputs and assess its ability to integrate them into coherent, tool-grounded responses. We adopt an LLM-as-a-judge protocol evaluating three criteria: faithfulness, completeness, and conciseness, each scored from 1 to 5, with a combined total of 3 to 15, defined in Section 4.5.3). For $\mathcal{M}$ , faithfulness measures consistency with the provided specialist outputs rather than clinical correctness relative to the original images. For direct-answer baselines, which do not receive specialist outputs, faithfulness is assessed against the original multimodal inputs (Section 4.5.3). The results across the three dialogue settings are summarized in Table 1, with modality- and task-level analyses presented in Figure 1b.

MMedAgent-Plus achieves the highest overall response quality scores across all three dialogue settings (Table 1). Its largest advantage is observed in the multi-tool single-round setting, where it scores 14.77 out of 15, compared with 10.89 for the strongest direct-answer baseline, Qwen3-VL-8B-Instruct [59]. The corresponding scores are 14.52 versus 13.27 in single-tool multi-round dialogues and 14.36 versus 12.86 in multi-tool multi-round dialogues. Improvements are most pronounced in faithfulness and completeness, with MMedAgent-Plus achieving perfect scores of 5.00 on both criteria in the multi-tool single-round setting. Conciseness scores are also higher across all three settings, reaching 4.77 compared with 4.10 for Qwen3-VL-8B-Instruct in the multi-tool single-round setting.

Modality-level analysis shows that MMedAgent-Plus consistently outperforms direct-answer baselines across the evaluated imaging modalities, with particularly large differences in retinal imaging (Figure 1b). For fundus images, all baselines score below 50%, whereas MMedAgent-Plus approaches 100%. A similarly large margin is observed for retinal OCT, while higher scores are also maintained across CT, MRI, X-ray, and ultrasound.

Task-level analysis shows that MMedAgent-Plus achieves higher response quality scores than direct-answer baselines across most evaluated tasks (Figure 1b), with particularly strong performance in report generation, RAG, and NER. An important distinction arises in grounding-segmentation tasks, where direct-answer baselines provide only textual descriptions without generating segmentation outputs. Consequently, response quality scores capture only the textual component of these tasks and do not fully reflect the differences in task completion between tool-grounded and direct-answer approaches.

Together, these results demonstrate that the strengths of $\mathcal{M}$ extend beyond tool selection to the synthesis of specialist-generated evidence. Given correct tool outputs, $\mathcal{M}$ produces more faithful and complete responses than direct-answer baselines under the evaluation protocol, with the largest margin observed in the multi-tool single-round setting. As this evaluation isolates response synthesis from tool selection and execution, it does not establish whether these advantages translate into end-to-end task performance. We investigate this question in Section 2.3, where $\mathcal{M}$ must independently select and execute specialist tools before generating its final responses.

## 2.3 MMedAgent-Plus preserves specialist-level performance

We evaluate the end-to-end performance of MMedAgent-Plus without pre-supplied specialist outputs, requiring the system to independently select and execute tools before generating final responses. The evaluation covers seven representative tasks, each associated with one of the 18 specialist tools, using 50 held-out cases per task (350 in total) drawn from the single-tool multi-round test set (Section 4.4, Table 4). These include QA / rationale generation (PMC-LLaMA [47]), named entity recognition (RaTE-NER [48]), report generation (LLaVA-Rad [14]), pathology classification (CONCH [2]), multimodal classification (BiomedCLIP [12]), fundus segmentation (IterNet [54]), and general medical image segmentation (MedSAM [3]). We assess required-tool invocation accuracy and task-specific performance across all seven tasks, with additional LLM-as-a-judge evaluation for the five text-producing tasks.

## 2.3.1 Specialist Invocation Accuracy

Across 350 held-out end-to-end cases, $\mathcal{M}$ correctly invokes the required specialist in 317 cases, achieving 90.6% invocation accuracy (95% Wilson CI, 87.1-93.2%; Figure 5b).

Perfect invocation accuracy (50/50 cases) is observed for QA / rationale generation, , named entity recognition, and multimodal classification. General medical image segmentation reaches 92.0% (81.2-96.9%), report generation reaches 90.0% (78.6-95.7%), and fundus segmentation reaches 82.0% (69.2-90.2%). Pathology classification shows the lowest accuracy at 70.0% (56.2-80.9%), consistent with the tool-routing difficulties observed for CONCH in Section 2.1.

## 2.3.2 Response Quality under LLM-as-a-judge

We assess end-to-end response quality using the same three-criterion LLM-as-a-judge protocol described in Section 2.2. MMedAgent-Plus is judged against the specialist outputs it receives, whereas the direct-answer baselines are judged against the original multimodal input. Figure 5d provides a shared task-specific reference. Figure 5c reports means and 95% confidence intervals for faithfulness, completeness, and conciseness on all five baseline-comparable tasks (QA / rationale generation, named entity recognition, report generation, pathology classification, and multimodal classification). Figure 5a summarizes the same five tasks as per-task means of the three criteria.

MMedAgent-Plus achieves the highest mean response quality score across all five evaluated tasks. The largest margins over direct-answer baselines are observed in radiology report generation (4.40 versus 2.41 for MedGemma-1.5-4B-IT), named entity recognition (4.96 versus 2.95), and multimodal classification (4.93 versus 3.21 for Qwen3-VL-8B). Smaller but consistent differences are observed in QA / rationale generation (4.53 versus 4.12 for MedGemma-1.5-4B-IT) and pathology classification (3.57 versus 2.29). For radiology report generation, MMedAgent-Plus achieves scores of 4.60 and 4.56, respectively, whereas all direct-answer baselines score below 2.0 on both criteria. Conciseness is generally comparable or higher, except in QA / rationale generation, where MedGemma-1.5-4B-IT and GPT-5.4-mini score 5.00 and 4.92, respectively, compared with 4.14 for MMedAgent-Plus.

## 2.3.3 Task-Specific Tool Output Quality

Figure 5d compares task-specific performance measured directly from specialist outputs with that obtained from the final responses of MMedAgent-Plus and direct-answer VLM baselines. This comparison assesses how well specialist capabilities are preserved during end-to-end execution.

For QA / rationale generation, MMedAgent-Plus matches the specialist accuracy of 0.98, compared with 0.28 for the strongest direct-answer baseline. In named entity recognition, it preserves the specialist RadGraph F1 of 0.64 and achieves a higher entity-level F1 (0.90 versus 0.84). For report generation and multimodal classification, MMedAgent-Plus also achieves scores closest to the specialist outputs and outperforms direct-answer baselines across the reported metrics. The exception is ROUGE-L for QA / rationale generation, where MedGemma-1.5-4B-IT scores 0.23 compared with 0.20 for MMedAgent-Plus.

Pathology classification exhibits a larger performance gap relative to the specialist, with micro-F1 decreasing from 0.72 to 0.38 across all 50 cases. Among the 35 cases with correct specialist invocation, the corresponding scores are 0.69 and 0.51, suggesting that both routing failures and subsequent response synthesis contribute to the overall performance loss. For the two segmentation tasks, Dice and HD95 are computed directly from masks generated during end-to-end execution. These metrics are unavailable for direct-answer baselines, which produce only textual responses rather than segmentation masks.

## 3 Discussion

## 3.1 Tool orchestration extends medical AI beyond isolated specialist models

MMedAgent-Plus demonstrates the potential of integrating heterogeneous specialist models through a unified vision-language planner. By coordinating 18 specialist tools spanning 12 medical tasks and eight modalities, the system extends beyond isolated predictions toward flexible tool-based medical workflows. Instruction tuning across three dialogue settings enables the planner to select appropriate specialists and integrate their outputs into coherent responses. The results show high tool-call accuracy across settings, reaching 98.1% tool-call accuracy in multi-tool multi-round setting, 91.0% in single-tool multi-round and 80.7% in multi-tool single-round (Figure 3a). Its response synthesis consistently achieves higher faithfulness and completeness scores than direct-answer VLM baselines. In the multi-tool single-round setting, for example, $\mathcal{M}$ achieves a total final response score of 14.77, compared with 10.89 for the strongest baseline (Table 1). End-to-end evaluations further demonstrate that the system can autonomously invoke specialist tools and retain task-specific performance in several medical tasks (Figure 5c)). Together, these results show that $\mathcal{M}$ can orchestrate heterogeneous specialist tools and preserve their outputs in the final response, combining specialized medical capabilities within a unified agent framework.

The value of this approach is particularly evident in medical workflows involving multiple interdependent analyses. MMedAgent-Plus is designed to coordinate specialist tools sequentially, allowing intermediate outputs to inform subsequent operations. This capability distinguishes tool-based orchestration from two common approaches. Generalist VLMs [8, 9, 10, 11] support diverse medical tasks within unified architectures but typically generate responses directly from the input without explicitly executing specialized operations.
Role-based medical agents, including MMedAgent-RL [25], TeamMedAgents [26], and MedLA [27], primarily coordinate language-model agents through collaborative reasoning rather than the execution and transfer of non-text medical artifacts. In contrast, MMedAgent-Plus explicitly models tool-call sequences, achieving whole-case accuracies of 95.8% in multi-tool multi-round dialogues and 77.1% in multi-tool single-round dialogues (Figure 3a).

MMedAgent-Plus also extends the scope of existing tool-augmented medical agents through a broader specialist library. As summarized in Table 2, many previous systems focus on specific imaging modalities or disease domains [34, 35, 38, 39, 40, 41, 42, 43]. MMedAgent [28], on which our system builds, supports five modalities and six tasks, whereas $\mathcal{M}$ covers eight modalities and twelve tasks, including training examples involving multiple specialist tools. This broader coverage is accompanied by high response quality across diverse modalities, including fundus imaging and retinal OCT, where the largest margins over direct-answer baselines are observed (Figure 1b). Averaged across eight modality axes, $\mathcal{M}$ achieves 96.1%, compared with 78.2% for the strongest baseline (Qwen3-VL-8B). These findings suggest that heterogeneous specialist integration can support broad medical task coverage while maintaining high tool-grounded response quality.

## 3.2 Orchestration errors reflect tool ambiguity and implicit workflow requirements

Tool orchestration performance varies substantially across the three dialogue settings (Figure 3). Tool-call accuracy is highest in multi-tool multi-round dialogues (98.1%), followed by single-tool multi-round (91.0%) and multi-tool single-round dialogues (80.7%), with whole-case accuracy following the same trend. These differences suggest that orchestration difficulty depends not only on the number of required tools but also on when information becomes available to the planner. In multi-round interactions, preceding requests and intermediate tool outputs can inform subsequent tool selections. By contrast, multi-tool single-round requests require the planner to determine the complete tool sequence upfront, before any specialist is executed or intermediate results become available.

The multi-tool multi-round setting achieves the highest performance, with 98.1% tool-call accuracy and 95.8% whole-case accuracy. In this setting, the planner selects tools incrementally rather than committing to the complete sequence from the initial request. Follow-up turns can therefore be interpreted together with previously executed tools and their returned intermediate outputs. Such contextual information may help narrow the choice of specialist tools by clarifying the imaging modality and current workflow stage. The closely aligned micro- and macro-averaged F1 scores (98.1% and 97.9%, respectively) further suggest that strong performance extends across the tool library rather than being dominated by frequently selected tools. Together, these findings highlight the potential value of intermediate context for sequential tool orchestration.

Single-tool multi-round dialogues achieve 91.0% tool-call and whole-case accuracy, with identical values because each dialogue contains only one tool invocation. Unlike multi-tool multi-round interactions, these dialogues provide no preceding specialist outputs to inform tool selection, requiring the planner to identify the appropriate tool primarily from the current request and input. This can introduce ambiguity when specialists accept similar input modalities or produce similar outputs. Per-tool F1 scores are lower for CONCH (0.50), LLaVA-Med (0.78), and CellSAM (0.80), whereas several other tools approach perfect performance (Figure 3b). For example, CONCH performs pathology classification, while LLaVA-Med supports general medical visual question answering. Both of them can process pathology images and generate textual outputs, potentially making their intended use difficult to distinguish from certain requests. Such ambiguity may contribute to the gap between micro- and macro-averaged F1, suggesting that orchestration errors are concentrated in a subset of tools with overlapping capabilities.

Multi-tool single-round dialogues show the lowest orchestration performance, with 80.7% tool-call accuracy and 77.1% whole-case accuracy. Micro-averaged recall equals tool-call accuracy (80.7%) and is lower than micro-averaged precision (87.5%), suggesting that missed tool invocations are more prevalent than unnecessary selections. This imbalance is less apparent under macro-averaging, where precision (82.7%) and recall (82.6%) are nearly identical, reflecting differences in tool frequency and error distribution. LLaVA-Rad illustrates the omission pattern, achieving 100% precision but only 78.0% recall across 350 required calls, with missed invocations routed to no tool. In these cases, report generation is an implicit intermediate step that the planner must infer from the request. Failure to recognize this dependency leads to incomplete tool sequences, contributing to the lower whole-case accuracy. These findings highlight the difficulty of planning multi-tool workflows when intermediate operations are not explicitly specified.

Taken together, these findings suggest two recurring challenges in tool orchestration (Figure 4). First, tools with overlapping input modalities and output formats can be difficult to distinguish when the user’s intended task is ambiguous, as illustrated by confusion between CONCH and LLaVA-Med. Second, required intermediate operations may be omitted when they are not explicitly specified in the request, leading to incomplete tool sequences. The higher accuracy observed in multi-tool multi-round dialogues suggests that preceding tool calls and intermediate outputs may help resolve such ambiguity by clarifying the current workflow state. Conversely, tools with more distinctive task and modality requirements, including ChatCAD-R, PMC-LLaMA, RaTE-NER, SpecialistVLMs, and CellViT, achieve recall above 99%, with similarly high recall observed across CT, MRI, ultrasound, retinal imaging, and X-ray tasks.

## 3.3 Residual errors arise from specialist selection and response synthesis

End-to-end evaluation shows that $\mathcal{M}$ successfully invokes the required specialist in 90.6% of cases and achieves higher LLM-as-a-judge scores than direct-answer baselines across all five evaluated tasks (Figure 5a-c). The largest margins are observed in tasks where specialist tools provide task-specific evidence, including radiology report generation and multimodal classification, whereas the difference is smaller for open-ended question answering. Task-specific metrics further indicate that specialist performance is largely retained during response synthesis for several tasks, with $\mathcal{M}$ matching the specialist accuracy of 0.98 in QA / rationale generation and achieving comparable performance in named entity recognition, report generation, and multimodal classification (Figure 5d). These findings suggest that the benefits of tool-grounded response generation can extend to autonomous end-to-end execution, although performance remains sensitive to errors in specialist selection and subsequent output integration.

End-to-end performance losses can arise at two stages: specialist selection and post-invocation response synthesis. Pathology classification illustrates both challenges. Its invocation accuracy is the lowest among the evaluated tasks (70.0%), consistent with the confusion between CONCH and LLaVA-Med observed in the tool orchestration analysis. Even when the correct specialist is invoked, converting its outputs into final responses can introduce further information loss. Across all 50 pathology classification cases, micro-F1 decreases from 0.72 for the specialist to 0.38 for $\mathcal{M}$ . Among the 35 cases with correct specialist invocation, the corresponding scores are 0.69 and 0.51, suggesting that both routing failures and post-invocation losses contribute to the overall performance gap. A related trade-off appears in QA / rationale generation, where $\mathcal{M}$ achieves higher response quality but lower conciseness scores than MedGemma and GPT-5.4-mini. For segmentation tasks, performance is assessed from generated masks using Dice and HD95, rather than from natural-language responses. Together, these findings highlight the importance of both accurate tool selection and faithful preservation of specialist outputs during end-to-end execution.

## 3.4 Extending tool orchestration to adaptive workflows and clinical practice

Our framework has several limitations. First, end-to-end performance depends on the quality of the underlying specialist tools, whose errors may propagate to final responses or subsequent workflow steps. This limitation is evident in general medical image segmentation, where output quality remains limited despite correct tool invocation (Figure 5d). Second, the evaluated workflows are less complex than those encountered in clinical practice. Our multi-tool settings involve chains of two to three tools constructed from public medical benchmarks rather than prospectively collected clinical workflows. Our evaluation does not cover substantially longer tool sequences, workflows in which the choice of a later tool depends on the specific output of an earlier step, or cases in which several valid tool sequences may address the same request. The reliability observed in the current experiments should therefore not be assumed to extend directly to these more complex settings. Finally, extending the tool library to new tasks or modalities requires additional instruction tuning. Although compatible specialists can replace existing tools without changing their interfaces, introducing new capabilities requires the planner to learn when and how to invoke them. Prior work on MMedAgent [28] suggests that incremental training can incorporate new tools without substantially degrading existing capabilities, but this remains unverified for our larger, more heterogeneous library.

These limitations suggest several directions for future work. More explicit workflow planning, targeted instruction tuning, and tool-selection verification may help address ambiguity between specialists and failures to identify implicit intermediate operations. Future systems should also support longer, adaptive workflows in which tool selection responds to intermediate findings, multiple execution paths are permitted, and workflows terminate once sufficient evidence is obtained. Beyond benchmark-based evaluation, clinician-designed cases and prospective clinical studies are needed to assess orchestration reliability under realistic variation in patient data, task requirements, and workflow complexity.

In conclusion, MMedAgent-Plus demonstrates the feasibility of coordinating heterogeneous specialist medical models through an instruction-tuned vision-language planner. Integrating 18 specialist tools across 12 tasks and 8 modalities, the system achieves high tool-selection accuracy and generates faithful, complete responses grounded in specialist outputs. End-to-end evaluation further demonstrates the preservation of specialist-level performance on selected tasks, while highlighting the effects of routing and post-invocation errors. These findings support tool orchestration as a complementary approach to generalist medical AI, although longer adaptive workflows and prospective clinical validation remain necessary before deployment in clinical practice.

## 4 Methods

## 4.1 System Overview

MMedAgent-Plus is a multimodal medical tool-use agent that integrates a vision-language planner $\mathcal{M}$ with a library of specialist medical tools $\mathcal{T}=\{t_{1},\ldots,t_{K}\}$ , where $K$ denotes the number of available tools. Rather than incorporating all medical capabilities into a single model, the framework coordinates medical foundation models and task-specific specialists with heterogeneous input and output interfaces. Given a medical request, the planner determines whether specialist tools are needed, selects the appropriate tools, and coordinates their execution to support the requested task or workflow.

At inference, $\mathcal{M}$ receives a multimodal input $x=(q,\mathcal{I})$ , where $q$ denotes the user query and $\mathcal{I}$ denotes the set of input images, which may be empty for text-only tasks. The planner also incorporates the dialogue history and any outputs returned by previously executed tools. Based on this context, $\mathcal{M}$ generates structured tool calls when external execution is required. The selected specialists are executed, and their outputs are returned to the planner to inform subsequent tool selections or final response generation. This iterative process enables the system to coordinate specialist operations across dialogue turns and incorporate intermediate results into subsequent steps. The following sections detail the planner architecture and fine-tuning (Section 4.2), instruction-tuning data construction (Section 4.3), and specialist tool library and interfaces (Section 4.4).

## 4.2 Backbone Model and Interaction Framework

MMedAgent-Plus employs Qwen2.5-VL-7B [60] as its vision-language tool-use planner $\mathcal{M}$ . The planner generates tool calls in JSON format, specifying the specialist tool and its input parameters. For example, a MedSAM call takes the form {"API_name": "MedSAM", "API_params": {"boxes": [[x1,y1,x2,y2]]}}. During multi-round interactions, the planner receives the dialogue history and outputs from previously executed tools, including segmentation masks, bounding boxes, and generated reports, to inform subsequent tool selection and response generation.

We adapt $\mathcal{M}$ for medical tool orchestration through supervised instruction tuning on the curated dataset $\mathcal{S}$ (Section 4.3). The vision-language projector and language decoder are fine-tuned while the vision encoder remains frozen. Training minimizes the causal language modeling loss in Eq. (2) over generated sequences comprising structured tool calls and natural-language responses.

 $\mathcal{L}=-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\log P_{\mathcal{M}}\!\left(y_{i}\mid y_{<i},\,x\right),$ 

(2)

where $y_{i}$ denotes the $i$ -th output token and $x$ represents the multimodal context, including the user input, dialogue history, images, and available tool outputs. This formulation allows $\mathcal{M}$ learn tool selection, tool sequencing, and response generation through instruction tuning.

## 4.3 Dataset Curation

We construct a multimodal instruction-tuning corpus $\mathcal{S}$ to train the planner $\mathcal{M}$ for specialist tool selection and workflow orchestration (Table 3). The corpus comprises three dialogue settings: single-tool multi-round, which trains individual specialist invocation across dialogue turns; multi-tool multi-round, which supports sequential tool selection based on preceding dialogue and available tool outputs; and multi-tool single-round, which requires the planner to determine a complete sequence of specialist tools from a single request. Each sample comprises a user query, optional medical images and preceding tool outputs, and a target sequence containing structured tool calls, natural-language responses, or both. Training and evaluation data are separated according to the protocol described in Section 4.5.

## 4.3.1 Data Sources

We curate training samples from 32 publicly available medical datasets and benchmarks spanning text, X-ray, CT, MRI, ultrasound, retinal imaging, and pathology. The corpus covers a broad range of medical tasks, including question answering, named entity recognition, retrieval-augmented generation, visual question answering, classification, detection, segmentation, registration, reconstruction, super-resolution, and report generation.

Text-based tasks draw on datasets associated with PMC-LLaMA [47], PubMedQA, and RaTE-NER [48]. Visual understanding samples are derived from resources associated with LLaVA-Med [13], BiomedCLIP [12], CONCH [2], and DSMIL [51]. Segmentation samples are curated from benchmarks used in MedSAM [3] for general medical imaging, UltraSAM [56] for ultrasound, IterNet [54] for retinal fundus imaging, and CellViT [50] and CellSAM [53] for pathology. Image transformation data cover registration and reconstruction or super-resolution, using resources associated with UniGradICON [55] and HealthGPT [16], respectively. Report generation samples are drawn from datasets associated with LLaVA-Rad [14] for radiology and SpecialistVLMs [49] for retinal OCT. The complete list of data sources and sample counts is provided in Table S3.

## 4.3.2 Instruction Dataset Construction

The instruction-tuning corpus $\mathcal{S}$ comprises 208,928 samples organized into three dialogue settings. The instruction format, including user inputs, structured tool calls, returned tool outputs, and final responses, is detailed in Supplementary Section S2.5.

 $\mathcal{S}_{1}$ contains 97,055 single-tool multi-round dialogues. Each dialogue is associated with one specialist tool $t_{k}\in\mathcal{T}$ and trains $\mathcal{M}$ to determine when the tool should be invoked during an ongoing conversation. Tool-use turns are constructed from the corresponding task-specific datasets, with general conversational turns randomly inserted before or after the tool-use turn. This design exposes the planner to contexts both requiring and not requiring specialist invocation, while preserving dialogue continuity across multiple turns.

 $\mathcal{S}_{2}$ contains 90,000 multi-tool multi-round dialogues, each involving two to four tool invocations distributed across multiple turns. Dialogues are constructed from seven abstract task-chain templates combined with four conversation scenarios: a single image reused throughout; two images provided initially for tasks such as registration; a second image introduced in a later turn; and a switch to an unrelated image during the dialogue. Each abstract task is mapped to a specialist through a tool-modality routing table, allowing the same template to yield different tool sequences across modalities (Supplementary Table S6). The resulting dialogues follow two interaction patterns. In dependent workflows, successive requests concern the same medical study, with later tools operating on findings returned by earlier specialists. Intermediate outputs, such as segmentation masks, reports, and extracted entities, are retained in the dialogue context for subsequent decisions. In independent workflows, each turn introduces a separate request with its own image or text, requiring the planner to select tools according to the current request rather than reuse previous selections. Candidate tool chains are constrained by modality compatibility in both patterns and by input-output interface compatibility in dependent workflows. All generated $\mathcal{S}_{2}$ dialogues undergo three levels of automatic validation. Structural validation checks dialogue-input consistency, including image-placeholder counts and tool-specific image requirements. Modality validation verifies that each selected tool supports its assigned input modality according to the compatibility matrix. Interface validation checks whether outputs from earlier tools can serve as valid inputs to downstream operations in dependent workflows. Dialogues failing any applicable check are excluded. The chain templates, interaction patterns, and corresponding tool combinations are detailed in Supplementary Table S6.

 $\mathcal{S}_{3}$ contains 21,873 multi-tool single-round instructions, each requiring $\mathcal{M}$ to select and order a complete sequence of two to three specialist tools from a single request. Unlike $\mathcal{S}_{2}$ , the planner must determine the entire sequence before any specialist output becomes available. Chain construction follows the same modality and interface compatibility constraints as $\mathcal{S}_{2}$ . It combines multimodal classification, question answering, segmentation, and report generation, including both two-tool and three-tool sequences. Intermediate tool outputs sampled from $\mathcal{S}_{1}$ are incorporated during dataset construction to provide realistic inputs for downstream operations, but are not available to the planner when it initially selects the complete tool sequence.

## 4.3.3 Dialogue Generation and Data Quality Control

New instruction-tuning dialogues are constructed through a template-based procedure without per-sample language model generation. For each task, predefined banks of user-request phrasings and assistant-response templates are created in a single offline pass using GPT-4 [61] and subsequently fixed (Supplementary Table S4). Single-tool samples are generated by pairing a randomly selected request template with a case from the corresponding source benchmark. Target responses combine structured tool calls, generated according to the tool registry schema, with response templates populated using the reference outputs of the source cases. Multi-tool dialogues are assembled from these samples according to the predefined chain templates and conversation scenarios, with transitions between turns drawn from a fixed phrase bank. A fixed random seed is used for sampling, and the construction scripts and template inventories are provided to support reproducibility.

The instruction-tuning corpus combines data from three sources. For the twelve newly introduced tools, dialogues are synthesized using the template-based procedure described above. For the six tools inherited from MMedAgent [28], we retain the original instruction data, which were generated using GPT-4 and source-dataset annotations according to the procedure described in that work. For template-based synthesis, medical inputs and reference outputs, including images, questions, labels, reports, entity annotations, and segmentation masks, are taken from the source benchmarks rather than generated by GPT-4. The language model is used only to construct the natural-language templates, while structured tool calls follow the predefined tool registry. Generated multi-tool dialogues are further checked for structural consistency, modality compatibility, and input-output interface compatibility as described above. Tool-specific data provenance and sample counts are reported in Supplementary Table S3.

## 4.4 Tool Integration

The tool library $\mathcal{T}$ comprises 18 specialist medical models grouped into four functional categories: (i) text understanding and generation, including question answering, named entity recognition, and retrieval-augmented generation; (ii) visual understanding, including visual question answering and image classification; (iii) image processing, including object detection, segmentation, grounding-segmentation, image registration, reconstruction, and super-resolution; and (iv) medical report generation, covering radiology and retinal OCT reporting. Collectively, these tools span 12 medical tasks and 8 input modalities: text, X-ray, CT, MRI, ultrasound, retinal fundus imaging, retinal OCT, and pathology.

The library integrates medical foundation models and task-specific specialists selected for their reported performance and public availability in the corresponding tasks and modalities. Each tool is registered through a standardized invocation interface specifying its supported input modalities, required parameters, and output types. This common interface allows the planner $\mathcal{M}$ to invoke specialists with heterogeneous architectures and input-output formats. It also supports multi-step workflows in which outputs from upstream tools can be passed to downstream tools when their interfaces are compatible. The complete inventory of specialist models, supported tasks and modalities, and associated data sources is provided in Supplementary Table S3.

## 4.4.1 Text Understanding and Generation

QA & Rationale Generation.
We use PMC-LLaMA [47] as the tool of QA and rationale generation. To incorporate domain knowledge, PMC-LLaMA undergoes initial pretraining on the MedC-K medical corpus, then is fine-tuned with instructions using the MedC-I domain-specific instruction dataset. This two-stage training approach ensures that PMC-LLaMA performs effectively across a variety of medical reasoning tasks.

Named Entity Recognition.
We employ RaTE-NER [48], a named entity recognition (NER) model designed for understanding radiology reports. RaTE-NER is trained on a newly introduced dataset comprising radiology reports annotated for key medical entities, including anatomical structures, findings, diagnoses, and negations. The model is designed to reflect human reasoning by aligning with RaTEscore, a framework that captures clinically relevant relationships between entities and report quality.

Retrieval-Augmented Generation.
We integrate ChatCAD-R [52], a retrieval-augmented generation framework designed to combine free-text radiology knowledge with structured databases. ChatCAD-R retrieves relevant entries from natural language text format. It merge the strengths of LLMs’ medical domain knowledge and logical reasoning with the vision understanding capability of existing medical-image CAD models.

## 4.4.2 Visual Understanding

Visual Question Answering (All).
We adopt LLaVA-Med [13] as the backbone tool for medical VQA. LLaVA-Med is trained on multimodal pairs curated from PMC articles, covering MRI, CT, histology, gross images, and X-rays. Its visual encoder is aligned with biomedical language corpora, allowing it to handle both conversational queries and detailed image-centric questions. LLaVA-Med shows excellent multimodal conversational capability and can follow open-ended instruction to assist with inquiries about a biomedical image.

Classification (All).
We employ BiomedCLIP [12], a biomedical foundation model trained on 15 million scientific image–text pairs, as the classification tool. BiomedCLIP achieves state-of-the-art performance in zero-shot and fine-grained classification across diverse imaging modalities. It classifies input images based on similarity between visual and textual embeddings, enabling robust cross-modal understanding.

Classification (Pathology).
We utilize CONCH [2], a specialized visual-language foundation model trained on over 1.17 million diverse histopathology image-caption pairs, for zero-shot pathology classification. We adapt CONCH to answer diagnostic queries by matching image features to a predefined dictionary of textual descriptions. It leverages variations of pathological class names (e.g., tumor subtypes, tissue types, or morphological features) inserted into standard templates to create ensembled text embeddings for each category. Images are then evaluated based on the highest cosine similarity in the shared image-text embedding space.

## 4.4.3 Image Processing

Tumor Detection and Subtyping (Pathology).
We employ DSMIL [51], a deep multiple instance learning network, for localized tumor detection and classification in whole slide images. To support distinct diagnostic tasks, we adapt the model into two specialized tools: DSMIL-C16 and DSMIL-TCGA. DSMIL-C16 grounds on the Camelyon16 dataset to serve as a general tumor classifier, distinguishing malignant from benign histology. DSMIL-TCGA grounds on the TCGA dataset to perform lung cancer malignancy classification and subtyping, such as differentiating adenocarcinoma from squamous cell carcinoma.

Grounding (All).
We use Grounding DINO [62] as our object detection tool. It merges Transformer-based detector DINO with grounded pre-training, fine-tuned for medical applications to localize anatomical structures, lesions, and cells across MRI, CT, X-ray, and histology. By transforming segmentation masks into bounding boxes during fine-tuning, the model learns to generalize across modalities and disease categories, achieving accurate detection and localization for downstream tasks.

Segmentation (All).
For general segmentation, we adopt MedSAM [3], a domain-adapted version of the Segment Anything Model (SAM) [63] fine-tuned on diverse medical datasets spanning MRI, CT, X-ray, histology, and gross images. It supports box- and point-based prompts, enabling interactive and delivering accurate and efficient segmentation across modalities.

Grounding-Segmentation (All).
For grounding-segmentation (G-Seg), we chain Grounding DINO with MedSAM. Grounding DINO first localizes the region of interest based on text prompts, and MedSAM refines these into pixel-level segmentation masks. This combination supports clinically realistic workflows in which a physician specifies an object of interest and the agent automatically outputs precise boundaries.

Segmentation (Ultrasound). We use UltraSAM [56], a Segment Anything–based visual foundation model for ultrasound image segmentation. UltraSAM was trained on US-43d, the largest public ultrasound dataset, and addressed anatomical complexity and limited annotations. It supports point- and box-based prompts, serving as a flexible backbone for various tasks.

Segmentation (Fundus).
We use IterNet [54] for retinal vessel segmentation tasks. IterNet extends the U-Net architecture, extracts fine-grained vascular details by iteratively refining the output of previous segmentations. The model comprises multiple mini-U-Net blocks, allowing it to achieve depths of up to four times that of standard U-Net.

WSI Cell Segmentation (Pathology).
We employ CellViT [50] for automated cell nuclei instance segmentation in gigapixel whole-slide images (WSIs). The model utilizes a U-Net-shaped encoder-decoder architecture where a ViT serves as the image encoder. CellViT is trained and evaluated on the PanNuke dataset. It automatically preprocesses the WSI by extracting manageable image patches, and subsequently performs precise, large-scale cell segmentation and detection across the entire slide. Our framework deploys the pre-trained SAM-H backbone variant.

2D Cell Segmentation (Pathology).
We employ CellSAM [53] for automated cell instance segmentation in 2D images. Built upon SAM foundation, CellSAM integrates a specialized object detector, CellFinder, which utilizes an Anchor DETR framework to automatically generate bounding box prompts for SAM’s mask decoder. The model is trained on a diverse corpus of cellular imaging data-incorporating datasets.

Registration (All).
We use uniGradICON [55] as a radiology registration tool. The model fuses the performance of deep learning with the adaptability of traditional methods. It enables zero-shot registration on untrained tasks and offers strong generalization across diverse datasets, anatomical regions, imaging modalities, and acquisition protocols.

Image Reconstruction & Super Resolution (All).
We use HealthGPT [16] for image reconstruction and super resolution tasks. This model is designed to perform both comprehension and generation tasks in complex medical scenarios. HealthGPT built upon a multi-LoRA PEFT architecture, employs a task-gated decoupling strategy to mitigate data conflict and enhance multi-task learning under limited supervision.

## 4.4.4 Medical Report Generation

Report Generation (All).
We use LLaVA-Rad [14] for generating radiology reports. It offers fast inference and efficient fine-tuning, which aligns well with visual abnormalities and report content. Its modular design bridges multimodal performance gaps while maintaining low computational costs, facilitating practical clinical integration.

Report Generation (Retina OCT).
We use SpecialistVLMs [49] for OCT report generation. This model was trained on domain-specific OCT images along with expert annotations, allowing for accurate identification of retinal structures and pathologies.

## 4.5 Implementation Details

## 4.5.1 Model Training

The planner $\mathcal{M}$ is initialized from Qwen2.5-VL-7B [60] and fine-tuned on the instruction-tuning corpus $\mathcal{S}$ (Section 4.3) using the objective in Eq. 2. The vision encoder is kept frozen, while the vision-language projector and language decoder are updated during training.

We use AdamW [64] with a learning rate of $1\times 10^{-5}$ , weight decay of $0.01$ , and a maximum gradient norm of $1.0$ . The learning rate follows a cosine decay schedule with linear warmup over the first 10% of optimization steps. Training is performed for one epoch using bfloat16 precision and gradient checkpointing on four NVIDIA A100-SXM4-80GB GPUs. A per-device batch size of 1 and gradient accumulation over 64 steps yield an effective global batch size of 256.

## 4.5.2 Deployment

MMedAgent-Plus is deployed as a set of containerized services coordinated by a central gateway. The planner $\mathcal{M}$ and each specialist tool $t_{k}\in\mathcal{T}$ run as separate services, with specialist tools exposed through standardized invocation interfaces. For each user request, the gateway forwards the multimodal input and dialogue context to the planner, parses the generated structured tool calls, and dispatches them to the specialist services. Tool outputs are returned to the planner for subsequent tool selection or final response generation. Multi-tool workflows are executed through the gateway, which coordinates the invocation of successive specialists according to the tool sequence specified by the planner. This modular architecture allows specialist implementations to be updated or replaced independently, provided that their interfaces remain compatible.

## 4.5.3 Evaluation Protocol

We evaluate MMedAgent-Plus across three aspects: tool orchestration accuracy, final response quality, and end-to-end task performance. The evaluations assess the correctness of specialist selection and sequencing, the quality of generated responses, and task-specific performance relative to direct-answer baselines and standalone specialist models.

Tool Orchestration.

We assess tool orchestration using five complementary metrics at the individual tool-call and dialogue levels.

Tool Call Accuracy.
Tool call accuracy measures the proportion of correctly selected tools across individual invocations:

 $\text{Tool Call Accuracy}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathbb{1}\bigl[\hat{t}_{i}=t_{i}\bigr],$ 

(3)

where $t_{i}$ and $\hat{t}_{i}$ denote the reference and predicted tool identities for the $i$ -th invocation, respectively, and $N$ is the total number of reference tool calls across the evaluation cases.

Whole-Case Accuracy.
Whole-case accuracy measures the proportion of dialogues in which the predicted tool sequence exactly matches the reference sequence, including both tool identities and execution order:

 $\text{Whole-Case Accuracy}=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{j=1}^{M}\mathbb{1}\bigl[\hat{\mathbf{t}}_{j}=\mathbf{t}_{j}\bigr],$ 

(4)

where $M$ denotes the number of evaluation dialogues, $\mathbf{t}j=(t{j,1},\ldots,t_{j,n_{j}})$ is the ordered reference tool sequence for dialogue $j$ , and $\hat{\mathbf{t}}_{j}$ is the corresponding predicted sequence. A dialogue is counted as correct only if all required tools are selected in the correct order, with no missing, additional, or incorrectly ordered calls. This metric provides a stricter assessment of orchestration correctness than tool-call accuracy in multi-tool settings.

Precision, Recall, and F1.
We compute precision, recall, and F1 to characterize tool selection errors independently of invocation order. Each specialist tool $t_{k}\in\mathcal{T}$ is treated as a binary label indicating its presence in a dialogue. A true positive ( $TP_{k}$ ) occurs when $t_{k}$ is both required and predicted, a false positive ( $FP_{k}$ ) when it is predicted but not required, and a false negative ( $FN_{k}$ ) when it is required but omitted. These counts are aggregated across evaluation dialogues for each tool.

Tool-specific precision, recall, and F1 are defined as:

 $P_{k}=\frac{TP_{k}}{TP_{k}+FP_{k}},\qquad R_{k}=\frac{TP_{k}}{TP_{k}+FN_{k}},\qquad F1_{k}=\frac{2P_{k}R_{k}}{P_{k}+R_{k}}.$ 

(5)

Macro-averaged metrics assign equal weight to each specialist tool:

 $P_{\mathrm{macro}}=\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}P_{k},\qquad R_{\mathrm{macro}}=\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}R_{k},\qquad F1_{\mathrm{macro}}=\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}F1_{k}.$ 

(6)

Micro-averaged metrics aggregate true positives, false positives, and false negatives across all tools before computing the scores:

 $P_{\mathrm{micro}}=\frac{\sum_{k=1}^{K}TP_{k}}{\sum_{k=1}^{K}(TP_{k}+FP_{k})},\qquad R_{\mathrm{micro}}=\frac{\sum_{k=1}^{K}TP_{k}}{\sum_{k=1}^{K}(TP_{k}+FN_{k})},$ 

(7)

 $F1_{\mathrm{micro}}=\frac{2P_{\mathrm{micro}}R_{\mathrm{micro}}}{P_{\mathrm{micro}}+R_{\mathrm{micro}}}.$ 

(8)

Precision reflects the frequency of unnecessary tool selections, whereas recall reflects the frequency of omitted tools. Macro averaging weights specialists equally, while micro averaging summarizes performance across tool-presence decisions. Unlike whole-case accuracy, these metrics do not account for invocation order or repeated calls to the same tool within a dialogue.

Per-Task and Per-Modality Accuracy.
To assess variation in orchestration performance, we report tool-call accuracy stratified by medical task and input modality. Task categories include segmentation, report generation, visual question answering, named entity recognition, etc. Modality groups include CT, MRI, X-ray, ultrasound, retinal fundus, OCT, pathology, and text. These analyses identify tasks and modalities associated with higher rates of tool-selection errors.

Error Pattern Analysis.
We further characterize orchestration errors by examining row-normalized recall and residual incorrect assignments at the tool and task levels (Figure 4). Row-normalized recall measures the proportion of reference calls correctly assigned to each tool or task, while residual assignments identify alternative predictions when the correct tool is not selected. These errors include substitutions between specialists with overlapping capabilities and omissions of required tool calls, represented by no tool. Modality-level residuals are also examined to identify errors associated with mismatches between reference and predicted input modalities (Section 2.1).

Together, these evaluations provide a complementary assessment of tool orchestration reliability. Tool-call accuracy and precision, recall, and F1 quantify selection performance at the invocation level, while whole-case accuracy assesses whether the complete tool sequence is correctly selected and ordered. Task- and modality-stratified analyses reveal where errors are concentrated, providing insight into the limitations of tool selection across heterogeneous medical tasks.

Task-Specific Tool Output Quality.

We assess end-to-end task performance using seven representative specialist tools spanning text-based and image-based medical tasks: PMC-LLaMA for question answering and rationale generation, RaTE-NER for named entity recognition, CONCH for pathology classification, BiomedCLIP for multimodal classification, IterNet for retinal fundus segmentation, MedSAM for general medical image segmentation, and LLaVA-Rad for radiology report generation. Each tool is evaluated using task-specific metrics appropriate to its output format, including textual responses, entity annotations, class predictions, segmentation masks, and radiology reports (Table 4). These metrics are used to compare the outputs of MMedAgent-Plus with those of the corresponding standalone specialists.

For radiology report generation, we combine lexical similarity metrics with clinical measures to capture complementary aspects of report quality [65]. ROUGE-L [66] and BLEU-4 [67] quantify textual overlap with reference reports. CheXpert F1 [68] measures agreement on extracted clinical observations, while RadGraph F1 [69] evaluates agreement on clinical entities and their relations. The latter metrics provide a more clinically focused assessment than lexical overlap.

Final Answer Quality.

Accurate tool selection does not guarantee that specialist outputs are faithfully incorporated into the final response. We therefore assess response quality using GPT-5.4-mini [57] as an LLM judge. Given the dialogue history and returned specialist outputs, MMedAgent-Plus generates a natural-language response that is evaluated on three criteria, each scored on a five-point integer scale.

• 

Faithfulness assesses whether the response accurately represents the returned specialist outputs without introducing unsupported claims or contradictions. This criterion is evaluated against the tool outputs alone, without reference to the ground-truth answer.

• 

Completeness assesses whether the response includes the relevant information from the specialist outputs needed to address the user request. The reference answer is used to identify task-relevant information rather than as a target for lexical matching.

• 

Conciseness assesses whether the response avoids irrelevant or redundant content while retaining the information necessary to address the user request. The reference answer serves only as a guide to the expected scope of the response.

We report the mean score for each criterion across evaluation dialogues. The three criterion scores are also summed to obtain a combined score ranging from 3 to 15, which serves as a secondary summary metric. For task- and modality-level visualizations, the combined score is normalized by the maximum possible score of 15 and expressed as a percentage. To reduce interference between criteria, each criterion is evaluated in a separate judge call using a dedicated rubric and three scored few-shot examples.

Two evaluation rules accommodate different output formats and workflow structures. For tools producing non-text outputs, including MedSAM, IterNet, HealthGPT, and UniGradICON, the generated image, mask, or file is treated as the primary output. A brief acknowledgment of successful output generation is considered sufficient when the user request does not require a textual description. For multi-tool workflows, response quality is assessed primarily against the terminal tool output. Intermediate outputs are not required to be separately described unless explicitly requested by the user.

We additionally identify two types of response-generation errors. A wrong-context response occurs when $\mathcal{M}$ produces a tool-call-style output instead of synthesizing a final response after tool execution. Such responses are assigned the minimum score of 1 for each criterion without LLM-judge evaluation. A redundant tool call occurs when an additional tool invocation accompanies an otherwise substantive final response. These responses are scored normally and separately recorded for error analysis.

For each evaluation instance, the judge receives the tool name, original user request, terminal tool output, generated response, reference answer, and indicators for non-text outputs and multi-tool workflows. Intermediate reasoning traces and training annotations are excluded. Each criterion is assessed in a separate judge call using a dedicated rubric and three scored few-shot examples to minimize interference between criteria. The judge returns an integer score and a brief justification. Judge responses are cached using input hashes to ensure consistent reuse of previously obtained evaluations.

Baseline Comparison.

We compare MMedAgent-Plus with four direct-answer multimodal models: Qwen3-VL-8B [59], LLaVA-Med [13], GPT-5.4-mini [57], and MedGemma-1.5-4B-IT [58]. These baselines span general-purpose and medical-domain vision-language models. The comparison assesses the final response quality of specialist-assisted generation relative to direct generation from the original multimodal input. Answer generation and response evaluation are conducted separately.

Answer generation.
Each baseline receives the original user request and associated medical images, without access to specialist outputs, intermediate workflow results, or reference answers. The models are instructed to respond directly and concisely using the provided inputs. Images are supplied in the format required by each model. LLaVA-Med processes only the first image in multi-image cases, whereas Qwen3-VL-8B and MedGemma-1.5-4B-IT accept multiple images within a single request. Locally hosted models use deterministic decoding with a maximum of 1,024 new tokens, matching the generation limit used for MMedAgent-Plus. API-based models use the most deterministic decoding configuration supported by their interfaces.

Evaluation.
Baseline responses are evaluated using GPT-5.4-mini with the same three criteria of faithfulness, completeness, and conciseness described above. Detailed evaluation rubrics and score visualizations are provided in Supplementary Section S4. Because direct-answer baselines do not receive specialist outputs, faithfulness is assessed against the original multimodal input rather than returned tool results. Reference answers are excluded from faithfulness evaluation but are used to identify task-relevant information and expected response scope for completeness and conciseness, respectively. All responses are scored on the same five-point scales using criterion-specific rubrics and three scored few-shot examples. We report the mean score for each criterion and the combined score.

This comparison evaluates specialist-assisted response generation against direct-answer models operating without tool outputs. Although the evaluation uses the same requests and scoring criteria, differences in model architectures, training, and available input evidence mean that the observed performance gaps cannot be attributed solely to specialist tool access.

End-to-end Task Evaluation.

To assess end-to-end task execution without providing reference tool calls or oracle tool outputs, we construct an evaluation set from the held-out single-tool multi-round corpus described in Section 4.3. From a pool of 9,128 held-out dialogues, we select 50 cases for each of seven specialist tools, yielding 350 cases in total. The selected tools are PMC-LLaMA, RaTE-NER, LLaVA-Rad, CONCH, BiomedCLIP, IterNet, and MedSAM, covering question answering and rationale generation, named entity recognition, radiology report generation, pathology classification, multimodal classification, retinal vessel segmentation, and general medical image segmentation. Cases are sampled using a fixed random seed of 42, except for BiomedCLIP, for which a predefined subset of 50 cases is used.

We first assess specialist invocation across all 350 cases. Each dialogue is replayed turn by turn, and the tool selected by MMedAgent-Plus is compared with the reference tool. Overall and per-tool invocation accuracy are calculated using the tool-call accuracy metric defined in Section 4.5.3.

We then evaluate final response quality for the 250 cases involving PMC-LLaMA, RaTE-NER, CONCH, BiomedCLIP, and LLaVA-Rad. IterNet and MedSAM are excluded from direct-answer baseline comparisons because their primary outputs are segmentation masks, which the evaluated baseline VLMs do not generate. For each case, baselines receive the user request immediately preceding the reference tool invocation and its associated images. Baseline responses are generated according to Section 4.5.3 and assessed using the LLM-as-a-judge protocol described above.

Finally, we assess end-to-end task performance across all seven tools using task-specific metrics, including Dice and HD95 for segmentation, ROUGE and BLEU for generated text, and micro- and macro-averaged F1 for structured predictions (Table 4). For PMC-LLaMA, RaTE-NER, and LLaVA-Rad, the same metrics are also computed from direct-answer baseline responses on the corresponding held-out cases. These evaluations complement response-quality scores by measuring how well specialist task performance is preserved during end-to-end execution.

The evaluation code, including the tool-calling evaluation and metric scripts and the LLM-as-a-judge evaluation with its prompts and rubrics, is available at https://github.com/RoxanneWAAANG/MMedAgent-Bench, together with example instruction-generation scripts and template inventories. The fine-tuned model weights are available from the MMedAgent organization on Hugging Face at https://huggingface.co/MMedAgent. An interactive demo and the project website are available at https://mmedagent.org. The deployment code, comprising the gateway and the containerized tool services, together with the task-specific metric implementations, the remaining instruction-generation scripts for the tools introduced in this work, and the evaluation sample indices required to reproduce the reported experiments, will be added to these repositories upon publication. Instruction data for the tools inherited from MMedAgent [28] was produced by that work’s pipeline and is available from its original release.

## S1.3 Interface Compatibility Rules

Tool call format.

The planner $\mathcal{M}$ requests tool execution by generating a structured action block containing one or more tool calls, each specifying a tool name and named parameters. Multiple calls can be specified within a single action block, allowing the planner to define a complete tool sequence before execution in the multi-tool single-round setting $\mathcal{S}_{3}$ . A response without an action block is treated as the final response and terminates the workflow.

Tool name resolution.

Tool names are resolved case-insensitively against a central registry. Each tool is registered under its canonical name together with a set of aliases, so that variants such as Grounding_DINO and grounding-dino both resolve to the same implementation. This makes execution robust to surface variation in the generated tool name without requiring $\mathcal{M}$ to reproduce a single exact string.

Parameter binding.

Each tool defines canonical parameter names and accepted aliases, which are mapped to the corresponding tool arguments during execution. Text inputs accept
text, input_text, query, input, context, and prompt; image inputs accept image_path, image, input_image, img_path, and input; registration accepts fixed, target, or reference for the fixed image and moving, source, or input for the moving image. Parameters that are not supplied fall back to the tool defaults reported in Table S1, so a call needs to name only the parameters it overrides.

Image binding.

User-provided images are handled separately from the language channel. Tool calls reference images through placeholders, which are resolved to the corresponding images at execution time. When an image-based tool is invoked without an explicit image parameter, the current image is bound automatically. For image registration, the first image is assigned as the fixed image and the second as the moving image, consistent with the structural validation requirement that registration requests contain paired CT or MRI images (Section 4.3). This mechanism also allows tools to access images provided in earlier dialogue turns.

Output types and error handling.

Each tool invocation returns a structured record containing a success flag, the resolved tool name, and either an output payload or an error message. Output formats are tool-specific. Generative tools return text, classification tools return predicted labels, grounding tools return bounding boxes with confidence scores, matched phrases, and image dimensions, segmentation tools return run-length-encoded masks and mask counts, and registration tools return warped images and deformation fields. Execution failures, including unavailable services, timeouts, and missing required parameters, are returned as structured observations rather than unhandled exceptions. This allows $\mathcal{M}$ to attempt recovery through a revised tool call or report the failure in its final response.

Interface compatibility.

Tool chains are validated by checking whether the output type of an upstream tool is compatible with the input requirements of the subsequent tool. Three classes of compatibility are supported. The first is bounding boxes to masks, in which Grounding DINO provides bounding boxes used as prompts by MedSAM through the composite G-Seg tool. The second is image to image or text, in which an image generated by HealthGPT or UniGradICON is passed to a downstream image-analysis tool. The third is text to text, in which generated reports are provided to RaTE-NER for entity extraction or to other text-based tools as contextual input. These compatibility rules are applied during the construction of $\mathcal{S}_{2}$ and $\mathcal{S}_{3}$ .

Iteration budget.

Execution alternates between planner generation and tool execution for a maximum of $T_{\max}$ interaction rounds. Workflows that do not produce a final response within this limit are terminated and labeled as truncated. Truncated workflows are counted as failures in the tool orchestration evaluation (Section 4.5.3).

## S2.2 Request and Response Templates

Instruction dialogues for newly integrated tools are constructed using predefined natural-language templates rather than per-sample language model generation. For each task, banks of user-request phrasings and assistant-response templates are generated in a single offline pass using GPT-4 [61] and subsequently fixed. Single-tool samples are instantiated by pairing a randomly selected request phrasing with a case from the corresponding source benchmark. The target assistant turn combines a structured tool call with a response template populated using the reference output of that case. Multi-tool dialogues are assembled from these samples according to predefined workflow templates, with transitions drawn from a fixed bank of 96 connective phrases, 28 greetings, and 43 follow-up turns. The size of each task-specific template bank is reported in Table S4.

All medical content in the corpus is taken from the source benchmarks. Images, questions, labels, reports, entity spans, and segmentation masks are those released with the corresponding datasets, and all tool calls are emitted from the tool registry schema of Section S1.1. GPT-4 contributes only the natural-language scaffolding of requests and responses, and no clinical content is model-generated.

## S2.3 Data Provenance

The instruction-tuning corpus combines newly constructed data with instruction data inherited from MMedAgent [28]. Data for the newly integrated specialist tools are generated using the template-based procedure described in Section S2.2. For the inherited tools, comprising five specialist models and the composite grounding-segmentation endpoint, we retain the original instruction data generated using GPT-4 and source-dataset annotations following the procedure described in MMedAgent. In addition to the automated validation procedures described in Section 4.3, the authors manually inspect 50 to 100 generated samples per specialist tool during dataset development to assess the consistency of user requests, selected tools, and target responses with their intended tasks and input modalities.

## S2.4 Tool Chains

The two multi-tool settings differ in how tool sequences are planned and executed. In the multi-tool single-round setting $\mathcal{S}_{3}$ , the planner $\mathcal{M}$ selects and orders the complete tool sequence from a single request before any specialist output becomes available. In the multi-tool multi-round setting $\mathcal{S}_{2}$ , tools are selected across dialogue turns, allowing subsequent decisions to incorporate preceding dialogue context and available tool outputs. Candidate chains in both settings are constructed according to the modality and interface compatibility rules described in Section S1.3.

Multi-tool multi-round chains.

Dialogues in $\mathcal{S}_{2}$ contain two to four tool invocations distributed across multiple turns. The subset comprises two complementary interaction patterns, distinguished by whether subsequent requests depend on earlier tool outputs.

The first pattern consists of 33,749 dependent dialogues, each containing two tool invocations. An image-based specialist first analyzes the medical input, and a text-based tool subsequently processes the returned findings. The upstream tool is selected from 15 visual understanding and image-processing specialists, while the downstream tool is selected from RaTE-NER, PMC-LLaMA, or ChatCAD-R, yielding 45 distinct tool chains. This pattern trains $\mathcal{M}$ to incorporate intermediate specialist outputs into subsequent tool-selection decisions.

The second pattern comprises 38,230 dialogues in which each round introduces a new request with its own image or text, so the invocations are independent rather than sequentially dependent. These dialogues contain two, three, or four invocations (13,177, 12,813, and 12,240 samples respectively) and span a much larger combination space, covering 342, 5,195, and 11,460 distinct chains. Because the tools are sampled from the set of modality-compatible candidates rather than from a curated workflow list, this pattern exposes $\mathcal{M}$ to combinations that no predefined chain inventory would contain, and trains it to re-select a tool for each request instead of continuing to apply the tool used in the previous round.

## S2.5 Instruction schema

Each training or evaluation sample is stored as a JSONL record containing an ordered dialogue between the user and the planner $\mathcal{M}$ , together with any associated specialist outputs. During training, tool outputs are inserted into the dialogue as subsequent messages following the corresponding tool calls. During inference, these outputs are obtained by executing the selected specialist tools and returned to the planner as additional context.

Dialogues follow the ShareGPT(https://sharegpt.com/) and LLaVA [70] conversation format used by the vision-language training pipeline. Planner messages are labeled "from": "gpt", while both user messages and specialist outputs are labeled "from": "human". These labels represent serialization conventions rather than the actual source of each message. User messages contain clinical requests and optional image placeholders, while tool-return messages contain structured or textual outputs prefixed by <ToolName> output: .... Specialist tools do not have separate role labels in the stored dialogue.

A typical tool-use cycle begins with a user request (human), followed by a planner response (gpt) specifying one or more specialist tools. The corresponding tool outputs are then inserted as human-tagged messages prefixed with <ToolName> output: .... The planner subsequently generates a final response or continues tool selection when additional steps are required. This message structure supports both single-round tool planning and multi-round interactions.

The three dialogue settings described in Section 4.3 use this schema with different interaction structures. In $\mathcal{S}_{1}$ (single-tool multi-round), each dialogue contains one specialist invocation and may include additional conversational turns, with the meta field omitted. In $\mathcal{S}_{2}$ (multi-tool multi-round), multiple tool-use cycles are distributed across dialogue turns, allowing later tool selections to incorporate preceding context and returned specialist outputs. The meta.tools field records the tools in execution order. In $\mathcal{S}_{3}$ (multi-tool single-round), the planner specifies a complete sequence of two to three tools from a single user request before any specialist output becomes available. The resulting tool outputs are concatenated into a single human-tagged message, while meta records the tool sequence and input modality.

## S2.6 Dataset Split Protocol

Training and evaluation data are partitioned at the dialogue level, with approximately 10% of each dialogue setting reserved for held-out evaluation. The resulting held-out pools contain 9,128 single-tool multi-round dialogues, 10,000 multi-tool multi-round dialogues, and 2,430 multi-tool single-round dialogues. Splitting each setting separately maintains its representation in the evaluation data, while dialogue-level partitioning prevents complete dialogues from appearing in both training and held-out sets.

Evaluation cases are sampled from these held-out pools rather than using the entire pools (Section 4.5.3). Within each setting, cases are selected by uniform random sampling without additional task- or tool-level stratification. Samples exceeding the 8,192-token context limit after rendering are excluded to ensure compatibility with the training context window.

We additionally examine image and request overlap between the training and held-out partitions of the multi-tool multi-round subset. The partitions contain 71,979 and 7,985 dialogues, respectively, comprising 42,958 and 11,352 distinct images. No source image or image-request pair is shared between these partitions. In contrast, request phrasing overlaps by construction because user requests are sampled from fixed task-specific template inventories (Section S2.2). Among the 3,657 distinct requests in the held-out partition, 67.0% also appear verbatim in training. Thus, the verified separation in this subset evaluates orchestration on unseen source images, rather than testing generalization to entirely unseen linguistic formulations.

## S3 Training Details

This section details the training configuration of the planner $\mathcal{M}$ , summarized in Section 4.5. The complete set of hyperparameters is provided in Table S9.

Initialization and trainable parameters.

The planner $\mathcal{M}$ is initialized from the pretrained Qwen2.5-VL-7B-Instruct checkpoint [60]. The vision encoder remains frozen (Section 4.2), while the vision-language projector and language decoder, including the output projection layer, are fully fine-tuned. No parameter-efficient adaptation methods, such as low-rank adaptation, are applied.

Training data and objective.

Training uses the combined instruction-tuning corpus $\mathcal{S}=\mathcal{S}_{1}\cup\mathcal{S}_{2}\cup\mathcal{S}_{3}$ (Section 4.3), with each sample included once and no subset reweighting or subsampling. Samples are formatted using the Qwen2.5-VL chat template with a fixed system prompt. The causal language modeling loss (Eq. (2)) is computed only over assistant-generated tokens, including structured tool calls and natural-language responses. System messages, user turns, and returned tool outputs are excluded from the loss through token masking. Sequences are limited to 8,192 tokens. To improve training efficiency, samples are packed into flattened sequences, with variable-length attention preventing interactions between tokens from different samples.

Image preprocessing.

Input images are resized by the Qwen2.5-VL image processor. The total pixel count lies between 256 and 12,288 pixels, with aspect ratios preserved and image resolution constrained by the configured minimum and maximum pixel counts.

Optimization.

We optimize the trainable parameters using AdamW [64] with a peak learning rate of $1\times 10^{-5}$ , weight decay of $0.01$ , and gradient clipping at a maximum norm of $1.0$ . The learning rate follows a cosine decay schedule with linear warmup over the first 10% of optimization steps. Training is performed for one epoch on four GPUs with a per-device batch size of 1 and gradient accumulation over 64 steps, yielding an effective global batch size of 256 and approximately 816 optimization steps. No validation set is used for checkpoint selection, and the final checkpoint is used for all evaluations.

Compute.

Training is conducted on four NVIDIA A100-SXM4-80GB GPUs using DeepSpeed ZeRO Stage 3 with CPU offloading of model parameters and optimizer states. We enable bfloat16 mixed precision, FlashAttention-2, and gradient checkpointing to reduce memory requirements. Training requires approximately 75 hours of wall-clock time, corresponding to 300 GPU hours.

## S4 Evaluation Protocol and Baselines

This section provides implementation details for the evaluation protocol described in Section 4.5.3, including the LLM-as-a-judge configuration, scoring rubrics, task-specific metrics, and baseline settings.

## S4.1 LLM-as-a-judge

Final response quality is evaluated using GPT-5.4-mini [57] (gpt-5.4-mini-2026-03-17). Faithfulness, completeness, and conciseness are assessed in separate judge calls using criterion-specific prompts to avoid combining the three judgments within a single evaluation. Each call returns a structured JSON response containing a brief rationale and an integer score from 1 to 5. Judge calls use deterministic decoding settings, and responses are cached by input hash to ensure consistent reuse of previously obtained evaluations.

For MMedAgent-Plus, the judge receives the tool name, original user request, terminal specialist output, generated final response, and indicators for non-text outputs and multi-tool workflows. The reference answer is additionally provided for completeness and conciseness evaluation but excluded from faithfulness evaluation. The judge is instructed to assess how accurately and effectively the planner communicates the specialist output, rather than independently evaluating the clinical correctness of that output. Faithfulness assesses consistency with the specialist output, while completeness and conciseness use the reference answer only to identify task-relevant information and the expected response scope.

The five-point scoring rubrics are illustrated in Figure S9.

Figure S9: LLM-as-a-judge scoring rubric. Each criterion is scored independently. Colour encodes quality from 1 (red) to 5 (blue).

Each criterion-specific prompt includes three scored few-shot examples corresponding to scores of 5, 3, and 1. For faithfulness, the examples illustrate accurate restatement of a CONCH classification, a LLaVA-Rad response containing an exaggerated severity assessment and an unsupported finding, and a CONCH response incorrectly reporting a benign result as malignant. Completeness examples use a LLaVA-Rad report to illustrate full coverage, omission of cardiomegaly, and an incomplete response stating only that the lungs appear clear. Conciseness examples contrast a direct one-sentence CONCH classification with a response containing an unsolicited referral disclaimer and an excessively verbose response that obscures the classification result.

Special evaluation rules apply to non-text outputs and malformed final responses. For MedSAM, IterNet, HealthGPT, and UniGradICON, a brief acknowledgment that the requested output was produced is considered sufficient for the maximum score on all three criteria when no textual description is required. Responses that remain in tool-call format after specialist execution are classified as wrong-context responses and assigned the minimum score of 1 for each criterion without judge evaluation. The complete judge prompts and few-shot examples are provided with the evaluation code.

Direct-answer baselines are evaluated using the same judge model, scoring scales, and criterion-specific rubrics. Because these models do not receive specialist outputs, faithfulness is assessed against the original multimodal input rather than a returned tool result (Section 4.5.3).

## S4.2 Task-specific metrics

Task-specific evaluation metrics reported in Table 4 are computed as follows.

ROUGE-L is calculated as the F1 score of the longest common subsequence after lowercasing and whitespace tokenization, without stemming. BLEU-4 is computed at the corpus level using add-one smoothing and the standard brevity penalty. Both metrics compare generated rationales or reports against their corresponding references.

CheXpert F1 is computed using a rule-based approximation of the 14 CheXbert observation labels. An observation is marked as present when a corresponding condition keyword appears in the text without a preceding negation cue within a 40-character window. Negation cues include no, not, without, absence of, free of, denies, negative for. Micro-averaged F1 is then calculated over the resulting binary observation labels. RadGraph F1 is F1RadGraphv2 from rrg_eval with reward_level=partial [69]. For RaTE-NER, entity-level F1 is based on exact matching of entity strings and types. RadGraph F1 is additionally computed by comparing reconstructed entity strings with the corresponding source sentences.

Classification performance is measured using micro- and macro-averaged F1 from scikit-learn. Predicted and reference labels are normalized through case folding and removal of surrounding braces or quotation marks before comparison. For PMC-LLaMA, answer accuracy is computed by exact matching of extracted yes/no or multiple-choice answers after the same normalization.

Dice is $2|A\cap B|/(|A|+|B|)$ on binary masks thresholded at 127; two empty masks score 1. HD95 is the 95th percentile of the symmetric surface-to-surface Euclidean distance, obtained from the distance transform of each mask after a one-pixel erosion to isolate the boundary. If one mask is empty and the other is not, HD95 is set to the image diagonal. In the end-to-end setting, Dice averages over all 50 cases per segmentation tool and scores a missing mask as 0; HD95 averages only over cases that produce a mask, because 0 is a perfect match on that metric (Section 2.3).

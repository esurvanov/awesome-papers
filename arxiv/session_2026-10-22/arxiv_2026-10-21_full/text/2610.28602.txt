Abstract

TS2Vec is one of the strongest self-supervised methods for time series classification, introduced in 2022. It is designed to be trained anew on every dataset: self-supervised pre-training followed by linear probing. Since then, the field has shifted towards foundation models that reuse one encoder across datasets. Yet TS2Vec offers a compact convolutional architecture that operates directly on temporal samples and accepts variable-length inputs without resampling. We ask whether these properties can be retained when the encoder is pre-trained only once, and show that they can: TS2Vec can essentially be turned into a foundation model. Three changes suffice: pre-train on a synthetic corpus, feed the encoder a raw amplitude view and an instance-normalized shape view of the input through two small stems, and distill one specialist encoder per view into a single encoder. The result, TS2Vec-FM, is a 1.6M-parameter dilated convolutional network that, kept frozen, reaches 83.3% on UCR-128 and 75.3% on UEA-27 under linear probing, within 0.3 pp of MantisV2, the current state of the art, and above TS2Vec trained separately on each target dataset. What we find most interesting is how different TS2Vec-FM is from other foundation models in design: near-parity is reached without attention, without a patch tokenizer, and without intermediate-layer extraction, using an “old-school” convolutional encoder and a two-layer MLP stem.

## 1 Introduction

Time series foundation models (TSFMs) aim to provide a single frozen encoder whose
features are useful across downstream datasets, removing the need to train a
specialized model per task (Feofanov et al., 2026a; Lin et al., 2024; Goswami et al., 2024).
Most of TSFMs use transformer architecture pre-trained on a large corpus of time series, real and / or synthetic (Xie et al., 2026).
Prior to foundation models, the same problem was attacked from another angle: self-supervised
methods such as TS2Vec (Yue et al., 2022), T-Loss (Franceschi et al., 2019) and
TNC (Tonekaboni et al., 2021) train a small encoder separately on each
target dataset. TS2Vec in particular remains a standard and strong baseline, but its
design is viewed as somewhat outdated, and it has never been evaluated as a
universal encoder itself. This paper asks whether it can become one.

The answer is yes, with three changes (Figure 1): pretrain once on synthetic data;
give the encoder raw and instance-normalized input views through separate stems; and
distill (Hinton et al., 2015) two resulting specialists into a single encoder.
The specialists retain the original TS2Vec architecture and contrastive objective,
while the student uses the same residual-block design with modified input and output layers and is trained by distillation.

The result is notable for two reasons. First, the resulting 1.6M-parameter
encoder is competitive: frozen, it outperforms per-dataset TS2Vec and the
evaluated forecasting-based extractors, and comes within a few tenths of a
percentage point of MantisV2 while using about 25% fewer parameters.
Second, it reaches this performance with a markedly different design —
convolutions instead of attention, a two-layer MLP stem instead of a
hand-designed multi-view tokenizer, and the final representation rather than a
selected intermediate layer. This near-parity suggests that the information
exposed to the encoder may matter more than the specific tokenizer design;
our ablations point in particular to the complementarity of amplitude and shape.

Figure 1: TS2Vec-FM architecture and training. (a) Raw and instance-normalized inputs pass through an MLP and a linear stem, respectively. Their concatenation feeds a shared convolutional trunk and temporal max pooling. (b) Two specialists are pretrained on synthetic data with the TS2Vec objective and then frozen. On the same data, each teacher supervises one 320-dimensional half of the student through timestamp-level and pooled cosine losses. Downstream, only a classifier on frozen student features is fitted.

## 2 From per-dataset TS2Vec to a foundation model

Original TS2Vec. The reference encoder first maps each timestamp to
64 channels, then applies ten hidden residual blocks and one output block with
exponentially increasing dilation, producing a 320-dimensional representation
at every timestamp. It is trained on aligned regions of two overlapping
contextual crops with a hierarchical objective that contrasts both timestamps
within a series and instances within a batch. A global series embedding is the
temporal maximum of these representations. Appendix A specifies
the architecture, crops, and losses exactly.

Step 1: the pre-training regime. We pretrain once on univariate synthetic series generated by CauKer (Xie et al., 2026) algorithm, so all encoder weight updates use synthetic data.
The advantage of the TS2Vec architercture is that a convolutional encoder needs no fixed input size,
so series keep their native lengths. However, similar to most time series foundation models,
our encoder is univariate, so for multivariate datasets, we encode channels independently
with the embeddings concatenated.

Step 2: normalization. Original TS2Vec does not apply instance-wise normalization.
For classification, however, absolute signal values may contain discriminative information. We therefore pretrain separate TS2Vec encoders on raw and instance-normalized inputs.
The results (Table 2) have been quite unexpected. The encoder without normalization
performs significantly better on the multivariate (UEA) datasets, while the encoder with normalization greatly outperforms
its counterpart on the univariate (UCR) datasets: instance normalization gains 2.9 pp on UCR but costs 1.5 pp on UEA,
and the raw-input encoder is the mirror image.
We suggest that this phenomenon is explained by the nature of contrastive pre-training which looks for shortcuts to
discriminate different samples in the embedding space.
In the un-normalized case, the shortcut is the amplitude of the signal, so embeddings encode mostly this kind of information.
In the normalized case, the shortcut is rather the shape of the signal.
More arguments to this hypothesis gives the fact that in many multivariate datasets a deviation of one channel
compared to the others gives a discriminative signal, so encoding signal amplitude is important.

Step 3: architecture. Given the aforementioned results, we slightly modify the encoder to include both normalized and raw views:
the instance-normalized view is linearly mapped into 32 channels, while the raw view is embedded
through a two-layer MLP with a GELU nonlinearity.
Their concatenation forms the 64-channel input of the shared convolutional part. So, we retain the TS2Vec residual-block design and increase the output dimension from 320 to 640, yielding a 1.64M-parameter encoder.
We note that the asymmetry between the two views matters as the raw view needs the extra nonlinearity to align its encodings with the normalized one.

Step 4: distillation.
The performance discrepancy between the normalized and un-normalized encoders has motivated us to use them as teachers (further also called specialists) to distill their knowledge into our dual-input single encoder. We use a factorized objective, matching each half of the 640-dimensional student representation to its corresponding teacher by cosine loss,
per timestamp and after pooling.
What is interesting is that directly pre-training the dual-input encoder with the usual TS2Vec contrastive objective yields inferior results (Table 2).

## 3 Experimental Results

following Feofanov et al. (2026a).

The recipe produces a competitive TSFM (Table 1).
The proposed TS2Vec-FM shows the second-best result, trailing MantisV2 by 0.19 / 0.60 pp with RF and 0.28 / 0.22 pp with LogReg,
respectively, while using about 25% fewer parameters. At the same time, it outperforms the forecasting- and vision-based extractors
(10–170 $\times$ as many parameters), except TiConvNext on UCR LogReg. We would like to note that unlike MantisV2, our encoder uses native lengths and does not need interpolate each series to a fixed length. In Appendix D, we show that interpolating series worsen the performance of TS2Vec-FM.

It also beats TS2Vec trained per dataset. Under both the random forest and logistic regression probe, the margin between the foundation model and per-dataset encoder is more that 1.9 % on UCR. The gap widens even more on multivariate UEA-27, even though TS2Vec-FM was pre-trained on univariate data. Note that in the original TS2Vec paper, they use an RBF-SVM with hyperparameters tuned, which increases the probing performance, though still below TS2Vec-FM with logistic regression. We have also tried to reproduce the results published by Yue et al. (2022) in two different setups: first, our reimplementation of the original protocol that reproduces the published UCR-128 performance to within 0.8 pp with a per-dataset correlation of 0.984, and second, we have run the
authors’ unmodified code that reproduces fifteen of the sixteen evaluable headline
metrics of the original paper (across all classification, forecasting and anomaly
detection) within 5% relative (see Appendix C).

Ablation of the recipe (Table 2). We first ask whether more synthetic data helps. For the ingredients taken alone the answer is negative: from 100K to 1M and 2M series, the two specialists and the dual-input encoder pre-trained directly with the contrastive TS2Vec loss stay within seed noise or slightly degrade (the instance-normalized specialist loses 0.5 pp on UCR). The distilled encoder is the only one that benefits: on UCR it gains 0.54 pp from 100K to 1M (81.08 to 81.62%, three pre-training seeds each, seed standard deviation 0.12 pp) and a further 0.14 pp at 2M, while on UEA it stays flat (73.9, 73.5 and 73.6%). We detail the scaling experiments further in Appendix D.
The comparison with the directly pre-trained dual-input encoder also isolates the contribution of distillation: it is superior at every scale, and its margin widens with data, from 0.56 pp at 100K to 1.10 pp at 1M and 1.24 pp at 2M on UCR. We also note that we had to use gradient clipping for the contrastive baseline due to numerical instability. The pattern suggests that direct training underuses the complementary views, while
already-specialized teachers keep both subspaces alive. This is, in effect, a
learned substitute for what hand-designed TSFM tokenizers provide by construction when
they devote separate branches to the normalized signal and to raw patch
statistics (Feofanov et al., 2026a; Lin et al., 2024).

## 4 Discussion

In this paper, we introduced TS2Vec-FM, showing that synthetic pretraining and specialist distillation can make a compact
convolutional encoder competitive for cross-dataset classification while retaining
native-length processing.
The results motivate revisiting earlier self-supervised architectures, adapting them to the current setup and thereby enriching the family of available time series foundation models.

Limitations.
The 2M headline model is trained at a single pre-training seed, although the
100K ablations and the 1M student use three seeds.
TS2Vec-FM remains slightly below MantisV2 on all four metrics, so our claim is
competitive performance with a smaller encoder rather than superiority.
Evaluation is limited to UCR and UEA, and whether specialist distillation
generalizes to other backbones and tokenizer designs remains open.

## Appendix A TS2Vec: architecture and objective

This section gives more details on the TS2Vec model and training objective. Our implementation follows the authors’ released
code [Yue et al., 2022].

Figure 2: Reference TS2Vec encoder. Ten width-64 residual blocks with dilation
 $2^{i}$ are followed by a width-320 output block; one block is detailed in
Figure 3.

Figure 3: One TS2Vec residual block: two GELU–dilated-convolution pairs
plus a skip connection (identity, or a $1\times 1$ convolution when the
width changes).

## Encoder.

Let $\mathbf{X}_{i}=(\mathbf{x}_{i,1},\ldots,\mathbf{x}_{i,T})\in\mathbb{R}^{T\times C}$ denote series $i$ . A learned affine map sends each
timestamp from $C$ input channels to a hidden width $H=64$ , with
 $\mathbf{W}_{\mathrm{in}}\in\mathbb{R}^{H\times C}$ and
 $\mathbf{b}_{\mathrm{in}}\in\mathbb{R}^{H}$ . During pre-training,
an independent mask $m_{i,t}\sim\operatorname{Bernoulli}(0.5)$ is sampled for
each contextual view, and a masked timestamp is replaced by the zero vector
after the affine map:

 $\mathbf{h}^{(0)}_{i,t}=m_{i,t}\bigl(\mathbf{W}_{\mathrm{in}}\mathbf{x}_{i,t}+\mathbf{b}_{\mathrm{in}}\bigr).$ 

(1)

Missing timestamps are masked in the same way. The trunk contains eleven
residual blocks indexed by $k=0,\ldots,10$ , each with two same-padded
one-dimensional convolutions of kernel size three and dilation
 $\delta_{k}=2^{k}$ . Writing $\mathcal{C}_{k,1}$ and $\mathcal{C}_{k,2}$ for these
convolutions, a block is

 $\displaystyle\mathbf{u}^{(k)}$ 
 $\displaystyle=\mathcal{C}_{k,1}\!\left(\operatorname{GELU}(\mathbf{h}^{(k)})\right),$ 

(2)

 $\displaystyle\mathbf{h}^{(k+1)}$ 
 $\displaystyle=\mathcal{C}_{k,2}\!\left(\operatorname{GELU}(\mathbf{u}^{(k)})\right)+\mathcal{P}_{k}(\mathbf{h}^{(k)}).$ 

(3)

The first ten blocks preserve width 64; the final block maps 64 to the
representation dimension $D=320$ . The residual projection $\mathcal{P}_{k}$ is
the identity when dimensions agree and a pointwise convolution otherwise.
Dropout with probability 0.1 follows the trunk. The output of the last block is
the timestamp representation
 $\mathbf{r}_{i,t}=\mathbf{h}^{(11)}_{i,t}\in\mathbb{R}^{D}$ ; we drop the series
index $i$ when it is not needed. For a univariate input this encoder contains
637,248 trainable parameters.

## Input length and receptive field.

The encoder is fully convolutional and the readout is a max-pool over all
timestamps, so the model accepts any length $T$ with cost linear in $T$ , and the
series embedding always depends on every timestamp. What is bounded is the
context of each timestamp representation: with symmetric padding, $\mathbf{r}_{t}$ 
sees at most $1+4\sum_{k=0}^{10}2^{k}=8189$ timestamps ( $\pm 4094$ around $t$ ).
For $T\leq 8189$ every $\mathbf{r}_{t}$ observes the whole series; this holds for
all 128 UCR datasets (longest: Rock, $T=2844$ ) and for 26 of the 27 UEA datasets
(MotorImagery, $T=3000$ ). Beyond that bound no single $\mathbf{r}_{t}$ sees the
whole series and the only cross-window integration is the order-free max-pool,
so a class that is defined by relating two events more than $\sim$ 8k steps apart
cannot be represented, whereas locally detectable patterns still are. The one
benchmark case beyond the bound, EigenWorms ( $T=17{,}984$ ), is nevertheless
handled well (88.0% RF and 90.8% LogReg versus 81.4% and 88.5% for MantisV2,
Appendix E). A second, more practical limitation is the
trained receptive field: pre-training used 512-timestamp series, which
the 8189-timestamp field covers many times over, so the long-range taps of the
last blocks (dilations 128 to 1024) were mostly fitted against zero padding
and only receive signal at both ends on long inputs. We do not quantify this
shift; downsampling very long inputs or pre-training on longer series are the
natural remedies.

## Contextual crops.

For each minibatch, TS2Vec samples an overlap length
 $L$ uniformly from $\{2,\ldots,T\}$ . It samples an overlap $[a,b)$ with $b-a=L$ , a left extension
boundary $a_{\mathrm{e}}\in\{0,\ldots,a\}$ , and an extended right boundary
 $b_{\mathrm{e}}\in\{b,\ldots,T\}$ . Each series receives a shift
 $o_{i}\in\{-a_{\mathrm{e}},\ldots,T-b_{\mathrm{e}}\}$ . The two encoder inputs are

 $\widetilde{\mathbf{X}}^{\,A}_{i}=\mathbf{X}_{i}[o_{i}+a_{\mathrm{e}}:o_{i}+b],\qquad\widetilde{\mathbf{X}}^{\,B}_{i}=\mathbf{X}_{i}[o_{i}+a:o_{i}+b_{\mathrm{e}}].$ 

(4)

Their contexts differ, but the last $L$ outputs of the first view and the first
 $L$ outputs of the second refer to the same timestamps. Stacking these aligned
timestamp representations $\mathbf{r}_{i,t}$ over the minibatch gives
 $\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B}\in\mathbb{R}^{B\times L\times D}$ , with minibatch size
 $B$ .

## Dual contrastive loss.

For an anchor $\mathbf{q}$ , positive $\mathbf{p}$ and negative set
 $\mathcal{N}$ , define

 $\ell(\mathbf{q},\mathbf{p};\mathcal{N})=-\log\frac{\exp(\mathbf{q}^{\top}\mathbf{p})}{\exp(\mathbf{q}^{\top}\mathbf{p})+\sum_{\mathbf{n}\in\mathcal{N}}\exp(\mathbf{q}^{\top}\mathbf{n})}.$ 

(5)

The implementation uses raw dot products: it applies neither $\ell_{2}$ 
normalization nor a temperature parameter. The negative collections below are
indexed lists rather than mathematical sets: equal-valued vectors remain
distinct denominator terms, and $\mathbin{\|}$ denotes list concatenation.
Instance contrast fixes a timestamp $t$ and treats the other series in both
views as negatives:

 $\displaystyle\mathcal{N}^{A}_{\mathrm{inst}}(i,t)$ 
 $\displaystyle=(\mathbf{A}_{j,t})_{j\neq i}\mathbin{\|}(\mathbf{B}_{j,t})_{j\neq i},$ 

(6)

 $\displaystyle\mathcal{N}^{B}_{\mathrm{inst}}(i,t)$ 
 $\displaystyle=(\mathbf{B}_{j,t})_{j\neq i}\mathbin{\|}(\mathbf{A}_{j,t})_{j\neq i},$ 

(7)

 $\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{inst}}(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B})$ 
 $\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2BL}\sum_{i=1}^{B}\sum_{t=1}^{L}\Bigl[\ell(\mathbf{A}_{i,t},\mathbf{B}_{i,t};\mathcal{N}^{A}_{\mathrm{inst}}(i,t))+\ell(\mathbf{B}_{i,t},\mathbf{A}_{i,t};\mathcal{N}^{B}_{\mathrm{inst}}(i,t))\Bigr].$ 

(8)

Temporal contrast instead fixes a series $i$ and treats all other aligned
timestamps in both views as negatives. Thus

 $\displaystyle\mathcal{N}^{A}_{\mathrm{temp}}(i,t)$ 
 $\displaystyle=(\mathbf{A}_{i,s})_{s\neq t}\mathbin{\|}(\mathbf{B}_{i,s})_{s\neq t},$ 

(9)

 $\displaystyle\mathcal{N}^{B}_{\mathrm{temp}}(i,t)$ 
 $\displaystyle=(\mathbf{B}_{i,s})_{s\neq t}\mathbin{\|}(\mathbf{A}_{i,s})_{s\neq t},$ 

(10)

 $\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{temp}}(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B})$ 
 $\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2BL}\sum_{i=1}^{B}\sum_{t=1}^{L}\Bigl[\ell(\mathbf{A}_{i,t},\mathbf{B}_{i,t};\mathcal{N}^{A}_{\mathrm{temp}}(i,t))+\ell(\mathbf{B}_{i,t},\mathbf{A}_{i,t};\mathcal{N}^{B}_{\mathrm{temp}}(i,t))\Bigr].$ 

(11)

Both objectives are symmetric across the two views. The positive is included
once in the denominator of Equation (5); only the anchor
self-similarity is excluded.

## Hierarchical contrasting.

Set $(\mathbf{A}^{(0)},\mathbf{B}^{(0)})=(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B})$ and obtain
scale $s+1$ by max-pooling each representation over adjacent pairs of
timestamps with stride two:
 $L_{s+1}=\lfloor L_{s}/2\rfloor$ . For odd $L_{s}$ , the unmatched final timestamp
is dropped, exactly as in the released implementation. This continues until
 $L_{S-1}=1$ , where
 $S=\lfloor\log_{2}L\rfloor+1$ . Define
 $\beta_{s}=\mathbf{1}[L_{s}>1]$ , which switches temporal contrast off at the
singleton scale. The optimized objective is

 $\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{hier}}=\frac{1}{S}\sum_{s=0}^{S-1}\Big[$ 
 $\displaystyle\alpha\,\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{inst}}(\mathbf{A}^{(s)},\mathbf{B}^{(s)})$ 

(12)

 $\displaystyle+\beta_{s}(1-\alpha)\,\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{temp}}(\mathbf{A}^{(s)},\mathbf{B}^{(s)})\Big],\qquad\alpha=\tfrac{1}{2}.$ 

Consequently, fine scales discriminate timestamps as well as series, while the
singleton top scale contributes instance contrast only. The original paper
writes the unweighted sum
 $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{inst}}+\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{temp}}$ at each non-singleton
scale; the released code uses $\alpha=\tfrac{1}{2}$ as above, which differs by the
global factor $\tfrac{1}{2}$ . Equation (12) states the
implementation used in our experiments.

## Optimization and readout.

We use AdamW with learning rate $10^{-3}$ , weight decay $10^{-2}$ and batch size
8 when reproducing per-dataset TS2Vec. The foundation-model specialists use
batch size 128 at the same learning rate; the throughput experiments use batch
size 512 and learning rate $4\times 10^{-3}$ . Checkpoints contain a running
arithmetic average initialized from the starting weights and updated after every
optimizer step. At test time masking and dropout are disabled, timestamp
representations are max-pooled over the complete series. Original per-dataset
TS2Vec maps all $C$ channels jointly through $\mathbf{W}_{\mathrm{in}}$ and
therefore always returns one 320-dimensional vector. Our foundation encoder is
pre-trained on univariate synthetic series; when evaluating it on multivariate
data, we instead encode the $C$ channels independently and concatenate them,
giving a $320C$ -dimensional frozen representation.

## Appendix B Related work

Self-supervised representation learning for time series.
A first line of work learns representations without labels by training an encoder on the
target dataset and then fitting a simple classifier on its frozen output. T-Loss uses a
triplet loss over subsequences [Franceschi et al., 2019], TNC contrasts temporal
neighborhoods [Tonekaboni et al., 2021], TS-TCC combines temporal and contextual
contrasting [Eldele et al., 2021], TST reconstructs masked timestamps with a
transformer [Zerveas et al., 2021], and TS2Vec optimizes a hierarchical contrastive
loss over two overlapping views of each series [Yue et al., 2022]. TS2Vec has remained a
standard baseline, and subsequent work refines its objective through soft contrastive
assignments [Lee et al., 2024], information-aware augmentation [Luo et al., 2023],
frequency-domain augmentation [Liu and Chen, 2024], and augmentation-free similarity
prediction [Foumani et al., 2024]. What these methods share is the training regime
rather than the objective: the encoder is fitted to the dataset on which it is evaluated.
Transfer between datasets has been examined within this line, but from a single real source
dataset and in comparison with other per-dataset
methods [Franceschi et al., 2019, Yue et al., 2022].

Foundation models for time series classification.
A second line pre-trains one encoder on a large corpus, keeps it frozen, and fits only a
probe on each downstream dataset. NuTime [Lin et al., 2024],
MOMENT [Goswami et al., 2024], Mantis [Feofanov et al., 2026a, Feofanov et al., 2026b] follow this protocol with transformer encoders and
tokenizers built for time series. Several of these tokenizers keep the normalized shape of
a window and its amplitude statistics in separate input
branches [Lin et al., 2024, Feofanov et al., 2026a], on the view that both are informative
for classification. MantisV2 additionally reports that test-time choices matter, in
particular which intermediate layer is read and how output tokens are
aggregated [Feofanov et al., 2026b], and UTICA keeps the same backbone while replacing
the contrastive objective with self-distillation from a slowly updated copy of the
student [Moakher et al., 2026]. A related body of work obtains competitive frozen
features from models never trained for classification, such as vision
transformers [Roschmann et al., 2025] and
forecasters [Auer et al., 2025a], and reports the same sensitivity to layer
selection and token aggregation.

Synthetic corpora and distillation.
Since large curated collections of real series are scarce, pre-training data for these
models is increasingly synthetic. CauKer generates series by composing Gaussian process
kernels inside structural causal models and reports regular scaling in both corpus size
and model capacity [Xie et al., 2026]. Other priors have been proposed for
classification [O’Rourke et al., 2026] and forecasting [Moroshan et al., 2025], and mixing several generators is reported to
be preferable to selecting one [Nagpal et al., 2026]. Distillation has been applied to time
series models mostly for compression, transferring a large pre-trained encoder into a
smaller one. Using it to merge several specialized models into a single one is a classical
idea from the distillation literature [Hinton et al., 2015], and has been studied
more extensively in vision, where teachers trained under different objectives may disagree
and have to be reconciled [Mandalika and V, 2025].

## Appendix C Per-dataset baseline reproduction

Our central comparison is against TS2Vec trained the way it was designed to be trained,
so the credibility of that baseline matters as much as the credibility of our own model.
We therefore validated it twice: once at the level of the per-dataset protocol we use for
the paired comparison (§C.1–C.3), and once by reproducing
the original paper end to end across all three of its tasks (§C.4).

## C.1 Protocol

For each of the 128 UCR datasets we train one TS2Vec encoder from scratch with the reference implementation and its defaults: ten
dilated residual blocks, hidden width 64, a 320-dimensional representation, learning rate
 $10^{-3}$ , batch size 8, maximum training length 3000, Bernoulli(0.5) timestamp masking,
and the upstream iteration rule of 200 iterations for training sets with at most
 $10^{5}$ values and 600 iterations otherwise. Frozen representations are then probed two ways: with the upstream grid-searched RBF-SVM, which
is what the original paper reports, and with the StandardScaler plus logistic regression
probe of Table 1, so that the comparison against our encoder is
probe-matched. Aggregated over all 128 datasets this gives 82.10% (RBF-SVM) and 81.36%
(logistic regression); the same frozen representations under the random-forest probe of
Table 1 (200 trees, three predictor seeds) give 79.83%.

The UEA-27 entries of Table 1 follow the same recipe with upstream’s
multivariate handling: one encoder per dataset whose input projection maps all $C$ 
channels jointly, series standardized per channel with training-split statistics, the
same optimizer, iteration rule and final-checkpoint readout, and the paper’s two probes on
the resulting 320-dimensional embeddings. This gives 69.50% (random forest) and 70.79%
(logistic regression); per dataset the numbers track the published TS2Vec UEA table
closely (e.g. 0.99 vs. 0.987 on ArticularyWordRecognition, 0.32 vs. 0.308 on
EthanolConcentration, 0.68 vs. 0.682 on PEMS-SF).

## C.4 Independent reproduction of the original paper

The protocol above validates only the classification numbers we compare against. To check
that the baseline is faithful more broadly, we separately reproduced the whole TS2Vec
paper — classification, forecasting and anomaly detection — by running the authors’
released code unmodified (commit b0088e14, no source edits) on its own
scripts and seeds. We couldn’t match exactly the environment and used torch 2.5.1 with Python 3.9, i.e., bit-exact reproduction is therefore impossible by construction.

We follow [Yue et al., 2022] exactly in metrics and evaluation protocol. For more details, please see their paper. Table 5 lists every headline metric. 15 of 16
metrics land within 5% relative of the published value and thirteen within 3%. The two
flagship accuracies, 0.830 on UCR-125 and 0.712 on UEA-29, come back as 0.8257 and 0.7055;
anomaly-detection precision on KPI matches to three decimals in both settings. Agreement
holds per dataset as well: 109 of 125 UCR datasets and 25 of 29 UEA datasets fall within
run-to-run noise, with mean deviations of $-0.39$ and $-0.68$ pp. Note that this table
uses the original paper’s benchmark subsets, UCR-125 and UEA-29, rather than the
UCR-128 and UEA-27 used elsewhere in this paper.

Three qualifications belong with these numbers. First, the reproduction is consistently
and slightly pessimistic on classification, so the residual is best attributed to the forced library and hardware
change rather than to seed selection; 70% of our per-dataset means still fall inside the
paper’s own batch-size sensitivity band. Second, the one genuine miss is the UEA average
rank (2.776 vs 2.40). That statistic is hypersensitive on a 29-dataset benchmark where
six methods cluster tightly: a 0.9% accuracy change produces a 15.7% rank change, and
three datasets (DuckDuckGeese, MotorImagery, EigenWorms) contribute 8 of the 11 shifted
rank units, in each case from an accuracy change below 0.02. TS2Vec still ranks first, but
its margin over T-Loss narrows from 0.72 to 0.26 rank units. Third, coverage is
incomplete as Yahoo Webscope has been discontinued and we have not performed the experiments on the associated data. All baseline numbers are taken as published;
no baseline was re-run, so every rank above compares our TS2Vec against the paper’s
reported baselines.

Taken together with §C.2, the two exercises corroborate each other. On
the paper’s 125-dataset subset, with the same upstream probe and the same seed, the
per-dataset protocol of §C.1 gives 82.25% and the unmodified-code
reproduction gives 82.61%, against a published 82.96%. We conclude that
the baseline our main claim is measured against is a faithful TS2Vec, and that the small
residual against the published aggregate is environmental rather than methodological.

## Appendix D Scaling and checkpoint selection

Figure 4: Colours are shared across panels; accuracy is UCR-128 with the random-forest
probe. (a) At 100K, the distilled student stabilizes after the first few epochs and
remains above the same trunk trained contrastively; bands span the three pre-training
seeds. (b) At 2M (one seed) the student peaks at epoch 7 and then declines,
while the gradient-clipped contrastive control stays within 0.56 pp of its epoch-1
accuracy across ten epochs.
(c) Probing each dilated residual block of the two 100K specialists: accuracy
increases with depth and the final block is best for both.

Checkpoint selection. Downstream accuracy is not monotone in pre-training epoch,
so we select the checkpoint on UCR random-forest accuracy at pre-training seed 42
(Figure 4a,b). At 100K the student is stable: it rises to 81.13/81.07/81.04%
at epoch 9 across the three seeds, and any epoch from 5 on is within 0.2 pp of that. At 2M
the same recipe peaks earlier and then decays — 81.70% at epoch 1, 81.76% at epoch 7,
81.35% at epoch 10 — while the pre-training loss keeps falling, so more optimization of
the contrastive teachers’ objective stops buying downstream accuracy well before
convergence. The teachers behave the same way and are selected independently: at 2M the
amplitude teacher improves over its three epochs (77.43 to 77.68%) while the shape teacher
degrades (79.89 to 79.49%), so they are taken at epochs 3 and 1. The specialists are
trained for three epochs only, and a 100-epoch run of both at 100K (batch 512, learning
rate $4\times 10^{-3}$ , seed 42) shows why: the shape specialist peaks at epoch 5 (80.65%)
and drifts down to 79.79% by epoch 100, the amplitude specialist peaks at epoch 10
(77.19%) and falls to 74.67%. Epoch 3 is within 0.4 pp of the best epoch of either
curve, and the frontier in Table 7 shows that 16 times more updates at
batch 8 do not help either, so longer specialist training buys nothing. The contrastive control
requires a separate stability qualification. Without clipping, nominally identical 1M
runs agree through roughly 200 updates but undergo gradient explosions at different
steps and differ by 4.15 pp at the same 1954-update checkpoint. With a global gradient
clip of 10, the 1M run and its exact-seed repeat agree within 0.15 pp over their shared
three epochs. The clipped 2M control is correspondingly stable, starting at 80.52% and
remaining between 79.97% and 80.52% over ten epochs.

Input length. A convolutional encoder accepts any length, so we evaluate at native
lengths; resampling every series to 512 steps, the preprocessing that fixed-context TSFMs
require, costs 0.20/0.22 pp on UCR and 0.90/0.56 pp on UEA with the random-forest and
logistic-regression probes (Table 6). Native lengths therefore matter more on
the multivariate archive, where series run from 25 steps (JapaneseVowels) to 17984
(EigenWorms), so one fixed context must both upsample and heavily compress, than on UCR.
The comparison matters because our baselines are evaluated at 512: the model stays
within 0.5 pp of MantisV2 on UCR under the baselines’ own input handling, so the result is
not an artifact of longer inputs.

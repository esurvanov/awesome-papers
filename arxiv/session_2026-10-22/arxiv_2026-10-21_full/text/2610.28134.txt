via Multi-domain Identifiable Representation Learning

Danru Xu

Aapo Hyvärinen

Sara Magliacane

University of Amsterdam

Abstract

Causal discovery with latent confounding is particularly challenging, but a common setting in real-world data. In this work, we focus on linear causal models with latent confounding, represented by acyclic directed mixed graphs (ADMGs).
Existing methods that fully identify ADMGs typically assume non-Gaussianity or graphical constraints, such as bow-freeness.
In contrast, we propose Linear ADMG Learning (LinADMG), a framework for learning ADMGs for linear causal models through identifiable representation learning. Rather than relying on non-Gaussianity or bow-freeness, LinADMG leverages multi-domain settings to identify the noise variables. We show that the identifiability of the noise variables is sufficient for recovering all direct causal relations, and that, under an additional latent-confounding no-cancellation condition, LinADMG can identify the whole ADMG. We implement LinADMG with four identifiable representation learning approaches from the literature and validate the methods on simulated and semisynthetic datasets.

## 1 Introduction

Figure 1: Overview of LiNGAM and LinADMG (our method) pipelines. LiNGAM applies ICA to the observed data $\mathbf{X}$ to estimate independent non-Gaussian noise variable $\widehat{\mathbf{Z}}$ , from which it infers the directed edge set $\widehat{E}_{\rightarrow}$ of a DAG. LinADMG replaces ICA with any appropriate IRL/CRL on a multi-domain dataset $\mathbf{X}^{\tau}$ with domain index $\tau$ to estimate dependent noise variable $\widehat{\mathbf{Z}}^{\tau}$ without specific parametric assumptions on the noise variables, and thereby enabling the additional recovery of bidirected edges $\widehat{E}^{\tau}_{\leftrightarrow}$ representing latent confounding.

Causal discovery aims to recover causal relationships from observational data (Spirtes et al., 1995b; Pearl and others, 2003; Peters et al., 2017). However, a common challenge in real-world data is the existence of latent confounding: an unobserved common cause of observed variables. Classical constraint-based methods allowing latent confounding, most notably FCI (Spirtes et al., 1995b), return only a Markov equivalence class rather than a unique causal structure.

To pursue a stronger identification goal for the causal structure with latent confounding, we first revisit the source-separation
perspective underlying LiNGAM (Shimizu et al., 2006). In a linear structural causal model without latent confounding, the observed variables can be regarded as linear mixtures of the noise variables. By assuming mutually independent and non-Gaussian noise variables, independent component analysis (ICA) (Hyvärinen and Oja, 2000) can be used to recover the unobserved noise variables and the corresponding mixing transformation up to permutation and component-wise scaling. The remaining ambiguities can be resolved using the acyclicity property of the causal graph, thereby fully recovering the underlying directed acyclic graph (DAG).

Latent confounding, however, fundamentally changes this picture. Suppose the underlying causal model is a DAG over both observed and latent variables. Marginalizing the latent variables generally destroys the DAG representation over the observed variables alone: latent common causes induce confounding relationships in addition to direct causal effects. Acyclic directed mixed graphs (ADMGs) provide a way to represent this structure, with directed edges encoding direct causal relations and bidirected edges encoding latent confounding (Richardson, 2003). At the same time, marginalization can induce statistical dependence among the noise terms associated with different observed variables, violating the independent-source assumption underlying standard ICA. As a result, learning an ADMG is substantially more challenging than learning a DAG.

Existing extensions of LiNGAM to latent-variable settings exploit non-Gaussian noise variables (Hoyer et al., 2008; Salehkaleybar et al., 2020; Cai et al., 2023; Schkoda et al., 2024), while Wang and Drton (2023) establish identification of the full ADMG under additional structural assumptions, including bow-freeness.

In this work, we also aim to fully identify the ADMG, but from a different perspective. As shown in Fig. 1, inspired by LiNGAM, we propose a framework, LinADMG, that follows the same principle: first identify the latent noises and then use the acyclicity property of ADMGs to recover the causal graph. However, unlike LiNGAM, latent confounding makes the noises dependent, so ICA is no longer suitable.

Recent developments in identifiable representation learning (IRL), including causal representation learning (CRL) (Schölkopf et al., 2021), provide promising alternatives to ICA for LinADMG. IRL studies conditions under which latent representations can be identified from observed data generated through an unknown mixing process. CRL can be viewed as a special form of IRL, where the recovered latent variables are additionally equipped with causal
semantics.
A key insight from IRL is that latent representations that are not identifiable from a single observational distribution may become identifiable when we can access multiple domains in which the representation distributions vary. Such variation may arise from mixture modeling (Kivva et al., 2022), sparse masking (Xu et al., 2024), hard interventions (Varıcı et al., 2024), or other changes in the latent distribution (Xu et al., 2026).

We adopt the same principle in LinADMG. Specifically, we consider settings in which the direct causal relations among the observed variables remain invariant, while the noise distribution varies across domains. We summarize our contributions as follows:

• 

We propose LinADMG, a general framework that views linear causal discovery with latent confounding, which first identifies a potentially dependent noise representation and then recovers the causal graph. This extends the source-separation perspective underlying LiNGAM beyond independent non-Gaussian noises to potentially dependent noises identifiable through distributional variability.

• 

We show that noise variable identifiability is sufficient to recover all direct causal relations. Under an additional latent-confounding no-cancellation condition, which captures a different aspect of the model than bow-freeness and faithfulness, LinADMG further identifies the complete bidirected structure and therefore the full ADMG.

• 

We implement LinADMG with four representative identifiable representation-learning settings: Diagonal Gaussian Mixture Models (Kivva et al., 2022), Unpaired Partial Observations (Xu et al., 2024), Multi-domain Scalings (Xu et al., 2026), and Unknown Hard Interventions (Varıcı et al., 2024). These are illustrative examples, and LinADMG applies to any method that identifies the latent variables.
We conduct experiments on synthetic and semisynthetic datasets and show accurate recovery under the linear model.

## 2 Preliminaries

We consider linear structural causal models (SCMs) with latent confounding and do not consider selection bias (Spirtes et al., 1995a). Let $\mathbf{X}=(X_{1},\ldots,X_{n})^{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}$ denote the observed variables and $\mathbf{U}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}$ the latent variables. Following Hoyer et al. (2008), we assume the underlying causal model is

 $\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{X}\\
\mathbf{U}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}B&B_{1}\\
\mathbf{0}&B_{2}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{X}\\
\mathbf{U}\end{pmatrix}+\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{E}_{1}\\
\mathbf{E}_{2}\end{pmatrix},$ 

(1)

where $B\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}$ encodes direct causal effects among the observed variables, $B_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}$ encodes effects from the
latent to the observed variables, and $B_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times m}$ encodes
causal relations among the latent variables. We assume that all components in $\mathbf{E}_{1}$ and $\mathbf{E}_{2}$ are mutually independent. The zero lower-left block excludes causal effects from observed variables to latent variables, so the latent variables are not descendants of the observed variables.

Solving the structural equations for $\mathbf{U}$ and substituting them into the equations for $\mathbf{X}$ gives

 $\mathbf{X}=B\mathbf{X}+\mathbf{Z},\qquad\mathbf{Z}:=B_{1}(I-B_{2})^{-1}\mathbf{E}_{2}+\mathbf{E}_{1}.$ 

(2)

Although $\mathbf{E}_{1}$ and $\mathbf{E}_{2}$ are mutually independent, marginalizing the latent variables can potentially induce dependence among the components of the composite noise $\mathbf{Z}:=\{Z_{1},...,Z_{n}\}$ .

We use an acyclic directed mixed graph (ADMG) (Richardson, 2003) to represent the causal structure among the observed variables induced by the SCM in Eq. 1. With a slight abuse of notation, we use $\mathbf{X}$ to denote the corresponding node set:

 $\mathcal{G}=(\mathbf{X},E_{\to},E_{\leftrightarrow}).$ 

(3)

A directed edge $X_{i}\to X_{j}\in E_{\to}$ is present when $B_{ji}\neq 0$ , representing a direct causal relation from $X_{i}$ to $X_{j}$ . We assume the directed subgraph $(\mathbf{X},E_{\to})$ is acyclic. A bidirected edge $X_{i}\leftrightarrow X_{j}\in E_{\leftrightarrow}$ is present when there exists a latent variable $U_{i}\in\mathbf{U}$ that is a common ancestor of $X_{i}$ and $X_{j}$ through directed paths whose intermediate nodes are all latent. Thus, $E_{\to}$ encodes the direct causal relation within $\mathbf{X}$ , while $E_{\leftrightarrow}$ captures the latent-confounding induced by $\mathbf{U}$ . An ADMG is bow-free if no pair of observed variables is connected by both a directed edge and a bidirected edge.

Our goal is to recover the ADMG
 $\mathcal{G}=(\mathbf{X},E_{\rightarrow},E_{\leftrightarrow})$ , including both the directed causal structure $E_{\rightarrow}$ and the latent-confounding structure
 $E_{\leftrightarrow}$ . Importantly, unlike latent variable LiNGAM (lvLiNGAM) (Hoyer et al., 2008) and its variants (Salehkaleybar et al., 2020; Cai et al., 2023; Schkoda et al., 2024), our work does not necessarily require the exogenous variables $\mathbf{E}_{1}$ and $\mathbf{E}_{2}$ to be non-Gaussian. Moreover, while the Markov property follows from our SCM assumptions,
we do not require faithfulness, which assumes that every conditional independence in the observed distribution corresponds to an $m$ -separation in the ADMG (Richardson, 2003; Spirtes et al., 2001).
Instead, we require that the noise variables $\mathbf{Z}$ in Eq. 2 can be identified by linear representation $W\mathbf{X}$ up to permutation and component-wise scaling (Comon, 1994), i.e., there exists a permutation matrix $P$ and an invertible diagonal matrix $D$ such
that $W\mathbf{X}=DP\mathbf{Z}$ , where $W\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}$ .

In general, however, even independent $\mathbf{Z}$ are not identifiable from the observed variables $\mathbf{X}$ 
without additional assumptions
(Comon, 1994; Hyvärinen and Pajunen, 1999; Locatello et al., 2019).
Moreover, if $\mathbf{Z}$ are potentially dependent, this is even more challenging. To solve this, a common strategy from IRL/CRL is to use distributional variability/heterogeneity in the latent variables across domains.
We adopt the same principle here. Specifically, we define $\mathbf{Z}^{\tau}$ as the noise variables in domain $\tau\in[T]$ and allow the noise variables to follow various distributions $P^{\tau}_{\mathbf{Z}}$ .
We assume the structural coefficient matrix $B$ is invariant. Then we have the domain-specific observation variable $\mathbf{X}^{\tau}$ :

 $\mathbf{X}^{\tau}=(I-B)^{-1}\mathbf{Z}^{\tau},\qquad\mathbf{Z}^{\tau}\sim P^{\tau}_{\mathbf{Z}},\qquad\tau\in[T].$ 

(4)

In practice, such domains can correspond to different environments, experimental conditions, populations, or interventions that change the distribution of noises.

Next, we present four representative identifiable settings adopted from prior IRL/CRL works as examples:

Multi-domain Scalings (MD-Scalings) (Xu et al., 2026).
Let $\mathbf{Z}$ follow any arbitrary distribution with finite second moment and define $\mathbf{Z}^{\tau}=D^{\tau}\mathbf{Z}$ ,
where $D^{\tau}=\operatorname{diag}(d^{\tau}_{1},...,d^{\tau}_{n})$ is domain-specific. Identifiability of $\mathbf{Z}^{\tau}$ up to permutation and scaling can be achieved with sufficient variability of the scaling matrix $D^{\tau}$ , formalized by the full-rank condition on the matrix whose rows contain all fourth-order monomials $\{d^{\tau}_{i_{1}}d^{\tau}_{i_{2}}d^{\tau}_{i_{3}}d^{\tau}_{i_{4}}\}_{i_{1}\leq i_{2}\leq i_{3}\leq i_{4}}$ .

Diagonal Gaussian Mixture Models (DiagGMMs)(Kivva et al., 2022).
Let $u^{\tau}$ denote the domain label. We generate
 $\mathbf{Z}^{\tau}\mid u^{\tau}\sim\mathcal{N}\!\left(\bm{\mu}(u^{\tau}),\,\bm{\Sigma}(u^{\tau})\right)$ ,
where $\bm{\Sigma}$ is a diagonal. The domain label is unobserved, so the samples from all domains are mixed, resulting in a Gaussian mixture distribution. Identifiability up to permutation and scaling holds when there exists a pair of domains $\tau\neq\tau^{\prime}$ such that the variance ratios
 $\operatorname{Var}(\mathbf{Z}_{i}^{\tau})/\operatorname{Var}(\mathbf{Z}_{i}^{\tau^{\prime}})$ 
are distinct across $i$ .

Unpaired Partial Observations (UP-Observations) (Xu et al., 2024).
Let $\mathbf{Z}$ follow an arbitrary distribution and define $\mathbf{Z}^{\tau}=M^{\tau}\mathbf{Z}$ , $M^{\tau}=\operatorname{diag}(m_{1}^{\tau},\ldots,m_{n}^{\tau})$ , where $m_{i}^{\tau}\in\{0,1\}$ refers whether the $i$ th component is observed. Thus, each sample uses only a subset of the latent variables, and the resulting representation $\mathbf{Z}^{\tau}$ is sparse. The masking pattern $\tau$ is unobserved and the samples are unpaired/mixed across patterns. Let $S^{\tau}:=\{i:m_{i}^{\tau}=1\}$ denote the active support under pattern $\tau$ . Identifying $\mathbf{Z}^{\tau}$ up to permutation and scaling holds under the sufficient support-variability condition: $\forall i\in[n]$ ,
 $\bigcup_{\tau\in[T]:i\notin S^{\tau}}S^{\tau}=[n]\setminus\{i\}$ .

Unknown Multi-node Hard Interventions (UMN-Hard Interventions) (Varıcı et al., 2024). Assume that the noise variables $\mathbf{Z}$ follow an additive-noise model. In each interventional domain $\tau$ , an unknown subset $I^{\tau}\subseteq[n]$ is targeted. A hard intervention on $i\in I^{\tau}$ removes the dependence of $Z_{i}$ on its parents and replaces its observational conditional distribution $p_{i}(z_{i}\mid z_{\mathrm{pa}(i)})$ with an interventional distribution $q_{i}^{\tau}(z_{i})$ that is independent of its parents. Define the intervention-signature matrix $D_{\mathrm{int}}\in\{0,1\}^{n\times T}$ by
 $[D_{\mathrm{int}}]_{i,\tau}=\mathbf{1}\{i\in I^{\tau}\}$ .
The intervention targets are unknown, but the collection of interventions must be sufficiently diverse: $D_{\mathrm{int}}$ must have full row rank. Under this condition, $\mathbf{Z}^{\tau}$ are identifiable up to permutation and scaling.

Importantly, our general framework requires no particular form of heterogeneity of $P^{\tau}_{\mathbf{Z}}$ . Any data-generating setting in which the noise variables are identifiable up to permutation and scaling, while keeping $B$ invariant across domains, is compatible with our framework.
The latent-confounding structure may itself vary across domains when the mechanism generating $P^{\tau}_{\mathbf{Z}}$ changes. For example, under UMN-Hard Interventions setting, interventions may change the causal structure among latent variables and then change the latent-confounding relations induced among the observed variables. Thus, we allow the bidirected edge set to be domain-specific and denote it by $E^{\tau}_{\leftrightarrow}$ . Finally, whether the domain index $\tau$ is observed depends on the identification setting, and our framework does not generally require access to domain labels.

## 3 Linear ADMG learning

We now describe a general framework called Linear ADMG Learning (LinADMG). It consists of three main steps. First, using observations $\{\mathbf{X}^{\tau}\}_{\tau=1}^{T}$ generated according to $\mathbf{X}^{\tau}=A\mathbf{Z}^{\tau}$ with the shared mixing matrix $A:=(I-B)^{-1}$ , we apply an IRL/CRL method to estimate $A$ and the corresponding domain-specific noise variables $\mathbf{Z}^{\tau}$ . Second, following the postprocessing procedure of LiNGAM (Shimizu et al., 2006), we use acyclicity to resolve the permutation and scaling ambiguities of the estimated mixing matrix and then recover the directed structural coefficients $B$ . Third, we estimate the bidirected edges by testing dependence among the estimated noise variables.

Step 1: Estimate the mixing matrix $A$ .
We apply a linear representation-learning method to the observed data across domains. When the domain labels are observed, the input is the collection $\{\mathbf{X}^{\tau}:\tau\in[T]\}$ ; when they are unobserved, samples from all domains
are pooled and treated as a mixture distribution. We assume that the representation learning method used is able to identify $\mathbf{Z}^{\tau}$ , which equivalently means identifying $A$ up to permutation and component-wise scaling, i.e.,
 $\widehat{A}=APD,$ 
for some permutation matrix $P$ and invertible diagonal matrix $D$ .

Steps 2: Recover the directed edge coefficients $B$ via LiNGAM postprocessing (Shimizu et al., 2006).
Since $B$ is a coefficient matrix of a DAG, we can recover it from $\widehat{A}$ using the LiNGAM
post-processing procedure (Shimizu et al., 2006).
Inverting $\widehat{A}$ gives $\widehat{A}^{-1}=D^{-1}P^{T}A^{-1}=D^{-1}P^{T}(I-B)$ .
Using the fact that $B$ is acyclic, $P$ can be identified since there is a unique row permutation of $\widehat{A}^{-1}$ such that every diagonal entry is nonzero (Shimizu et al., 2006). Once the permutation is chosen, the remaining scaling ambiguity $D$ can be removed because $I-B$ has a unit diagonal. $B$ is then estimated as

 $\widehat{B}=I-PD\widehat{A}^{-1}.$ 

(5)

Accordingly, the estimated directed edge set is

 $\widehat{E}_{\to}:=\{X^{\tau}_{j}\to X^{\tau}_{i}:\widehat{B}_{ij}\neq 0\},$ 

(6)

which is invariant across domain by assumption. Details of the post-processing procedure are in App. B.

Step 3: Recover the bidirected edge set $E^{\tau}_{\leftrightarrow}$ .
Given $\widehat{B}$ , we can estimate the noise variables. When the domain label $\tau$ is observed, this can be done separately for each domain:

 $\widehat{\mathbf{Z}}^{\tau}:=(I-\widehat{B})\mathbf{X}^{\tau}.$ 

(7)

For each pair of recovered noise variables, we add a bidirected edge if they are dependent in domain $\tau$ :

 $\widehat{\mathbf{Z}}^{\tau}_{i}\not\!\perp\!\!\!\perp\widehat{\mathbf{Z}}^{\tau}_{j}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad X^{\tau}_{i}\leftrightarrow X^{\tau}_{j}\in\widehat{E}^{\tau}_{\leftrightarrow}.$ 

(8)

Empirically, we can test for dependence between each pair of recovered exogenous variables with an appropriate independence test given a significance $\alpha$ .

We can also apply our method to settings where the domain index is unobserved, and we have a mixture of observations $\mathbf{X}$ . In this case, we apply the test over $\widehat{\mathbf{Z}}$ .

## 4 ADMG identification via LinADMG

We next provide conditions under which the proposed framework, LinADMG, identifies the underlying ADMG. By identification of an ADMG, we mean exact recovery of both directed and bidirected edge sets.

We proceed in two stages. First, in Lemma 4.1, we show that, without imposing any additional assumption, LinADMG can identify all directed edges but a subset of the bidirected edges, because in cases of fine-tuned parameters, the effect from latent confounding may be canceled and therefore cannot be detected by our method. The proof is in App. C.1.

Lemma 4.1 (Directed-edge identification and bidirected-edge subset recovery).

Consider the domain-specific ADMG $\mathcal{G}^{\tau}=(\mathbf{X}^{\tau},E_{\rightarrow},E^{\tau}_{\leftrightarrow})$ 
generated by the linear SCM in Sec. 2. Following the Steps in LinADMG, we have $\widehat{B}=B$ ,
and the directed edge set is identified and no false-positive estimated bidirected edges, i.e.,
 $\widehat{E}_{\rightarrow}=E_{\rightarrow}$ , $\widehat{E}^{\tau}_{\leftrightarrow}\subseteq E^{\tau}_{\leftrightarrow}$ .

To identify all the bidirected edges, we introduce a generic no-cancellation condition ensuring that latent confounding is detectable in the noise dependencies.

Assumption 4.2 (Latent-confounding no-cancellation).

For every domain $\tau$ and every pair of distinct noise variables $Z^{\tau}_{i},Z^{\tau}_{j}$ , $i\neq j$ ,

 $X_{i}^{\tau}\leftrightarrow X_{j}^{\tau}\in E^{\tau}_{\leftrightarrow}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad Z_{i}^{\tau}\not\!\perp\!\!\!\perp Z_{j}^{\tau}.$ 

(9)

Asm. 4.2 connects our latent confounding definition of bidirected edges to the error-dependence parameterization commonly used in linear structural equation models (SEM) with unobserved confounding. In correlated-error SEMs, Bhattacharya et al. (2021) define a bidirected edge
 $X_{i}\leftrightarrow X_{j}$ whenever the corresponding error covariance is nonzero, while Wang and Drton (2023) use bidirected edges to encode dependence between the corresponding error terms. In these formulations, the relation between a bidirected edge and dependence among the noise variables is therefore built directly into the model.

Different from their definition, we define $E^{\tau}_{\leftrightarrow}$ through the latent projection of the underlying causal DAG: $X_{i}^{\tau}\leftrightarrow X_{j}^{\tau}$ represents latent confounding between the two observed variables. After the latent variables are marginalized, however, the latent effects can cancel at the level of the noise dependence, as shown in Fig 2. (b), where the dependence between $Z_{1}^{\tau}$ and $Z_{2}^{\tau}$ vanishes. Asm. 4.2 rules out such cancellations, and thus aligns the definition of bidirected edges used in our formulation with the noise-dependence parameterization used in prior work.

Previous work commonly assumes bow-freeness with faithfulness because disentangling the causal effect from directed causal relations and a common latent confounder is challenging. We provide two examples in Fig. 2 showing that these assumptions, compared with Asm. 4.2, capture different aspects of the model and do not imply one another.

Figure 2: Examples showing that Asm. 4.2 and bow-freeness/faithfulness capture different properties.
(a) $X_{1}$ and $X_{2}$ share the latent parent $U_{1}$ while $X_{1}\to X_{2}$ , resulting a violation of bow-freeness. Nevertheless, the latent confounding is detectable by the dependency of noise variables, so Asm. 4.2 holds.
(b) Conversely, bow-freeness and faithfulness do not guarantee Asm. 4.2. Let $U_{1}$ and $U_{2}$ be independent Gaussian variables with equal variance. The resulting composite noise terms are $Z_{1}=U_{1}+U_{2}$ and $Z_{2}=U_{1}-U_{2}$ , so
 $\operatorname{Cov}(Z_{1},Z_{2})=0$ and hence $Z_{1}\perp\!\!\!\perp Z_{2}$ , despite the existence of $X_{1}$ and $X_{2}$ as they share latent confounders.

In Thm. 4.3, we show that under Asm. 4.2, LinADMG identifies the complete ADMG, with proof in App. C.2.

Theorem 4.3 (Identifiability of LinADMG).

Suppose that the conditions of Lemma 4.1 hold and that Asm. 4.2 is satisfied. Then, LinADMG identifies the complete domain-specific ADMG $\mathcal{G}^{\tau}=(\mathbf{X}^{\tau},E_{\rightarrow},E^{\tau}_{\leftrightarrow})$ 
for every domain $\tau$ , i.e., $\widehat{E}_{\rightarrow}=E_{\rightarrow}$ 
and $\widehat{E}^{\tau}_{\leftrightarrow}=E^{\tau}_{\leftrightarrow}$ .

## 5 Implementation

We implement LinADMG under four identifiable data-generating settings described in Sec. 2. Each setting has a corresponding IRL/CRL method for Step 1, taking $\{\mathbf{X}^{\tau}\}_{\tau=1}^{T}$ as input and outputting $\widehat{A}$ or its inverse. We provide a broader discussion of IRL/CRL methods compatible with our framework in App. D.

LinADMG-MuDoNLL with MD-Scalings.
Our implementation, following Xu et al. (2026), first computes the empirical covariance of $\mathbf{X}^{\tau}$ , denoted as $S^{\tau}$ , and define the model-implied covariance as

 $\widehat{\Sigma}^{\tau}=AD^{\tau}(I-L)^{-1}\operatorname{diag}(\sigma^{2})(I-L)^{-T}D^{\tau}A^{T},$ 

(10)

where $\sigma$ and a zero-diagonal latent structural matrix $L$ is used to model the covariance structure of latent $\mathbf{Z}$ .
While Xu et al. (2026) choose a covariance-matching objective, we instead optimize a Gaussian negative log-likelihood (NLL) since it is a scale-adaptive measure of covariance mismatch:

 $\displaystyle\sum_{\tau=1}^{T}[\log\det\!\left(\widehat{\Sigma}^{\tau}+\epsilon I\right)+\operatorname{tr}\!\left(\left(\widehat{\Sigma}^{\tau}+\epsilon I\right)^{-1}S^{\tau}\right)],$ 

(11)

where $\epsilon$ is a small positive value for numerical stability.

LinADMG-diagGMMs with DiagGMM.
Kivva et al. (2022) proved that when we apply a Gaussian mixture estimator, identifiability can hold with a (piecewise-) linear decoder-encoder structure. Inspired by this, we implement the special case, linear model, by maximizing the likelihood of a $T$ -component diagonal Gaussian mixture with weights $\pi^{\tau}$ , latent means $\mu^{\tau}$ , latent variances $v^{\tau}$ , and a shared linear decoder $W$ :

 $\displaystyle-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}$ 
 $\displaystyle\log\!\left[\sum_{\tau=1}^{T}\pi^{\tau}\,\mathcal{N}\!\left(x_{i};\,W\mu^{\tau},\,W\operatorname{diag}(v^{\tau})W^{T}+\epsilon I\right)\right].$ 

(12)

We initialize the parameters using $k$ -means on observed data and optimize them with Adam. The final estimator of $W$ is the inverse of $\widehat{A}$ .

LinADMG-SP with UP-Observations.
We follow the implementation of Xu et al. (2024), using a linear autoencoder with a sparsity constraint. The encoder $g$ and decoder $f$ are MLPs without nonlinear activations. After training, we extract the linear part $F$ of the affine decoder as $F_{:j}=f(\mathbf{e}_{j})-f(\mathbf{0})$ where $\mathbf{e}_{j}$ is the $j$ th standard basis vector. Since $F$ maps the recovered noises to observations, we return $\widehat{A}=F$ .

LinADMG-UMNI with UMN-Hard Interventions.

We follow the implementation for the case of hard-intervention from Varıcı et al. (2024), computing Gaussian score differences from sample covariances of each domain. The method recovers a linear encoder $\widehat{H}$ which maps observations to latent variables. We return $\widehat{A}=\widehat{H}^{-1}$ with Moore-Penrose pseudoinverse.

## 6 Related Work

Classical constraint-based causal discovery methods, most notably FCI (Spirtes et al., 1995b), handle latent confounding by using conditional-independence tests. While these methods do not have parametric assumptions, and can be used with non-parametric tests, they return a Markov equivalence class containing multiple causal graphs, rather than a unique underlying graph.

A different line of work relies on additional functional, distributional, or graphical assumptions to obtain stronger identification than is possible from conditional independences alone. Focusing on linear models, prior work can be roughly divided into two groups.

The first group develops differentiable or score-based methods for learning ADMGs through continuous constrained optimization. Bhattacharya et al. (2021) consider linear Gaussian structural equation models with correlated errors and derive differentiable algebraic constraints for several restricted ADMG classes: ancestral graphs, which exclude a bidirected edge between any pair connected by a directed path; arid graphs, which exclude c-trees; and bow-free graphs, which exclude pairs connected simultaneously by a directed and a bidirected edge. Their consistency guarantee recovers the true graph up to equivalence with respect to the equality constraints implied by the observed distribution. More recently, Morinishi and Shimizu (2025) extend this approach by assuming multivariate generalized normal errors, allowing non-Gaussian information to further resolve edge orientations, and provide almost-everywhere parameter identifiability for bow-free ADMGs. However, these approaches either recover the graph only up to an observational equivalence class or rely on bow-freeness and specific error-distribution assumptions.

A second group of work leverages non-Gaussianity or independent noise variables. This line of work is inspired by LiNGAM (Shimizu et al., 2006), which assumes no latent confounding, but connects causal discovery with source separation: under linearity and independent non-Gaussian noises, recovering the ICA mixing matrix identifies the underlying DAG. This idea has been extended to time-series models (Hyvärinen et al., 2010) and nonlinear causal models (Monti et al., 2020; Reizinger et al., 2023; Montagna, 2026). Recently, Heurtebise et al. (2026) replace non-Gaussianity with variance diversity in a multi-view setting, while still assuming independent noises.

A few recent works extend LiNGAM to handle latent confounding by leveraging overcomplete independent component analysis (OICA), beginning with Hoyer et al. (2008), which considers linear latent-variable models in which the latent variables and exogenous variables are mutually independent and non-Gaussian. Salehkaleybar et al. (2020) extend this to latent confounding by identifying causal orders among observed variables, while
Maeda and Shimizu (2020) additionally infer direct causal relations between unconfounded pairs and detect pairs sharing latent confounders. These results, however, do not in general result in exact recovery of the ADMG. Cumulant-based methods (Cai et al., 2023; Schkoda et al., 2024) provide asymptotically correct recovery of causal structure under different specialized assumptions on the latent-variable model, such as at least three observed children per latent confounder and a local bound on the number of latent confounders, respectively. In addition, Wang and Drton (2023) provided full recovery of the graph under the bow-free assumption. In contrast, our method chooses a different perspective, identifiable representation learning, and does not require non-Gaussianity or the specific
graphical and latent-structural restrictions required by these approaches.

## 7 Experimental Results

We assess the performance of the proposed LinADMG- methods using synthetic data and then present an application to a semisynthetic dataset causalAssembly.

Estimation of the directed (left) and bidirected (right) edges of the ground-truth ADMG under four DGPs listed above. Bars report means over 20 random seeds, with 95% confidence intervals. Warm colors are the proposed LinADMG- methods, while hatched cold colors are baselines. The top row reports F1 score ( $\uparrow$ ), and the bottom row reports SHD ( $\downarrow$ ). Synthetic Data generation. 
We consider four linear data-generating processes (DGPs) described in Sec. 2: MD-Scalings, UP-Observations, UMN-Hard Interventions, and Diag-GMMs. For the first three DGPs, we generate $m=\{5,10,20\}$ latent variables from a linear SCM, $\mathbf{U}=B_{2}\mathbf{U}+\mathbf{E}_{2}$ , where the latent causal graph is sampled from an Erdős-Rényi (ER) model with $k_{U}\in\{0,1,2,3\}$ , where each ER $-k_{U}$ graph contains $n\cdot k_{U}$ directed edges. The nonzero coefficients of $B_{2}$ are uniformly sampled from $[-0.5,-0.2]\cup[0.2,0.5]$ . We sample the scales of each component of $\mathbf{E}_{2}$ independently from $\mathrm{Unif}(0.2,0.5)$ and consider three noise distributions: Gaussian, Exponential, and Gumbel distributions. The three DGPs differ in how $\mathbf{U}$ generates $\mathbf{Z}^{\tau}$ with domain number $T=\{1,2,3\}\times m$ .

For UMN-Hard Interventions, we generate $T$ intervention domains and one additional observational reference domain. In each intervention domain $\tau$ , we select a target set $\mathcal{I}^{\tau}$ and replace the structural equation of each targeted latent variable by an independent noise term. We sample target sets such that every latent variable is targeted in at least one domain and the binary domain-by-variable target matrix has full column rank. Specifically, we construct $B_{2}^{\tau}$ by setting the rows of $B_{2}$ corresponding to $\mathcal{I}^{\tau}$ to zero and generate $\mathbf{Z}^{\tau}=(I-B_{2}^{\tau})^{-1}\mathbf{E}_{2}$ .

For UP-Observations, we generate a domain-specific binary mask: $M^{\tau}\in\{0,1\}^{m}$ with half active components. If a component is inactive in every masked domain, we activate it in a randomly selected domain. We additionally generate a reference domain with $M^{\tau}=I$ , giving $T+1$ domains in total. Then apply it to $\mathbf{U}$ to get $\mathbf{Z}^{\tau}=M^{\tau}\mathbf{U}$ .

For MD-Scalings, we generate the domain-specific scaling matrix as
 $D^{\tau}=\operatorname{diag}(d_{1}^{\tau},\ldots,d_{n}^{\tau})$ , where the diagonal entries are sampled independently from $\mathrm{Unif}(0.2,1)$ . We additionally generate an unscaled reference domain with $D^{\tau}=I$ , giving $T+1$ domains in total. Then apply it to $\mathbf{U}$ to get $\mathbf{Z}^{\tau}=D^{\tau}\mathbf{U}$ .

For Diag-GMMs, we generate five domains indexed by an observed auxiliary variable $a^{\tau}\in\{-2,-1,0,1,2\}$ . Conditional on $a^{\tau}$ , we directly sample the composite noise from $\mathbf{Z}^{\tau}\mid a^{\tau}\sim\mathcal{N}\bigl(\mu(a^{\tau}),\Sigma(a^{\tau})\bigr)$ , where $\mu(a)=\alpha+a\beta$ and $\Sigma(a)=(L_{0}+aL_{1})(L_{0}+aL_{1})^{T}+10^{-6}I$ . $\alpha$ and $\beta$ are sampled independently from $\mathcal{N}(0,0.5^{2})$ . The matrices $L_{0}$ and $L_{1}$ are diagonal, and sampled independently from $\mathrm{Unif}(0.2,0.5)$ and $\mathcal{N}(0,0.08^{2})$ , respectively. Thus, though $\mathbf{Z}^{\tau}$ are independent conditional on $a^{\tau}$ , pooling the domains can induce dependence through the shared auxiliary variable.

For all four DGPs, we consider $n=m$ observed variables $\mathbf{X}$ . We sample the observed causal graph $\mathcal{D}$ from an ER model with $k_{X}\in\{0,1,2,3\}$ , where each ER $-k_{X}$ graph contains $n\cdot k_{X}$ directed edges. Given $\mathcal{D}$ , we sample the linear edge weight $B$ uniformly from $[-0.5,-0.2]\cup[0.2,0.5]$ . For each domain $\tau$ , we generate the observed variable $\mathbf{X}^{\tau}=(I-B)^{-1}\mathbf{Z}^{\tau}$ with sample size $N\in\{1000,5000,50000\}$ . We run each setup over 20 random seeds.

Baselines and Evaluation Metrics. We
compare our methods with DirectLiNGAM (Shimizu et al., 2006), BANG (Wang and Drton, 2023), DCD (Bhattacharya et al., 2021), and RCD (Maeda and Shimizu, 2020). We pool observations generated via scaling and hard intervention DGPs since the baselines do not require the domain index. Due to computational constraints, we fit DCD using at most $10000$ pooled observations, while RCD uses at most $300$ observations. We evaluate directed and bidirected edges separately using F1 score and structural Hamming distance (SHD). Higher F1 and lower SHD mean better estimation.

## 8 Conclusion and limitation

We introduced LinADMG, a general framework for learning linear ADMGs through identifiable representation learning. We show that LinADMG can recover the directed causal structure whenever the noise variables is identifiable up to permutation and scaling; it can further identify the full ADMG under a latent-confounding no-cancellation condition. Our results show how noise heterogeneity can provide an alternative source of identifiability for causal discovery with latent confounding.

The current framework is limited to linear structural causal models. Extending the framework to nonlinear causal structural models is an important direction for future work. In addition, we currently consider only a selected set of identifiable representation-learning settings. Extending the framework to incorporate other sources of identifiability, such as temporal structure, could further broaden its applicability.

## Appendix B Details of LiNGAM-Based Directed Structure Recovery

In this section, we provide the details of the LiNGAM-style post-processing procedure used to recover the directed structure from the estimated mixing matrix $\hat{A}$ . We break it into 3 steps to resolve the permutation, scaling, and numerical error.

We start by introducing new notation. Let $W:=A^{-1}=I-B$ . Inverting $\widehat{A}$ gives

 $\widetilde{W}:=\widehat{A}^{-1}=D^{-1}P^{T}W.$ 

(13)

Hence, $\widetilde{W}$ recovers the structural demixing matrix $W$ up
to a row permutation and row-wise scaling.

## Step i: Resolve the permutation ambiguity.

By assuming $B$ is a DAG weighted adjacency matrix, we have $B_{ii}=0$ , which implies $W=I-B$ must have exactly one diagonal entries, i.e. $W_{ii}=0$ . We therefore search a row permutation of $\widetilde{W}$ that aligns the recovered components with the observed variables.

Specifically, we choose the permutation $\widehat{P}$ solving

 $\widehat{P}\in\mathop{\mathrm{argmax}}_{P\in\mathcal{P}_{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left|(P\widetilde{W})_{ii}\right|,$ 

(14)

where $\mathcal{P}_{n}$ denotes the set of $n\times n$ permutation matrices. This optimization is a linear assignment problem and can be solved efficiently using, for example, the Hungarian algorithm with complexity $O(n^{3})$ . We then define

 $\overline{W}:=\widehat{P}\widetilde{W}.$ 

(15)

## Step ii: Remove the scaling ambiguity.

After permutation alignment, the remaining ambiguity is row-wise scaling. Since $W=I-B$ satisfies $W_{ii}=1$ , we normalize each row by its diagonal entry:

 $\widehat{W}_{ij}:=\frac{\overline{W}_{ij}}{\overline{W}_{ii}},\qquad i,j\in[n].$ 

(16)

## Step iii: Recover the directed structural coefficients.

We obtain the estimated weighted adjacency matrix as

 $\widetilde{B}:=I-\widehat{W}.$ 

(17)

Sparsification. However, finite-sample estimation generally produces small spurious nonzero coefficients. We therefore sparsify $\widetilde{B}$ using either an absolute threshold,

 $\widetilde{B}_{ij}\leftarrow 0\quad\text{if}\quad|\widetilde{B}_{ij}|<\lambda,$ 

(18)

or a relative threshold defined with respect to the magnitude of the recovered coefficients.

Acyclicity. Thresholding alone need not yield an acyclic directed graph. Following Shimizu et al. [2006], if directed cycles remain, we iteratively remove the weakest edge participating in a directed cycle until it becomes acyclic. Denote the resulting matrix by $\widehat{B}$ . The estimated directed edge set is then

 $\widehat{E}_{\to}:=\{X_{j}\to X_{i}:\widehat{B}_{ij}\neq 0\}.$ 

(19)

## C.1 Proof of Lemma 4.1

Before proving Lemma 4.1, we recall the following result underlying the LiNGAM postprocessing procedure.

Lemma C.1 (LiNGAM permutation lemma Shimizu et al., 2006, Lemma 1).

Let $M$ be a lower-triangular matrix whose diagonal entries are all nonzero.
Among matrices obtained from $M$ by independently permuting its rows and columns, the diagonal contains no zero entries if and only if the row and column permutations coincide.

Consequently, if a matrix $W$ can be made lower triangular with nonzero diagonal by applying the same permutation to its rows and columns, then, for any row-permuted version of $W$ , there exists a unique row permutation that restores a matrix with an everywhere nonzero diagonal.

The second statement is the form of Lemma C.1 used in the LiNGAM postprocessing procedure. After the correct row permutation is identified, row-wise scaling is removed by normalizing each row by its diagonal entry.
See 4.1

Proof.

From $\widehat{A}=APD$ , and $A^{-1}=I-B$ , we obtain $\widehat{A}^{-1}=D^{-1}P^{T}(I-B)$ .

Thus, $\widehat{A}^{-1}$ differs from $I-B$ only by a row permutation and nonzero row-wise scaling.

Since $B$ represents a DAG, there exists a permutation of the variables under which $B$ is strictly lower triangular. Hence, under the same permutation, $I-B$ is lower triangular with all diagonal entries equal to one. By Lemma C.1, there is a unique row permutation of $\widehat{A}^{-1}$ whose diagonal entries are all nonzero. Therefore, the LiNGAM permutation step recovers the correct correspondence between the rows of $\widehat{A}^{-1}$ and the observed variables.

After correcting the row permutation, the resulting matrix differs from $I-B$ only by row-wise scaling. Since the diagonal of $I-B$ is equal to one, normalizing each row by its diagonal entry removes this scaling ambiguity exactly. Therefore, the normalized demixing matrix satisfies $\widehat{W}=I-B$ , and consequently

 $\widehat{B}=I-\widehat{W}=B.$ 

(20)

It follows immediately that

 $\widehat{E}_{\rightarrow}=E_{\rightarrow}.$ 

(21)

Next, we show that $\widehat{E}^{\tau}_{\leftrightarrow}\subseteq E^{\tau}_{\leftrightarrow}$ . We prove this by contradiction. Suppose

 $\exists\quad X_{i}^{\tau}\leftrightarrow X_{j}^{\tau}\in\widehat{E}^{\tau}_{\leftrightarrow},\text{ such that }X_{i}^{\tau}\leftrightarrow X_{j}^{\tau}\not\in E^{\tau}_{\leftrightarrow}.$ 

(22)

Since the bidirected-edge recovery procedure adds an edge only when the corresponding estimated disturbance components are dependent, we must have

 $\widehat{Z}_{i}^{\tau}\not\!\perp\!\!\!\perp\widehat{Z}_{j}^{\tau}.$ 

(23)

From the previous result, we know $\widehat{B}=B$ , and therefore

 $\widehat{\mathbf{Z}}^{\tau}=(I-\widehat{B})\mathbf{X}^{\tau}=(I-B)\mathbf{X}^{\tau}=\mathbf{Z}^{\tau}.$ 

(24)

Thus we have,

 $Z_{i}^{\tau}\not\!\perp\!\!\!\perp Z_{j}^{\tau}.$ 

(25)

To obtain a contradiction, recall from Eq. 2 that

 $\mathbf{Z}^{\tau}=B_{1}^{\tau}(I-B_{2}^{\tau})^{-1}\mathbf{E}_{2}^{\tau}+\mathbf{E}_{1}^{\tau}.$ 

(26)

Define $M^{\tau}:=B_{1}^{\tau}(I-B_{2}^{\tau})^{-1}$ . We write it out component by component:

 $Z_{i}^{\tau}=\sum_{k}M^{\tau}_{ik}\mathbf{E}_{2k}^{\tau}+\mathbf{E}_{1i}^{\tau},\qquad Z_{j}^{\tau}=\sum_{k}M^{\tau}_{jk}\mathbf{E}_{2k}^{\tau}+\mathbf{E}_{1j}^{\tau}.$ 

(27)

 $M^{\tau}_{ik}$ represents the total linear effect of the latent exogenous component $\mathbf{E}_{2k}^{\tau}$ on the observed disturbance $Z_{i}^{\tau}$ . Thus, a common latent source can induce dependence between $Z_{i}^{\tau}$ and $Z_{j}^{\tau}$ only if there exists some $k$ such that

 $M^{\tau}_{ik}\neq 0\qquad\text{and}\qquad M^{\tau}_{jk}\neq 0.$ 

(28)

By construction of the domain-specific ADMG, such a shared latent source induces the bidirected edge $X_{i}\leftrightarrow X_{j}\in E^{\tau}_{\leftrightarrow}$ , which is contradictory with $X_{i}\leftrightarrow X_{j}\notin E^{\tau}_{\leftrightarrow}$ 
in Equ. 22. Therefore, we conclude

 $\widehat{E}^{\tau}_{\leftrightarrow}\subseteq E^{\tau}_{\leftrightarrow}.$ 

(29)

∎

## Appendix D Identifiable and causal representation learning.

A broad literature studies conditions under which latent representations become identifiable from observed data. In blind source separation, source independence can sometimes be relaxed by assuming additional temporal structure [Hyvärinen and Hurri, 2004, Gómez-Herrero et al., 2008, Zhang and Hyvärinen, 2011]. Nonlinear ICA
further uses auxiliary variables, temporal structure, or nonstationarity to identify otherwise non-identifiable latent components [Hyvarinen and Morioka, 2017, Hyvarinen et al., 2019, Khemakhem et al., 2020, Kivva et al., 2022]. More recent causal representation learning (CRL) methods leverage additional information such as interventions [Brehmer et al., 2022, Lippe et al., 2022, von Kügelgen et al., 2023, Buchholz et al., 2023, Zhang et al., 2023, Ahuja et al., 2023], temporal structure [Lippe et al., 2023, Lachapelle et al., 2022, Lachapelle et al., 2026, Li et al., 2025], multi-view observations
[Yao et al., 2024, Ahuja et al., 2024, Morioka and Hyvarinen, 2024], hierarchical structure [Kong et al., 2023, Kong et al., 2024], or restrictions on the latent causal graph
[Zhang et al., 2024, Dong et al., 2024, Song et al., 2024]. Particularly relevant to our setting are linear representation-learning methods based on hard interventions [Varıcı et al., 2024], multi-domain scaling
[Xu et al., 2026], and sparse latent support [Xu et al., 2024].

These works primarily aim to identify latent representations and, in some settings, causal relations among the latent variables. LinADMG only uses representation identifiability as an intermediate step toward recovering causal structure among the observed variables. Among them, Multi-view cannot be converted to ADMG learning under the LinADMG framework. Different views generally have distinct mixing functions, and the recovered representations may be identifiable only up to view-specific permutation and component-wise scaling ambiguities. As a result, there need not exist a single shared mixing matrix corresponding to an invariant directed structure, as required by our framework.

## F.2 All configurations of synthetic data experiments

We evaluate the estimation of both the directed and bidirected edges of the underlying ADMG under four multi-domain DGPs. The UP-Observation DGP applies domain-specific binary masks to the latent variables, the MD-Scalings DGP applies domain-specific continuous scaling factors, the hard-intervention DGP replaces the structural equations of intervened latent variables, and the Diag-GMMs DGP generates five groups whose latent distributions vary with an unobserved auxiliary variable. For each DGP, its designated method: LinADMG-SP , -MuDoNll, -UMNI, and -diagGMM, respectively, is compared with all four baselines LiNGAM, BANG, DCD, and RCD.

The anchor configuration uses $n=10$ , $T=n$ domains, ER graphs with directed-edge density $k_{X}=2$ and confounding density $k_{U}=0.5$ , and Gaussian noise. Each domain contains $N=5000$ observations, except for the hard-intervention anchor, which uses $N=50000$ . We conduct one-factor-at-a-time studies over dimension $n\in\{5,10,20\}$ , number of domains $T/n\in\{1,2,3\}$ , sample size $N\in\{1000,5000,50000\}$ , directed-edge density $k_{X}\in\{1,2,3\}$ , confounding density $k_{U}\in\{0,0.5,1\}$ , and Gaussian, exponential, and Gumbel noise. The Diag-GMMs DGP always contains five Gaussian groups, so the domain, confounding-density, and noise sweeps do not apply to it. We report the mean and sample standard deviation of edge-wise F1, for which larger values are better, and structural Hamming distance (SHD), for which smaller values are better, over 20 independently generated random seeds.

## MD-Scalings

Tables 3 and 4 show that LinADMG-MuDoNLL benefits substantially from domain-specific scaling and is the strongest method in most configurations. Its performance improves systematically with the amount of multi-domain information: increasing the number of domains from $T=n$ to $T=3n$ raises directed F1 and bidirected F1, while increasing $N$ from $1000$ to $50000$ raises the respective scores as well. LiNGAM is strongest when there is no confounding. In contrast, BANG, DCD, and RCD recover little of the graph under Gaussian MD-Scalings, although BANG and RCD improve under non-Gaussian noise.

Tables 5 and 6 show LinADMG-diagGMM consistently provides the best directed estimation across all configurations. For bidirected edges, LinADMG-diagGMM and BANG are the two strongest methods. BANG is slightly better at $n=5$ and $n=10$ and approaches perfect recovery at $N=50000$ . Overall, LinADMG-diagGMM offers the best overall recovery of the complete ADMG, while BANG is especially effective for the complete bidirected structure
when sufficient data are available. Results of BANG and DCD at $n=20$ are missing due to computational cost.

## UP-Observations.

Tables 7 and 8 show that LiNGAM provides the strongest overall performance under the UP-Observations DGP for directed edge estimation. LinADMG-SP is less accurate for bidirected-edge estimation and shows relatively large SHD, indicating that it tends to introduce additional bidirected edges. Nevertheless, LinADMG-SP becomes competitive for directed recovery under non-Gaussian noise and achieves the best directed results under Gumbel noise, whereas BANG is best under exponential noise. Performance generally decreases as the dimension $n$ and confounding density $K_{U}$ increase, although the increase in bidirected F1 at higher confounding density partly reflects the larger number of true bidirected edges. Overall, LiNGAM in general performs the best across the configurations, since the masking layer introduces independence to the noises, while the main advantage of LinADMG-SP is limited to directed recovery under Gumbel noise.

## F.3 VaDE vs. diagGMM likelihood loss

In this section, we compare LinADMG-diagGMM and VaDE, as in Kivva et al. [2022], under the DiagGMMs data-generating process. Starting from the anchor configuration $n=10,N=5000,k_{X}=2$ , it varies the number of variables $n\in\{5,10,20\}$ , sample size $N\in\{1000,5000,50000\}$ , and graph density $k_{X}\in\{1,2,3\}$ . We evaluate each configuration over 20 random seeds and report performance separately for directed and bidirected edges using mean F1 and SHD, with standard deviations. LinADMG-diagGMM clearly outperforms VaDE in estimating directed edges across every configuration, achieving significantly higher F1 and lower SHD. The performance of both methods decreases as dimension increases, whereas increasing sample size has relatively little effect. Higher graph density improves directed F1 but generally increases SHD. For bidirected edges, both methods perform well, and their F1 scores are much closer: VaDE is slightly better in several low- or moderate-complexity settings, while LinADMG-diagGMM performs better for $n=20$ , $N=1000$ , $N=50000$ , and $k_{X}=3$ .

## F.5 Comparing with FCI

We compare LinADMG-MuDoNLL , LinADMG-diagGMM , LinADMG-UMNI , LinADMG-SP , and FCI on four data-generating processes: DiagGMMs, UMN-Hard Interventions, UP-Observations, and MD-Scalings. In every experiment, we set $n=10,N=5000,T=1,k_{X}=2.0,k_{U}=0.5$ and Gaussian noise. Results are averaged over 20 random seeds. Because FCI returns a partial ancestral graph (PAG), the ADMG estimated by each LinADMG method, as well as the ground-truth ADMG, is converted to its corresponding PAG, using a maximal ancestral projection where necessary. All methods are then evaluated in the common PAG representation using skeleton F1, arrowhead F1, and tail F1. Each bar reports the mean over the 20 seeds, while the error bar represents one standard deviation.

LinADMG-diagGMM provides the strongest overall performance, particularly for the DiagGMMs and UP-Observations DGPs, where it accurately recovers both the skeleton and the endpoint marks. LinADMG-MuDoNLL performs best in the MD-Scalings DGP. Under UMNI-Hard Interventions DGP, FCI is competitive in skeleton recovery, but the LinADMG methods generally recover arrowheads and tails more accurately. LinADMG-SP often recovers a reasonable skeleton but is less successful in finding endpoint orientation, while LinADMG-UMNI shows greater variability, especially for tail recovery.

Abstract

A growing body of work proposes dynamic expert-skipping methods for Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) models that adjust the number of experts activated per token during inference, aiming to preserve model quality while further reducing the number of active parameters. These methods have added calibrated importance scores, inter-expert similarity metrics, or learned per-token allocators in order to identify experts to skip. However, different calibration regimes, different reductions in the effective number of experts, and evaluation suites make comparisons difficult. We classify four representative prior methods into a unified taxonomy and benchmark them against a natural, simple baseline: statically reducing the number of experts $k$ (i.e., same reduced $k$ across all layers and tokens). We provide a fair comparison of these methods at matched expert-compute budget, on two modern MoEs (Gemma4-26B-A4B and Qwen3-30B-A3B) in a text-only setting and a contemporary vision-language model (Qwen3-VL) in a multimodal setting. Surprisingly, we find that the simple baseline is hard to outperform consistently across models, modalities, benchmarks, and calibration settings. The methods we benchmark often underperform the baseline and achieved gains, when present, are minor. We further find that the effective $k$ achieved (or optimized for) in calibration can diverge from the effective $k$ in testing, resulting in over- or under-estimating the compute at inference time. Based on these findings, we recommend a calibration-free per-modality static $k$ reduction and validate this on a held-out model and modality. We further argue that a static control at matched measured compute is the baseline any dynamic skipping method must beat.

## 1 Introduction

Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) models have become one of the leading paradigms for model architectures, activating only a small subset of all parameters (called “experts”) during each forward pass. Typical models (Yang et al., 2025a; DeepSeek-AI et al., 2025; Gemma Team et al., 2026) activate $k=8$ out of 128 to 512 available experts—fixed across all layers and tokens. Some methods for selecting a reduced number of experts already during (pre)training have been proposed (Raposo et al., 2024; Zhou et al., 2022; Gao et al., 2025; Jin et al., 2025; Guo et al., 2025), however these approaches are not yet adopted in major open-weight model families.
We therefore focus on the question: given a trained model, can we reduce the active number of experts while preserving quality by reducing this $k$ during inference, potentially dynamically per token and/or per layer?

Static, fixed- $k$ MoE inference has clear practical advantages: it is simple to implement, requires no additional calibration (i.e., selecting $k$ given an input token and layer), and, most importantly, matches the routing behavior learned during training. It further produces predictable, uniform compute and memory-access patterns across tokens. However, research has shown that not every token requires the same number of experts (Lu et al., 2024). As a result, different approaches have been proposed to optimize inference costs by varying $k$ . These approaches include static pruning, which permanently reduces the set of available experts to conserve memory, and (dynamic) skipping, which instead varies the number of active experts per token at inference time. Yang et al. (2025b) describe three benefits dynamic skipping can offer over fixed- $k$ inference: first, it reduces the total number of computations performed during each forward pass. Second, in small-batch scenarios, it reduces the number of unique experts active, lowering memory bandwidth requirements. Third, in a distributed deployment scenario, it can reduce communication overhead and improve load balancing.

Some recent methods have considered dynamic expert skipping as an add-on to static model pruning, rather than evaluating its benefits on its own, making it difficult to disentangle how much of the reported benefit comes from skipping itself versus the accompanying pruning. Beyond that, the proposed methods have become increasingly complex and costly. These range from calibrated scores (Lu et al., 2024) to similarity approaches (Bai et al., 2025b), and learned allocators (Yue et al., 2025). One of the most recent approaches by Huang et al. (2026) is MoDES, which uses calibrated modality-aware thresholds and reports near original performance while reducing the number of active experts by over $80\%$ . The combination of different calibration regimes, operating points (method configurations), and evaluations makes comparison difficult.
To close this gap, this study aims to provide a fair comparison across methods, benchmarks and inference-compute budget.

## 2 Background

A number of methods have been proposed to leverage the different expert needs of tokens. We distinguish between expert skipping, which reduces the set of experts activated per token, and expert rerouting, which selects different experts than those chosen by the router.

Expert Skipping

methods target existing static top- $k$ models. An early such strategy, “Not all Experts are Equal” (NAEE) (Lu et al., 2024),
skips a tail expert whenever its routing score falls short of the top expert’s
by a calibrated margin; Bai et al. (2025b) extend this criterion with an
inter-expert redundancy term. Further criteria include per-token sensitivity
(Zhong et al., 2024), confidence-triggered re-evaluation with the full
expert set (Zhao et al., 2025), and data-free per-layer expert budgets
(Chitty-Venkata et al., 2025). Huang et al. (2026) extend
threshold-based skipping to the multimodal domain with per-layer importance
scores and per-modality thresholds, while Yue et al. (2025) train a
per-token classifier via reinforcement learning to predict the required expert
count.

Expert Rerouting

methods alter which experts are selected rather than how many,
steering tokens toward cached or already-active experts to reduce
weight-loading overhead (Skliar et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2025; Wu et al., 2026; Oncescu et al., 2025). Their benefit hinges on shrinking
the unique set of active experts per forward pass. This reduces overhead due to memory bandwidth, but only works in small batch sizes. At sufficiently large batch sizes nearly all experts are required.

For this paper we focus on dynamic skipping, as rerouting offers limited benefit in common (large batch) inference scenarios. We explicitly study skipping in isolation from the pruning stages that some of these methods originally include. As skipping is orthogonal and composable with any pruning method, this lets us attribute quality differences to the skipping decision itself. Closest to our baseline (next section), Xia et al. (2025) reduce the expert
count for vision tokens to a fixed $k_{\text{vision}}<k$ from a chosen
onset layer onward, while text tokens always retain the full $k$ ; they
ablate ( $k_{\text{vision}}$ , onset layer) at moderate reductions
( $k_{\text{vision}}=4$ – $5$ of $8$ ) and deploy the mechanism jointly
with visual token pruning.

## 3 Methodology

## 3.1 Baselines

Our natural baseline for expert skipping methods builds on the model’s native routing: statically reducing the number of experts $k$ . We refer to this baseline as softmax top- $k$ , based on the selection criteria. As a reminder, for popular mixture of expert models like Qwen3, the router generates logits, which are normalized with softmax before the top- $k$ experts are selected. We expand on this approach for multimodal models by defining separate $k$ values for each modality: $k_{\text{text}}$ and $k_{\text{vision}}$ . This separate treatment of modalities is inspired by prior work (Huang et al., 2026; Xia et al., 2025). For models that support image and video tokens separately, we use $k_{\text{vision}}$ for both token types. Both baseline variants share the same benefit: they are calibration-free and static.

We evaluate four representative dynamic skipping methods:
NAEE (Lu et al., 2024), DiEP (Bai et al., 2025b), MoDES (Huang et al., 2026), and Ada-K (Yue et al., 2025), summarized in Table 1. We describe them based on a number of concrete properties of the methods: their criteria, decision rule, calibration phase and tunable parameters. We further discuss the calibration time necessary for a single operating point.
Both NAEE and DiEP also provide methods for expert pruning that they combine with the skipping approach. We only consider the dynamic skipping approach here.

Skipping Criteria

The first major differentiating factor between the methods is the skipping criteria. While our baselines and NAEE skip experts based entirely on the routing scores (also called gate weights), the other methods expand on this: DiEP uses an inter-expert Centered Kernel Alignment (CKA) (Kornblith et al., 2019) statistic that measures redundancy in individual experts, while MoDES adds a global layer importance factor. Ada-K is different here, as it uses the hidden state of the token rather than the routing scores as a criteria.

Decision Rule

Our baselines have the simplest rules, as they retain the top- $k$ experts and vary only the $k$ . NAEE on the other hand uses the cumulative routing scores and compares their ratio against a calibrated threshold. DiEP and MoDES adapt the concept from NAEE with a more complex threshold and score. All methods except for DiEP always keep the expert order intact. Further, all dynamic methods except for MoDES will always retain at least the first selected expert. Ada-K differs from the other methods in its decision rule: rather than deciding whether a given expert should be skipped, it instead predicts the number of experts that should be retained using what they call an Allocator.

Calibration

While our baselines are calibration free, NAEE, DiEP and MoDES all require calibration. NAEE calibrates the threshold value used for skipping, while DiEP calibrates this ( $\gamma_{1}$ ) as well as the CKA similarity metric between experts ( $\gamma_{2}(e_{0},e_{i})$ ). MoDES first calibrates the importance $\alpha^{(l)}$ of each layer and then identifies the ideal thresholds ( $\tau_{v,t}$ ) needed to achieve a target skipping ratio. Ada-K uses a unique approach, as it optimizes the linear layer of the Allocator using Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) (Schulman et al., 2017), a type of reinforcement learning.

Tunable Parameters

Tunable parameters can be adjusted to vary the targeted operating point. For NAEE this comes in the form of the $\beta$ quantile. When calibrating, the cumulative ratio between the routing scores is collected for each layer. By selecting an appropriate quantile we can sweep across operating points, skipping more or less experts. NAEE natively uses only the median. For DiEP we again sweep the quantile for the skipping parameter $\gamma_{1}$ , in their paper they primarily use the median as an operating point. For MoDES the tunable parameter is the target skip ratio, as part of their calibration procedure they find the ideal thresholds ( $\tau_{v,t}$ ) that achieve the target skip ratio. Lastly Ada-K has one main parameter $\lambda$ . This defines the cost of adding additional experts in the strategy. A higher cost $\lambda$ results in fewer experts being predicted to be needed.

Calibration Cost

We calculate the calibration cost using the original code release for MoDES and use our own implementation for the other methods on a single A100 80GB. While our static baselines are free, since they are calibration free, the advanced dynamic skipping methods have significantly different requirements. DiEP and NAEE are both relatively fast to compute, requiring approximately half an hour to calibrate for Qwen3-30B-A3B, depending on calibration data. Ada-K also varies significantly, but uses a much larger training dataset, resulting in around 2.5 hours of training time. MoDES requires the longest calibration at nearly 20 hours, as both the layer importance score measurement and the frontier search involve many forward passes through the full model.

We selected these methods as representative: they are current and use significantly different approaches to skipping. DiEP with its consideration of expert redundancy, Ada-K with its training approach, MoDES due to the multimodality and NAEE as the simplest calibration-based approach all reflect distinct criteria for selecting experts that can be skipped.

## 3.3 Quantifying Per-Token Expert Effort

All considered skipping strategies activate fewer experts for some tokens. To compare these savings on a common, hardware-agnostic axis, we define effective K ( $K_{\text{eff}}$ ) as a proxy metric. $K_{\text{eff}}$ is the mean number of routed experts per token, averaged across all tokens and layers: $K_{\text{eff}}\;=\;\frac{1}{T\cdot L}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\sum_{l=1}^{L}k_{t,l}$ ,
where $k_{t,l}$ is the number of experts activated for token $t$ in layer $l$ . For the static softmax top- $k$ baseline, $K_{\text{eff}}=k$ by construction, whereas for the dynamic strategies, $K_{\text{eff}}$ is a measured average that depends on the inputs and the strategy’s configuration. We distinguish between calibrated $K_{\text{eff}}^{\text{calibrated}}$ and measured $K_{\text{eff}}$ . $K_{\text{eff}}^{\text{calibrated}}$ is based on the number of active experts for the calibration set (Huang et al., 2026). Our analysis uses measured $K_{\text{eff}}$ , which counts expert activations at test time during the quality evaluation. The two may diverge because any threshold only enforces the target value on the calibration set, but may transfer imperfectly across datasets and depends heavily on the modality split, as demonstrated in Section 4.3.3.

The bottleneck for MoE inference depends on the deployment regime. At sufficiently large batch sizes, the expert feed-forward evaluation becomes compute-bound. In this case reducing the number of FLOPs provides the largest inference speed-up. The FLOPs of the expert matmuls scale linearly with the number of active experts per token: $K_{\text{eff}}$ is thus directly proportional to expert compute. Reducing active experts also lowers secondary costs such as all-to-all communication as described by Yang et al. (2025b). $K_{\text{eff}}$ counts only expert activations, ignoring other factors like routing, load imbalance, and communication overhead. This makes it a hardware-agnostic proxy for runtime cost, with true cost larger than what $K_{\text{eff}}$ implies. Figure 1 shows the monotonic but sublinear relationship between $K_{\text{eff}}$ and core inference metrics; we discuss this relation in Appendix A.5.

## 4 Results

## 4.1 Implementation

Several of these methods provide incomplete or no code releases, so we reimplement them on a best-effort basis. Ada-K has no release at all, and we reproduce its PPO-trained classifier ourselves (Appendix A.4), referring to it as the Allocator throughout. NAEE and DiEP release code only for Mixtral, a coarse-grained model, so we extend both to the $k=8$ regime. Where a method natively exposes only a single operating point, we sweep its free parameter: the quantiles from $0.1$ to $0.9$ for NAEE and DiEP, thresholds for MoDES in order to trace a full frontier. We reproduce reported operating points from each original paper to confirm our implementations are faithful (Appendix A.7).

## 4.2 Text-only Evaluation

We compare all strategies at matched $K_{\text{eff}}$ on Qwen3-30B-A3B
(Yang et al., 2025a) and Gemma4-26B-A4B (Gemma Team et al., 2026), two popular
mid-sized MoE models with $k=8$ active experts. While most methods report only a single
operating point (i.e., a single $K_{\text{eff}}$ ) at which they report performance, we sweep each method’s
tunable parameters to trace a full quality–compute frontier, providing clearer evidence
of each method’s benefit at any given compute level. We evaluate on the
zero-shot multiple-choice benchmarks HellaSwag (Zellers et al., 2019)
and ARC-C (Clark et al., 2018), as well as 5-shot GSM8K (Cobbe et al., 2021) and zero-shot HumanEval (Chen et al., 2021)
as generative benchmarks. A full overview of the benchmarks and corresponding
calibration sets is given in Table 4. We use
lm-eval (Gao et al., 2024) for
consistency with related literature. $K_{\text{eff}}$ is measured at test time during each evaluation (Section 3.3); for GSM8K and HumanEval this includes
the autoregressive decoding passes. Since most methods require calibration, we define a realistic calibration dataset, consisting of a mixed corpus of English, Chinese, code,
and math built from C4_en and C4_zh (Raffel et al., 2023),
open-web-math (Paster et al., 2023), and
python-codes-25k (flytech, 2023). This realistic regime
simulates the calibration–evaluation mismatch typical of actual deployments. We also evaluate the impact of calibration by evaluating a “best-case” scenario on the benchmarks’ own train sets.

## 5 Discussion

While the trained or calibrated methods such as NAEE and the Allocator (Ada-K) can provide quality benefits compared to the baseline in some cases, these are in our experiments limited to $2.6$  pp, often also in regimes where quality has already degraded substantially. Furthermore, the high dependence on calibration data suggests that any minor performance benefit from one of the calibrated strategies comes at the cost of performance in another area or domain. Beyond the standard calibration, identifying operating points and hyperparameters is a challenge, especially for RL-based Ada-K (Eimer et al., 2023).

The multimodal results support the same overall conclusions: MoDES outperforms both NAEE and the modality-unaware static baseline, showing that modality is valuable for deciding whether to skip experts for a given token. However, MoDES’s advantage over a calibration-free modality-aware baseline is confined to the sub-one $K_{\text{eff}}$ regime; for larger $K_{\text{eff}}$ the static baseline matches or outperforms it. We conclude that calibrated thresholds provide a benefit beyond treating vision and text tokens independently only in this sub-one $K_{\text{eff}}$ regime, but at the cost of complexity with only a modest runtime improvement as shown in Appendix A.5.

Beyond the one-time calibration effort, matching strategies at equal $K_{\text{eff}}$ also
understates the true runtime cost. Every dynamic method adds a routing
decision to each forward pass. Dynamic skipping also forfeits the uniform per-token
compute of static top- $k$ , and with it the load balancing and latency guarantees
quantified in Appendix A.5. Both effects are excluded from our $K_{\text{eff}}$ -matched analysis, which
therefore favors the adaptive strategies. Finally, the
calibrated methods offer no guarantee on the realized $K_{\text{eff}}$ : as Table 2
shows, measured $K_{\text{eff}}$ can deviate substantially from the
calibrated target.

Practical Recommendation

The added benefit of the dynamic skipping methods rarely outweighs the required calibration overhead and complexity. For a practical multimodal inference scenario, statically reducing $k_{\text{vision}}$ to 1–2 and $k_{\text{text}}$ to 6 for a model with eight active experts provides a strong trade-off between quality ( $<5\%$ drop) and inference speed-up. For text-only models, the approach is similar: reducing $k$ to 6 provides a measurable inference speed-up with only a minor quality loss. Going beyond this point becomes heavily model-dependent and should be validated with a generative benchmark, as models tend to perform differently at those points.

To verify that this recommendation is not an artifact of the benchmarks used for its selection, we apply the operating point unchanged to MVBench (Li et al., 2024b), a held-out video benchmark. With $k_{\text{vision}}=1$ 
and $k_{\text{text}}=6$ , the model achieves an accuracy of $69.95$ 
versus $71.40$ 
for the full model, a relative drop of $2.0\%$ at a measured $K_{\text{eff}}$ of 1.19. As video sequences are dominated by vision tokens, this represents an $85\%$ reduction in active experts. This minimal quality loss, on a workload and modality mix entirely outside the data used for calibration or selection, demonstrates the transferability of our recommendation. We also evaluate a larger model, GLM-4.6V (GLM-V Team et al., 2025), where the same operating point incurs an average drop of 4.2% (Appendix A.8).

Method design

Our results further highlight a significant gap in existing research: across compared methods as well as concurrent work such as ACE (Xu et al., 2026) comparisons lack a consistent static- $k$ control at matched measured compute and modality. When controlling for both, methods fail to consistently outperform the baseline. We therefore recommend that every future method compares against a static control at matched (measured) $K_{\text{eff}}$ that also uses any structural signal (e.g. modality) the method exploits.

## 6 Conclusion

Across three models as well as two held-out checks, none of the calibrated or learned skipping methods consistently outperform a static reduction of $k$ at matched measured $K_{\text{eff}}$ . The methods add calibration and tuning overhead and their realized $K_{\text{eff}}$ can deviate from the calibrated target ( $\geq 6\times$ in our experiments). This holds for text-only and multimodal models alike. We recommend static
per-modality $k$ as the default, and a static control at matched measured $K_{\text{eff}}$ as the baseline for future methods.

## A.3 Calibration Sensitivity

All considered methods except the static baselines require calibration data.
To establish the impact of the calibration data choice, we compare two regimes on
ARC-C and HellaSwag: best-case, calibrated on the benchmark’s own train
split, and realistic, calibrated on a mixed corpus of English, Chinese, code,
and math built from C4_en and C4_zh (Raffel et al., 2023),
open-web-math (Paster et al., 2023), and
python-codes-25k (flytech, 2023). The realistic regime
simulates the calibration–evaluation mismatch typical of actual deployments, while
the best-case regime represents the most favorable calibration setting.

Figure 7 highlights the impact of the calibration data. We select the middle point of the corresponding threshold sweep and observe the change in quality between best-case and realistic calibration. In some cases this best-case calibration does provide benefits compared to the realistic calibration. However, these improvements are marginal and do not materially affect the performance relative to the baseline. Further, we observe that the change in calibration set leads to substantial shifts in $K_{\text{eff}}$ at identical thresholds. NAEE, the best-performing dynamic skipping method in the practically relevant regime, is also the most robust to the choice of calibration data, showing only minimal shifts under best-case calibration. Preliminary experiments highlighted this as well: even a mismatched calibration set (MATH only) led to very similar performance.

Figure 7: Comparison of best-case and realistic calibration for Qwen3 and Gemma4 across ARC-C and HellaSwag. We select the middle threshold/quantile/cost parameter of the realistic calibration as our representative point. Error bars show the $95\%$ Confidence Interval (CI) from lm-eval standard errors. Any shift in $K_{\text{eff}}$ (as for example observed for MoDES) is due to the change in calibration data alone. Panel annotations provide this shift in realized $K_{\text{eff}}$ , realistic $\rightarrow$ best-case. Even in a best-case calibration scenario the dynamic skipping strategies do not consistently outperform the baseline.

## A.5 Runtime Performance

To validate $K_{\text{eff}}$ as a proxy for inference performance, we measure end-to-end inference with statically constrained expert counts using vLLM (Kwon et al., 2023). This constitutes an upper bound on the gains achievable by any strategy at a given $K_{\text{eff}}$ , as it excludes both routing-decision overhead and raggedness in per-token expert allocation; the static top- $k$ baseline attains this bound by construction. Standardized evaluation uses GuideLLM (Neural Magic, Inc., 2024) to benchmark Qwen3-30B-A3B on a single NVIDIA A100 80GB GPU, in two scenarios: synchronous (batch size 1, memory-bandwidth bound) and throughput (batch size 256, compute bound). We use GuideLLM’s ‘chat’ scenario to generate prompts. Figure 1 reports time-to-first-token (TTFT), inter-token latency (ITL), and output throughput. Reducing $K_{\text{eff}}$ from 8 to 1 lowers TTFT and ITL by 18% in the synchronous scenario and lowers TTFT by $\sim 30\%$ and ITL by $\sim 25\%$ in the throughput scenario, while output throughput rises from 111 to 135 tokens/s ( $1.22\times$ ) and from $\sim 1.9$ k to $\sim 2.65$ k tokens/s ( $1.39\times$ ), respectively. Notably, these gains are far below the $8\times$ reduction in expert compute: even under ideal static skipping, the end-to-end benefit is bounded well below proportionality, further compressing the value of the marginal quality differences between skipping strategies. The MoE blocks of the model only make up part of the overhead incurred; components such as attention, KV-Cache traffic, and non-expert weights are not addressed by any skipping technique and still incur computational costs.

## A.6 Detailed Benchmark Results

To highlight the differences in performance we provide the detailed per-benchmark sweep results in Figures 8 and 9. A number of differences can be observed in both the text and multimodal context.

Text-only

In the text-only results, the performance characteristics for our generative benchmark and the multiple-choice benchmarks differ. ARC-C and HellaSwag performance decays smoothly towards the random selection floor, with all methods performing reasonably close to one another. For GSM8K this changes dramatically: for both models, the drop towards zero score is apparent and linked to dropping the number of experts beyond a certain point. Especially for Gemma4, no strategy is able to retain reasonable performance once fewer than 3 experts remain active, and some strategies already fail at 4. This is likely a combination of factors: first, we use strict-match for evaluation. This means that the model will score 0 when it fails to match the correct output format. Second, MoDES in particular can reduce the number of experts to 0 for some tokens, potentially breaking causal generation or reasoning. HumanEval performs similar to GSM8K.

Multimodal

For multimodal evaluation the performance varies less across benchmarks with a notable exception. The MME perception score shows limited drop even when vision tokens receive zero experts, provided enough experts are allocated to the text tokens. This differs from the MME cognition score, where the drop is more apparent and MoDES markedly outperforms the baseline rather than simply matching it.

## A.7 Validating Implementations

Due to the incomplete nature of some of the code releases, we validate each method in the setting reported by its original authors.

NAEE

The NAEE code release only contains implementations for Mixtral 8x7B. To validate our implementation, we aim to reproduce their domain-specific calibration and evaluation regime. We calibrate Mixtral 8x7B (Jiang et al., 2024) on the MATH dataset (Hendrycks et al., 2021b) and evaluate performance on 5-shot GSM8K (Cobbe et al., 2021) using strict-match accuracy. We use NAEE’s default $q=0.5$ quantile (median) skip criterion, as described in their methodology. The reported accuracy from the NAEE paper is $54.28$ (Lu et al., 2024), which corresponds to a $7.4\%$ relative drop compared to the original $58.61$ . Our reimplementation achieved a baseline score of $58.52$ and an expert skipping score of $55.42$ (a 5.3% relative drop). This minor difference could be attributable to a number of factors, including slight differences between the cumulative-rule from the appendix and the two expert rule they describe in the paper. However, as the scores are very close, we are confident our implementation does not disadvantage NAEE in the comparison.

DiEP

Validation on DiEP was conducted on Mixtral 8x7B, evaluating the average accuracy across MMLU (Hendrycks et al., 2021a), OpenBookQA (Mihaylov et al., 2018), BoolQ (Clark et al., 2019), and RTE (Dagan et al., 2005). Calibration was done on the “mixed” set, as it has significant overlap with the original C4 (Raffel et al., 2023) calibration data used in the DiEP paper (Bai et al., 2025b). Since the complete skip configuration is not explicitly reported, we again use the $q=0.5$ quantile calibration that serves as their operating point elsewhere in the paper. Bai et al. (2025b) report an average accuracy of $65.1$ for the base model and $64.1$ after skipping, a $1.6\%$ relative drop; per-task scores are reported for the base model only. As Table 6 shows, our measured baseline matches theirs on every task when OpenBookQA is scored with unnormalized accuracy ( $65.6$ vs. $65.1$ on average); the apparent baseline gap under our default reporting ( $68.6$ ) stems entirely from using length-normalized accuracy on OpenBookQA ( $49.0$ vs. $37.2$ unnormalized). Our reimplementation, which includes the from-scratch CKA computation absent from the public release, yields a relative drop of $2.6\%$ in DiEP’s metric convention ( $65.6\rightarrow 64.0$ ; $2.1\%$ with length-normalized OpenBookQA). The difference to the reported $1.6\%$ is dominated by RTE ( $71.5\rightarrow 67.5$ ), the smallest of the four tasks ( $277$ test examples, standard error $\approx 2.7$  pp), and is therefore within evaluation noise; the remaining tasks degrade by at most $1.4$ points. We conclude that our implementation reproduces the reported behavior closely.

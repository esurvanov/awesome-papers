Abstract

Humanoid–human interaction requires robots to operate under severe partial observability: during close physical contact, the human partner is occluded, truncated, or outside the robot’s field of view, causing pose estimation to fail and often necessitating external tracking or privileged human state. At the same time, heavy mutual occlusions introduce errors into the human–human motion-capture data used for learning. We present REACT, a framework for learning deployable humanoid–human interaction policies from human–human demonstrations. REACT treats semantic contact structure as the key supervisory signal connecting demonstration processing, interaction learning, and onboard control. First, a VLM-guided contact modeling and retargeting pipeline identifies semantically meaningful contacts and transfers them onto humanoid–human geometry while preserving interaction structure. Using these annotations, privileged interaction policies learn physically grounded behaviors through interaction-aware contact rewards and are then distilled into deployable egocentric depth policies trained entirely from onboard sensing using photorealistic human depth rendering. To address severe partial observability, we introduce generative partner-state modeling objectives that predict partner motion from occluded egocentric views. We evaluate REACT in simulation and on a real Unitree G1 humanoid across five contact-rich interactions. Semantic contact modeling substantially improves preservation of demonstrated interactions, while generative partner-state modeling improves contact recall on four of five interactions, most under heavy occlusion. On real hardware, REACT performs all five interactions using onboard sensing alone.

## 1 Introduction

Humanoid hardware and learning-based control have advanced rapidly in recent years, bringing the deployment of humanoid robots in homes, workplaces, and care settings increasingly within reach. Realizing this vision, however, requires robots capable of sustained, reactive physical interaction with humans using only onboard sensing. Unlike existing humanoid controllers, which primarily focus on isolated motion tracking or interaction with static scenes and objects [14, 21, 3], humanoid–human interaction (HHI) requires continuous perception, prediction, and physical coordination with a human partner whose motion evolves under independent intent and frequent contact.

A central challenge in HHI is that close-contact interaction fundamentally operates under severe partial observability. During interaction, the human partner is frequently self-occluded, truncated, or partially outside the robot’s field of view, causing off-the-shelf human pose-estimation systems to fail precisely when accurate perception matters most. As a result, many existing approaches rely on external tracking systems, motion-capture instrumentation, or privileged access to human state during deployment, limiting robustness and real-world deployability. The problem begins even earlier in the learning pipeline: heavy mutual occlusions also corrupt the underlying human–human motion-capture data itself, introducing reconstruction artifacts and inconsistent contact geometry that propagate through downstream retargeting [2, 46] and policy-learning pipelines.

We argue that deployable humanoid–human interaction fundamentally requires recovering the interaction state from incomplete observations, and that semantic contact structure provides the key supervisory signal connecting noisy demonstrations, physics-based learning, and onboard control. While motion trajectories may vary substantially across embodiments, interactions are often defined by a much more stable structure: who touches whom, where, and when. We therefore propose to represent interactions through semantic contacts and train policies to infer latent partner state under severe partial observability.

To this end, we introduce REACT (Reactive Egocentric Agent for Contact-rich Humanoid-Human InTeraction), a framework for learning deployable humanoid–human interaction policies from large-scale human–human motion-capture data (Figure 1). REACT first runs a VLM-guided semantic contact pipeline that retargets the demonstrated interaction contacts onto humanoid–human geometry while preserving interaction structure across the embodiment gap. These semantic contacts then supervise a privileged interaction teacher through interaction-aware contact rewards, anchoring the policy in physics. The teacher is distilled into an egocentric depth student trained entirely on onboard sensing via photorealistic in-simulator SMPL-X rendering of dynamic human partners. To address severe partial observability, the student is further trained with a generative partner-state prediction objective that infers past, present, and future partner motion from egocentric observations.

We evaluate REACT on a large corpus of close-contact human–human interactions in simulation and on a real Unitree G1 humanoid. Our experiments show that semantic contact modeling substantially improves preservation of demonstrated interaction structure compared to existing retargeting approaches, and its contact annotations improve downstream interaction learning. We further show that generative partner-state prediction improves contact recall on four of five interactions, most where the partner is hardest to see, enabling depth-based policies to recover most of the contact recall of privileged interaction teachers ( $75\%$ at a $30\,$ cm tolerance). On real hardware, REACT successfully executes diverse contact-rich interactions, including high-fives, hugs, handshakes, link-arms, and pats on the back, using onboard sensing alone.

In summary, our contributions are
(1) A semantic contact modeling and retargeting framework that transfers human–human demonstrations to humanoid–human interactions while preserving interaction structure across embodiments,
(2) An interaction-learning framework that uses semantic contacts as supervision for physics-based humanoid–human behavior learning,
(3) Generative partner-state prediction objectives that enable latent interaction-state inference under severe partial observability from egocentric observations, and
(4) extensive simulation ablations and real-world deployment on a Unitree G1 across diverse contact-rich humanoid–human interactions.

## 2 Related Work

Humanoid motion tracking and scene interaction

Recent work has achieved impressive progress in humanoid teleoperation and motion tracking through imitation learning, teacher–student distillation, and large-scale behavioral modeling [14, 16, 21, 13, 5, 49, 31, 48, 25]. A growing body of work further extends humanoid control toward object and scene interaction through manipulation and loco-manipulation, physics-based human–object interaction, scene-conditioned motion generation, and visual sim-to-real learning [50, 42, 36, 23, 44, 40, 47, 15, 45, 22, 1]. However, these methods either treat the humanoid in isolation or restrict interaction to static scenes and inanimate objects, whereas REACT targets closed-loop humanoid–human interaction with a dynamically moving human partner.

Interaction Modeling and Human–Human Motion Synthesis

Human–human interaction synthesis has advanced rapidly through diffusion-based and generative motion models.
Recent methods such as InterMask [19], PINO [35], UMO [9], and MAGNet [32] further improve motion diversity, long-horizon interaction synthesis, and flexible multi-agent conditioning. However, these approaches primarily operate in kinematic space and therefore lack physical grounding and closed-loop interaction control. More recent physics-based interaction methods couple diffusion-based motion generation with physics-based tracking [20, 39] or forward-dynamics-guided imitation [27], but they generate the reference interaction offline [39] or observe the partner fully, either in simulation [27] or through an unoccluded third-person capture [20], rather than through deployable onboard sensing.

Interaction retargeting

Motion retargeting offers a promising avenue for transferring large-scale human interaction data to humanoid robots. Optimization-based methods, ranging from inverse kinematics to trajectory optimization [7, 11, 2], adapt the source motion to the humanoid’s kinematics frame by frame or over the whole trajectory, while learning-based retargeting [8] improves stylistic consistency and motion realism. However, because their objectives are defined on each body separately, they carry no notion of which body parts are meant to touch the partner, so contacts present in the source interaction are easily lost once the motion is transferred to a different embodiment. Graphics-oriented approaches have explored geometry-aware and contact-aware retargeting objectives [6, 18], while recent interaction-preserving methods [46] emphasize maintaining relational geometry for downstream policy learning. Beyond Mimicry [17] retargets human–human interactions to a humanoid by preserving pairwise keypoint distances between the two agents, without reasoning about which of them correspond to physical contact. In contrast, REACT introduces semantic contact-aware retargeting that explicitly preserves interaction contacts and relational geometry while suppressing interpenetration between the humanoid and its partner.

Interaction under Partial Observability

Closer to our setting, recent work has approached human–robot interaction by learning from human–human–object demonstrations [4], forecasting social motion to generate robot behavior [33], and adding compliance to whole-body control for physical contact with people [29].
However, these systems depend on robot-side teleoperation data [4], external motion capture [29], or a separate human-pose estimator [17, 20], which becomes unreliable at the close range where physical contact occurs.
Privileged-to-perceptual distillation has produced visual humanoid policies [15, 45, 47], but only for interaction with objects and scenes rather than a moving human partner. In contrast, REACT maps egocentric depth to actions end-to-end and explicitly trains policies to infer hidden
partner motion under occlusion through generative
partner-state modeling.

## 3 Method

Figure 2: Overview of REACT. REACT first annotates semantic contacts in human–human demonstrations with a VLM and retargets them onto the humanoid while preserving them. A privileged teacher then learns the interaction with contact rewards and is finally distilled into an egocentric depth student that predicts the partner’s motion and runs on the real robot.

We formulate deployable humanoid–human interaction as recovering and preserving interaction state across three stages: (i) inferring semantic contact structure from noisy human–human demonstrations, (ii) learning physically grounded interaction policies that reproduce this contact structure under physics, and (iii) inferring partner state from egocentric observations during deployment.

Figure 2 provides an overview of REACT. Given a human–human motion-capture sequence, REACT learns a humanoid policy that reproduces the demonstrated interaction from onboard sensing alone, in three stages. First, semantic contact modeling identifies meaningful contacts and transfers them across the embodiment gap into humanoid–human references. Second, a privileged teacher learns the interaction with contact rewards derived from these annotations. Finally, a deployable egocentric student is distilled from the teacher using only onboard depth and infers partner state through generative prediction.

## 3.1 Semantic Contact Modelling and Retargeting

Existing retargeting methods optimize pose similarity or relational geometry but do not explicitly reason about the contact structure that defines an interaction [2, 46]. REACT instead represents an interaction as a collection of spatiotemporal semantic contacts and preserves them throughout embodiment transfer. Semantic contact modeling proceeds in three steps, detailed below:

Human-to-humanoid surface correspondence Semantic contacts are expressed on SMPL-X body vertices, but the retargeted humanoid has a different morphology with no direct vertex correspondence. We therefore register the SMPL-X mesh to the humanoid surface using Laplacian-regularized non-rigid ICP (Fig. 2, Surface mesh projection), producing an offline, pose-independent mapping from every SMPL-X vertex to a corresponding humanoid surface location $(\text{link},\mathbf{p}_{\text{local}})$ . The resulting correspondence transfers semantic contacts from the human body onto the humanoid embodiment and provides the geometric reference used by both retargeting and interaction-reward computation.

VLM-guided semantic contact annotation A key challenge is that existing motion-capture datasets do not provide annotations of who touches whom, where, and when. Furthermore, reconstructed human meshes are often noisy under heavy mutual occlusion, making geometric proximity alone an unreliable indicator of interaction contact. Simple distance thresholds either miss meaningful contacts or produce large numbers of spurious contact events.

We therefore separate geometric contact discovery from semantic verification. A permissive mesh-distance pass first proposes candidate spatiotemporal contacts between the two interacting humans. These candidates are subsequently verified by a vision-language model (Gemini 2.0 Flash), which receives multi-view renderings of the interaction, the candidate contact list, and a description of the interaction type depicted in the video. The VLM rejects contacts inconsistent with the semantics of the demonstrated interaction and retains only meaningful contact events (Fig. 2, Distance based contacts to VLM filtered contacts). For each contact site $h$ at timestep $t$ , the pipeline emits a flag $c_{t}^{h}$ , the actor vertex $v_{t}^{h}$ , and the contacted partner vertex $j_{t}^{h}$ used downstream by retargeting and the contact reward. Implementation details and the annotation-quality evaluation are in Appendix D.1, and the VLM prompt is in Appendix H.

Semantic contact retargeting Given surface correspondences and semantic contact annotations, we retarget human–human demonstrations into humanoid–human interaction references while explicitly preserving demonstrated contact structure across the embodiment gap.

Stage 1: Interaction-aware kinematic retargeting.
We begin with an OmniRetarget-style initialization [46] (Fig. 2, Interaction preserved retargeting). Only the human mapped to the G1 is rescaled to the G1 embodiment, because rescaling the partner would move the contact targets it supplies and destabilize the G1 retarget. This breaks the inter-body interaction geometry across the embodiment gap, which we mitigate by anchoring the humanoid arm end-effector targets to the demonstrated contact locations on labeled frames.

Stage 2: Semantic contact driven human-humanoid co-optimization.
The initialization is subsequently refined through a contact-preserving optimization stage (Fig. 2, Humanoid-human co-optimization). For every annotated contact $(c_{t}^{h}=1)$ , the corresponding humanoid contact point is driven toward the target partner vertex $j_{t}^{h}$ . At the same time, two complementary regularizers preserve the remainder of the interaction geometry. A distance-preserving proximity field maintains the demonstrated separation between non-contact body regions, preventing collapse of the interaction configuration, while a volumetric SDF penalty [34] suppresses interpenetration. Optimization alternates between humanoid and partner kinematics while preserving the locomotion generated during Stage 1.

The resulting trajectories preserve both the motion and the semantic contact structure of the original interaction. These retargeted demonstrations provide the reference motions used for policy learning and the semantic contact annotations used by the interaction rewards described in Sec. 3.2.1.

## 3.2 Interaction Learning from Semantic Contacts

We use the semantic contact annotations of Sec. 3.1 to learn a privileged interaction teacher that reproduces the demonstrated contact structure under physics.

## 3.2.1 Privileged Interaction Teacher

We train a privileged policy $\pi_{T}$ using PPO [38] (Fig. 2, Teacher Policy). The teacher has access to the full state of the human partner and therefore serves as an upper bound on interaction performance before addressing the challenges of onboard perception.
The teacher receives three groups of observations: (i) proprioceptive robot state $\mathbf{o}^{\text{prop}}_{t}$ , including base motion, joint states, end-effector positions, and previous actions; (ii) reference-motion observations $\mathbf{o}^{\text{ref}}_{t}$ from the retargeted demonstrations; and (iii) privileged partner-state observations $\mathbf{o}^{\text{partner}}_{t}$ containing the partner’s pose and motion expressed in the humanoid’s egocentric frame. More observation details are provided in Appendix F.

Rather than directly controlling all robot joints, the teacher predicts a hybrid action consisting of locomotion commands and upper-body interaction actions. Specifically, we leverage the pretrained HOMIE controller[3] for stable locomotion and predict only four locomotion commands $(v_{x},v_{y},\omega_{z},h)$ together with residual upper-body joint offsets. This factorization allows the policy to focus its capacity on learning contact-rich interaction while inheriting robust locomotion capabilities.

Interaction-Aware Contact Rewards

Tracking the reference alone does not guarantee reproducing the
interaction: a policy can match the reference trajectory while still
failing to establish or maintain contact. We therefore supervise the
teacher with the semantic contact annotations of
Sec. 3.1 (Fig. 2, Vertex-anchored Contact Reward). Prior contact-based rewards rely on
hand-annotated or geometric-proximity contacts [39, 20];
ours instead consume the automatically extracted semantic contacts.
The analytic form of the rewards is in Appendix F.1.

## 3.2.2 Deployable Perceptive Student

The privileged interaction teacher assumes among others access to the full state of the human partner and therefore cannot be deployed on a real humanoid. In practice, close-contact interaction must operate from onboard sensing alone, where severe occlusions, truncation, and limited field of view make the partner’s state only partially observable. We therefore distill the privileged teacher into a deployable egocentric student policy $\pi_{S}$ (Fig. 2, Student Policy) that learns to infer interaction state directly from an onboard depth stream and proprioception history. Depth input images are processed by a shared ResNet encoder [12], while a temporal convolutional network aggregates long-horizon proprioceptive history into a compact motion-context representation. The resulting deployable observation embedding $\mathbf{c}_{t}$ is passed to a conditional variational autoencoder. The student policy is trained using on-policy DAgger [37].

Photorealistic Human Rendering

Most physics simulators represent humans with simplified collision
primitives such as capsule colliders [30],
producing depth observations that diverge sharply from real human
depth. We close this gap with an in-loop rendering pipeline (Fig. 2, In-loop human rendering) that
attaches a visual SMPL-X mesh to the partner: the capsule collider is
kept for physics, while a batched SMPL-X forward pass updates the
visual mesh every control step so the robot’s depth camera sees
realistic human geometry, which closes the sim-to-real gap (Fig. 9 in Appendix C.1).

Generative Partner-State Modeling

We additionally train the student to infer the hidden state of the interaction.
In humanoid–human interaction, the partner is rarely fully observable: during close contact, large portions of their body are frequently occluded or outside the camera field of view. Consequently, many different partner configurations may be consistent with the same visual observation.
We therefore train the student to predict how the interaction evolves from these partial observations: a generative partner-state prediction objective predicts the partner’s past, present, and future motion from the deployable observation representation $\mathbf{c}_{t}$ . A diffusion-based prediction head (Fig. 2, Interaction Denoiser) implements this objective, outputting the partner’s SMPL-X body pose, joint velocities, and orientations, $P^{\text{partner}}_{t}$ , together with the humanoid’s short-horizon future trajectory, $P^{\text{humanoid}}_{t}$ (time offsets in Appendix C.3). These auxiliary predictions provide dense supervision for the visual encoder and encourage the learned latent representation to capture interaction-relevant information that is not directly observable from a single depth frame. Rather than reacting only to visible motion, the student learns to anticipate future interaction dynamics.

Training objective

The final training objective combines teacher-action imitation, latent-state distillation, and generative partner-state prediction:

 $\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{L}_{\text{BC}}+\lambda_{\text{KL}}\mathcal{L}_{\text{KL}}+\lambda_{\text{gen}}\mathcal{L}_{\text{gen}}+\lambda_{\text{pose}}\mathcal{L}_{\text{pose}},$ 

(1)

where the individual loss terms correspond to behavior cloning, latent-state alignment, generative partner-state prediction, and single-step pose estimation, respectively. By jointly learning interaction control and inference of the occluded partner’s state, the resulting policy remains robust under severe occlusion and partial observability while operating entirely from onboard sensing.

Q5. 

Does REACT transfer successfully to real humanoid hardware?

## 4.2 Teacher Policy: Tracking the Interaction

We next evaluate the privileged-state teacher (Sec. 3.2), which must reproduce the retargeted interaction under physics. We ablate the two components that distinguish it from a standard tracking controller. The first is the mesh contact reward, which utilizes the ICP-based mesh-level contact positions and directions of humanoid and SMPL. In the (-) mesh contact reward baseline, it is replaced by a link-level reward that pulls the center of each G1 contact link (the palm for the hands) toward the annotated partner joint, without the mesh registration or the directional and force-band terms. In the Ours w/o VLM contact filtering baseline, the contact positions and timings used for this reward are instead calculated purely based on distance.

Contact supervision for policy learning (Q2) Table 2 demonstrates that both contact-related components contribute on the most contact-rich actions: the mesh contact reward lifts Link-arms Contact Recall by $+34.3\,$ pt and Hug by $+21.7\,$ pt, while VLM contact filtering lifts Hug by $+7.0\,$ pt, High-five by $+3.2\,$ pt, and Link-arms by $+1.4\,$ pt, with the two effects complementary across actions.

## 4.4 Real-World Deployment

We deploy the per-interaction students on a physical Unitree G1 with a human partner across all five interactions. The policies are trained entirely in simulation and run zero-shot, with no real-world fine-tuning; at test time each receives only egocentric depth and proprioception, with no external motion capture or privileged partner state. Because the onboard stereo depth loses the partner’s silhouette while the robot moves, we feed the policy metric depth estimated from the onboard RGB image by DepthAnything 3 [26] (Fig. 2, Real Deployment), which keeps the deployed observation close to the rendered training depth (Sec. 3.2.2).

Real-robot results (Q5) Across ten trials per interaction, the students succeed in $31$ of $50$ trials; per-interaction success rates, success criteria, and the model setting used for these trials are in Appendix A. Figure 1 shows the deployment on the real robot, and videos are on the project page. The deployed policies further generalise across partner distances and orientations and sustain repeated interaction, completing three consecutive hugs in one ${\sim}30\,$ s episode without reset (Appendix A.1).

## 5 Conclusion

We presented REACT, a scalable framework for learning deployable humanoid-human interaction policies from large-scale human-human motion-capture data. REACT addresses two key challenges in humanoid-human interaction: the difficulty of learning from noisy human-human demonstrations under heavy mutual occlusion, and the challenge of deploying interaction policies from onboard sensing under severe partial observability. Our framework combines VLM-guided contact-aware retargeting, privileged interaction learning with interaction-aware contact rewards, egocentric depth-policy distillation with photorealistic in-simulator human rendering, and auxiliary generative objectives that predict the partner’s pose so that the student can infer the partner’s state even under occlusion.

Through extensive simulation experiments and real-world deployment on a Unitree G1 humanoid, we showed that REACT improves preservation of demonstrated interaction contacts and enables robust interaction learning from onboard sensing during close-contact interaction. Our results suggest that scalable humanoid-human interaction may require jointly addressing semantic contact understanding, physically grounded interaction learning, and predictive inference under partial observability. More broadly, we believe REACT provides a step toward deployable humanoid systems capable of safe and adaptive physical interaction with humans in real-world environments. We discuss the limitations of REACT in Appendix G.

## A.1 Qualitative results

Figure 1 of the main paper shows the real-world deployment of each interaction. Here we provide additional qualitative results.

The deployed policy is also robust to where the partner stands and how they are oriented. ## A.3 Deployment-time depth processing

The student is trained on the in-simulator SMPL-X mesh depth described in Sec. C.1, which provides clean human geometry and sharp
body silhouettes. On the physical robot, the onboard Intel RealSense D435 does
not recover these silhouettes reliably: while the robot is moving, its stereo
depth becomes noisy and loses the partner’s contour exactly where the policy
needs it. We therefore do not use the RealSense depth stream at deployment. Instead we run monocular
metric depth estimation with DepthAnything 3 (Large, metric) [26] on the onboard RGB
image and feed the estimated depth to the policy. Inference runs in real time
on the onboard compute through a TensorRT/DeepStream
pipeline.
This keeps the deployed observation close to the sharp, contour-rich depth the
student was trained on, while requiring only a single RGB camera. The demonstration videos on the project page instead use Fast-FoundationStereo [41] on the RealSense left and right infrared images.

## A.4 Safe Interaction through Force Regulation

In close-contact interaction the magnitude of the force the robot applies to the person is a safety concern. We make this force observable and controllable during training by spawning the human partner as a physically simulated, collidable body that tracks the reference motion through PD control rather than a purely kinematic reference, so that every contact the policy makes produces a real reaction force on the corresponding G1 link. The force-regulation reward $r^{\text{force}}_{t}$ of Sec. F.1 (Eq. 7) then shapes how hard the robot touches. It rewards contact forces inside a comfortable band and penalises force at links that should not be in contact. Training the teacher with this term drives the policy toward gentle, bounded contact instead of forceful pushing, so the resulting interaction stays soft enough to be comfortable for the human while remaining firm enough to be felt. The deployable student inherits this behavior through distillation, imitating the actions of the force-regulated teacher.

## Appendix B Composing Per-Interaction Students with VLM Skill Selection

We train one student per interaction. This is a design choice rather than a limit on generalisation: adding a new interaction costs one additional policy instead of retraining all of them. To run the students as a single autonomous system, we treat them as a skill library and add a VLM-based selection layer on top, evaluated in simulation. The VLM agent observes only the robot’s own egocentric RGB and depth, never the partner’s state, and decides both where the robot should move and which student should take control.

Finite state machine

The selection layer is a four-state machine (Figure 7). In Search, the robot rotates in place through the HOMIE locomotion controller until a person becomes visible. In Approach, it walks toward the person and returns to Search if the person is lost. Once the robot is close and the VLM has confirmed which interaction the partner’s configuration calls for, the machine enters Interact and hands control to the matching student (hug, hand-shake, pat-on-back, link-arms, or high-five). The selected interaction is locked for a fixed period; when the lock expires, the robot re-approaches if the partner is still visible but far, or enters Retreat and walks backward if the partner is lost, after which it resumes Search or re-approaches as soon as the partner reappears.

Figure 7: VLM skill-selection state machine. Solid boxes are states and dashed boxes are the policies they invoke: the HOMIE locomotion controller for Search, Approach, and Retreat, and one of the five per-interaction students for Interact.

Figure 8: One continuous rollout with VLM skill selection. No interaction is specified in advance. From left to right: the partner opens their arms, and the VLM selects the hug student; the partner is then displaced mid-rollout, and the robot re-finds them, approaches, and switches to the hand-shake student, all without a reset.

Qualitative result

Figure 8 shows one continuous rollout in which no interaction is specified. The robot first selects and executes a hug from the partner’s open-arm posture. After the partner is displaced mid-rollout, the robot re-finds the partner, approaches, and selects the hand-shake student, without any reset or operator intervention. A safe abort for a partner who pulls away mid-contact is left to future work.

## Appendix C Student Policy

## C.1 Realistic Human Depth Rendering

A depth policy transfers to the real robot only if its training depth resembles what the onboard sensor will see, so closing the sim-to-real depth gap is essential. Physics simulators represent the human partner with capsule colliders, whose egocentric depth (Fig. 9, left) looks nothing like a real person. We hide these capsules from the depth camera and, for rendering only, attach a visual SMPL-X body mesh that we update in the loop, so that at every control step a single batched GPU SMPL-X forward pass advances every environment’s mesh to its current frame and the camera sees a realistic moving human (Fig. 9, middle). The capsule colliders stay in the physics scene so contact is still simulated, while the mesh is used only for rendering. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first system to model physical interaction with a human in a physics simulator that simultaneously renders the human as a photorealistic SMPL-X mesh for perception and simulates contact through capsule colliders. The surrounding scene is further randomised with mesh assets refreshed during training, so the depth also carries realistic background geometry (Fig. 9, right). This in-loop human-and-scene depth is what lets the student deploy zero-shot on the real robot.

## C.2 Why End-to-End Depth Beats Pose-Estimation-Based Methods

A natural way to drive interaction with a human partner is to attach an external human-pose-estimation module, recover the partner’s body from the camera stream, and feed the estimated pose to the policy as the partner-state input [17, 10]. This design factors perception from control and reuses mature off-the-shelf pose estimators, but it breaks down in the close-range regime that physical interaction requires.
At contact range, the partner is largely occluded by the robot’s own body or leaves the camera field of view, so an external estimator either loses the partner or returns a stale pose precisely when accurate partner state matters most.

REACT instead relies on no external pose-estimation module: it maps the egocentric depth image to action end-to-end, and uses partner pose estimation only as an auxiliary task that improves training efficiency.

Table 5 shows the quantitative comparison between these two approaches. We build the external-pose baseline following 4D Humans [10]. At each step, a person detector localizes the partner and HMR2.0 reconstructs the partner’s body. The recovered partner joint positions are concatenated with the robot’s proprioception and fed to the policy, replacing the egocentric depth input that REACT uses. This substitution collapses mesh-contact recall on every interaction, falling from $78.6$ to $8.2$ on hand-shake and from $15.3$ to $5.4$ on high-five, because the external estimator degrades under exactly the self-occlusion and field-of-view loss that close, contact-rich interaction induces. The end-to-end student, by contrast, stays reliable in this regime, where an external estimator is least able to recover the partner state.

To better understand why the external-pose baseline fails, Figure 10 overlays the ground-truth partner mesh (green) and the HMR2.0 reconstruction (red) in the same scene as the robot, for a high-five and a hug episode.
The two interactions expose complementary failure modes of external pose estimation at contact range.
In the high-five case (left), the partner raises a hand toward the robot, and as that hand crosses the edge of the robot’s egocentric field of view, the reconstructed arm jumps to a completely wrong configuration. Such an implausible pose drives the policy’s partner-state input out of the distribution it was trained on, so the policy reaches to the wrong location. In the hug case (right), the partner remains in view during the approach, but at the instant of embrace, the partner’s torso fills and occludes the camera. With almost no visible body surface to fit, HMR2.0 produces a large, unstable error and the reconstruction breaks apart behind the true mesh.
In both cases the estimator degrades exactly when the interaction enters close contact, which is when accurate partner state matters most, and this is the mechanism behind the recall collapse in Table 5.

## C.3 Depth-based Student Policy: architecture, losses, and distillation

Observations

At every control step the student receives (i) a 5-frame egocentric depth stack at offsets $\{0,0.5,1.0,1.5,2.0\}\,\text{s}$ into the past, (ii) a 5-step short-term proprioception history ( $5\times 108=540$ dims), and (iii) a $100$ -step ( $2\,\text{s}$ at $50\,\text{Hz}$ ) long-term proprioception history.

Depth backbone

A shared ResNet [12] embeds each of the five depth frames independently. The per-frame embeddings are concatenated into a temporal depth representation.

Long-term proprio encoder

A 1D-CNN maps the $100$ -step proprioception window to a compact embedding.

Conditional VAE

Let $\mathbf{c}_{t}$ concatenate the temporal depth embedding, the short-term proprioception history, and the long-term proprioception embedding. A prior MLP maps $\mathbf{c}_{t}$ to a diagonal Gaussian $\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{p},\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{p}^{2})$ ; a training-only encoder MLP maps the current reference joint positions and velocities $\mathbf{o}^{\text{ref}}_{t}$ to $\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{e},\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{e}^{2})$ . We combine the two heads residually, $\boldsymbol{\mu}_{z}=\boldsymbol{\mu}_{p}+\boldsymbol{\mu}_{e}$ , and a decoder MLP maps $[\mathbf{c}_{t},\mathbf{z}_{t}]$ to the same 18-dim HOMIE-plus-arm action mean as the teacher. At deployment the encoder is dropped and $\mathbf{z}_{t}$ is drawn from the prior $\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{p},\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{p}^{2})$ .

Pose-anticipation auxiliary head

A DDPM denoiser conditioned on the temporal depth embedding and the long-term proprioception embedding predicts the partner’s past, current, and short-horizon future SMPL-X key-body state (positions, velocities, and orientations), $P^{\text{partner}}_{t}$ , together with the G1’s own near-future trajectory, $P^{\text{humanoid}}_{t}$ , over 5 past/current frames at $\{0,0.5,1.0,1.5,2.0\}\,\text{s}$ and 3 future frames at $\{0.5,1.0,1.5\}\,\text{s}$ ahead. The objective sums a multi-step DDIM forward-prediction loss and a GENMO-style [24] single-step pose-estimation loss in equal proportion, and the combined pose loss enters the total distillation loss with weight $0.5$ , i.e. $\lambda_{\text{gen}}=\lambda_{\text{pose}}=0.5$ . Per-frame losses are gated by how much of the human partner is visible in the depth image. Frames in which the partner occupies $15\%$ or less of the depth pixels contribute no gradient.

Distillation loss

The full distillation loss defined in the main paper combines an L2 behavior-cloning term against the teacher action, the conditional-VAE KL between the privileged encoder posterior and the deployable prior (with $\lambda_{\text{KL}}$ ramped from $10^{-4}$ to $10^{-2}$ between epochs 500 and 2000), and the two pose objectives above. We use on-policy DAgger [37].

Other student training details

The egocentric camera is a simulated Intel RealSense D435 mounted on the G1’s upper body and held level, so it faces forward rather than down at the floor and keeps the partner inside the frustum during close interaction. The stock G1 mount is pitched about $48^{\circ}$ downward for locomotion, so on the real robot we use a custom level mount that the simulated camera matches. It runs at $30\,\text{Hz}$ with an $87^{\circ}$ horizontal field of view and a $3\,\text{m}$ far clip, renders at $192{\times}108$ without down-sampling, and these intrinsics match the physical D435 we deploy. Because such an $87^{\circ}$ -HFOV camera cannot see a partner who is, in the demonstration, beside or behind the humanoid, we restrict the training and evaluation corpus to clips in which the partner stays within the frustum. We train on 128 parallel Isaac Sim environments on a single L40S GPU with the partner driven by a reference PD controller and rendered as in Sec. C.1. Demonstration-time augmentation includes human motion speed warping $[0.8,1.2]\times$ , a random start-time offset up to $1\,\text{s}$ , a random G1 spatial offset of $0.15\,\text{m}$ , a random yaw offset up to $0.32\,\text{rad}$ ( $\approx 18.5^{\circ}$ ), and segment slowdown up to $2\times$ over windows of up to $3\,\text{s}$ . Depth-image augmentation applies edge displacement, distance-dependent additive Gaussian and sparkle noise, background-occlusion rectangles, and random rectangular and corner masks, and the deepest pixels are clamped to the $3\,\text{m}$ far clip. We distil a separate student per interaction, so each of the five actions has its own deployable checkpoint.

## Appendix D Contact Label Filtering and Surface Projection

## D.1 VLM Contact Annotation: distance candidates and semantic verification

We annotate five contact-rich Inter-X [43] actions: hug, hand-shake, pat-on-back, high-five, and link-arms by following these steps shown below.

Stage 1: distance candidates

We run SMPL-X forward kinematics on both bodies and, for every other frame, group each person’s arm mesh vertices by skinning weights into a hand (wrist and fingers), forearm, and upper-arm region on each side. Each region’s vertices are compared against every vertex of the partner mesh; pairs within $15\,\text{cm}$ are grouped by (contacting arm region, contacted SMPL-X joint), the closest pair is retained per group, and detections separated by gaps of up to $0.5\,\text{s}$ are merged into events. Events shorter than $0.1\,\text{s}$ are discarded. Each event records its time window, the contacting region, the contacted partner joint, the closest actor- and partner-side vertices, and the minimum and average mesh-to-mesh distances.

Stage 2: VLM verification

Each event is verified against the corresponding video by a vision-language model (Gemini 2.0 Flash). We render the SMPL-X motion from three camera viewpoints with person-1 in blue and person-2 in orange, concatenate the views horizontally with per-view foreground cropping, sample at $5\,\text{fps}$ , and split the video into $3\,\text{s}$ chunks; each chunk is sent with the action label and a textual description of every candidate contact event that overlaps it. The VLM assigns each contact candidate one of four semantic labels (essential, incidental, unnecessary, or avoid), together with the contacting and contacted surfaces (palm/inner vs. back/outer) and the views in which the contact is clearly visible. Only events labelled essential or incidental survive; Figure 11 shows representative kept and removed candidates across the five actions. The verbatim prompt is given in Sec. H.

Output

For each of the six contact sites (left or right hand, forearm, upper-arm) and every timestep $t$ , the annotation provides a binary contact flag, the closest vertex on the actor mesh, the contacted target vertex on the partner mesh, and the name of the contacted SMPL-X joint. The contact reward of Eq. 5 and the Stage 2 contact co-optimisation in retargeting consume these flags for all six sites, mapped to the G1 wrist, elbow, and shoulder links.

Label-quality evaluation against manual ground truth

To isolate the contribution of Stage 2, we evaluate the contact labels themselves, independently of any retargeting, against a hand-annotated ground-truth set. We manually annotate $80$ Inter-X clips ( $16$ per action, five actions: hug, hand-shake, pat-on-back, high-five, link-arms), marking the actor SMPL-X vertex, the contacted partner SMPL-X vertex, and the time window of each true contact event. We then treat the Stage 1 distance candidates and the Stage 2 VLM-verified events as predicted events and match them to GT events on a per-frame basis. At every frame, an actor–partner contact pair is counted as a true positive iff (i) the actor vertices of the GT and the prediction lie within $\epsilon=10\,\text{cm}$ on the SMPL-X rest-pose template (matching the retargeting threshold used in the main paper), and (ii) the same holds on the partner side. To avoid double-counting the same physical contact when the predictor independently emits its mirrored (partner-actor) candidate, we deduplicate predictions whose vertex pair coincides with that of an existing same-frame actor–partner candidate. We report per-clip precision, recall, and $F_{1}$ as the across-clip mean $\pm$ standard deviation, both per action and on the full $80$ -clip set.

VLM verification cannot recover contacts that were never proposed, so its hug gain is small and comes from cleaning the few arm-region candidates rather than from added coverage.

Annotation tool and ground truth

The $80$ GT clips are annotated with a custom GUI that records, for each true contact event, the actor SMPL-X vertex, the contacted partner SMPL-X vertex, and the start/end frames. GT events are authored in an actor-centric convention and remapped to the Inter-X canonical P1/P2 frame before scoring (the same remap used in Sec. E.4); without this remap roughly half of the clips would compare the wrong person. Each clip is annotated by a single expert.

D.2 establishes a dense body correspondence that maps every SMPL-X body vertex to a $(\text{link},\,\mathbf{p}_{\text{local}})$ pair, so the same mesh can be projected onto either target surface, the Unitree G1 robot or the partner’s SMPL-X collision capsules. The three columns show one identical set of colour-coded SMPL-X vertices in three configurations, from left to right: projected onto the G1 robot surface, in their SMPL-X template positions, and projected onto the partner capsule surface. Each colour denotes the G1 link a vertex is registered to, and the connecting lines join the same representative vertices across the three configurations, so that any contact vertex can be carried onto the corresponding robot or capsule surface by forward kinematics. The dense map covers the full template at a mean vertex-to-surface residual of $0.89\,\text{mm}$ , well below the $\epsilon=10\,\text{cm}$ contact threshold.

We construct an offline map from every SMPL-X body vertex to a $(\text{link},\,\mathbf{p}_{\text{local}})$ pair on the Unitree G1 zero-pose surface. At run time, the world-space location of any SMPL-X contact vertex used in Stage 2 retargeting (Sec. E.2) and in the contact reward
is recovered by forward kinematics on G1 from the stored link-local point. The registration runs in four stages on the SMPL-X mean-shape body and the G1 zero-pose surface mesh.

Stage 1: limb-direction initialisation

A scale-invariant initial alignment between the SMPL-X body and the G1 zero pose is obtained by matching cosines between corresponding limb directions on the two bodies. Using direction rather than length yields a robust initialisation despite the substantial proportion mismatch between the human body and the humanoid robot.

Stage 2: weighted Procrustes alignment

A weighted Procrustes solve over corresponding skeletal joints further aligns the two articulations while preserving their global geometric consistency, providing a clean reference frame for the subsequent surface-level deformation.

Stage 3: Laplacian-regularised non-rigid ICP

We deform the SMPL-X mesh onto the G1 surface with a Laplacian-regularised non-rigid ICP, establishing dense surface-level correspondences. Two safeguards prevent erroneous matches across unrelated body parts despite the surface-proximity criterion. A decreasing-stiffness schedule establishes coarse rigid-body-part structure before allowing fine-grained deformations, and ray-cast normal projection restricts each correspondence query to surfaces visible along the outward SMPL-X vertex normal, blocking matches that would otherwise wrap around the robot from the opposite side.

Stage 4: post-snap and storage

A final post-snap stage projects the remaining outlier vertices, those whose Stage 3 target falls in free space or on a kinematically unrelated link, onto the nearest valid robot surface point, ensuring complete surface coverage. For each SMPL-X vertex we store the resulting G1 link identifier together with the link-local position $\mathbf{p}_{\text{local}}$ .

Runtime use

At every control step, the contact reward of Eq. 5 and retargeting (Sec. E.2) retrieve the G1-side contact point as $\mathbf{p}^{h}_{t}=T_{\text{link}^{h}}(\boldsymbol{q}_{t})\,\mathbf{p}_{\text{local}^{h}}$ , where $T_{\text{link}^{h}}(\boldsymbol{q}_{t})$ is the forward-kinematics transform of the stored link at joint configuration $\boldsymbol{q}_{t}$ and $(\text{link}^{h},\mathbf{p}_{\text{local}^{h}})$ is the registered pair for contact site $h$ .

Registration quality

Across the full SMPL-X template the recovered correspondence attains a mean vertex-to-surface residual of $0.89\,\text{mm}$ on the G1 zero-pose mesh, far below the $\epsilon=10\,\text{cm}$ contact threshold used throughout evaluation (Sec. E.4); registration error is therefore negligible relative to the metric tolerance. Figure 12 visualises the colour-coded link assignment recovered by this procedure.

## E.2 Contact-Aware Motion Retargeting

We retarget each human–human demonstration onto the humanoid in two stages. Stage 1 (kinematic retargeting) height-scales only the humanoid-side body along gravity and, on contact-labelled frames, blends the arm end-effector targets toward the demonstrated contact positions, so the touches are anchored rather than averaged away. It builds on OmniRetarget [46]. Stage 2 (contact co-optimisation), detailed next, closes the residual contact gap.

Co-optimisation

Stage 2 refines the Stage-1 kinematic initialisation by
alternating inverse kinematics between the humanoid and the partner, rather than by
descending a single weighted-sum loss. We run $3$ outer co-optimisation iterations. Each
iteration first solves a humanoid IK step and then a partner SMPL-X IK step, holding the
Stage-1 locomotion (root trajectory and leg motion) fixed throughout.

Targets

Each annotated interaction is converted into per-frame IK targets of two
kinds. The first kind is a binary contact, a touch that the VLM filter has verified. For
a binary contact the humanoid contact site $h$ is driven toward a meeting point
 $\mathbf{m}^{h}_{t}=(1-w)\,cg_{h}(\mathbf{q}_{t})+w\,rc_{j}$ , where $cg_{h}$ is the G1 link-surface
point, $rc_{j}$ the targeted partner SMPL-X vertex, and $w$ a slack weight, with
 $\mathbf{m}^{h}_{t}$ projected onto the partner surface mesh. The second kind is a proximity
pair, a body part that stays near the partner without touching and must keep its demonstrated
separation. We flag a proximity pair whenever a partner arm or hand vertex approaches a non-arm
G1 link within $0.08\,\text{m}$ , and we drive the humanoid site toward the distance-preserving
point $rc_{j}+\hat{\mathbf{d}}\,d^{\text{ref}}$ , where $\hat{\mathbf{d}}$ is the current
partner-to-G1 direction and $d^{\text{ref}}$ the demonstrated distance. This distance-preserving
proximity field keeps the interaction configuration from collapsing. Binary targets enter the
humanoid IK with weight $1.0$ , scaled by a $2$ -frame approach ramp, and proximity targets with
weight $0.3$ .

Solvers

The humanoid IK is a damped least-squares step over the arm DOFs ( $10$ inner iterations, orientation weight $0.3$ , $N_{\text{sub}}=5$ 
sub-steps). The partner SMPL-X IK is a gradient step (learning rate $0.003$ , $100$ inner
iterations, pose-regularisation weight $0.003$ toward the Stage-1 pose, temporal-smoothness
weight $0.02$ ). Interpenetration is suppressed by a volumetric SMPL signed-distance
field [34] evaluated against the G1 collision mesh (collision weight
 $10.0$ , margin $0.005\,\text{m}$ ), making the IK collision-aware. At most $8$ contact pairs per frame
are optimised, and clips are truncated to $10\,\text{s}$ .

Contact-adaptive temporal smoothing

We smooth the optimised arm body-pose and
arm joint DOFs with a Gaussian temporal filter whose width adapts to how much contact the clip
contains. The effective width is $\sigma_{\mathrm{eff}}=\sigma\,\min(1,\,n_{c}/n_{0})$ with base
width $\sigma=2$ frames, $n_{c}$ the number of contact frames, and $n_{0}$ a contact-count
threshold, so dense-contact clips are smoothed at the full width while sparse-contact actions
such as high-five receive proportionally less smoothing and keep their brief contact peaks. The
contact source is the VLM-verified set for Ours and the raw distance candidates for
Ours w/o VLM filtering. All other settings are identical between the two.

## E.3 Dataset Statistics and Filtering

We draw from five contact-rich Inter-X action categories: hug, hand-shake, pat-on-back,
high-five, and link-arms. The master pool of reconstructed human–human sequences contains
 $1355$ clips (hug $282$ , hand-shake $278$ , pat-on-back $275$ , high-five $252$ , link-arms $268$ ).

Teacher pool (1100)

We curate the master pool using the Inter-X action label
attached to each clip by our VLM annotation: clips whose realised motion does not match the
nominal interaction (e.g. lift-up and other off-label variants that are infeasible or
unsafe to reproduce on the G1) are removed. This drops $255$ clips ( $18.8\%$ ) and leaves
 $N{=}1100$ valid sequences (hug $244$ , hand-shake $233$ , pat-on-back $196$ , high-five $205$ ,
link-arms $222$ ). This $1100$ -clip pool is the teacher training corpus and also the pool on
which Jitter and Penetration are reported (Sec. E.4); the
 $80$ manually GT-annotated clips used for Recall/Precision are a subset of it.

Student pool (human-scored subset)

For the deployable student we apply a second,
stricter curation: two human raters score the retargeted result of each candidate clip on a
 $3$ -point scale, and only clips that both rate at the top score ( $3$ ) are kept, so the student
trains on the cleanest contact reproductions. The student additionally restricts to the
FOV-filtered subset (Sec. C.3), in which the partner stays inside the
torso-camera frustum for the entire horizon, and uses a $9{:}1$ train/evaluation split.

(head, torso, pelvis, left/right {arm, hand, leg, foot})
by SMPL-X skinning-weight argmax; the same labels apply to the
partner mesh and, through the registration, to the humanoid surface.
For each ordered pair of body parts $(b_{\text{act}},b_{\text{par}})$ and each frame $t$ we compute the per-vertex
minimum distance between the two parts. A predicted contact event is
a contiguous frame run of at least $\delta_{\min}=4$ frames
( $\approx 0.13\,\text{s}$ at $30\,\text{Hz}$ ) on which this distance stays below
 $\epsilon$ . A predicted event matches a GT event if
(a) both body-part identities agree strictly (no left/right
or hand/arm equivalence; the GT body-part identity is the
skinning-argmax label of $v_{e}^{\text{act}},v_{e}^{\text{par}}$ ) and
(b) the predicted and GT time windows overlap (Jaccard-style
 $\geq\!1$ shared frame; we report identical conclusions under
 $\tau_{0.5}$ and $\text{IoU}_{0.3}$ overlap variants). Per-clip
Precision is the fraction of predicted events with at least one
match; clips with no predicted events are excluded.

Why the two metrics use different granularities

Recall verifies known GT events, each of which already names the exact
actor and partner vertices, so it can use the tightest per-vertex test. Precision
must instead discover unknown predicted contacts and penalise spurious ones,
which means scanning the whole retargeted motion for emergent G1–partner
proximities. A per-vertex scan is ill-posed here because the actor side is the
G1 surface, a different embodiment, and exhaustive vertex-vs-vertex testing is
dominated by mesh-resolution noise, so we score Precision at the coarser body-part
level. We did try a vertex-pair precision and discarded it. Scoring only the
GT-named pairs rewards conservative methods that attempt few contacts, which earns
high precision by abstention, and never counts the spurious near-contacts a method
generates elsewhere. By contrast, the VLM contact-label evaluation of
Sec. D.1 is a label-vs-label setting, with
both sides annotations on the same SMPL-X mesh, so it uses the per-vertex test
for both precision and recall.

Jitter and Penetration

Jitter measures motion smoothness as
the mean magnitude of the third time derivative (jerk) of the humanoid
body-keypoint trajectories. With $\mathbf{p}_{t,k}$ the world position of G1
body keypoint $k$ at frame $t$ , taken over the $23$ link origins including the
root, and $\Delta t=1/\text{fps}$ the clip timestep,

 $\dddot{\mathbf{p}}_{t,k}=\frac{\mathbf{p}_{t+1,k}-3\,\mathbf{p}_{t,k}+3\,\mathbf{p}_{t-1,k}-\mathbf{p}_{t-2,k}}{\Delta t^{3}},\qquad\mathrm{Jitter}=\operatorname*{mean}_{t,k}\,\big\|\dddot{\mathbf{p}}_{t,k}\big\|,$ 

(3)

averaged over interior frames and keypoints within a clip and then over clips,
in $\text{m}/\text{s}^{3}$ , where lower is smoother. Penetration measures how
far the humanoid interpenetrates the partner’s body. For each frame we sample
surface points $\mathbf{x}_{i,t}$ on the contact-relevant G1 links and compute,
directly against the partner SMPL-X body mesh, the depth $\delta_{i,t}$ by which
each point lies inside it, with $\delta_{i,t}=0$ for points outside the mesh.
The per-frame penetration is the deepest such depth,
 $\mathrm{pen}_{t}=\max_{i}\delta_{i,t}$ , which we average over frames within a clip
and then over clips and report in centimetres, where lower is better.
Penetration of the humanoid with itself is not counted.

Pool definitions

Jitter and Penetration are reported on the full $N{=}1100$ 
valid-sequence pool, the VLM-label-filtered Inter-X corpus defined in
Sec. E.3, of which the $80$ GT-annotated clips are a
subset. OmniRetarget is scored on $N{=}766$ of these, because its hard
collision solver drops the remaining ${\approx}30\%$ of the clips as infeasible. Recall and
Precision are reported on the $80$ GT-annotated clips only.

Reading the per-action results

Table 10 places
Recall and Precision side by side per action, which separates two failure modes that the
aggregate numbers hide. Stage 2 co-optimisation is what creates contact at all. Without it
Recall averages $13.4\%$ and is $0\%$ on hand-shake, because the Stage-1 initialisation never
closes the palm-to-palm gap. Given Stage 2, the VLM filter helps most where contact is short
and palm-localised. On high-five it lifts both axes (R $49.0\!\to\!70.8$ , P
 $27.8\!\to\!33.3$ ), the best case for our method, whereas on hand-shake the distance
candidates already localise the grip, so the filter trades a little Recall ( $84.4\!\to\!81.2$ )
for cleaner Precision. The actions where $F_{1}$ is lowest split by which axis is binding.
Pat-on-back is recall-rich but precision-poor (close-range arm motion fires many spurious
near-contacts), while hug is recall-bound for every method, because $33\%$ of its GT events put
the actor on the torso/pelvis, outside the hand/forearm/upper-arm regions the predictor
enumerates, a coverage limit of the candidate generator, not of the retargeter.

Reading the absolute values and spread

Both metrics are deliberately strict and
are not binary interaction-success rates. Recall demands the registered actor point fall
within $\epsilon{=}10\,\text{cm}$ of the exact GT partner vertex. Relaxing $\epsilon$ shows the
contact is present but slightly displaced (Ours $59.4\%\!\to\!80.0\%\!\to\!92.0\%$ at
 $10/15/20\,\text{cm}$ , Table 9). Precision counts every body-part near-pass
as a predicted event, so a method that genuinely reaches contact sweeps past many partner parts
during approach and retraction, emitting $\approx\!9\times$ more predicted events than GT
( $1740$ vs. $203$ for Ours) and capping Precision near $14\%$ . The large across-clip std in
the main paper’s retargeting Recall (Ours $59.43_{\pm 38.91}$ ) is a property of this binary
per-event metric on short clips. Per-clip Recall averages only $2$ – $3$ events and so takes
coarse values in $\{0,\tfrac{1}{2},\tfrac{2}{3},1\}$ , whereas the action-level means span a far narrower
 $32$ – $81\%$ , i.e. the spread is per-clip difficulty, not systematic action differences.
Finally, OmniRetarget is scored on only its $58$ feasible clips, which favours it at
larger thresholds (unreachable clips are dropped, not counted as misses). The $\epsilon{=}10\,\text{cm}$ 
comparison where Ours leads on both axes is therefore the conservative reading. What these
metrics do not capture, namely whether the interaction visually works, is addressed by the
user study (Table 11) and the real-robot results (Sec. A.1).

## Appendix F Teacher Policy: observation breakdown, reward weights, and training

Observation

The teacher’s proprioception vector $\mathbf{o}^{\text{prop}}_{t}=[\boldsymbol{\omega}_{b},\,\mathbf{q},\,\dot{\mathbf{q}},\,\mathbf{a}^{\text{joint}}_{t-1}]$ concatenates base angular velocity (3), 29 joint positions, 29 joint velocities, and the previous joint-level action (29) output by the HOMIE conversion below. The reference and partner inputs comprise the target G1 joint positions and velocities at the current frame and at four future offsets ( $[1,2,4,8]$ steps), the reference root orientation in the base frame, and the partner’s root and body positions relative to the robot base. Small per-term observation noise (joint positions $\sigma{=}0.01$ , joint velocities $\sigma{=}0.5$ , base angular velocity $\sigma{=}0.2$ , partner positions $\sigma{=}0.1$ ) regularises the policy during rollouts. The critic is noise-free and additionally observes privileged terms unavailable to the actor, namely base linear velocity, the robot’s body positions and orientations in the base frame, and the reference body position.

Action

The teacher emits $\mathbf{a}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{18}$ : four HOMIE locomotion commands $(v_{x},v_{y},\omega_{z},h)$ and 14 residual upper-body joint offsets. The HOMIE tracker converts the 4-d command into 15 lower-body joint actions, and the 14 residuals are added in action space to the demonstration’s reference arm pose. The tracker is kept frozen throughout teacher training.

Reward weights

The aggregate per-step reward is

 $r_{t}=3\,r^{\text{dir}}_{t}+5\,r^{\text{dist}}_{t}+0.5\,r^{\text{force}}_{t}+0.5\,r^{\text{root\,pos}}_{t}+0.5\,r^{\text{root\,ori}}_{t}+r^{\text{body\,pos}}_{t}+r^{\text{body\,ori}}_{t}+r^{\text{body\,vel}}_{t}+r^{\text{body\,ang}}_{t}+r^{\text{no\,fly}}_{t}+r^{\text{reg}}_{t},$ 

where each motion-tracking term has the exponential form $\exp(-e/\sigma^{2})$ , with $e$ the per-body mean squared tracking error and $\sigma$ the error scale at which the reward decays to $1/e$ (a smaller $\sigma$ enforces tighter tracking). The relative-frame body terms use body-position $\sigma=0.3\,\text{m}$ , body-orientation $\sigma=0.4\,\text{rad}$ , body-velocity $\sigma=1.0\,\text{m/s}$ , and body-angular-velocity $\sigma=3.14\,\text{rad/s}$ . Regularisation: action rate $-3{\times}10^{-2}$ , joint velocity $-2{\times}10^{-4}$ , joint acceleration $-1{\times}10^{-7}$ , torque $-1{\times}10^{-4}$ , DOF-position soft limit $-100$ (with $0.9$ soft-limit ratio), DOF-velocity soft limit $-1{\times}10^{-3}$ , torque soft limit $-3{\times}10^{-2}$ . The no-fly term rewards at least one foot in contact ( $+1$ when satisfied).

Initialisation

Episodes use a hybrid physical state initialisation: with probability $0.5$ the episode restarts from the policy’s own recent on-policy state; otherwise it is seeded from the demonstration’s first frame perturbed by Gaussian noise with joint positions $\sigma=0.1$ , root translation $\sigma_{xyz}=(0.05,0.05,0.01)\,\text{m}$ , root orientation $\sigma=(0.1,0.1,0.2)\,\text{rad}$ , root linear velocity $\sigma=(0.5,0.5,0.2)\,\text{m/s}$ , and root angular velocity $\sigma=(0.52,0.52,0.78)\,\text{rad/s}$ . The initial action noise std is $\sigma_{\text{init}}=0.5$ . The partner is driven by a reference PD controller that tracks the demonstration.

PPO

Both actor and critic are 5-layer MLPs with hidden sizes $[512,512,512,256,128]$ and ELU activations. We use PPO [38] with $\gamma=0.99$ , GAE $\lambda=0.95$ , clipping $0.2$ , 8 update epochs per rollout, 4 minibatches, entropy coefficient $0.005$ , and AdamW [28] with learning rate $10^{-5}$ for both actor and critic. We collect 24 environment steps per iteration across 8,192 parallel Isaac Sim environments on a single L40S GPU.

Evaluation metrics: MPJPE and Contact Recall

The teacher is
evaluated with MPJPE and Contact Recall, and the student that distils it with
Contact Recall. MPJPE (mean
per-joint position error) measures tracking fidelity, the mean Euclidean distance
between the humanoid body keypoints under physics and the same keypoints of the
retargeted reference the policy tracks,

 $\mathrm{MPJPE}=\operatorname*{mean}_{t,k}\,\big\|\mathbf{p}^{\text{sim}}_{t,k}-\mathbf{p}^{\text{ref}}_{t,k}\big\|,$ 

(4)

reported in centimetres. Contact Recall measures how often the policy
realises the demonstrated contacts under physics. Manual annotation does not
scale to the policy evaluation pools (all student-pool motions for the teacher, the held-out
split for the student), so we use the VLM-verified contact
annotations of Sec. D.1 as ground truth. For every
annotated contact frame we carry the prescribed actor contact site onto the G1
surface through the registration of Sec. D.2, measure its
distance to the annotated partner vertex under partner forward kinematics, and
count the frame as satisfied when this distance is below $15\,\text{cm}$ for the
teacher and $30\,\text{cm}$ for the student. Contact
Recall is the fraction of satisfied (frame, contact site) pairs, pooled over all
evaluated sequences.

## F.1 Contact Reward: coefficients and force gate

Each VLM-verified annotation specifies, per frame, the active contact pairs between fixed G1 contact links and partner surface points. The six candidate links are the left and right wrist, elbow, and shoulder. For a pair $h$ active at time $t$ , let $\mathbf{p}^{h}_{t}$ denote the G1 contact point, $\mathbf{j}^{h}_{t}$ the target partner vertex, $\mathbf{n}^{h}_{t}$ the partner surface normal at that vertex, and $F^{h}_{t}$ the contact-force magnitude on the corresponding G1 link. The G1 contact point is recovered by forward kinematics from the mesh-projection registration of Sec. D.2, $\mathbf{p}^{h}_{t}=T_{\text{link}^{h}}(\mathbf{q}_{t})\,\mathbf{p}_{\text{local}^{h}}$ , where $(\text{link}^{h},\mathbf{p}_{\text{local}^{h}})$ is the registered G1 link and surface point for the contact. The world-frame target $\mathbf{j}^{h}_{t}$ is the partner contact-vertex position from the SMPL-X forward kinematics of the reference motion.

We supervise the teacher with three complementary contact rewards. The first drives the demonstrated contact closed and gates it on real force,

 $r^{\text{dist}}_{t}=\Big\langle\,\phi(F^{h}_{t})\,\exp\!\big(-\sigma_{d}\,|\mathbf{p}^{h}_{t}-\mathbf{j}^{h}_{t}|^{2}\big)\Big\rangle_{h},\qquad\phi(F)=\min\!\big(\exp(F-\tau),\,1\big),$ 

(5)

averaged over the active pairs $h$ , with sharpness $\sigma_{d}=50\,\text{m}^{-2}$ and force-gate threshold $\tau=1\,\text{N}$ (the gate is active because robot–partner collisions are enabled). The second encourages the contact to be approached from the physically correct side and discourages satisfying the distance objective through interpenetration,

 $r^{\text{dir}}_{t}=\Big\langle\,\mathrm{clamp}\!\big(\hat{\mathbf{v}}^{h}_{t}\cdot\mathbf{n}^{h}_{t},\,0,\,1\big)\,\cdot\,\mathrm{clamp}\!\big(\mathbf{n}^{h,\text{G1}}_{t}\cdot(-\hat{\mathbf{v}}^{h}_{t}),\,0,\,1\big)\Big\rangle_{h},\qquad\hat{\mathbf{v}}^{h}_{t}=\frac{\mathbf{p}^{h}_{t}-\mathbf{j}^{h}_{t}}{\max\!\big(|\mathbf{p}^{h}_{t}-\mathbf{j}^{h}_{t}|,\,\epsilon\big)},$ 

(6)

with $\epsilon=0.02\,\text{m}$ . The first factor requires the approach direction to align with the partner surface normal $\mathbf{n}^{h}_{t}$ , and the second requires the robot’s own contact-surface normal $\mathbf{n}^{h,\text{G1}}_{t}$ to face the partner. The third regulates the established contact force. Per pair, with contact label $l^{h}_{t}$ and force band $[F_{\min},F_{\max}]=[5,50]\,\text{N}$ ,

 $r^{\text{force},h}_{t}=\begin{cases}1,&l^{h}_{t}=1,\ F^{h}_{t}\in[F_{\min},F_{\max}],\\
\exp\!\big(-c_{u}(F_{\min}-F^{h}_{t})\big),&l^{h}_{t}=1,\ F^{h}_{t}<F_{\min},\\
\exp\!\big(-c_{o}(F^{h}_{t}-F_{\max})\big),&l^{h}_{t}=1,\ F^{h}_{t}>F_{\max},\\
-c_{w}F^{h}_{t},&l^{h}_{t}=0,\ F^{h}_{t}>F_{\min},\\
0,&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}$ 

(7)

summed over pairs to give $r^{\text{force}}_{t}=\sum_{h}r^{\text{force},h}_{t}$ , with $c_{u}=0.5$ , $c_{o}=0.05$ , and $c_{w}=0.005$ .

In the aggregate teacher reward these terms are weighted $3.0\,r^{\text{dir}}_{t}+5.0\,r^{\text{dist}}_{t}+0.5\,r^{\text{force}}_{t}$ .

## Appendix G Limitations

Fixed-view, passive perception

The student observes a single forward-facing depth camera and does not control where it looks. Under heavy self-occlusion the partner can leave the field of view entirely, and the policy cannot actively reposition the camera to recover the missing state. Coupling the interaction policy with active head or gaze control, so the robot moves to keep the relevant contact region in view, is a natural extension.

Limited dexterity

REACT drives the body through HOMIE locomotion commands plus residual upper-body joint offsets and treats the hands as rigid end effectors. It does not actuate fingers, so interactions that require grasping, fine in-hand manipulation, or force-sensitive hand shaping, such as a firm but gentle hand-hold or adjusting a grip, are out of scope. Extending the action space to dexterous hands is needed for this class of contact.

Simple interaction repertoire

We evaluate on five short, largely upper-body interactions (hug, high-five, handshake, link-arms, pat-on-back). More complex and longer-horizon behaviors that involve sustained multi-contact load bearing and tighter coupling to the partner’s intent are beyond what the current contact reward and demonstration pipeline target, and scaling to them is left to future work.

Abstract.

Multimodal large language models (LLMs) are increasingly deployed for structured information extraction from financial documents, yet their failure modes under visual ambiguity remain poorly understood. This paper investigates a previously underexplored failure mode in vision-language models for financial document processing, which we term CARLAR (Courtesy Amount Recognition and Legal Amount Recognition) influence. In cheque understanding tasks, multimodal models can return agreeing monetary fields even where the numeric annotations disagree after a blank annotation is encoded as zero, producing outputs that appear internally consistent and may evade standard discrepancy checks. Through a systematic evaluation across ten visual prompting strategies applied to GPT-4.1 and Claude 4.5 Sonnet — evaluated on 243 primary cheques (124 with two observed unequal amounts) and 598 strict mismatches from two other datasets — with bootstrap confidence intervals and formal significance testing, we show that CARLAR rates reach 73.7% [68.3, 79.0] under courtesy redaction ( $p<0.001$ vs. baseline), and that LLM confidence scores can remain high when the predicted fields agree (Expected Calibration Error up to 0.93 in CARLAR cases). To address this, we propose a lightweight compressed image few-shot remedy that encodes structured example tables as compact visual context without requiring model fine-tuning, improving Claude’s joint extraction accuracy by up to 11.5 percentage points [6.2, 16.9] (paired, adjusted $p=0.00054$ ) on the 243-case cohort; the strict-124 Control gain is smaller and not significant after correction. The effect on predicted-field agreement remains model-dependent, decreasing for Claude but increasing for GPT-4.1. These findings underscore the need for context-aware multimodal validation strategies in high-stakes document processing workflows.

Keywords: 
Document understanding, multimodal learning, LLM calibration, hallucination, financial document extraction, confidence estimation

## 1. Introduction

The automation of financial document processing has long been a critical application domain for AI systems. Cheque processing, in particular, represents a multi-billion-dollar annual operation across financial institutions. Cheques contain two critical amount fields: (1) the courtesy amount, printed digitally on the lower-right corner (numerals), and (2) the legal amount, written in words on the center of the cheque. These fields are legally required to match; discrepancies are red flags for fraud or processing errors.

Old neural approaches (Xu et al., 2020) have helped improve accuracy in automation pipeline. While modern OCR solutions such as Azure OCR are capable of operating across diverse document types, they function primarily as transcription tools — converting visual text into machine-readable characters without inherent understanding of document semantics, field relationships, or contextual meaning. Extracting structured information from unstructured financial documents using OCR therefore still requires carefully engineered downstream pipelines: template matching, rule-based field localization, and explicit validation logic that must be maintained and updated as document formats evolve.

The emergence of multimodal Large Language Models (such as GPT-4.1 (OpenAI, 2025) and Claude 4.5 Sonnet (Anthropic, 2025)), which combine vision and language understanding, has enabled directly interpreting layouts and extracting values rather than transcribing text and deferring interpretation to hand-crafted rules. These models jointly reason over visual layout and linguistic content to directly infer structured field values — understanding, for instance, that a spelled-out word amount and a numeric amount on a cheque represent the same underlying value and should be consistent. This semantic awareness allows a single model to generalize across diverse document formats without domain-specific engineering.

We study a failure signature we term CARLAR influence: equal predicted amounts where numeric annotations differ, including when a blank raw annotation is encoded as zero. A model may infer one amount from the other when visual evidence is ambiguous, but prediction agreement alone does not establish that mechanism. In banks, when numerically and verbally written amounts conflict, the legal amount (in words) prevails; an extraction that makes the two fields appear equal could conceal such a discrepancy.

Selective classification provides a general mechanism for deferring uncertain predictions for review (Geifman and El-Yaniv, 2017). In cheque extraction, routing low-confidence predictions or discrepancies between courtesy and legal amounts to a human operator is one possible safeguard, not a bank policy or numerical threshold established by this study. Our findings show why either signal alone can miss errors: LLMs can assign high confidence to incorrect outputs, and CARLAR cases may yield matching predictions that do not trigger discrepancy-based review.

Incorrect amount extraction carries direct financial, regulatory, and reputational risk, motivating robust validation mechanisms beyond confidence thresholding. Collectively, these risks underscore the importance of robust validation mechanisms in AI-driven payment automation systems.

To address these issues, this paper makes the following contributions:

(1) 

Empirical Characterization of CARLAR Influence: We systematically measure predicted-field agreement across ten visual presentation strategies on 243 cheques, including a strict sensitivity analysis on the 124 cases with two observed unequal amounts. With courtesy redacted and legal visible, agreement increases to 73.7% [68.3, 79.0] (GPT-4.1) and 49.0% [42.8, 55.6] (Claude 4.5).

(2) 

Failure of Confidence-Based Defenses: Using formal calibration metrics (ECE, Brier score, risk-coverage curves, and selective accuracy), we demonstrate that LLM confidence scores are poor proxies for extraction fidelity, with the model maintaining high confidence on CARLAR-affected outputs. This challenges the assumption that confidence thresholding alone suffices for safe deployment.

(3) 

Compressed Image $n$ -Shot Remedy: We evaluate structured example tables as compact visual context; the strongest paired joint-accuracy gain is 11.5 percentage points [6.2, 16.9] for Claude 4.5 with Control examples (BH-adjusted $p=0.00054$ ), without model fine-tuning. GPT-4.1’s Prediction-Guided and Comprehensive changes are not significant in the 243-case six-test family, while Claude’s Control gain is not significant on the smaller strict-124 subset after separate adjustment. The example sets’ effect on measured CARLAR is model-dependent: the verified example sets reduce predicted-field agreement for Claude 4.5 but increase it for GPT-4.1, both in the primary cohort and in the strict two-field subset. These observations do not identify the causal direction of any individual prediction.

(4) 

Multimodal Error Analysis: We show that CARLAR influence varies with visual context and input representation choices. Redacting courtesy while preserving legal produces substantially more predicted-field agreement than the reverse intervention; Relative Deviation Asymmetry (RDA) characterizes numeric drift toward the other annotated amount that exact-match metrics miss. Per-sample persistence profiling across all ten experiments shows that 70% of samples are condition-sensitive rather than intrinsically vulnerable.

## 2. Related Work

## 2.1. LLMs for Document Understanding

Document-VQA benchmarks such as DocVQA (Mathew et al., 2021) established evaluation tasks for document images before recent multimodal LLM extraction. Complementary foundations include few-shot in-context learning in language models (Brown et al., 2020), text-to-text transfer learning (Raffel et al., 2020), and learned prompts for CLIP-like vision-language models (Zhou et al., 2022). These studies do not directly evaluate cheque extraction or the failure modes that arise when related visual fields disagree.

The term “hallucination” in LLM literature refers to generating factually incorrect or unsupported outputs (Huang et al., 2025). Object hallucination is a documented failure in large vision-language models: generated descriptions can name objects absent from an image (Li et al., 2023). In financial LLM evaluation, objective bias can arise when training or evaluation rewards confident completion rather than calibrated uncertainty and abstention (Kong et al., 2026). A similar pressure could contribute to unsupported agreement between cheque fields, but neither that finance-evaluation study nor the CARLAR metric establishes such a mechanism in visual question answering. Predicted-field agreement is consistent with a possible relational error but can also reflect other errors or common defaults. Our single-pass experiments show condition sensitivity rather than determinism: we did not repeat identical image–prompt inputs to measure within-condition variability.

Task-aware image captions have been used to provide relevant visual context for language-model VQA (Hu et al., 2023), and prompting techniques have been studied for zero- and few-shot VQA (Awal et al., 2023). These works motivate studying how context affects extraction, but do not evaluate our compressed example-table format or cheque-specific field agreement.

Confidence-based review has limitations. Verbalized uncertainty in language and vision-language models can be overconfident (Groot and Valdenegro-Toro, 2024), although language models can sometimes assess whether their responses are correct (Kadavath et al., 2022). TruthfulQA documents false answers that reflect common human misconceptions (Lin et al., 2022); calibration of image classifiers is a distinct research setting (Minderer et al., 2021), not direct evidence about LLM self-reported confidence. LLM-as-judge assessments can also diverge from human evaluations (Thakur et al., 2025).

To our knowledge, no prior work has systematically studied hallucination modes in LLM-based cheque processing specifically, nor explored how additional image context of few-shot examples can contribute. In addition to covering these gaps, our paper also proposed new defense mechanisms tailored to financial workflows beyond the conventional practice of having additional humans in the loop or LLMs as judge.

## 3. Proposed Intervention: A Compressed Image Few-Shot Remedy

A natural question is whether CARLAR influence could be mitigated
using text-only few-shot prompting. However, CARLAR arises specifically
under visual ambiguity — illegible handwriting, occlusion, or poor
scans — conditions that cannot be faithfully represented in text without
resolving the ambiguity or introducing artificial placeholders. Our
compressed image few-shot approach instead preserves the original visual
signal while providing structured contextual examples, ensuring mitigation remains grounded in the multimodal setting where CARLAR occurs.

To mitigate CARLAR influence without model fine-tuning, we propose a lightweight compressed image few-shot remedy that incorporates structured examples as compact visual context.

Step 1 — Example Selection: We first identify a set of $n$ representative examples to guide the model. These examples are chosen based on their relevance to the target task and the specific failure modes of interest.

Step 2 — Example Compression: As a design motivation, we explore whether the selected examples can be supplied as one visual table rather than as $n$ separate image inputs. We organize them into a structured table, where each row corresponds to an example and columns capture relevant fields (e.g., courtesy amount, legal amount, and ground truth). We then convert this table into a single image by capturing a screenshot of the first page, which contains all $n$ examples (Figure 8). This representation provides compact visual context without establishing a resource saving.

Step 3 — Multimodal Integration: During inference, we prepend the table screenshot as an additional input to the multimodal LLM, followed by the query document. This lets the model condition on multiple examples from one table image. We did not benchmark token usage, billed API charges, or latency against separate example images; potential efficiency is a motivation, not a measured result.

## 4. Design

## 4.1. Dataset

We evaluate our approach using three publicly available cheque datasets.

Primary dataset. The handwritten cheque VQA dataset introduced by aniketVerma07 (Verma, 2024) contains 3,312 question–answer pairs spanning cheque formats from the U.S., Singapore, and India. The original amount-query filter selected 252 rows from its 3,000-row train split; nine corresponded to non-cheque documents and are excluded from all reported cheque analyses. Of the 243 remaining cheques, 124 have two nonblank, unequal amount annotations; the other 119 have a blank raw annotation (72 courtesy, 47 legal), represented as zero for evaluation. An audit found no nonblank annotations that failed the amount parser among these 119. A blank annotation does not establish that the corresponding writing is physically absent from every image. We report the 243-case unequal-or-blank-annotation cohort for detailed experiments and the strict-124 subset separately for comparability with the other datasets. This is a selected subset of the dataset’s train split, not its held-out test split.

Cross-dataset validation. To assess whether CARLAR generalizes beyond a single dataset, we additionally evaluate the Experiment 1 (Full Image) baseline on two independent cheque corpora:

• 

arunchincheit (Arunchincheit, 2024): 2,500 images of Indian bank cheques (ICICI, AXIS, SBI, HSBC), yielding 200 mismatched samples after filtering.

• 

shivalikasingh (Singh, 2024): 2,800 images of Indian bank cheques, yielding 398 mismatched samples after filtering.

Together these three datasets provide 841 evaluated cheques: 243 primary unequal-or-blank-annotation cases and 598 external cases with two observed unequal amounts. The strict subset contains 722 two-field mismatches across all three datasets (124 + 200 + 398). The detailed visual experiments and compressed image few-shot comparisons use the primary 243 cheques; the two additional Indian cheque corpora are evaluated only for the full-image baseline. Cross-dataset interpretation uses the strict primary subset, rather than treating blank raw annotations as equivalent to two observed conflicting amounts.

Although these subsets are small and domain-specific by design, predicted-field agreement is particularly concerning when two amounts are known to differ. The primary cohort also includes cheques with a blank raw annotation; these evaluate responses to an unlabeled field under the zero convention, not substitution between two observed annotated amounts. These cases reflect ambiguity patterns encountered in proprietary banking workflows where CARLAR was originally identified. Due to confidentiality constraints, we cannot release internal datasets, but the public subsets serve as representative, reproducible testbeds rather than claims of broad coverage.

## 4.2. Method

We design ten experiments to systematically explore the conditions under which CARLAR influence emerges. Each experiment varies the visual presentation of the cheque and the extraction strategy.

1) Full Image: The unmodified 512 $\times$ 256 px cheque image. This serves as baseline.

2) Annotated Both: Full image with red bounding box around legal amount (center, word-based) and green bounding box around courtesy amount (lower-right, numeric). Provides unambiguous spatial guidance via color.

3) Annotated Separate: Two separate calls—one with red box on legal region only (prompt: “extract legal amount”), one with red box on courtesy region only (prompt: “extract courtesy amount”). Tests whether isolating each extraction reduces CARLAR influence.

4) Cropped Separate: Two separate calls—one with tight crop of legal region, one with tight crop of courtesy region. Tests whether removing visual context (other fields, cheque borders) helps focus attention. Cropping coordinates: Legal (0, 80, 430, 145), Courtesy (350, 95, 510, 150).

5) Cropped Courtesy + Full Legal: Asymmetric—courtesy amount extracted from cropped image, legal amount from full image. Tests whether different fields benefit differently from cropping.

6) Cropped Legal + Full Courtesy: Inverse asymmetry.

7) Redacted Separate: Two separate calls—one receives full image with legal region blacked out (prompt: “ignore the black box”), one receives full image with courtesy region blacked out. Tests whether providing context while redacting the relevant field helps.

8) Redacted Courtesy + Full Legal: Asymmetric redaction—courtesy extracted from redacted image, legal from full image.

9) Redacted Legal + Full Courtesy: Asymmetric redaction—inverse.

10) Gridlines: Full image with 16 $\times$ 8 numbered grid overlay (128 cells, $\approx 32\times 32$ px each). Prompt explicitly specifies which grid cells contain each amount. Tests textual spatial guidance vs. visual/color-based guidance.

For model selection, we chose to evaluate two state-of-the-art multimodal LLMs: GPT-4.1 and Claude 4.5 Sonnet. Both models support multimodal in-context learning and have demonstrated strong document understanding capabilities in preliminary tests. We were not able to run Gemini for ablations due to our firm’s closer commercial relationships with Anthropic and OpenAI.

For each experiment, we craft scenario-specific prompts to extract courtesy and legal amounts into JSON; confidence is elicited in separate confidence experiments. We evaluate field accuracy and predicted-field agreement on the 243 selected cheque images. Agreement on cases with two known unequal amounts is a stronger signal of cross-field substitution than agreement when one raw annotation is blank; neither measure alone proves that one amount was copied from the other.

Let $C_{i}^{\ast}$ and $L_{i}^{\ast}$ denote the ground-truth courtesy and legal amount fields for row $i$ , respectively, and let $\hat{C}_{i}$ and $\hat{L}_{i}$ denote the corresponding model predictions.

 $\mathrm{CARLAR\%}=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{S}|}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{S}}\mathbf{1}\!\left[\,\hat{C}_{i}=\hat{L}_{i}\,\right]$ 

where $\mathcal{S}$ contains 243 primary cheques whose annotated values differ after blank raw annotations are encoded as zero. The 124-case strict subset requires both original annotations to be present and unequal; external datasets use that same criterion. Each cohort-specific rate uses its own stated cohort size as the denominator.

This formulation measures unexpected agreement between the two predicted fields, complementing ordinary prediction-error metrics. Standard metrics such as partial string match, edit distance, or numeric deviation are unsuitable for this purpose because they quantify proximity to ground truth, not dependency between fields. To illustrate why this distinction matters, consider a case where the ground-truth legal amount is 20 and the courtesy amount is 17, but the model predicts the courtesy amount as 20 — for instance, due to illegible handwriting causing the model to default to the legal field. A numeric deviation metric would treat this prediction as plausible, since 20 is close to 17, and would fail to distinguish it from ordinary extraction noise. CARLAR, by contrast, flags agreement between predictions ( $\hat{C}_{i}=\hat{L}_{i}$ ) despite unequal numeric ground truth, including blank annotations encoded as zero ( $C_{i}^{\ast}\neq L_{i}^{\ast}$ under the zero convention). It is a diagnostic indicator of possible cross-field contamination; the strict-subset analysis isolates cases where both ground-truth amounts are actually observed.

We additionally define CARLAR-NZ (Non-Zero CARLAR) as the percentage of the same eligible cohort for which the model predicts equal, nonzero values for both fields:

(1)

 $\mathrm{CARLAR\text{-}NZ\%}=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{S}|}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{S}}\mathbf{1}\!\left[\hat{C}_{i}=\hat{L}_{i}\neq 0\right].$ 

Unlike a rate conditional on two nonzero predictions, this measure has the same denominator as CARLAR%. It removes agreement caused by two zero predictions, which may reflect abstention or a blank annotation rather than a nonzero assertion. Nonzero agreement is stronger evidence of a committed prediction, but does not on its own establish the direction or cause of substitution.

To move beyond binary detection of cross-field agreement, we introduce a complementary fine-grained metric.

RDA (Relative Deviation Asymmetry).
CARLAR captures only exact agreement between predicted fields; partial drift — where the model nudges one prediction toward the other field’s value without fully copying it — goes undetected. To capture this gradient, we define:

 $\mathrm{RDA}_{i}=\frac{\hat{C}_{i}-C_{i}^{\ast}}{L_{i}^{\ast}-C_{i}^{\ast}}$ 

computed over non-CARLAR samples with non-zero predictions and $L_{i}^{\ast}\neq C_{i}^{\ast}$ . An RDA of 0 means no drift (courtesy prediction matches its ground truth); an RDA of 1 means the courtesy prediction has drifted fully to the legal ground truth value. A positive RDA indicates that a courtesy prediction lies toward the legal ground-truth amount on the numeric scale; we use a Wilcoxon signed-rank test to assess departure from zero, without interpreting it as direct evidence of copying.

## 4.3. Statistical Methods

Because our per-dataset evaluation sets are modest ( $n=243$ for the primary cohort, $n=124$ for its strict subset, and $n=200$ and $n=398$ for cross-dataset validation), we supplement the visual-experiment CARLAR rates with 95% bootstrap confidence intervals (10,000 resamples, percentile method) and use formal hypothesis testing to avoid over-interpreting small differences.

Pairwise comparisons.
For paired binary outcomes (correct/incorrect, CARLAR/non-CARLAR)
across two experimental conditions on the same 243 cheques, we use
McNemar’s test (exact binomial variant when the number of discordant
pairs $<25$ ).
For GPT-4.1 versus Claude 4.5 within each visual condition, we align the same 243 cheque IDs and apply the exact two-sided McNemar/binomial test to the discordant CARLAR outcomes. This uses image-level pairing rather than treating the models’ predictions as independent samples.
Confidence-score distributions (LLM-as-Judge vs. Azure OCR) are
compared with the Wilcoxon signed-rank test.
The 68 visual-condition, model-comparison, and confidence-distribution tests form one Benjamini–Hochberg-adjusted family. For six model-by-example-set comparisons of joint accuracy against prompt-v1, we align the same 243 cheque IDs, bootstrap paired correctness differences (10,000 resamples), use exact two-sided McNemar/binomial tests on discordant pairs, and adjust those six $p$ -values as a separate Benjamini–Hochberg family. We repeat these six comparisons on the 124 strict two-field mismatches as a separate exploratory sensitivity family, with its own six-test adjustment. Raw and adjusted test results are retained in the analysis output. RDA Wilcoxon tests are exploratory and unadjusted, not multiplicity-controlled evidence.

Calibration metrics.
To formally evaluate whether model-reported confidence tracks
extraction correctness, we compute the Expected Calibration
Error (ECE) (Naeini et al., 2015; Guo et al., 2017) with 10 equal-width bins for the full 243-case cohort and five bins for the smaller run-specific CARLAR subsets, and the Brier score (Brier, 1950)
 $\mathrm{BS}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i}(c_{i}-y_{i})^{2}$ , where $c_{i}$ is
the reported confidence (scaled to $[0,1]$ ) and $y_{i}\in\{0,1\}$ is
the binary correctness indicator. The CARLAR-only cohorts in Table 6 contain 22–55 observations per model and confidence method; their five-bin ECE values are descriptive and cannot be interpreted as directly comparable changes from ten-bin overall ECE.
We further report risk-coverage curves and
selective accuracy at key confidence thresholds to
quantify how effectively confidence can filter unreliable
extractions.

For evaluation of the compressed image few-shot remedy, we construct three example table variants, each drawn from the broader public dataset (3,000-row train split of the Hugging Face cheque VQA corpus). The example image IDs were checked against the 243 evaluation image IDs and had no intersection; each API call receives a single static table image prepended to the query cheque.

(1) 

Control Example Set ( $n{=}10$ ): Contains ground-truth amounts, model predictions, and human reasoning for 10 cheques — 5 with matching courtesy/legal amounts and 5 with mismatched amounts — providing diverse extraction patterns without targeting specific failure modes (Figure 8).

(2) 

Prediction-Guided Example Set (Type 1) ( $n{=}7$ ): Contains cheque images, model predictions, confidence scores (1–5 scale), and reasoning for seven examples with varying visual clarity. It omits ground-truth amounts, testing whether prediction-and-feedback context alone changes extraction. This is distinct from the separate Error-Similar confidence-elicitation prompts discussed later.

(3) 

Comprehensive Example Set ( $n{=}5$ ): Contains ground-truth amounts, model predictions, confidence scores, and reasoning for 5 cheques with mismatched amounts, providing the richest per-example context.

Because all examples must fit in a single legible table image (Step 2), the number of examples $n$ varies inversely with per-example information density: the Comprehensive variant (5 columns per row) accommodates $n{=}5$ , while the Control variant (4 columns, shorter reasoning) fits $n{=}10$ . This reflects the practical breadth-vs-depth tradeoff inherent to the compressed-image approach — richer per-example context necessarily reduces the number of examples that remain legible in one screenshot.

Cross-dataset generalizability.
The strict-primary baseline (9.7% GPT, 4.0% Claude; $n=124$ ) uses the same two-observed-unequal-amount criterion as the two independent Indian cheque datasets ( $n=200$ and $n=398$ ). The latter have CARLAR rates of 5.0–6.0% for GPT-4.1 and 4.0–4.8% for Claude 4.5. These baseline results show that predicted-field agreement is not restricted to the primary dataset, although the wider primary cohort (5.3% GPT, 5.8% Claude; $n=243$ ) includes blank raw annotations and is not directly comparable. We have not evaluated the intervention or fine-grained ablations on the external cohorts.

From Table 1, we note several findings within the primary dataset’s detailed ablations. CARLAR rates depend strongly on which field is obscured: redacting courtesy while showing legal yields 49.0% for Claude and 73.7% for GPT (roughly 8–14 $\times$ their inclusive baselines), whereas the reverse yields 5.8% and 6.2% (Exps 8–9). McNemar’s test finds Exp 8 significantly different from baseline for both models ( $p<0.001$ , Benjamini–Hochberg corrected), while Exp 9 is not (adjusted $p>0.05$ ). A paired exact McNemar test also finds a between-model difference in Exp 8 (94 GPT-only versus 34 Claude-only CARLAR cases; Benjamini–Hochberg-adjusted $p=2.38\times 10^{-7}$ within the 68-test family). On the strict subset, Exp 8 yields 52.4% and 71.0% agreement, respectively, so the redaction effect is not solely due to blank-annotation cases. These rates do not by themselves establish which ground-truth amount was copied in each case.

Ruling out alternative explanations.
A natural concern is whether predicted field agreement could arise from mechanisms other than cross-field inference — for example, correlated OCR-level errors, normalization to a shared default, or independent reliance on shared contextual cues (layout, borders, background). Experiments 4–9 vary the visibility and visual presentation of the amount fields, allowing us to compare how predicted-field agreement changes across cropping and redaction conditions without treating those interventions as perfectly controlled counterfactuals.

Correlated OCR errors. If both fields suffered correlated pixel-level misreads, the CARLAR rate should remain roughly constant regardless of whether the other field is visible in the input. Yet Experiment 5 (courtesy extracted from a tight crop with no legal field visible) yields only 0.4–1.6% CARLAR, whereas the full-image baseline (Exp 1) shows 5.3–5.8%. More strikingly, Experiment 8 (courtesy redacted but legal fully visible) drives CARLAR to 49.0–73.7%. The large changes in agreement rates across these conditions are difficult to attribute to independent pixel-level reading errors alone, although the conditions also change how the image is presented.

Normalization to a shared default. If both fields were independently normalized to a common value (e.g., rounding or format coercion), agreement rates should be roughly symmetric with respect to which field is visible. Instead, Exp 8 (legal visible, courtesy redacted) yields 49.0–73.7%, while Exp 9 (courtesy visible, legal redacted) yields only 5.8–6.2%. This asymmetry is not what field-independent normalization alone would predict, but the agreement metric does not identify which ground-truth value, if either, was copied.

Shared contextual cues. To isolate whether shared visual context (cheque borders, layout, background patterns) independently anchors both predictions to the same value, we compare cropping (which removes all surrounding context) against redaction (which preserves it). Experiment 5 (courtesy cropped, no surrounding context) and Experiment 8 (courtesy redacted, full surrounding context preserved) both deny the model direct visual access to the courtesy amount. Yet CARLAR jumps from 0.4–1.6% to 49.0–73.7%. Experiment 8 leaves the legal field visible in a full image, while Experiment 5 removes it from the courtesy crop; the difference is consistent with cross-field influence but is also confounded by cropping versus redaction.

We acknowledge that these manipulations approximate rather than perfectly implement pixel-level counterfactual controls: cropping changes image dimensions, and redaction introduces a salient black region that may itself influence model behavior (as discussed in our cropping-vs-redaction analysis below). Nevertheless, the converging evidence across three distinct manipulation types supports the interpretation that access to the other field can influence predicted-field agreement, while the redaction and cropping conditions do not isolate every possible confound.

Secondly, bounding box annotations do not seem to be effective in mitigating CARLAR. Experiment 2 (legal annotated with a red bounding box, courtesy with a green bounding box) shows higher CARLAR agreement (9.9% for Claude and 14.4% for GPT) than baseline (5.8% and 5.3%, respectively). Even when the two fields are distinguished by colour, explicitly highlighting both may prime the model to assume they should match.

Thirdly, cropping out fields seems to be an unstable way of mitigating CARLAR. Experiments 4 (separate crops) and 5 (courtesy cropped, legal full image) show lower CARLAR rates (0.4–5.3%), but Experiment 6 (legal cropped, courtesy full image) shows 15.2% (Claude) and 16.0% (GPT). The difference suggests that the field chosen for cropping and the context retained for each extraction matter; it does not establish which field supplied the agreed value.

Fourthly, we observe that redaction triggers CARLAR influence more than cropping. This may be due to redaction (black box) preserving visual context (full cheque layout, borders, fields); the model knows a field exists but is obscured, triggering inference. On the other hand, cropping removes context entirely, so the model may not even be aware that a field is missing; it simply processes the cropped region. Thus, redaction seems to cue the model to infer missing information, whereas cropping suggests the presented region constitutes the entire context.

Moreover, gridlines do not mitigate CARLAR in this setting. Experiment 10 (gridlines with textual cell references) yields 24.3% CARLAR for Claude and 39.1% for GPT, higher than both the full-image baseline (5.8% and 5.3%) and the annotated-both condition (9.9% and 14.4%), though lower than the courtesy-redacted condition (49.0% and 73.7%). These rates do not support a claim that gridline-based textual spatial guidance is more robust than color-coded bounding boxes.

Lastly, we explore the ordering and length of amount descriptions in the extraction prompt. Among four prompt variants on the same 243 cheques, Shorter Courtesy has the lowest CARLAR rate for GPT-4.1 (2.1%, 5 cases) and is tied for the lowest rate for Claude (5.8%, 14 cases). Legal First has the highest rate for GPT-4.1 (6.2%, 15 cases), but not for Claude, whose rates range from 5.8% to 7.0%. These descriptive differences suggest that prompt wording can matter, but do not establish a common sequence effect across models.

## 5.3. Fine-Grained Cross-Field Influence Analysis

The binary CARLAR metric detects exact predicted-field agreement. We now apply RDA and persistence analysis to characterize partial drift and sensitivity to experimental conditions.

Partial drift (RDA).
CARLAR captures only exact agreement between fields; a courtesy prediction can also lie partway toward the annotated legal value without matching the predicted legal amount. RDA measures this gradient: a value of 0 means no drift, while 1 means the courtesy prediction has shifted entirely to the legal ground truth (Figure 5).

At baseline (Exp 1), neither model shows evidence of systematic drift in this exploratory analysis: GPT-4.1 has mean RDA 0.023 (unadjusted Wilcoxon $p=0.155$ ), and Claude 4.5 has mean 0.040 (unadjusted $p=0.469$ ). Under Experiment 7 (both fields redacted separately), the median RDA reaches 1.0 for both models (unadjusted $p<0.001$ ), even among non-CARLAR predictions. These RDA tests were not included in the pairwise Benjamini–Hochberg family. RDA uses the blank-annotation-as-zero convention of the primary analysis; its values describe numeric proximity, not proof of copying from the other field.

Figure 5. Distribution of Relative Deviation Asymmetry (RDA) for courtesy predictions in Experiment 1 (Full Image baseline). RDA = 0 indicates no drift from courtesy ground truth; RDA = 1 indicates full drift to the legal ground truth value. Black vertical line marks the median. Sample sizes differ between models because the RDA filter excludes CARLAR cases (exact agreement) and zero predictions, which occur at different rates for each model.

CARLAR persistence.
A natural question is whether CARLAR is a property of specific cheque images or of the experimental condition applied to them. Across the same 243 cheques in all ten experiments, 73.3% of GPT cases and 68.7% of Claude cases show CARLAR in only one to three conditions. No cheque exhibits CARLAR in eight or more conditions for either model. This pattern suggests that agreement often depends on the image–condition interaction rather than persisting uniformly across inputs.

The transition heatmap (Figure 6) shows asymmetric overlap between conditions. For GPT, 76.9% of baseline CARLAR cases also exhibit CARLAR in Exp 8, but only 5.6% of Exp 8 CARLAR cases do so at baseline; for Claude these conditional proportions are 42.9% and 5.0%. Redacting courtesy therefore elicits agreement in many cheques that do not exhibit it under full-image prompting.

## 6. Conclusion

This work shows that multimodal LLMs can return agreeing amount predictions under visual ambiguity even when both annotations disagree or one raw annotation is blank. We call this measurable failure signature CARLAR influence; its possible mechanisms, including cross-field inference, warrant further testing before a causal interpretation. Our experiments reveal three principal findings.

First, unexpected predicted-field agreement occurs across three cheque datasets: among 722 strict two-field mismatches, full-image baseline rates range from 4.0% to 9.7%; the primary 243-cheque cohort also includes 119 blank-annotation cases. Redacting courtesy increases agreement sharply even on the strict subset (71.0% GPT, 52.4% Claude). RDA shows that non-CARLAR predictions can exhibit partial numeric drift, while 73.3% of GPT cheques and 68.7% of Claude cheques exhibit CARLAR in only one to three of the ten visual conditions. These findings concern sensitivity to input presentation, not a demonstrated copying mechanism in every case.

Second, the three verified compressed-image variants do not help uniformly. Claude’s Control condition gives the largest paired gain on all 243 cheques, 11.5 percentage points [6.2, 16.9] (six-test BH-adjusted $p=0.00054$ ); its strict-124 gain is 8.1 points with adjusted $p=0.105$ . GPT’s Prediction-Guided Type-1 variant does not improve joint accuracy on the full cohort, whereas GPT’s strict Control gain is 23.4 points [14.5, 32.3] (adjusted $p<0.00001$ ). Predicted-field agreement falls for Claude but rises for GPT-4.1 across all three variants. We do not claim a universal CARLAR countermeasure.

Third, LLM-as-Judge confidence does not reliably filter courtesy errors at the tested threshold, whereas OCR confidence improves selective courtesy accuracy within its own extraction run. Whether either method detects CARLAR cases specifically remains untested; purpose-built calibration and detection for multi-field extraction remain open challenges.

Overall, reliable deployment of multimodal LLMs in high-stakes domains requires context-aware prompting, principled input design, and validation mechanisms that account for cross-field dependencies and model-specific biases.

## 7. Future Directions

Several promising directions emerge from this work. Our baseline cross-dataset evaluation observes predicted-field agreement in three cheque corpora spanning U.S., Singaporean, and Indian formats (722 strict mismatches in total), supporting limited within-domain generalizability. CARLAR-like influence likely extends further to other structured extraction tasks with correlated fields — invoices, forms, or medical records — and to a broader range of document formats and models. Wider geographic coverage could reveal whether CARLAR’s persistence and the remedy’s effectiveness are format-dependent, given that models may carry inherent biases toward the formats they were trained on. Additionally, access to open-source models was constrained by our institution’s existing LLM infrastructure; future work extending this analysis to open-source models would enable direct fine-tuning and weight-level investigation of the underlying mechanism.

We acknowledge several limitations in the current example-selection design. As noted in Section 4.2, the single-image constraint couples $n$ with content type (the breadth-vs-depth tradeoff), so their effects are not independently isolated. Example selection was not randomized or repeated: a single fixed set of cheques was used per condition. Example and evaluation image IDs were compared and had no overlap, even though both were drawn from the broader public train split. Randomized example selection from a separately held-out pool with repeated draws would provide stronger evidence about generality. All three example-set conditions have matched-ID prediction outputs, but they differ in content as well as number; observed model-specific CARLAR effects therefore cannot be attributed to either factor in isolation.

A further direction is a systematic ablation over the number of examples $n$ in the compressed image few-shot remedy, disentangling it from content variation. Too few examples may under-specify the desired reasoning pattern, while too many may exceed the model’s effective context capacity or introduce noise; given that GPT-4.1 and Claude 4.5 already respond differently to example selection strategy, their sensitivity to $n$ likely also differs. Future work should ablate $n$ across prediction-guided, control, and comprehensive example sets while holding content type fixed, to measure its isolated effect on joint accuracy, CARLAR, and CARLAR-NZ, and to determine whether performance gains plateau quickly or scale with richer example sets. Such analysis would inform principled example-count selection for deploying this remedy reliably in production settings.

Finally, improving confidence calibration and example selection for structured extraction tasks remain open challenges. Existing calibration techniques are largely designed for classification settings and may not transfer effectively to multi-field generation with inter-field dependencies, while the verified prediction-guided, control, and comprehensive variants have different model-specific effects — motivating retrieval strategies that balance diversity, relevance, and stability. Jointly addressing calibration and example selection could meaningfully improve both accuracy and deployment safety in real-world financial workflows.

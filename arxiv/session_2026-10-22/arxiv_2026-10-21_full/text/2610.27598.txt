Abstract

As model parameters scale up, sparsely activated Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) approaches have become essential for model scaling. However, mainstream models currently adopt hierarchical architectures, where experts in different layers, although topologically independent, exhibit substantial similarity and redundancy in their stored content. Compared to the hierarchically arranged Local Expert in existing mainstream models, we hypothesize that inter-layer shared Meta Expert hold greater potential.
To this end, we conduct a multi-dimensional empirical comparison between Local and Meta Experts, and find that models incorporating Meta Experts exhibit lower parameter redundancy, together with lower cross-entropy loss and higher downstream task accuracy. With the activated quantity and the expert granularity held fixed, our model matches the performance of Vanilla MoE using only 70–80% of the experts, substantially improving the model’s scaling potential.
Beyond removing inter-layer redundancy among experts and improving information storage efficiency, our theoretical analysis further shows that, compared with Local Experts, Meta Experts provide hidden states with more flexible residual pathways, thereby improving information retrieval efficiency.

## 1 Introduction

Figure 1: Comparison between Vanilla and Meta-Expert models. All models are
trained under the optimal setting of the Vanilla model, at varying depths
and scales. Models with Meta Experts achieve comparable loss to the Vanilla
baselines with only 70–80% as many experts, or 75–90% as many
parameters, and the savings in both experts and parameters grow as the
models get deeper and larger, indicating the favorable scalability of Meta

In recent years, large language models (LLMs) have gradually approached or even surpassed human-level ability in general text, mathematics, and code (Brown et al., 2020; OpenAI et al., 2024). This progress owes much to the increasing scale of model parameters and training data: intelligence has been improved substantially as both factors grow (Kaplan et al., 2020; Hoffmann et al., 2022b).

To enable ever-larger models, parameter sparsification has become an important approach (Shazeer et al., 2017; Fedus et al., 2022; Muennighoff et al., 2025). As the module that accounts for the vast majority of model parameters, the feed-forward network was originally a densely computed block. (Vaswani et al., 2017a). However, as model size grows, such dense computation gradually becomes prohibitively expensive, creating efficiency bottlenecks for both training and inference (Lepikhin et al., 2021; Fedus et al., 2022). Meanwhile, although feed-forward networks are densely computed, only a sparse subset of parameters is actually activated for a given token during its forward pass (Li et al., 2023; Mirzadeh et al., 2024); the remaining inactive parameters still participate in the computation, leading to substantial waste.

To address this, researchers have proposed designing feed-forward networks with a natively sparse-activation structure, most notably the Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) (Shazeer et al., 2017; Fedus et al., 2022). MoE splits the feed-forward network into multiple sub-networks along the intermediate dimension and routes each token to only a limited number of them before computation. This design endows the model with a large total parameter count while ensuring that each token’s forward pass computation uses only a small fraction of parameters, reconciling scale with efficiency.

With the advent of increasingly sparse MoE models, parameter efficiency along the width dimension has been steadily improved (Dai et al., 2024). In this work, however, we ask how parameter efficiency can be improved along the depth dimension (Raposo et al., 2024). On the one hand, interpretability studies suggest that feed-forward networks store relatively static knowledge (Geva et al., 2021; Dai et al., 2022), and that different layers may store similar knowledge, suggesting nontrivial redundancy across layers (Meng et al., 2022; Takase and Kiyono, 2023; Fan et al., 2019; Chen et al., 2025; Men et al., 2025). On the other hand,
Looped Transformers reuse the same parameters across recurrent applications
of a block (Dehghani et al., 2018; Giannou et al., 2023; Bai et al., 2019; Csordás et al., 2024; Chen et al., 2026a),and recent work
shows that this design can attain the reasoning performance of much larger
models with far fewer parameters (Bae et al., 2025; Saunshi et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2026). This benefit, however, comes with
additional recursions and greater serial depth, incurring higher computational
and latency costs.

To balance parameter storage efficiency and computational cost, this work explores the potential of another Parameter Loop scheme — Cross-Layer Expert Sharing. Unlike Looped Transformers, which keep the total parameters of each layer unchanged but increase the number of recursions, we aim to keep the number of recursions unchanged while increasing the candidate experts of each layer, and use the sparse activation of MoE to keep both the activated parameters and the computation per layer unchanged. We refer to the experts shared across layers as Meta Experts, and the layer-private experts in the conventional design as Local Experts.

Our experiments show that, compared with the Local Expert of hierarchical architectures, Meta Experts reduce parameter redundancy and achieve lower cross-entropy loss together with higher downstream task accuracy. At a fixed number of activated experts and expert granularity, our model achieves comparable performance to Vanilla MoE with only 70% as many experts, indicating a substantially higher effective capacity per parameter.
Further analysis reveals another reason why cross-layer expert sharing is effective: Meta Experts provide hidden states with more flexible paths for storage and retrieval. Compared with hierarchical architectures, cross-layer expert sharing implicitly provides more flexible residual connections: beyond the forward residual connections, it introduces a backward residual connection at the activation level—one that operates within a single forward pass and is therefore absent from existing models.

Our contributions are summarized as follows:
(1) A systematic analysis of the effect of Meta Experts on model
performance, covering their activation and total-number ratios among all
experts, their presence in every layer, and their partial hierarchical
organization. Although these configurations moderately affect performance,
Meta Experts consistently bring significant improvements.
(2) An explanation of why Meta Experts are effective from the perspective
of residual connections. By comparing the forward hidden-state updates and
the backward parameter updates of models with and without Meta Experts, we
show that this design grants shallow and deep layers bidirectional access
to representations across depth, enabling more flexible information
retrieval.

A MoE-based Transformer layer $l$ consists of an attention block and a MoE
block (Fig 2.(a)). The MoE block contains a layer-specific
router $R_{l}$ and $N$ Local Experts $\{\phi_{e}(\cdot;\theta_{l}^{e})\}_{e=0}^{N-1}$ ,
both of which are initialized and accessed independently across layers.
Given the hidden state $\hat{x}_{l}$ of a token entering the MoE block, the
router computes gating scores from its logits and selects the top- $k$ 
experts belonging to the current layer to process the token:

 $y_{l}\;=\;\sum_{e\in\mathrm{topk}(R_{l},\hat{x}_{l})}R_{l}(\hat{x}_{l})_{e}\,\phi_{e}(\hat{x}_{l};\theta_{l}^{e}).$ 

(1)

the layer-private MoE blocks are replaced with a single shared block:
one router $R$ and one pool of $M$ experts
 $\{\phi_{e}(\cdot;\theta^{e})\}_{e=0}^{M-1}$ could be activated by every layer:

 $y_{l}\;=\;\sum_{e\in\mathrm{topk}(R,\hat{x}_{l})}R(\hat{x}_{l})_{e}\,\phi_{e}(\hat{x}_{l};\theta^{e}),$

## 3 Empirical Comparison between Meta and Local Experts

To systematically analyze the effect of Meta Experts on model performance,
we start from a model with only Local Experts and incorporate Meta Experts
in various forms, comparing model capabilities with and without them.

## 3.1 Setups

Core Variables.
We consider two types of relationship between Meta Experts and Local Experts: (1) the quantitative relationship: the ratio of their total numbers within the whole model and their activated numbers within each layer (See Fig 3.(a)); (2) the topological relationship: whether every layer contains both Meta and Local Experts (See Fig 3.(b)), and whether the Meta Experts are also hierarchically organized (See Fig 3.(c)).

Common Settings.
During the experiments, we first sweep all the variables on a 2B-A0.5B
model, and then select the relatively better-performing configurations for
the scaling ladder experiments.
Throughout the experiments, we keep the total number of experts and the
total activation count fixed, which indirectly ensures that different
models have similar FLOPs, enabling a fairer comparison. When comparing
models at different FLOPs, all models adopt the training-optimal
configuration of the Vanilla MoE structure, rather than a configuration
tuned specifically for our design. Detailed experimental setups can be found in A.3

Metrics.
In addition to cross-entropy loss and downstream task accuracy, we introduce
an expert-redundancy metric as a complementary perspective for analyzing
expert specialization under different architectural settings. It allows us to
examine how expert topology affects the overlap among learned expert
functions. The exact training configurations and the definition of the
redundancy metric are deferred to Appendix A.2.

Figure 3: The Quantitative and Topological Comparison between Meta and Local Experts.

## 3.2 Meta/Local Experts’ ratio

We first study the effect of the ratio between the total numbers of Meta
Experts and Local Experts, and of their activation ratio within each layer,
on model performance.

Settings.
For the total-number ratio, we consider Meta Expert proportions of
0%, 25%, 50%, 75%, or 100%; for the activation ratio, we consider proportions
of 25%, 50%, or 75%. For concreteness, consider a two-layer model with 256
experts in total and 8 experts activated per layer. A Meta/Local number
ratio of 75% then corresponds to 192 Meta Experts, shared across all
layers, and 32 Local Experts per layer; an activation ratio of 75%
corresponds to activating 6 Meta Experts and 2 Local Experts per layer.

Figure 4: Pilot experiments on the Meta/Local Expert ratio. We vary the
total-number proportion of Meta Experts ( $0\%$ – $100\%$ ) and their
per-layer activation proportion ( $25\%$ – $75\%$ ), while keeping the total
expert count and the total activation count fixed so that all models share
similar FLOPs. Higher Meta-Expert proportions consistently reduce both the
cross-entropy loss and the expert redundancy $\rho$ ; increasing the
activation share of Local Experts reduces redundancy but degrades the
loss.

Pilot Experiments (Fig 4).
The results show that, at any activation ratio, a higher total-parameter
proportion of Meta Experts consistently leads to lower loss and redundancy;
meanwhile, allocating a higher activation ratio to Meta Experts also helps
reduce the loss. Taken together, these observations attest to the
effectiveness of Meta Experts. On the other hand, while assigning a higher
activation ratio to Local Experts is detrimental to the loss, it does help
reduce the redundancy among experts. This suggests that, compared with Meta
Experts, Local Experts—each of which is activated in only one layer under
the traditional architecture—may be harder to train sufficiently, and
this under-training may give rise to redundancy.

Figure 5: Scaling-ladder on the Meta/Local Expert ratio. With the
per-layer activation fixed to 4 Meta Experts and 4 Local Experts, we scale
the total-number ratio of Meta Experts ( $25\%$ / $50\%$ / $75\%$ ) across
training compute (FLOPs). Although slightly worse than Pure-Meta-Expert Setting,
the $75\%$ ratio achieves the best cross-entropy loss over all Partial-Meta Experts
setting at every compute budget, and is adopted as the base configuration in all subsequent experiments.

Scaling Ladder (Fig 5).
Given that activating 4 Meta Experts and 4 Local Experts per layer yields
consistently better loss, we hold this activation ratio fixed and conduct a
full scaling-ladder validation across the total-number ratios of
25%/50%/75%. The results confirm that, although still slightly worse
than the Pure-Meta-Expert setting, the $75\%$ ratio achieves the best
performance over all Partial-Meta settings at every compute budget, in
terms of both cross-entropy loss and expert redundancy. Considering that
a model with only Meta Experts may suffer from training instability,
we adopt the 75% configuration in all subsequent experiments.

## 3.3 Meta/Local Experts’ topology

We next investigate the effect of the topology of Meta/Local Experts on
model performance. On the one hand, we explore whether every layer should
include Meta Experts; on the other hand, we explore whether the Meta
Experts should themselves be partially hierarchically organized.

Settings.
On the one hand, we consider variants in which Local Experts are used
exclusively, with no Meta Experts introduced at all: either in only the
first 1, 2, 4, or 8 layers of the model, in only the last 1, 2, 4, or 8
layers, or in both the first and the last 1, 2, or 4 layers. On the other
hand, we consider partitioning the Meta Experts into 2, 4, or 8 sub-pools,
which are then accessed in a looped manner—either in a chunked or an
interleaved fashion.

Figure 6: Pilot experiments on the Meta/Local Expert topology. We vary
where Local Experts are used exclusively (the first and/or the last
1/2/4/8 layers), and how the Meta Experts are hierarchically organized
( $2$ / $4$ / $8$ sub-pools, accessed in a looped manner, either chunked or
interleaved). Both interventions generally degrade the loss, corroborating
the benefit of Meta Experts, while a lighter degree of intervention—only
the first and the last two layers restricted to Local Experts, or the Meta
Experts looped only twice—slightly improves it.

Pilot Experiments (Fig 6).
It can be observed that both operations—introducing Local-Experts-only
layers, and organizing the Meta Experts hierarchically so as to mimic the
topology of Local Experts—generally incur a loss degradation, which
further demonstrates the positive role of Meta Experts in model
performance. Notably, under a lighter degree of intervention, the loss
slightly improves: for example, when only the first two and the last two
layers are restricted to Local Experts, or when the Meta Experts are looped
only twice. This suggests, on the one hand, that experts in the shallowest
and deepest layers serve more differentiated functions, with essentially
no mutual redundancy between these layers—a conclusion also supported by
the expert redundancy measurements. On the other hand, mildly hierarchical
Meta Experts help improve the model capability slightly; hierarchy,
however, introduces a certain amount of expert redundancy, and why this
redundancy does not translate into a higher loss remains an open question
for further investigation.

Figure 7: Scaling-ladder on the Meta/Local Expert topology. We scale the two
best pilot configurations—Local Experts exclusively at both shallow and
deep ends, and interleaved sub-pool partitioning of the Meta
Experts—across training compute (FLOPs). Restricting the first two and
the last two layers to Local Experts, together with $2$ sub-pools,
achieves the best performance at every compute budget; as the model
becomes deeper, more Local-Experts-only layers and a larger hierarchy of
Meta Experts further help, suggesting that deeper models call for a larger
hierarchical expert topology.

Scaling Ladder (Fig 7).
Compared with other configurations, introducing Local-Experts-only layers at
both the shallow and the deep ends, and partitioning the Meta Experts into
sub-pools in an interleaved manner, yield consistently better results; we
therefore supplement full scaling-ladder validation under these two
configurations. The results show that, at every FLOPs configuration, the
best performance is achieved by using Local Experts exclusively in the
first two and the last two layers, together with partitioning the Meta
Experts into 2 sub-pools. We also observe that, as the model becomes
deeper, using more Local-Experts-only layers and a larger hierarchy of
Meta Experts likewise exhibits better results. This suggests, on the one
hand, that the proportion of specialized shallow and deep layers relative
to the total number of layers may remain roughly constant as model depth
changes; on the other hand, deeper models also call for a larger hierarchical
expert topology; the Local Experts of the Vanilla model, however, are
overly hierarchical, and can therefore only achieve suboptimal results.

Overall Comparison (Fig 1 & 8)
We combine the best configurations identified in each ablation and find
that the combined model consistently outperforms every individual
configuration in terms of loss. Building on this best configuration, we
further reduce the number of Meta Experts and find that, compared with the
Vanilla model, models with Meta Experts save approximately 20–30% of the
experts (or 15–25% of the parameters, since other modules also account
for a certain fraction of the parameters), with the advantage becoming
more pronounced as the model size grows.

Figure 8: Scaling-ladder on cumulative Meta-Expert designs. Starting from a
 $75\%$ Meta-Expert total-number ratio (MR75), we incrementally
add topology refinements—Local Experts only in the first and last two
layers (F2L2) and two looped Meta-Expert sub-pools (Loop2)—and
finally combine both (F2L2 + Loop2). All models are compared against the
Vanilla MoE (dashed line at $1.0$ ) in loss ratio $L/L_{\mathrm{Vanilla}}$ 
(left) and expert redundancy ratio $R/R_{\mathrm{Vanilla}}$ (right) across
training compute (FLOPs). Every refinement improves over Vanilla at every
compute budget, with the largest gains under the combined design; the
advantage in both loss and redundancy becomes more pronounced as training
compute grows.

## 3.4 Downstream Performance of Meta Experts-based Model

For a more comprehensive comparison, beyond cross-entropy loss, we further
compare the downstream task capabilities of different configurations
through training at a larger scale.

Settings.
Using the best configuration identified in each of the ablation studies above, we train 6B-A1.6B models for 200B tokens and evaluate their downstream task performance. We compare five variants: the Vanilla baseline (Vanilla MoE, Local Experts only), the MR75 model, which activates Meta Experts at a 75% meta ratio, and three topological refinements of MR75—MR75 + Loop2, which partitions the Meta Experts into two interleaved sub-pools; MR75 + F2L2, which restricts the first two and last two layers to Local Experts only; and their combination, MR75 + F2L2 + Loop2. All variants use the same total number of experts, per-layer activation budget, and training recipe, ensuring that performance differences are attributable to the expert topology. We evaluate
the models across a diverse set of benchmarks covering complementary
capabilities: world knowledge and factual question answering in both English
(MMLU (Hendrycks et al., 2021)) and Chinese
(C-Eval (Huang et al., 2023) and
CMMLU (Li et al., 2024)); multi-step and commonsense
reasoning (BBH (Kazemi et al., 2025) with chain-of-thought and
OpenBookQA (Mihaylov et al., 2018)); and open-ended,
knowledge-intensive generation
(TriviaQA (Joshi et al., 2017)).

## 4 Meta Experts as Bidirectional Residual Pathways

Consider a stack of $L$ MoE layers with hidden size $H$ . Writing $x_{l}$ for
the residual stream entering the MoE sub-layer of layer $l$ ,
 $\hat{x}_{l}=\mathrm{RMSNorm}(x_{l})$ , and $\gamma$ for the residual scaling
coefficient, the sub-layer updates the stream as

 $x_{l+1}\;=\;x_{l}\;+\;\gamma\,E_{\theta_{l}}(\hat{x}_{l};\,R_{l}),$ 

(3)

where $E_{\theta_{l}}$ is an expert block of $N$ SwiGLU experts, each with
parameter dimension $d$ , $R_{l}$ is the router, and top- $k$ routing activates
 $k$ experts per token. In a layer-wise MoE, $\theta_{l}$ and $R_{l}$ are
layer-private; in Meta Experts, a single block $\theta$ and router $R$ are
learned once and shared by all layers.

## 4.1 Layer-wise MoE.

Forward (read). The expert of layer $l$ injects its output into the
residual stream at layer $l$ (Eq. equation 3) and propagates only
forward: hidden state $x_{m}$ can contain layer $l$ ’s content only if $l<m$ ,
and even then only as a frozen component, because the readout operator of
layer $m$ ( $E_{\theta_{m}},R_{m}$ ) differs from the write operator of layer
 $l$ ( $E_{\theta_{l}},R_{l}$ ).

Training (write). The residual connection does run backward as a gradient
highway: the adjoint $\bar{x}_{l}=\partial\mathcal{L}/\partial x_{l}$ 
inherits $\bar{x}_{l+1}$ through the identity branch with unit gain, so
the training signal of deeper layers does reach shallower parameters
 $\theta_{l}$ without attenuation. This channel, however, is parameter-level
rather than activation-level, lands in layer-private parameters that only
layer $l$ ever reads, and over the $m-l$ hops between a pair of layers
 $l<m$ , the deep-layer component is progressively mixed into the composite
adjoint, diluted by the training signals of all intermediate layers rather
than arriving at $\theta_{l}$ as an isolated, addressable contribution.

Net effect. In one forward pass, a hidden state includes only
earlier layers’ expert content; deeper layers’ signal reaches a
shallow layer solely as a parameter update during training, never as
content in its readout.

## 4.2 MoE with Meta Experts.

Training (write).
Let $y_{l}=\gamma E_{\theta}(\hat{x}_{l};R)$ and
 $\bar{y}_{l}=\partial\mathcal{L}/\partial y_{l}$ . Since all layers share
the same pool, layer $l$ ’s gradient contribution

 $g_{l}\;=\;\Bigl(\frac{\partial y_{l}}{\partial\theta}\Bigr)^{\!\top}\bar{y}_{l}\;=\;\left.\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial\theta}\right|_{l}\;\in\;\mathbb{R}^{Nd}$ 

(4)

accumulates with all other layers’ by the chain rule,
 $\bar{\theta}=\sum_{l}g_{l}$ (the shared router obeys the same
accumulation; we focus on the expert parameters). Thus, with learning rate
 $\eta$ , after $T$ training steps the storage contains the superposition of
all layers’ writes:

 $\theta^{(T)}\;=\;\theta^{(0)}\;+\;\sum_{l=0}^{L-1}\bar{\Delta\theta}_{l},\qquad\bar{\Delta\theta}_{l}\;=\;-\eta\sum_{t<T}g_{l}^{(t)}.$ 

(5)

Equivalently, at training step $t$ , layer $l$ contributes the local
parameter update
 $\Delta\theta_{l}^{(t)}=-\eta g_{l}^{(t)}$ .

Forward (read).
After training, every layer reads from the same shared expert parameters;
in particular, layer $m$ computes
 $y_{m}=\gamma E_{\theta^{(T)}}(\hat{x}_{m};R)$ .
To characterize how a write from layer $l$ affects the readout at layer
 $m$ , we consider its local first-order effect at training step $t$ ,
evaluated at $\theta^{(t)}$ while holding the layer inputs fixed. Define

 $J_{m}^{(t)}\;=\;\left.\frac{\partial E_{\theta}(\hat{x}_{m}^{(t)};R)}{\partial\theta}\right|_{\theta=\theta^{(t)}}\;\in\;\mathbb{R}^{H\times Nd}.$ 

(6)

Since $y_{l}=\gamma E_{\theta}(\hat{x}_{l};R)$ ,
Eq. equation 4 gives

 $g_{l}^{(t)}\;=\;\gamma{J_{l}^{(t)}}^{\top}\bar{y}_{l}^{(t)}.$ 

Therefore, the write from layer $l$ at step $t$ induces the following
first-order change in layer $m$ ’s readout:

 $\Delta y_{m\leftarrow l}^{(t)}\;\approx\;\gamma J_{m}^{(t)}\Delta\theta_{l}^{(t)}\;=\;-\eta\gamma^{2}J_{m}^{(t)}{J_{l}^{(t)}}^{\top}\bar{y}_{l}^{(t)}.$ 

(7)

Accumulating these local first-order influences over training gives

 $\mathcal{I}_{m\leftarrow l}\;\coloneqq\;\sum_{t<T}\Delta y_{m\leftarrow l}^{(t)}\;\approx\;-\eta\gamma^{2}\sum_{t<T}J_{m}^{(t)}{J_{l}^{(t)}}^{\top}\bar{y}_{l}^{(t)}.$ 

(8)

The update of the shared router contributes an analogous term, omitted
because top- $k$ selection is piecewise constant in $R$ .
The factor
 $J_{m}^{(t)}{J_{l}^{(t)}}^{\top}$ 
has the form of a tangent kernel of the shared block evaluated
across the two layers. The total shared-parameter influence on
layer $m$ ,
 $\mathcal{I}_{m}=\sum_{l=0}^{L-1}\mathcal{I}_{m\leftarrow l}$ ,
contains contributions from every layer, in contrast to both
limitations of layer-wise MoE:

• 

 $l<m$ (deep reads shallow). Versus the frozen
forward carrying of layer-wise MoE: because the write at layer $l$ 
and the readout at layer $m$ are two evaluations of the
same operator, layer $m$ retrieves, re-encodes, and further
transforms shallow content in the code space in which it was
written.

• 

 $l>m$ (shallow reads deep). Versus the gradient
highway of layer-wise MoE: deep content reaches layer $m$ not as a
parameter update of its private $\theta_{m}$ , but as an
activation-level term in its readout, in the common code space,
through an $O(1)$ -length path independent of $m-l$ .

Net effect.
A single forward pass provides bidirectional access along depth—deep
hidden states reach shallow expert content, and shallow hidden states
reach deep expert content—through one shared storage. More discussions
are done in the Appendix A.4.

## 5 Conclusion

In this work, we present an empirical and theoretical analysis of
cross-layer expert sharing in MoE models, contrasting the hierarchically
arranged Local Experts of standard architectures with inter-layer
shared Meta Experts. Our systematic comparisons—over the
Meta/Local ratio, the activation ratio, and the topology of the shared
pool—show that Meta Experts consistently reduce expert redundancy and
cross-entropy loss while improving downstream task accuracy: at a fixed
number of activated experts and expert granularity, models built on shared
experts match the performance of Vanilla MoE with only 70–80% of the
expert budget, substantially improving scaling potential. Beyond these
empirical observations, our analysis shows that a shared expert block is
not merely a parameter-saving device: it widens the forward residual
connections and grants hidden states bidirectional access to expert content
across depth, improving both the storage and the retrieval of information.
Taken together, our results suggest that inter-layer redundancy, long
treated as an intrinsic cost of deep MoE models, can instead be converted
into effective capacity. Future directions include extending this analysis
to larger models and broader architectures, and understanding why mildly
hierarchical shared experts introduce redundancy without degrading
performance.

## Appendix A Appendix

## A.1 Related Work

Weight Tying was originally introduced to share
parameters between the embedding layer and the language model
head (Press and Wolf, 2017; Inan et al., 2016): since the two matrices map tokens
to and from the hidden space, tying them nearly halves the corresponding
parameters while often improving perplexity, and the practice has become
standard in modern language models (Vaswani et al., 2017b; Raffel et al., 2020).
Beyond tying modules at the same depth, weight tying can be pushed further,
to reusing parameters across depth (Dehghani et al., 2018; Lan et al., 2019).

Looped Transformer pushes weight tying to extreme:
the same block is applied multiple times per token, unrolling a small set
of weights into arbitrary depth (Dehghani et al., 2018; Giannou et al., 2023; Hua et al., 2025). This line of work shows that weight reuse
across depth can substitute for parameter growth, and that a compact
recurrent model can match the reasoning performance of much larger ones
(Bae et al., 2025; Saunshi et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2026), at
the cost of additional recursions and a higher degree of serialization.
Meta Experts share the same motivation—extracting more effective capacity
from a fixed parameter budget—but apply tying at a finer granularity and
within a single feed-forward pass: only the expert block is reused across
layers, without extra recursions, so training and inference retain the
parallelism of standard Transformers, while attention may remain
layer-specific. Depending on what is reused across depth, this
paradigm subsumes several lines of work.

Attn Weight Sharing reuses the attention projections (or
entire attention blocks) of different layers, yielding moderate parameter
savings at little accuracy cost (Xiao et al., 2019; Lan et al., 2019; Mu et al., 2026). These results suggest that the per-layer
parameter budget of Transformers is partially redundant—but leave open
where the redundancy concentrates and whether the same principle
applies to the rest of the block. Attn KV-Cache Sharing reuses not
parameters but activations: in long-context or serving scenarios, multiple
layers attend to a common KV cache, trading representational independence
for inference memory and throughput (Sun et al., 2024; Brandon et al., 2024; Zuhri et al., 2025; Wu and Tu, 2024; Wu et al., 2025). Such sharing is typically applied at deployment
time, affects the cache rather than the learned weights, and is often found
to be nearly lossless—suggesting that layers can tolerate reading
the same content, but not that they should store the same content.
Meta Experts operate on the storage side: the shared expert pool is written
by every layer during training, so cross-layer sharing reshapes what the
model stores and how hidden states retrieve it, rather than merely reusing
cached activations at inference.

FFN Weight Sharing is the closest line to our work: it
reuses feed-forward parameters across layers, either by tying the FFN of
all layers or by sharing it among a subset (Lan et al., 2019; Pires et al., 2023; Thawakar et al., 2024). Existing
studies, however, focus on dense FFNs and report mixed results, and sharing
is typically applied uniformly to every layer. MoE Weight Sharing has been explored in
several recent works: experts are shared across layers, but almost always
under an every-layer sharing topology, whose communication and memory
overhead is difficult to scale (Li et al., 2025; Tan et al., 2025; Chen et al., 2026a; Huang et al., 2026; Chen et al., 2026b; Jiao et al., 2026). Meta Experts
generalize this idea to a more flexible regime: a shared pool of
experts is accessed through several topology that is more friendly for
communication and memory (reserving boundary layers for local experts,
sub-pool partitioning with chunked or interleaved access), allows the model
to decide which content is worth sharing across depth—and, as our analysis
shows, implicitly turns the shared experts into bidirectional residual
pathways between layers.

## A.2 Redundancy Metric

To quantify how much of a model’s expert capacity is redundant, we need a
measure that is (i) continuous, with no similarity threshold; (ii)
comparable across architectures with different expert counts and
depths; and (iii) faithful to the correlation structure among
experts in high dimensions, where naive cosine similarities collapse toward
zero. Our metric is built from a single similarity kernel and a single
spectral statistic.

## Similarity kernel: linear-CKA on centered covariances.

For expert $i$ , let $Y_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{T\times H}$ be its response
matrix to a shared probe set of $T$ L2-normalized tokens, and let $Y_{i}^{c}$ 
denote $Y_{i}$ with its sample mean subtracted. We map each expert to the
vectorized centered covariance

 $\phi_{i}\;=\;\mathrm{vec}\bigl({Y_{i}^{c}}^{\top}Y_{i}^{c}\bigr),$ 

(9)

and define the pairwise similarity as the cosine in this feature space,

 $K_{ij}\;=\;\frac{\langle\phi_{i},\phi_{j}\rangle}{\|\phi_{i}\|\,\|\phi_{j}\|},$ 

(10)

which is exactly linear CKA evaluated on the experts’ functional responses.
Being invariant to orthogonal transformations and isotropic scaling,
 $K_{ij}$ reflects the correlated directions between experts rather than
their absolute orientation or norm, and avoids the collapse of raw
high-dimensional cosine. The resulting Gram matrix
 $K\in\mathbb{R}^{E\times E}$ covers the $E$ experts under analysis:
for models mixing Meta and Local Experts the two pools are concatenated on
the expert axis and analyzed jointly, and for layer-wise models the layer
index serves as the grouping axis.

## Spectral statistic: how many directions collapse.

Since $K$ is positive semi-definite, its eigen-spectrum
 $\lambda_{1}\geq\lambda_{2}\geq\dots\geq\lambda_{E}\geq 0$ distributes the
total similarity energy across $E$ principal directions. We measure the
concentration of this energy by the Gini coefficient of the spectrum,

 $\rho\;=\;\mathrm{Gini}(\lambda)\;=\;\frac{\sum_{i,j}|\lambda_{i}-\lambda_{j}|}{2E\sum_{i}\lambda_{i}}\;\in\;[0,1],$ 

(11)

where $\rho=0$ means the energy is uniform across all directions (experts
fully complementary, no redundancy) and $\rho\to 1$ means it concentrates
on a few directions (strong redundancy). The reading admits an exact
interpretation: for a uniform rank- $k$ spectrum,
 $\mathrm{Gini}(\lambda)=1-k/E$ , so $\rho$ equals the fraction of
expert directions that have collapsed into redundant ones. Because $\rho$ 
is a ratio, it is directly comparable across models with different $E$ ,
depths, and training budgets, and we report it as our headline
Expert Redundancy metric.

depth-dependent knobs (width, batch size, learning rate, token budget, and
expert parallelism) change across rungs (Tables 2
and 3).

## Hyperparameter selection.

We do not tune the training hyperparameters per architectural variant. Instead we
fix a single compute-optimal configuration for the vanilla baseline backbone at
each rung and reuse it unchanged for every Meta Experts variant. The baseline
configuration—the joint depth–width scaling, and the per-depth global batch
size, peak learning rate, and token budget—is obtained by sweeping these
settings and retaining the compute-optimal choice at each depth, following
established practice on compute-optimal training and its schedule
(Hoffmann et al., 2022a; Hägele et al., 2024) and on scaling laws for the
learning rate and batch size (Bi et al., 2024; Team et al., 2025). The
Mixture-of-Experts structural choices (expert count, granularity, and the
shared-expert ratio) are likewise set in line with recent MoE scaling-law
analyses (Tian et al., 2026; Zhao et al., 2025). Because every
Meta Experts variant inherits the baseline configuration without re-tuning, a
difference in loss at a rung reflects the architectural change rather than a
difference in hyperparameters; this reuse is further supported by evidence that
optimal learning rate and batch size are governed primarily by the compute
budget and are largely insensitive to Mixture-of-Experts architectural
variation (Team et al., 2025). Moreover, at a given depth every variant keeps
the per-token top- $8$ routing and hence the same activated parameters, and shares
the same token budget, so all variants at a rung incur the same training FLOPs
and are plotted at a single shared compute coordinate; the compute-versus-loss
comparison at that rung is thus iso-FLOP by construction, and the only free axis
across variants is the loss.

## Meta Expert configurations.

Table 4 catalogues every architecture actually run in
the ladder—the vanilla baseline and each model incorporating Meta Experts—each swept over
all four depths $L\in\{12,16,20,24\}$ . A Meta Expert variant changes
only the routed-expert layers of the shared backbone
(Table 2): it splits each layer’s $128$ routed experts into a
Meta pool that is shared across depth and a set of Local experts
private to that layer, and routes each token to $m$ meta $+\,l$ local experts. We
write a split as (meta width / local width) per layer; the recurring
r75 base is $96$ meta $/\,32$ local with $m{+}l=4{+}4$ . Two invariants
hold for every variant at every depth and render the rungs comparable: the total
routed-expert count is $128\times L$ (iso-param, as the shared Meta pool is
counted once across the $L$ layers) and the per-token routing is top- $8$ 
(iso-FLOP). The attention stack, gated shared expert, and training configuration
are identical to the vanilla baseline, so every variant at a rung occupies the
single shared compute coordinate of Table 3.

The variants span two axes. The quantitative axis is the
ratio family, which varies the meta share of each layer’s $128$ experts
over $25/50/75\%$ —MR25/MR50/MR75, i.e. $32/64/96$ meta with the complementary
 $96/64/32$ local—while routing $4$ meta $+\,4$ local per token. The
topological axis fixes the r75 split and varies where and how the Meta
pool is shared across depth. The first/last local-only family converts the
first and last $1/2/4$ layers (f1l1/f2l2/f4l4) to
 $128$ Local-only layers—no meta, with all $8$ routed among the locals—while
the interior layers retain r75; f2l2 is the F2L2 point of the main text.
The meta-loop family retains r75 at every layer but replaces the single
globally shared Meta pool with $2/4/8$ interleaved sub-pools, each reused by
every $2$ nd $/4$ th $/8$ th layer (Loop2/4/8); this tests whether Meta experts must
be shared across the full depth or can instead be shared within smaller
depth-local groups. The combined point F2L2 $+$ Loop2 applies both topological
modifications.

The pure-Meta family removes Local experts entirely and
routes the full top- $8$ from a single Meta pool shared across all layers
( $128$ per layer, $128\times L$ in total). It constitutes the $100\%$ -meta end of
the ratio axis and, like every other variant, preserves the $128\times L$ budget
and the top- $8$ activation.

 $128\times L$ . Starting from the F2L2 $+$ Loop2 point, we reduce the per-layer Meta
width from the r75 value of $96$ through $\{90,84,72,60,48\}$ without
refilling the freed slots with Local experts. We denote each setting
mpl $M$ , for meta-per-layer width $M$ 
(Table 5).

top- $8$ ( $4$ meta $+\,4$ local), leaving the activated experts and the training

against loss at fixed compute. It is run at all four depths, each on its standard
per-depth schedule (Table 3), so the comparison remains

## A.4 Discussion for the Residual Analysis.

Two remarks keep the claim honest. First, the path through the shared pool
has $O(1)$ length—one hop from layer $l$ into $\theta$ and one hop out to
layer $m$ —independent of the layer distance $|m-l|$ , whereas both
pre-existing routes of layer-wise MoE (forward carrying and the gradient
highway) pass through $|m-l|$ Jacobians. The effective local gain is
governed by the spectrum of the cross-layer tangent factor
 $J_{m}^{(t)}{J_{l}^{(t)}}^{\top}$ appearing in
Eq. equation 8; the analysis therefore characterizes access and
path length, rather than guaranteeing against vanishing signals.
Second, the causal direction of a single forward pass is unchanged—
 $\hat{x}_{m}$ does not depend on the activations of deeper layers within the
same pass; what a shallow layer accesses is the content written into
the shared pool by deeper layers during training. This is exactly
analogous to how any trained weight carries information about the data it
was trained on, with the difference that here the carrier is shared across
depth, making the information accessible at every layer in a common code
space.

Finally, layer-wise gradient reweighting,
 $\bar{\theta}=\sum_{l}\alpha_{l}g_{l}$ 
(e.g., norm equalization or conflict projection), amounts to gating this
backward pathway: it modulates how much of each layer’s write
 $\Delta\theta_{l}^{(t)}$ is allowed to influence the other layers’ reads
through $\Delta y_{m\leftarrow l}^{(t)}$ , and hence through the accumulated
cross-layer influence $\mathcal{I}_{m\leftarrow l}$ .

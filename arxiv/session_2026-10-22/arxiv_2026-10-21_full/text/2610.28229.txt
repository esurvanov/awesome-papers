Abstract

Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) minimizes empirical risk over observed preference comparisons, implicitly assuming that the training preference distribution matches the deployment preference distribution. In practice, this i.i.d. assumption often fails, as preference distribution shift exhibits a natural hierarchical structure across three levels: group-level shift, prompt-level shift, and preference-level shift. Such multi-level distributional mismatch poses a critical challenge for safe LLM deployment, yet existing robust DPO methods typically either model uncertainty over the joint preference distribution or focus on individual sources of shift, leaving the hierarchical structure of OOD preference shifts underexplored. We propose TPO, a tri-level distributionally robust framework for OOD preference alignment, which jointly models all three levels of distributional uncertainty through a hierarchically structured rectangular ambiguity set. By instantiating it with Wasserstein distance, we derive a tractable and interpretable objective that combines a closed-form preference-level margin penalty, prompt-level adversarial embedding perturbation, and worst-case group reweighting, each governed by an independent radius. We establish finite-sample convergence guarantees and empirically validate the effectiveness of TPO over existing robust DPO methods.

## 1 Introduction

Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) (Rafailov et al., 2023) has emerged as a prominent method for aligning large language models (LLMs) with human preferences. Unlike RLHF (Christiano et al., 2017; Stiennon et al., 2020; Ouyang et al., 2022), DPO directly optimizes a preference objective from pairwise comparison data, bypassing explicit reward modeling and improving optimization stability. However, standard DPO minimizes empirical risk over observed pairwise preference data, without explicitly accounting for distribution shifts between training and deployment. Such out-of-distribution (OOD) shifts can degrade preference alignment performance (Wu et al., 2025; Xu et al., 2025).

In preference alignment, OOD preference shift exhibits a natural hierarchical structure across multiple levels. Specifically, the preference data distribution may shift at three distinct levels.
First, the distribution over groups may change between training and deployment, inducing a group-level shift, which has been studied in group robust preference alignment (Ramesh et al., 2024; Sagawa et al., 2020).
Second, even within the same group, the prompt marginal distribution may vary across domains, time, or user interaction patterns, yielding a prompt-level shift, which corresponds to covariate shift in the prompt distribution and has been observed in robust prompt optimization (Li et al., 2023; Seegmiller and Preum, 2026).
Third, the conditional preference distribution may also change due to noisy feedback or heterogeneous preferences within the same group, giving rise to a preference-level shift (Yao et al., 2025; Chowdhury et al., 2024; Poddar et al., 2024).

Recent robust DPO methods have begun to address distributional robustness, but they typically do not explicitly model the hierarchical structure of preference distribution shift. WDPO and KLDPO (Xu et al., 2025) construct Wasserstein and KL ambiguity sets over the entire preference distribution, while rDPO (Chowdhury et al., 2024) and Dr.DPO (Wu et al., 2025) focus on preference label noise and on pointwise and pairwise data noise, respectively.
As summarized in Table 1, existing methods either model overall uncertainty or focus on a single robustness level. Motivated by these observations, we propose TPO,
a tri-level distributionally robust framework for OOD
preference alignment, which jointly models all three levels through a hierarchically structured ambiguity set instantiated with Wasserstein distances and provides interpretable robustness controls for each shift level. Overall, our main contributions are as follows:

## 2 Preliminaries

Notation.
Unless otherwise specified, we use uppercase letters for random variables and lowercase letters for their realizations, with probability distributions denoted by $P$ and $Q$ . We use $\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathcal{X}$ for a prompt, $\boldsymbol{y}\in\mathcal{Y}$ for a response, and $\boldsymbol{h}\in\mathcal{H}$ for the prompt embedding, with $\tilde{\boldsymbol{h}}=\boldsymbol{h}+\boldsymbol{\delta}$ denoting an adversarially perturbed embedding. A pairwise preference example is modeled as a random variable $U=(X,V,S)$ , where $V=(Y_{1},Y_{2})$ is a pair of candidate responses and $S\in\{+1,-1\}$ is the binary preference label indicating which response is preferred. In standard DPO training, each comparison $(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}^{+},\boldsymbol{y}^{-})$ is encoded as $V=(\boldsymbol{y}^{+},\boldsymbol{y}^{-})$ and $S=+1$ . The group index is $g\in\mathcal{G}=[m]$ , where $[m]=\{1,\dots,m\}$ , and $P_{g}$ denotes the group-specific distribution over $U$ .
Let $I_{g}$ be the index set of the $n_{g}=|I_{g}|$ training samples from group $g$ .
We factorize $P_{g}(U)=P_{g}^{X}(X)\,P_{g}^{V}(V\mid X)\,P_{g}^{S}(S\mid V,X)$ , where $P_{g}^{X}$ , $P_{g}^{V}$ , and $P_{g}^{S}$ are the prompt marginal distribution, conditional candidate response distribution, and conditional preference distribution, respectively. We write $\Delta^{m-1}$ for the probability simplex over $m$ groups, $\|\boldsymbol{v}\|_{2}$ for the Euclidean norm, $\phi(\boldsymbol{h},\boldsymbol{y})\in\mathbb{R}^{d}$ for the log-linear feature map, and $\varphi(t)=\log(1+e^{-t})$ for the logistic loss.

## Direct Preference Optimization.

DPO learns a policy $\pi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}$ from pairwise preference data
 $\mathcal{D}=\{(\boldsymbol{x}_{i},\boldsymbol{y}_{i}^{+},\boldsymbol{y}_{i}^{-})\}_{i=1}^{n}$ , where $\boldsymbol{y}_{i}^{+}\succ\boldsymbol{y}_{i}^{-}$ indicates that
 $\boldsymbol{y}_{i}^{+}$ is preferred over $\boldsymbol{y}_{i}^{-}$ .
Following Rafailov et al. (2023), the DPO margin for a candidate pair $(\boldsymbol{y}_{1},\boldsymbol{y}_{2})$ is defined as

 $m(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}_{1},\boldsymbol{y}_{2};\boldsymbol{\theta})=\beta\!\left(\log\frac{\pi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{y}_{1}\mid\boldsymbol{x})}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(\boldsymbol{y}_{1}\mid\boldsymbol{x})}-\log\frac{\pi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{y}_{2}\mid\boldsymbol{x})}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(\boldsymbol{y}_{2}\mid\boldsymbol{x})}\right),$ 

(1)

where $\beta>0$ is a scaling parameter and $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$ is a reference policy.
Given a binary preference label $s\in\{+1,-1\}$ , the labeled DPO loss is $\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}_{1},\boldsymbol{y}_{2},s;\boldsymbol{\theta})=\varphi\!\left(s\cdot m(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}_{1},\boldsymbol{y}_{2};\boldsymbol{\theta})\right)$ . For the standard DPO setting, we set $(\boldsymbol{y}_{1},\boldsymbol{y}_{2})=(\boldsymbol{y}^{+},\boldsymbol{y}^{-})$ and $s=+1$ , which gives the empirical DPO risk minimization objective:

 $\widehat{\mathcal{R}}_{\mathrm{DPO}}(\boldsymbol{\theta}):=\mathbb{E}_{\hat{P}}\bigl[\mathcal{L}(U;\boldsymbol{\theta})\bigr]=\frac{1}{n}\sum\nolimits_{i=1}^{n}\varphi\bigl(m(\boldsymbol{x}_{i},\boldsymbol{y}_{i}^{+},\boldsymbol{y}_{i}^{-};\boldsymbol{\theta})\bigr).$ 

(2)

Standard DPO minimizes this empirical risk under the assumption that the training distribution $\hat{P}$ is equivalent to the true preference distribution at deployment. When the i.i.d. assumption fails, DPO can suffer from reduced preference alignment quality (Wu et al., 2025; Xu et al., 2025).

## Group DRO.

Group DRO (Sagawa et al., 2020) introduces a group variable
 $G\in\mathcal{G}=\{1,\dots,m\}$ , so that each preference observation
 $U=(X,V,S)$ is associated with a group index $g$ .
Let $P_{g}$ denote the population distribution over $U$ for group $g$ . The overall training
distribution is expressed as $P=\sum_{g=1}^{m}\alpha_{g}P_{g}$ , where $\boldsymbol{\alpha}\in\Delta^{m-1}$ 
are the group proportions.
Through the lens of distributionally robust
optimization (Jiao et al., 2022; Esfahani and Kuhn, 2015), Group DRO replaces the ERM objective in Eq. (2) with the following min-max optimization problem:

 $\inf_{\boldsymbol{\theta}\in\Theta}\sup_{\boldsymbol{\omega}\in\mathcal{Q}}\sum\nolimits_{g=1}^{m}\omega_{g}\,\mathbb{E}_{P_{g}}\bigl[\mathcal{L}(U;\boldsymbol{\theta})\bigr],$ 

(3)

which minimizes the worst-case expected loss over an ambiguity set $\mathcal{Q}$ of group mixture weights, a paradigm that has also been adopted in robust RLHF (Chakraborty et al., 2024; Ramesh et al., 2024). However, this formulation keeps the within-group distributions $P_{g}$ fixed and does not explicitly
model shifts in either the prompt marginal distribution
 (Li et al., 2023; Koh et al., 2021)
or the conditional preference distribution
 (Sorensen et al., 2024; Yao et al., 2025).
This motivates the hierarchical robust formulation
in Section 3.1.

## 3 Method

## 3.1 Hierarchical Distribution Shifts and Uncertainty in Preference Alignment

In preference alignment, distribution shift exhibits a natural three-level hierarchical structure. At the global level, the group proportions may shift between training and deployment (Qiao et al., 2025; Ramesh et al., 2024; Jian et al., 2024). Within each group, shift arises at the local level in two forms: the prompt marginal distribution $P_{g}^{X}$ may shift across environments (Li et al., 2023; Koh et al., 2021), and the conditional preference distribution $P_{g}^{S}(S\mid V,X)$ may vary due to noisy (Chowdhury et al., 2024) or pluralistic (Poddar et al., 2024; Yao et al., 2025) human feedback. We refer to these as group-level, prompt-level, and preference-level shifts, respectively. We next formalize these three levels of distributional uncertainty and construct a hierarchical ambiguity set for robust preference optimization.

## Global group ambiguity set.

At the global level, following the standard DRO framework (Sagawa et al., 2020), we model group-level shift by an ambiguity set over the group mixture weights, centered at the training group proportions $\boldsymbol{\alpha}$ :
 $\mathcal{U}^{\omega}=\bigl\{\boldsymbol{\omega}\in\Delta^{m-1}:\mathcal{D}_{\omega}(\boldsymbol{\omega},\boldsymbol{\alpha})\leq\rho^{\omega}\bigr\}$ ,
where $\mathcal{D}_{\omega}$ is a discrepancy measure on the group simplex and $\rho^{\omega}\geq 0$ is the group-level radius.

## Local rectangular ambiguity sets.

At the local level, within-group shift arises from two sources: the prompt marginal distribution and the conditional preference distribution. For each group $g$ , the shifted joint distribution factorizes as $Q_{g}(U)=Q_{g}^{X}(X)\,P_{g}^{V}(V\mid X)\,Q_{g}^{S}(S\mid X,V)$ , where $Q_{g}^{X}$ and $Q_{g}^{S}$ are each constrained by their own ambiguity set. Since the candidate response pairs are fixed in the offline preference dataset, we do not introduce an ambiguity set for $P_{g}^{V}$ and keep $P_{g}^{V}(V\mid X)$ unchanged. We first constrain the prompt marginal distribution by $\mathcal{U}_{g}^{X}=\{Q_{g}^{X}:\mathcal{D}_{X}(Q_{g}^{X},P_{g}^{X})\leq\rho_{g}^{X}\}$ , where $\mathcal{D}_{X}$ is a discrepancy measure on the prompt (embedding) space and $\rho_{g}^{X}\geq 0$ is the prompt-level radius.

For each prompt and response pair $(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{v})$ , we constrain the conditional preference distribution by

 $\mathcal{U}_{g,\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{v}}^{S}=\Bigl\{Q_{g}^{S}(\cdot\mid\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{v}):\mathcal{D}_{S}\bigl(Q_{g}^{S}(\cdot\mid\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{v}),P_{g}^{S}(\cdot\mid\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{v})\bigr)\leq\rho_{g}^{S}\Bigr\},$ 

(4)

where $\mathcal{D}_{S}$ measures discrepancies between distributions over binary preference labels, and $\rho_{g}^{S}\geq 0$ is the preference-level radius. Jointly, these constraints induce the local ambiguity set

 $\mathcal{U}_{g}=\Bigl\{Q_{g}:Q_{g}^{X}\in\mathcal{U}_{g}^{X},\;Q_{g}^{S}(\cdot\mid\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{v})\in\mathcal{U}_{g,\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{v}}^{S}\ \text{for }Q_{g}^{X}P_{g}^{V}\text{-a.e. }(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{v})\Bigr\}.$ 

(5)

Note that $\mathcal{U}_{g}$ is rectangular with respect to this factorization: the prompt marginal and the conditional preference distribution can be chosen separately under their respective constraints. Under standard measurability conditions, this rectangularity allows the adversary to first select a worst-case preference distribution for each fixed $(X,V)$ , and then a worst-case prompt marginal distribution (Shapiro, 2016; Li and Shapiro, 2025), which yields the nested worst-case optimization in Section 4.2.

## Hierarchical ambiguity set.

Combining the global and local constraints, the full ambiguity set is

 $\begin{array}[]{l}\mathcal{U}=\Bigl\{Q=\textstyle\sum_{g=1}^{m}\omega_{g}Q_{g}:\boldsymbol{\omega}\in\mathcal{U}^{\omega},\;Q_{g}\in\mathcal{U}_{g}\ \text{for all }g\in\mathcal{G}\Bigr\}.\end{array}$ 

(6)

Compared with group robustness methods that only consider group-level shift, our formulation more faithfully characterizes preference distribution shift by explicitly accounting for within-group variation in both the prompt marginal $Q_{g}^{X}$ and the conditional preference distribution $Q_{g}^{S}(S\mid X,V)$ .

## 3.2 Tri-level Distributionally Robust Preference Optimization

Based on the hierarchical ambiguity set $\mathcal{U}$ in Eq. (6), we now formulate the robust preference optimization problem. Since the true distribution is unknown but assumed to lie in $\mathcal{U}$ , we follow standard DRO principles (Wiesemann et al., 2014; Esfahani and Kuhn, 2015; Sinha et al., 2018) and minimize the worst-case risk over $\mathcal{U}$ :

 $\inf_{\boldsymbol{\theta}\in\Theta}\sup_{\boldsymbol{\omega}\in\mathcal{U}^{\omega}}\sum\nolimits_{g=1}^{m}\omega_{g}\sup_{Q_{g}\in\mathcal{U}_{g}}\mathbb{E}_{U\sim Q_{g}}\big[\mathcal{L}(U;\boldsymbol{\theta})\big].$ 

(7)

The outer maximization over $\boldsymbol{\omega}$ captures global group-level shift, and the inner maximization over $Q_{g}$ captures local prompt- and preference-level shifts. To solve the inner maximization, we first define the worst-case preference loss of group $g$ at a fixed prompt and response pair $(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{v})$ as

 $\psi_{g}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{v};\boldsymbol{\theta}):=\sup_{Q_{g}^{S}\in\mathcal{U}_{g,\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{v}}^{S}}\mathbb{E}_{S\sim Q_{g}^{S}}\big[\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{v},S;\boldsymbol{\theta})\big].$ 

(8)

Since $\mathcal{U}_{g}$ is rectangular, the adversarial choices of the prompt marginal $Q_{g}^{X}$ and the conditional preference kernel $Q_{g}^{S}(\cdot\mid X,V)$ are decoupled, which enables a nested decomposition of the worst-case expectation over $Q_{g}$ (Shapiro, 2016; Li and Shapiro, 2025). Consequently, Eq. (7) can be rewritten as

 $\inf_{\boldsymbol{\theta}\in\Theta}\sup_{\boldsymbol{\omega}\in\mathcal{U}^{\omega}}\sum\nolimits_{g=1}^{m}\omega_{g}\sup_{Q_{g}^{X}\in\mathcal{U}_{g}^{X}}\mathbb{E}_{X\sim Q_{g}^{X},\;V\sim P_{g}^{V}(\cdot\mid X)}\bigl[\psi_{g}(X,V;\boldsymbol{\theta})\bigr].$ 

(9)

The above tri-level minimax structure mirrors the three sources of distribution shift identified in Section 3.1. Hierarchical optimization structures have been
studied in trilevel learning (Jiao et al., 2025b; Jian et al., 2024), whereas our formulation focuses on nested distributional uncertainty in preference alignment. When $\rho_{g}^{X}=\rho_{g}^{S}=0$ for all groups, TPO reduces to standard Group DRO.

## 3.3 Finite-Sample Guarantees

We establish a finite-sample convergence guarantee for the fixed-penalty Wasserstein dual objective of TPO, different from algorithmic convergence guarantees
for bilevel and trilevel optimization (Jiao et al., 2024; Jiao et al., 2023). The analysis relies on the following two assumptions.

Assumption 1 (Log-linear policy class and boundedness)

Let $\phi:\mathcal{H}\times\mathcal{Y}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}$ be a feature map satisfying $\sup_{\boldsymbol{h}\in\mathcal{H},\,\boldsymbol{y}\in\mathcal{Y}}\|\phi(\boldsymbol{h},\boldsymbol{y})\|_{2}\leq C_{\phi}$ , where $\mathcal{H}$ includes both clean and perturbed prompt embeddings. Let $\Theta=\{\boldsymbol{\theta}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:\|\boldsymbol{\theta}\|_{2}\leq B\}$ , and $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}=\pi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\rm ref}}$ satisfy $\|\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\rm ref}\|_{2}\leq B$ . We consider the log-linear policy class

 $\Pi=\left\{\pi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}:\pi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(y\mid\boldsymbol{h})=\frac{\exp(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\top}\phi(\boldsymbol{h},\boldsymbol{y}))}{\sum_{\boldsymbol{y^{\prime}}\in\mathcal{Y}}\exp(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\top}\phi(\boldsymbol{h},\boldsymbol{y}^{\prime}))},\ \boldsymbol{\theta}\in\Theta\right\}.$ 

Assumption 2 (Uniform covariance coverage)

Let $z(\boldsymbol{h},\boldsymbol{y}^{+}\!,\boldsymbol{y}^{-})\!=\!\phi(\boldsymbol{h},\boldsymbol{y}^{+})-\phi(\boldsymbol{h},\boldsymbol{y}^{-})$ .
For each group $g$ and $\boldsymbol{\theta}\in\Theta$ , assume the inner maximization in Eq. (20) admits a measurable maximizer $\boldsymbol{\delta}_{g,\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\star}(\boldsymbol{h},\boldsymbol{y}^{+},\boldsymbol{y}^{-})$ , and let $\tilde{\boldsymbol{h}}=\boldsymbol{h}+\boldsymbol{\delta}_{g,\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\star}(\boldsymbol{h},\boldsymbol{y}^{+},\boldsymbol{y}^{-})$ .
There exists $\lambda>0$ such that, for all $\boldsymbol{\theta}\in\Theta$ , $\boldsymbol{\omega}\in\mathcal{U}^{\omega}$ , and all such maximizers,
 $\sum_{g=1}^{m}\omega_{g}\,\mathbb{E}_{P_{g}}\!\left[z(\tilde{\boldsymbol{h}},\boldsymbol{y}^{+},\boldsymbol{y}^{-})\,z(\tilde{\boldsymbol{h}},\boldsymbol{y}^{+},\boldsymbol{y}^{-})^{\top}\right]\succeq\lambda I_{d}.$ 

For each group $g$ , we fix $\gamma_{g}>0$ in Eq. (19) and define the empirical risk as $\widehat{\mathcal{R}}_{g}(\boldsymbol{\theta})=\gamma_{g}\rho_{g}^{X}+\frac{1}{n_{g}}\sum_{i\in I_{g}}\ell_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{g}(\boldsymbol{z}_{i};\gamma_{g})$ , where $\ell_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{g}$ is given by Eq. (20). The corresponding population risk $\mathcal{R}_{g}(\boldsymbol{\theta})$ replaces the empirical average with $\mathbb{E}_{P_{g}}$ . The hierarchical population and empirical objectives are $\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{\textsc{TPO}{}}}(\boldsymbol{\theta})=\sup_{\boldsymbol{\omega}\in\mathcal{U}^{\omega}}\sum_{g=1}^{m}\omega_{g}\mathcal{R}_{g}(\boldsymbol{\theta})$ and $\widehat{\mathcal{R}}_{\mathrm{\textsc{TPO}{}}}(\boldsymbol{\theta})=\sup_{\boldsymbol{\omega}\in\mathcal{U}^{\omega}}\sum_{g=1}^{m}\omega_{g}\widehat{\mathcal{R}}_{g}(\boldsymbol{\theta})$ , respectively. Let $\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\star}\in\arg\min_{\boldsymbol{\theta}\in\Theta}\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{\textsc{TPO}{}}}(\boldsymbol{\theta})$ and $\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\in\arg\min_{\boldsymbol{\theta}\in\Theta}\widehat{\mathcal{R}}_{\mathrm{\textsc{TPO}{}}}(\boldsymbol{\theta})$ denote the population and empirical minimizers, respectively. The following theorem establishes a convergence rate governed by the smallest group sample size $n_{\min}=\min_{g\in[m]}n_{g}$ .

Theorem 1 (Finite-sample convergence of TPO)

Let $M_{\beta}=4\beta BC_{\phi}$ and $\kappa_{\beta}=\frac{\beta^{2}e^{M_{\beta}}}{(1+e^{M_{\beta}})^{2}}$ . Suppose Assumptions 1 and 2 hold. With probability at least $1-\delta$ , we have

 $\|\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}-\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\star}\|_{2}\leq 2\sqrt{\frac{\max_{g\in[m]}\left[2A_{g}\sqrt{\frac{d}{n_{g}}}+K_{g}\sqrt{\frac{\log(2m/\delta)}{2n_{g}}}\right]}{\kappa_{\beta}\lambda}},$ 

where $K_{g}=\log(1+e^{M_{\beta}})+\rho_{g}^{S}M_{\beta}$ ,
 $L_{g}=2\beta C_{\phi}(1+\rho_{g}^{S})$ ,
 $A_{g}=CK_{g}\sqrt{\log\!\big(e+3BL_{g}/K_{g}\big)}$ ,
and $C>0$ is a universal constant. The proof is in Appendix A.3.

## 4 Implementation Algorithm

## 4.1 Wasserstein Instantiation

To obtain a tractable learning objective, we instantiate the discrepancy measures $\mathcal{D}_{\omega}$ , $\mathcal{D}_{X}$ , and $\mathcal{D}_{S}$ with the Wasserstein distance (Wiesemann et al., 2014; Esfahani and Kuhn, 2015; Sinha et al., 2018).

Definition 1 (Wasserstein Distance)

Let $P$ and $Q$ be probability measures on a measurable space $\mathcal{Z}$ equipped with a cost function $c:\mathcal{Z}\times\mathcal{Z}\to[0,\infty)$ .
The Wasserstein distance induced by $c$ is

 $\mathcal{W}_{c}(P,Q):=\inf_{\xi\in\Gamma(P,Q)}\int_{\mathcal{Z}\times\mathcal{Z}}c(u,u^{\prime})\,\mathrm{d}\xi(u,u^{\prime}),$ 

(10)

where $\Gamma(P,Q)$ denotes the set of all couplings of $P$ and $Q$ .

At the global level, the uncertainty concerns the mixture weights over the finite group set $\mathcal{G}=\{1,\dots,m\}$ . Since the groups have no natural geometry, we equip $\mathcal{G}$ with the discrete cost $d_{G}(g,g^{\prime})=\mathbf{1}[g\neq g^{\prime}]$ . Under this cost, the Wasserstein distance between two group mixtures reduces to the total variation distance, i.e., $\mathcal{W}_{d_{G}}(\boldsymbol{\omega},\boldsymbol{\alpha})=\frac{1}{2}\|\boldsymbol{\omega}-\boldsymbol{\alpha}\|_{1}$ .
At the local level, we model within-group uncertainty through Wasserstein balls:

 $\mathcal{W}_{c_{X}}(Q_{g}^{X},P_{g}^{X})\leq\rho_{g}^{X},\qquad\mathcal{W}_{c_{S}}\!\left(Q_{g}^{S}(\cdot\mid\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{v}),P_{g}^{S}(\cdot\mid\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{v})\right)\leq\rho_{g}^{S}.$ 

Here, $c_{X}$ is the transport cost on the prompt space and $c_{S}$ is the label-flip cost on $\{+1,-1\}$ . The radii $\rho_{g}^{X}$ and $\rho_{g}^{S}$ independently control prompt-level and preference-level robustness within group $g$ .

## 4.2 Tractable Objective

In this section, we derive a tractable reformulation by successively robustifying the conditional preference distribution, the prompt marginal distribution, and the distribution over group weights.

## Preference-level robustification.

Recall that, for group $g$ , the conditional distribution factorizes as $P_{g}(V,S\mid X)=P_{g}^{V}(V\mid X)\,P_{g}^{S}(S\mid X,V)$ . Since the candidate response pair $V$ is observed and fixed in the offline dataset, the remaining preference-level uncertainty lies in the conditional distribution over binary labels $P_{g}^{S}(S\mid X,V)$ . We therefore model preference-level shift as a perturbation of this binary label distribution, holding the response pair fixed. In standard DPO data, each comparison is encoded with $S=+1$ , so $P_{g}^{S}(\cdot\mid\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{v})=\delta_{+1}$ . For each group $g$ and each prompt and response pair $(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{v})=(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}_{1},\boldsymbol{y}_{2})$ , we define

 $\mathcal{U}_{g,\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{v}}^{S}=\Bigl\{q\in\Delta(\{+1,-1\}):\mathcal{W}_{c_{S}}(q,\delta_{+1})\leq\rho_{g}^{S}\Bigr\},$ 

(11)

with the label-flip cost $c_{S}(s,s^{\prime})=\mathbf{1}[s\neq s^{\prime}]$ . Under this cost, $\mathcal{W}_{c_{S}}(q,\delta_{+1})$ equals the probability of $s=-1$ under $q$ , so the ball in Eq. (11) allows at most a fraction $\rho_{g}^{S}$ of the label mass to be flipped from $+1$ to $-1$ . The worst-case preference-level robust loss is then

 $\bar{\mathcal{L}}_{g}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}_{1},\boldsymbol{y}_{2};\boldsymbol{\theta}):=\sup_{q\in\mathcal{U}_{g,\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{v}}^{S}}\mathbb{E}_{S\sim q}\bigl[\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}_{1},\boldsymbol{y}_{2},S;\boldsymbol{\theta})\bigr].$ 

(12)

Proposition 1 (Closed-form preference-level robust loss)

For any group $g$ , prompt and candidate response pair $(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}_{1},\boldsymbol{y}_{2})$ , we have

 $\bar{\mathcal{L}}_{g}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}_{1},\boldsymbol{y}_{2};\boldsymbol{\theta})=\varphi\bigl(m(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}_{1},\boldsymbol{y}_{2};\boldsymbol{\theta})\bigr)+\rho_{g}^{S}\bigl[m(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}_{1},\boldsymbol{y}_{2};\boldsymbol{\theta})\bigr]_{+},$ 

(13)

where $[t]_{+}=\max\{t,0\}$ . The proof is provided in Appendix A.1.

Proposition 2 (Optimal robust preference margin)

For any group $g$ , let $M=m(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}_{1},\boldsymbol{y}_{2};\boldsymbol{\theta})$ denote the DPO margin defined in Eq. (1). Then $\mathcal{L}_{g}(M)=\varphi(M)+\rho_{g}^{S}[M]_{+}$ is minimized at

 $M_{g}^{\star}=\begin{cases}+\infty,&\rho_{g}^{S}=0,\\[4.0pt]
\log\dfrac{1-\rho_{g}^{S}}{\rho_{g}^{S}},&0<\rho_{g}^{S}<\tfrac{1}{2},\\[8.0pt]
0,&\rho_{g}^{S}\geq\tfrac{1}{2}.\end{cases}$ 

(14)

The proof is provided in Appendix A.2.

Remark 1

Proposition 1 shows that preference-level Wasserstein robustness yields a robustified loss that appends a one-sided margin penalty scaled by $\rho_{g}^{S}$ .
Proposition 2 indicates that, unlike standard DPO, preference-level Wasserstein robustness does not drive the margin to grow unboundedly. Instead, it yields a finite optimal target margin. Moreover, this target margin decreases as $\rho_{g}^{S}$ increases, implying that stronger uncertainty in the conditional preference distribution yields a more conservative optimum and reduces overconfidence in weak or potentially misaligned preferences.

## Prompt-level robustification.

We next robustify the prompt marginal distribution. For each group $g$ , the local robust risk is

 $\mathcal{R}_{g}(\boldsymbol{\theta}):=\sup_{Q_{g}^{X}\in\mathcal{U}_{g}^{X}}\mathbb{E}_{X\sim Q_{g}^{X},\;V\sim P_{g}^{V}(\cdot\mid X)}\bigl[\psi_{g}(X,V;\boldsymbol{\theta})\bigr],$ 

(15)

where $\psi_{g}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{v};\boldsymbol{\theta})$ is the worst-case preference loss in Eq. (8). Under the label-flip Wasserstein ball in Eq. (11), $\psi_{g}$ admits the closed form $\bar{\mathcal{L}}_{g}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}_{1},\boldsymbol{y}_{2};\boldsymbol{\theta})$ given in Proposition 1.

To obtain a tractable empirical formulation, we follow the robust NLP fine-tuning literature and perturb prompts in the embedding space rather than the discrete token space (Miyato et al., 2017; Zhu et al., 2020; Jiang et al., 2020). Let $\boldsymbol{h}=\mathcal{E}(\boldsymbol{x})\in\mathbb{R}^{d}$ denote the prompt embedding and $\boldsymbol{z}_{i}=(\boldsymbol{h}_{i},\boldsymbol{y}_{i}^{+},\boldsymbol{y}_{i}^{-})$ the $i$ -th embedded observation. For group $g$ , the empirical distribution over embedded preference observations is $\widehat{P}_{g}=\frac{1}{n_{g}}\sum_{i\in I_{g}}\delta_{\boldsymbol{z}_{i}}$ .

Following Wasserstein DRO with local perturbations (Kwon et al., 2020) and embedding-space adversarial training in NLP (Zhu et al., 2020; Miyato et al., 2017; Jiang et al., 2020), we perturb the prompt embedding while keeping the response pair fixed. Accordingly, we define the transport cost

 $c_{X}\bigl((\boldsymbol{h},\boldsymbol{y}^{+},\boldsymbol{y}^{-}),(\boldsymbol{h}^{\prime},\tilde{\boldsymbol{y}}^{+},\tilde{\boldsymbol{y}}^{-})\bigr)=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{2}\|\boldsymbol{h}-\boldsymbol{h}^{\prime}\|_{2}^{2},&(\boldsymbol{y}^{+},\boldsymbol{y}^{-})=(\tilde{\boldsymbol{y}}^{+},\tilde{\boldsymbol{y}}^{-}),\\
+\infty,&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}$ 

(16)

The empirical local robust risk is then

 $\widehat{\mathcal{R}}_{g}(\boldsymbol{\theta})=\sup_{\widetilde{Q}_{g}^{X}:\,\mathcal{W}_{c_{X}}(\widetilde{Q}_{g}^{X},\widehat{P}_{g})\leq\rho_{g}^{X}}\mathbb{E}_{(\boldsymbol{h},\boldsymbol{y}^{+},\boldsymbol{y}^{-})\sim\widetilde{Q}_{g}^{X}}\bigl[\bar{\mathcal{L}}_{g}(\boldsymbol{h},\boldsymbol{y}^{+},\boldsymbol{y}^{-};\boldsymbol{\theta})\bigr].$ 

(17)

Lemma 1 (Wasserstein DRO duality (Sinha et al., 2018; Zhang et al., 2025))

Under standard regularity conditions for Wasserstein DRO duality, for any reference distribution $Q$ , radius $\rho\geq 0$ , loss $\ell$ , and transport cost $c$ ,

 $\sup_{P:\,\mathcal{W}_{c}(P,Q)\leq\rho}\mathbb{E}_{P}[\ell(Z)]=\inf_{\gamma\geq 0}\Bigl\{\gamma\rho+\mathbb{E}_{Q}\bigl[\ell_{p}(Z;\gamma)\bigr]\Bigr\},$ 

(18)

where $\ell_{p}(u_{0};\gamma)=\sup_{u}\{\ell(u)-\gamma\,c(u,u_{0})\}$ is the Wasserstein dual surrogate loss.

Applying Lemma 1 to Eq. (17) with $\ell=\bar{\mathcal{L}}_{g}$ and $c=c_{X}$ yields the dual form

 $\widehat{\mathcal{R}}_{g}(\boldsymbol{\theta})=\inf_{\gamma_{g}\geq 0}\left\{\gamma_{g}\rho_{g}^{X}+\frac{1}{n_{g}}\sum_{i\in I_{g}}\ell_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{g}(\boldsymbol{z}_{i};\gamma_{g})\right\},$ 

(19)

where $\boldsymbol{z}_{i}=(\boldsymbol{h}_{i},\boldsymbol{y}_{i}^{+},\boldsymbol{y}_{i}^{-})$ . Under the transport cost in Eq. (16), changing the response pair incurs infinite cost, so the inner maximization only searches over perturbations of the prompt embedding:

 $\ell_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{g}(\boldsymbol{z}_{i};\gamma_{g})=\sup_{\boldsymbol{\delta}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left\{\bar{\mathcal{L}}_{g}(\boldsymbol{h}_{i}+\boldsymbol{\delta},\boldsymbol{y}_{i}^{+},\boldsymbol{y}_{i}^{-};\boldsymbol{\theta})-\frac{\gamma_{g}}{2}\|\boldsymbol{\delta}\|_{2}^{2}\right\}.$ 

(20)

## Final empirical objective.

Combining the global Wasserstein reweighting with the dual form of the local risks, the final empirical TPO objective is

 $\min_{\boldsymbol{\theta}\in\Theta}\sup_{\boldsymbol{\omega}\in\mathcal{U}^{\omega}}\sum\nolimits_{g=1}^{m}\omega_{g}\,\widehat{\mathcal{R}}_{g}(\boldsymbol{\theta}),$ 

(21)

where $\widehat{\mathcal{R}}_{g}(\boldsymbol{\theta})$ is given in Eq. (19) and $\bar{\mathcal{L}}_{g}$ in Eq. (13). Eq. (21) reduces the abstract hierarchical Wasserstein robust objective to a concrete min-max training problem.

## 4.3 Optimization Algorithm

While the dual reformulation in Eq. (20) is exact,
explicitly optimizing over $\gamma_{g}$ at each training step is computationally
prohibitive. Following standard practice in Wasserstein robust
optimization (Sinha et al., 2018; Kwon et al., 2020; Zhang et al., 2019; Bai et al., 2025), we treat
 $\gamma_{g}$ as a fixed hyperparameter and constrain each perturbation to
 $\mathcal{B}_{g}^{X}=\{\boldsymbol{\delta}:\|\boldsymbol{\delta}\|_{2}^{2}\leq 2\rho_{g}^{X}\}$ . For each group $g$ , the resulting
penalty-based surrogate objective is

 $\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}_{g}(\boldsymbol{\theta};\gamma_{g}):=\gamma_{g}\rho_{g}^{X}+\frac{1}{n_{g}}\sum\nolimits_{i\in I_{g}}\sup_{\boldsymbol{\delta}\in\mathcal{B}_{g}^{X}}\left[\bar{\mathcal{L}}_{g}(\boldsymbol{h}_{i}+\boldsymbol{\delta},\boldsymbol{y}_{i}^{+},\boldsymbol{y}_{i}^{-};\boldsymbol{\theta})-\frac{\gamma_{g}}{2}\|\boldsymbol{\delta}\|_{2}^{2}\right].$ 

(22)

The full training procedure is summarized in Algorithm 1.
Each iteration proceeds in three stages.
(i) Adversarial Perturbation.
For each embedded preference observation
 $(\boldsymbol{h}_{i},\boldsymbol{y}_{i}^{+},\boldsymbol{y}_{i}^{-})$ in the mini-batch,
we approximate the prompt-level supremum in
Eq. (22) by running $K$ steps of projected gradient ascent on
 $\boldsymbol{\delta}$ with the fixed quadratic penalty.
(ii) Group-weight update.
Using the perturbed embeddings from stage (i), we compute group-wise stochastic
estimates $\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}_{g}^{(t)}$ and perform one step of exponentiated
gradient ascent projected onto $\mathcal{U}^{\omega}$ , which implements the
worst-case group reweighting over the $L_{1}$ -constrained simplex.
(iii) Policy update. We update the policy parameters $\boldsymbol{\theta}$ 
by taking a gradient descent step on the weighted
surrogate objective
 $\sum_{g=1}^{m}\omega_{g}^{(t)}\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}_{g}^{(t)}$ ,
with the adversarial perturbations and group weights fixed.
The alternating procedure is widely used in standard adversarial training and worst-group reweighting in robust optimization (Jiao et al., 2025a; Madry et al., 2018; Sagawa et al., 2020; Sinha et al., 2018).

## 5 Experiments

## 5.1 Experimental Setup

We evaluate TPO on three tasks: OOD preference alignment, preference poisoning, and downstream generalization. For OOD preference alignment and downstream generalization, we compare against standard DPO and several recent robust baselines: GRPO (Ramesh et al., 2024), Dr.DPO (Wu et al., 2025), and WDPO (Xu et al., 2025). We also include three variants of TPO: TPO (Group), TPO (Prompt), and TPO (Preference), which activate only the group-level, prompt-level, and preference-level robustness components, respectively. Note that TPO (Group) reduces to GRPO (Ramesh et al., 2024) as a special case. For preference poisoning, we compare against rDPO (Chowdhury et al., 2024) and Dr.DPO (Wu et al., 2025), two robust DPO baselines designed to mitigate noisy preferences.

## OOD Preference Alignment.

Following prior emotion-based evaluations of preference shift (Xu et al., 2025; Sahu and Wells, 2025), we construct a benchmark from the Emotion dataset (Saravia et al., 2018) with four shift environments: (1) Group-level shift: we partition samples into six latent groups via $K$ -means clustering on prompt and response embeddings, and change the group mixture weights at test time; (2) Prompt-level shift: we apply synonym substitution to prompts to simulate prompt (covariate) shift; (3) Preference-level shift: we construct two reward functions $r_{1}$ and $r_{2}$ and simulate preference shift via the convex interpolation $r_{\alpha}=\alpha r_{1}+(1{-}\alpha)r_{2}$ , which shifts the conditional preference distribution $P_{g}^{S}(S\mid V,X)$ ; and (4) Combined shift: all three shifts are applied simultaneously. We use Qwen2.5-0.5B-Instruct (Yang et al., 2025) and LLaMA-3.2-1B-Instruct (Grattafiori et al., 2024) as base models, and report average and worst-group preference accuracy (Wu et al., 2025) to measure overall performance and worst-case robustness, respectively. Training details are provided in Appendix B.1.

## Preference Poisoning Attack Robustness.

To evaluate robustness against training-time preference poisoning, we follow established poisoning protocols in RLHF (Wang et al., 2024b; Pathmanathan et al., 2025; Kusaka et al., 2026) and construct poisoned training sets by flipping the preference labels (i.e., swapping the preferred and rejected responses) of a fraction of the training pairs, while keeping the test set clean. We consider two attack strategies: (1) Random preference flip attack (Pathmanathan et al., 2025), where poisoned pairs are selected uniformly at random; and (2) Margin-based preference flip attack (Pathmanathan et al., 2025), where the adversary targets the pairs with the largest margins computed by the reward model used for preference construction. We use LLaMA-3.2-1B-Instruct (Grattafiori et al., 2024) as the base model and evaluate all methods on clean test preferences. Details are provided in Appendix B.2.

## Downstream Generalization.

To assess the practical robustness of TPO under naturally occurring distribution shift, we evaluate on the Open LLM Leaderboard v2 (Hugging Face, 2024). We fine-tune LLaMA-3.2-1B-Instruct, LLaMA-3.2-3B-Instruct, and LLaMA-3.1-8B-Instruct for one epoch. Following the pipeline of Meng et al. (2024), we collect on-policy data annotated by the ArmoRM reward model (Wang et al., 2024a). Full training and evaluation details are provided in Appendix B.3.

## 5.2 Experimental Results

## OOD Preference Alignment Results.

As shown in Table 2, TPO achieves the best average accuracy across all shift environments on both backbones, while attaining competitive or best worst-group accuracy in most settings. Notably, single-level variants TPO (Group), TPO (Prompt), and TPO (Preference) each provide partial gains, but their combination in TPO yields more consistent performance across both metrics and nearly all shift environments, suggesting the complementary nature of the three robustness levels.

## Preference Poisoning Attack Robustness Results.

Figure 1: Preference accuracy under preference poisoning.

As shown in Figure 1, all methods degrade as the poisoning rate increases. Under random preference flip attacks, rDPO performs best as expected, given its explicit debiasing design for random label flips. TPO is not specifically designed for this setting, yet generally outperforms DPO and remains competitive with Dr.DPO. Under margin-based flip attacks, Dr.DPO performs strongly at low poisoning rates, but TPO surpasses all baselines at higher poisoning rates. Unlike rDPO and Dr.DPO, which rely on identifying potentially corrupted pairs, TPO penalizes excessively large margins, encouraging the model to remain conservative under uncertain preference distributions, which explains its more stable robustness against strong adaptive poisoning.

## Downstream Generalization Results.

As shown in Table 3, TPO achieves competitive or improved performance over DPO on most benchmarks across the three model scales, suggesting that the proposed robustness mechanisms largely preserve general capabilities. Gains are most pronounced on IFEval and also appear on BBH, while performance on GPQA and MMLU-Pro remains largely stable. We also observe that different preference-level robustness radii lead to varying trade-offs across tasks, which is expected given the heterogeneous nature of the leaderboard benchmarks. Interestingly, the improvements tend to become larger on IFEval as model scale increases, suggesting that larger models may benefit more from the proposed robustness mechanisms on instruction-following tasks. Overall, these results suggest that hierarchical robustness can improve downstream generalization while largely retaining broad task performance.

## 6 Related Work

## Robust Preference Optimization.

DPO simplifies preference alignment by directly optimizing over pairwise data (Rafailov et al., 2023; Azar et al., 2024), but remains vulnerable to distribution shift at deployment (Ramesh et al., 2024; Wu et al., 2025). Recent robust DPO methods address this from two angles: WDPO and KLDPO (Xu et al., 2025) robustify DPO using ambiguity sets over the overall preference distribution, while rDPO (Chowdhury et al., 2024) and Dr.DPO (Wu et al., 2025) target preference noise. Separately, group robust methods such as GRPO (Ramesh et al., 2024) and pluralistic alignment work (Sorensen et al., 2024; Yao et al., 2025; Tao et al., 2024) motivate modeling preference heterogeneity across groups and cultural contexts. However, none of these methods provide a unified robust objective that simultaneously captures group-level shift, prompt-level shift, and conditional preference shift.

## Distributionally Robust Optimization.

DRO provides a min-max framework for learning under distribution shift (Jiao et al., 2022; Jiao et al., 2026; Esfahani and Kuhn, 2015; Wiesemann et al., 2014). Wasserstein DRO (Sinha et al., 2018; Esfahani and Kuhn, 2015) admits tractable dual reformulations and connects naturally to adversarial perturbations in embedding spaces (Madry et al., 2018; Zhu et al., 2020; Jiang et al., 2020). Group DRO (Sagawa et al., 2020) optimizes worst-case group performance but treats each group distribution as fixed, leaving within-group shifts unaddressed (Koh et al., 2021; Chowdhury et al., 2024). Recent work on hierarchical ambiguity sets (Jo et al., 2026) argues that group-level robustness alone is insufficient. Our work brings this perspective to preference optimization via a hierarchically structured rectangular ambiguity set that further decomposes intra-group uncertainty into prompt-level and preference-level shifts, thereby providing a more complete and interpretable robust alignment framework.

## 7 Conclusion

We proposed tri-level distributionally robust preference optimization (TPO), which addresses OOD preference alignment by jointly modeling three levels of distribution shift through a hierarchically structured rectangular ambiguity set. By instantiating it with Wasserstein distances, we obtain an objective that combines worst-case group reweighting, prompt-level adversarial perturbation, and a closed-form preference-level margin penalty. Theoretical analysis establishes finite-sample convergence guarantees, and comprehensive experiments demonstrate the effectiveness of our method.

## Appendix A Proofs

## A.1 Proof of Proposition 1

Recall that, for a fixed group $g$ and a fixed prompt and response pair
 $(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}_{1},\boldsymbol{y}_{2})$ , the preference-level robust loss is defined as

 $\bar{\mathcal{L}}_{g}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}_{1},\boldsymbol{y}_{2};\boldsymbol{\theta})=\sup_{q\in\mathcal{U}_{g}^{S}}\mathbb{E}_{S\sim q}\big[\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}_{1},\boldsymbol{y}_{2},S;\boldsymbol{\theta})\big],$ 

(23)

where

 $\mathcal{U}_{g}^{S}=\left\{q\in\Delta(\{+1,-1\}):\mathcal{W}_{c_{P}}(q,\delta_{+1})\leq\rho_{g}^{S}\right\},\qquad c_{P}(s,s^{\prime})=\mathbf{1}[s\neq s^{\prime}].$ 

(24)

Here, $\delta_{+1}$ corresponds to the canonicalized observed preference label
 $S=+1$ , obtained by ordering the candidate pair as
 $(\boldsymbol{y}_{1},\boldsymbol{y}_{2})=(\boldsymbol{y}^{+},\boldsymbol{y}^{-})$ .

Since the label space contains only two points, any
 $q\in\Delta(\{+1,-1\})$ can be parameterized as
 $q(+1)=1-\varepsilon,$ 
where $q(-1)=\varepsilon$ for some $\varepsilon\in[0,1]$ . Under the discrete label-flip cost $c_{P}$ , the
Wasserstein distance from $q$ to $\delta_{+1}$ is exactly the mass assigned to
the flipped label $\mathcal{W}_{c_{P}}(q,\delta_{+1})=\varepsilon$ .
Therefore, assuming $0\leq\rho_{g}^{S}\leq 1$ , the Wasserstein constraint is equivalent to $0\leq\varepsilon\leq\rho_{g}^{S}$ .

Let $M=m(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}_{1},\boldsymbol{y}_{2};\boldsymbol{\theta})$ . Using the labeled DPO loss $\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}_{1},\boldsymbol{y}_{2},S;\boldsymbol{\theta})=\varphi(S\cdot M)$ , we have

 $\displaystyle\bar{\mathcal{L}}_{g}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}_{1},\boldsymbol{y}_{2};\boldsymbol{\theta})$ 
 $\displaystyle=\sup_{0\leq\varepsilon\leq\rho_{g}^{S}}\Big[(1-\varepsilon)\varphi(M)+\varepsilon\,\varphi(-M)\Big]$ 

(25)

 $\displaystyle=\varphi(M)+\sup_{0\leq\varepsilon\leq\rho_{g}^{S}}\varepsilon\bigl(\varphi(-M)-\varphi(M)\bigr).$ 

For $\varphi(t)=\log(1+e^{-t})$ , we have $\varphi(-t)-\varphi(t)=t$ . Thus, we can rewrite the preference-level robust loss as

 $\bar{\mathcal{L}}_{g}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}_{1},\boldsymbol{y}_{2};\boldsymbol{\theta})=\varphi(M)+\sup_{0\leq\varepsilon\leq\rho_{g}^{S}}\varepsilon M.$ 

(26)

Since the last term is linear in $\varepsilon$ , its supremum is attained at
 $\varepsilon=\rho_{g}^{S}$ when $M\geq 0$ , and at $\varepsilon=0$ when $M<0$ .
Therefore, we have

 $\sup_{0\leq\varepsilon\leq\rho_{g}^{S}}\varepsilon M=\rho_{g}^{S}[M]_{+}.$ 

(27)

Substituting this back, we obtain

 $\bar{\mathcal{L}}_{g}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}_{1},\boldsymbol{y}_{2};\boldsymbol{\theta})=\varphi\bigl(m(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}_{1},\boldsymbol{y}_{2};\boldsymbol{\theta})\bigr)+\rho_{g}^{S}\bigl[m(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}_{1},\boldsymbol{y}_{2};\boldsymbol{\theta})\bigr]_{+},$ 

(28)

which completes the proof of Proposition 1.

## A.2 Proof of Proposition 2

Let $\mathcal{L}_{g}(M)=\varphi(M)+\rho_{g}^{S}[M]_{+}$ and $\varphi(M)=\log(1+e^{-M})$ . We analyze the minimizer over $M\in\mathbb{R}$ . First, when $\rho_{g}^{S}=0$ , the objective reduces to $\mathcal{L}_{g}(M)=\varphi(M)$ . Since $\varphi^{\prime}(M)=-\frac{1}{1+e^{M}}<0$ , $\varphi(M)$ is strictly decreasing on $\mathbb{R}$ . Therefore, its infimum is approached as $M\to+\infty$ , and the optimal target margin is $M_{g}^{\star}=+\infty$ .

We now consider the case $\rho_{g}^{S}>0$ . Since $[M]_{+}$ is piecewise linear, we
analyze the cases $M<0$ and $M>0$ separately. For $M<0$ , we have $[M]_{+}=0$ , hence, we obtain $\mathcal{L}_{g}(M)=\varphi(M)$ . Its derivative satisfies $\mathcal{L}_{g}^{\prime}(M)=\varphi^{\prime}(M)=-\frac{1}{1+e^{M}}<0$ . Therefore, $\mathcal{L}_{g}(M)$ is strictly decreasing on $(-\infty,0)$ .

For $M>0$ , we have $[M]_{+}=M$ , and thus

 $\mathcal{L}_{g}(M)=\varphi(M)+\rho_{g}^{S}M.$ 

(29)

Its derivative is

 $\mathcal{L}_{g}^{\prime}(M)=-\frac{1}{1+e^{M}}+\rho_{g}^{S}.$ 

(30)

Setting $\mathcal{L}_{g}^{\prime}(M)=0$ yields

 $\frac{1}{1+e^{M}}=\rho_{g}^{S}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad e^{M}=\frac{1-\rho_{g}^{S}}{\rho_{g}^{S}},$ 

(31)

so any stationary point on $(0,\infty)$ must satisfy

 $M=\log\frac{1-\rho_{g}^{S}}{\rho_{g}^{S}}.$ 

(32)

This stationary point is positive if and only if
 $0<\rho_{g}^{S}<\tfrac{1}{2}$ .

When $0<\rho_{g}^{S}<\tfrac{1}{2}$ , the derivative
 $\mathcal{L}_{g}^{\prime}(M)$ changes sign from negative to positive at

 $M_{g}^{\star}=\log\frac{1-\rho_{g}^{S}}{\rho_{g}^{S}},$ 

(33)

so this point is the global minimizer.

When $\rho_{g}^{S}\geq\tfrac{1}{2}$ , the stationary point above is non-positive.
Moreover, for all $M>0$ , we have

 $\mathcal{L}_{g}^{\prime}(M)=-\frac{1}{1+e^{M}}+\rho_{g}^{S}>0,$ 

(34)

so $\mathcal{L}_{g}(M)$ is increasing on $(0,\infty)$ . Since it is decreasing on
 $(-\infty,0)$ , the global minimizer is attained at the boundary $M=0$ .

Therefore, we have shown that the optimal target margin is

 $M_{g}^{\star}\in\arg\min_{M\in\mathbb{R}}\mathcal{L}_{g}(M)=\begin{cases}+\infty,&\rho_{g}^{S}=0,\\[4.0pt]
\log\dfrac{1-\rho_{g}^{S}}{\rho_{g}^{S}},&0<\rho_{g}^{S}<\frac{1}{2},\\[8.0pt]
0,&\rho_{g}^{S}\geq\frac{1}{2},\end{cases}$ 

(35)

which proves Proposition 2.

## A.3 Proof of Theorem 1

Theorem 1 analyzes the population and empirical
minimizers of the fixed-penalty Wasserstein dual objective, with each
penalty parameter $\gamma_{g}>0$ held fixed. Throughout this subsection,
 $\mathcal{R}_{g}$ and $\mathcal{R}_{\rm\textsc{TPO}{}}$ refer to this
fixed-penalty objective, without minimization over $\gamma_{g}$ .
For a sample, the DPO margin is defined as

 $m(\boldsymbol{h},\boldsymbol{y}^{+},\boldsymbol{y}^{-};\boldsymbol{\theta})=\beta\left(\log\frac{\pi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{y}^{+}\mid\boldsymbol{h})}{\pi_{\rm ref}(\boldsymbol{y}^{+}\mid\boldsymbol{h})}-\log\frac{\pi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{y}^{-}\mid\boldsymbol{h})}{\pi_{\rm ref}(\boldsymbol{y}^{-}\mid\boldsymbol{h})}\right).$ 

(36)

The logistic loss is denoted by $\varphi(t)=\log(1+e^{-t})$ . The
preference-level robust loss for group $g$ is

 $\bar{\mathcal{L}}_{g}(\boldsymbol{h},\boldsymbol{y}^{+},\boldsymbol{y}^{-};\boldsymbol{\theta})=\varphi(m(\boldsymbol{h},\boldsymbol{y}^{+},\boldsymbol{y}^{-};\boldsymbol{\theta}))+\rho_{g}^{S}[m(\boldsymbol{h},\boldsymbol{y}^{+},\boldsymbol{y}^{-};\boldsymbol{\theta})]_{+}.$ 

(37)

The fixed-penalty local robust sample loss is

 $\ell_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{g}(U;\gamma_{g})=\sup_{\boldsymbol{\delta}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left[\bar{\mathcal{L}}_{g}(\boldsymbol{h}+\boldsymbol{\delta},\boldsymbol{y}^{+},\boldsymbol{y}^{-};\boldsymbol{\theta})-\frac{\gamma_{g}}{2}\|\boldsymbol{\delta}\|_{2}^{2}\right].$ 

(38)

The population and empirical local robust risk for group $g$ are

 $\mathcal{R}_{g}(\boldsymbol{\theta})=\gamma_{g}\rho_{g}^{X}+\mathbb{E}_{P_{g}}\big[\ell_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{g}(U;\gamma_{g})\big],\quad\widehat{\mathcal{R}}_{g}(\boldsymbol{\theta})=\gamma_{g}\rho_{g}^{X}+\frac{1}{n_{g}}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{g}}\ell_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{g}(U_{g,i};\gamma_{g}).$ 

(39)

The hierarchical population and empirical TPO objectives are defined as

 $\mathcal{R}_{\rm\textsc{TPO}{}}(\boldsymbol{\theta})=\sup_{\boldsymbol{\omega}\in\mathcal{U}^{\omega}}\sum_{g=1}^{m}\omega_{g}\mathcal{R}_{g}(\boldsymbol{\theta}),\quad\widehat{\mathcal{R}}_{\rm\textsc{TPO}{}}(\boldsymbol{\theta})=\sup_{\boldsymbol{\omega}\in\mathcal{U}^{\omega}}\sum_{g=1}^{m}\omega_{g}\widehat{\mathcal{R}}_{g}(\boldsymbol{\theta}).$ 

(40)

Throughout the proof, we assume that samples are independent within each group, $U_{g,1},\ldots,U_{g,n_{g}}\overset{\rm i.i.d.}{\sim}P_{g}$ .

Lemma 2 (Boundedness and Lipschitzness of the DPO margin)

Suppose Assumption 1 holds. Then, for each original
or adversarially perturbed prompt embedding $\tilde{\boldsymbol{h}}$ , each response pair $(\boldsymbol{y}^{+},\boldsymbol{y}^{-})$ , and each $\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\prime}\in\Theta$ ,

 $|m(\tilde{\boldsymbol{h}},\boldsymbol{y}^{+},\boldsymbol{y}^{-};\boldsymbol{\theta})|\leq M_{\beta},\qquad M_{\beta}:=4\beta BC_{\phi},$ 

(41)

and

 $|m(\tilde{\boldsymbol{h}},\boldsymbol{y}^{+},\boldsymbol{y}^{-};\boldsymbol{\theta})-m(\tilde{\boldsymbol{h}},\boldsymbol{y}^{+},\boldsymbol{y}^{-};\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\prime})|\leq 2\beta C_{\phi}\|\boldsymbol{\theta}-\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\prime}\|_{2}.$ 

(42)

Proof.
Under the log-linear policy class, we have

 $\log\pi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{y}^{+}\mid\tilde{\boldsymbol{h}})-\log\pi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{y}^{-}\mid\tilde{\boldsymbol{h}})=\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\top}\big(\phi(\tilde{\boldsymbol{h}},\boldsymbol{y}^{+})-\phi(\tilde{\boldsymbol{h}},\boldsymbol{y}^{-})\big).$ 

(43)

Since $\pi_{\rm ref}$ belongs to the same class with parameter $\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\rm ref}$ , we have

 $m(\tilde{\boldsymbol{h}},\boldsymbol{y}^{+},\boldsymbol{y}^{-};\boldsymbol{\theta})=\beta\left\langle\phi(\tilde{\boldsymbol{h}},\boldsymbol{y}^{+})-\phi(\tilde{\boldsymbol{h}},\boldsymbol{y}^{-}),\boldsymbol{\theta}-\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\rm ref}\right\rangle.$ 

(44)

By Assumption 1, we have

 $\|\phi(\tilde{\boldsymbol{h}},\boldsymbol{y}^{+})-\phi(\tilde{\boldsymbol{h}},\boldsymbol{y}^{-})\|_{2}\leq 2C_{\phi},\qquad\|\boldsymbol{\theta}-\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\rm ref}\|_{2}\leq 2B.$ 

(45)

Therefore,

 $|m(\tilde{\boldsymbol{h}},\boldsymbol{y}^{+},\boldsymbol{y}^{-};\boldsymbol{\theta})|\leq\beta\cdot 2C_{\phi}\cdot 2B=4\beta BC_{\phi}=M_{\beta}.$ 

(46)

So we can obtain

 $\displaystyle|m(\tilde{\boldsymbol{h}},\boldsymbol{y}^{+},\boldsymbol{y}^{-};\boldsymbol{\theta})-m(\tilde{\boldsymbol{h}},\boldsymbol{y}^{+},\boldsymbol{y}^{-};\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\prime})|$ 

(47)

 $\displaystyle=\beta\left|\left\langle\phi(\tilde{\boldsymbol{h}},\boldsymbol{y}^{+})-\phi(\tilde{\boldsymbol{h}},\boldsymbol{y}^{-}),\boldsymbol{\theta}-\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\prime}\right\rangle\right|$ 

 $\displaystyle\leq\beta\|\phi(\tilde{\boldsymbol{h}},\boldsymbol{y}^{+})-\phi(\tilde{\boldsymbol{h}},\boldsymbol{y}^{-})\|_{2}\|\boldsymbol{\theta}-\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\prime}\|_{2}$ 

 $\displaystyle\leq 2\beta C_{\phi}\|\boldsymbol{\theta}-\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\prime}\|_{2},$ 

which completes the proof.

Lemma 3 (Boundedness and Lipschitzness of $\bar{\mathcal{L}}_{g}$ )

Suppose Assumption 1 holds. Then, for each group $g$ , each original or adversarially perturbed prompt embedding $\tilde{\boldsymbol{h}}$ , each response pair $(\boldsymbol{y}^{+},\boldsymbol{y}^{-})$ , and each $\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\prime}\in\Theta$ , we have

 $0\leq\bar{\mathcal{L}}_{g}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{h}},\boldsymbol{y}^{+},\boldsymbol{y}^{-};\boldsymbol{\theta})\leq K_{g},$ 

where $K_{g}=\log(1+e^{M_{\beta}})+\rho_{g}^{S}M_{\beta}$ and $M_{\beta}=4\beta BC_{\phi}$ .
Moreover, $\bar{\mathcal{L}}_{g}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{h}},\boldsymbol{y}^{+},\boldsymbol{y}^{-};\boldsymbol{\theta})$ is $L_{g}$ -Lipschitz, and $L_{g}=2\beta C_{\phi}(1+\rho_{g}^{S})$ .

Proof. 
By definition,

 $\bar{\mathcal{L}}_{g}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{h}},\boldsymbol{y}^{+},\boldsymbol{y}^{-};\theta)=\varphi(m(\tilde{\boldsymbol{h}},\boldsymbol{y}^{+},\boldsymbol{y}^{-};\theta))+\rho_{g}^{S}[m(\tilde{\boldsymbol{h}},\boldsymbol{y}^{+},\boldsymbol{y}^{-};\theta)]_{+}.$ 

(48)

Since $\varphi(t)\geq 0$ , $[t]_{+}\geq 0$ , and $\rho_{g}^{S}\geq 0$ , we have $\bar{\mathcal{L}}_{g}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{h}},\boldsymbol{y}^{+},\boldsymbol{y}^{-};\boldsymbol{\theta})\geq 0$ .

According to Lemma 2, we have $|m(\tilde{\boldsymbol{h}},\boldsymbol{y}^{+},\boldsymbol{y}^{-};\boldsymbol{\theta})|\leq M_{\beta}$ . Hence, we obtain

 $\displaystyle\varphi\!\left(m(\tilde{\boldsymbol{h}},\boldsymbol{y}^{+},\boldsymbol{y}^{-};\boldsymbol{\theta})\right)$ 
 $\displaystyle\leq\log(1+e^{M_{\beta}}),$ 

(49)

 $\displaystyle\left[m(\tilde{\boldsymbol{h}},\boldsymbol{y}^{+},\boldsymbol{y}^{-};\boldsymbol{\theta})\right]_{+}$ 
 $\displaystyle\leq M_{\beta}.$ 

(50)

Combining (49) and (50), we get

 $\bar{\mathcal{L}}_{g}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{h}},\boldsymbol{y}^{+},\boldsymbol{y}^{-};\boldsymbol{\theta})\leq\log(1+e^{M_{\beta}})+\rho_{g}^{S}M_{\beta}=K_{g}.$ 

(51)

For Lipschitzness, $\varphi(t)=\log(1+e^{-t})$ is $1$ -Lipschitz because $|\varphi^{\prime}(t)|=\frac{1}{1+e^{t}}\leq 1$ . And $t\mapsto[t]_{+}$ is also $1$ -Lipschitz. Therefore,
 $f(t)=\varphi(t)+\rho_{g}^{S}[t]_{+}$ is
 $(1+\rho_{g}^{S})$ -Lipschitz. Combining this with
Lemma 2, we have

 $\displaystyle\left|\bar{\mathcal{L}}_{g}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{h}},\boldsymbol{y}^{+},\boldsymbol{y}^{-};\boldsymbol{\theta})-\bar{\mathcal{L}}_{g}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{h}},\boldsymbol{y}^{+},\boldsymbol{y}^{-};\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\prime})\right|$ 

(52)

 $\displaystyle\leq(1+\rho_{g}^{S})\left|m(\tilde{\boldsymbol{h}},\boldsymbol{y}^{+},\boldsymbol{y}^{-};\boldsymbol{\theta})-m(\tilde{\boldsymbol{h}},\boldsymbol{y}^{+},\boldsymbol{y}^{-};\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\prime})\right|$ 

 $\displaystyle\leq 2\beta C_{\phi}(1+\rho_{g}^{S})\|\boldsymbol{\theta}-\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\prime}\|_{2},$ 

which completes the proof.

Lemma 4 (Rademacher complexity of the local robust loss class)

Let $\mathcal{F}_{g}=\{\ell_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{g}(\cdot;\gamma_{g}):\boldsymbol{\theta}\in\Theta\}$ , where $\gamma_{g}>0$ is fixed. Under Assumption 1,
the class $\mathcal{F}_{g}$ satisfies

 $\mathfrak{R}_{n_{g}}(\mathcal{F}_{g})\leq A_{g}\sqrt{\frac{d}{n_{g}}},$ 

where $A_{g}=CK_{g}\sqrt{\log\left(e+\frac{3BL_{g}}{K_{g}}\right)}$ , $C>0$ is a universal numerical constant, $K_{g}=\log(1+e^{M_{\beta}})+\rho_{g}^{S}M_{\beta}$ , $M_{\beta}=4\beta BC_{\phi}$ , and $L_{g}=2\beta C_{\phi}(1+\rho_{g}^{S}).$ 

Proof. 
We first prove that the fixed-penalty local robust loss
 $\ell_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{g}(U;\gamma_{g})$ is bounded and Lipschitz in $\boldsymbol{\theta}$ .
By Lemma 3, for every admissible
perturbation $\boldsymbol{\delta}$ , we have $0\leq\bar{\mathcal{L}}_{g}(\boldsymbol{h}+\boldsymbol{\delta},\boldsymbol{y}^{+},\boldsymbol{y}^{-};\boldsymbol{\theta})\leq K_{g}$ .

Since $-\frac{\gamma_{g}}{2}\|\boldsymbol{\delta}\|^{2}\leq 0$ , we can obtain

 $\ell_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{g}(U;\gamma_{g})=\sup_{\boldsymbol{\delta}}\left[\bar{\mathcal{L}}_{g}(\boldsymbol{h}+\boldsymbol{\delta},\boldsymbol{y}^{+},\boldsymbol{y}^{-};\boldsymbol{\theta})-\frac{\gamma_{g}}{2}\|\boldsymbol{\delta}\|_{2}^{2}\right]\leq K_{g}.$ 

(53)

Moreover, because $\boldsymbol{\delta}=0$ is admissible, $\ell_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{g}(U;\gamma_{g})\geq\bar{\mathcal{L}}_{g}(\boldsymbol{h},\boldsymbol{y}^{+},\boldsymbol{y}^{-};\boldsymbol{\theta})\geq 0$ . Thus, we have $0\leq\ell_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{g}(U;\gamma_{g})\leq K_{g}$ .

For Lipschitzness, we define

 $\mathcal{J}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{\delta})=\bar{\mathcal{L}}_{g}(\boldsymbol{h}+\boldsymbol{\delta},\boldsymbol{y}^{+},\boldsymbol{y}^{-};\boldsymbol{\theta})-\frac{\gamma_{g}}{2}\|\boldsymbol{\delta}\|_{2}^{2}.$ 

(54)

Then, we have

 $\displaystyle|\ell_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{g}(U;\gamma_{g})-\ell_{\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\prime}}^{g}(U;\gamma_{g})|$ 
 $\displaystyle=\left|\sup_{\boldsymbol{\delta}}\mathcal{J}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{\delta})-\sup_{\boldsymbol{\delta}}\mathcal{J}_{\boldsymbol{\theta^{\prime}}}(\boldsymbol{\delta})\right|$ 

(55)

 $\displaystyle\leq\sup_{\boldsymbol{\delta}}|\mathcal{J}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{\delta})-\mathcal{J}_{\boldsymbol{\theta^{\prime}}}(\boldsymbol{\delta})|$ 

 $\displaystyle=\sup_{\boldsymbol{\delta}}\left|\bar{\mathcal{L}}_{g}(\boldsymbol{h}+\boldsymbol{\delta},\boldsymbol{y}^{+},\boldsymbol{y}^{-};\boldsymbol{\theta})-\bar{\mathcal{L}}_{g}(\boldsymbol{h}+\boldsymbol{\delta},\boldsymbol{y}^{+},\boldsymbol{y}^{-};\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\prime})\right|$ 

 $\displaystyle\leq L_{g}\|\boldsymbol{\theta}-\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\prime}\|_{2}.$ 

Hence, $\ell_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{g}$ is $L_{g}$ -Lipschitz in $\boldsymbol{\theta}$ with $L_{g}=2\beta C_{\phi}(1+\rho_{g}^{S})$ .

Thus, an $\varepsilon/L_{g}$ -cover of $\Theta$ under
 $\|\cdot\|_{2}$ induces an $\varepsilon$ -cover of $\mathcal{F}_{g}$ under
 $\|\cdot\|_{\infty}$ . Indeed, for any $\boldsymbol{\theta}\in\Theta$ , choose
 $\boldsymbol{\theta}_{j}$ such that
 $\|\boldsymbol{\theta}-\boldsymbol{\theta}_{j}\|_{2}\leq\varepsilon/L_{g}$ . Then

 $\|\ell_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{g}(\cdot;\gamma_{g})-\ell_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{j}}^{g}(\cdot;\gamma_{g})\|_{\infty}\leq L_{g}\|\boldsymbol{\theta}-\boldsymbol{\theta}_{j}\|_{2}\leq\varepsilon.$ 

(56)

Hence, we can get

 $N(\varepsilon,\mathcal{F}_{g},\|\cdot\|_{\infty})\leq N(\varepsilon/L_{g},\Theta,\|\cdot\|_{2}).$ 

(57)

By Dudley’s entropy integral for Rademacher complexity (Wainwright, 2019), the entropy bound above gives

 $\mathfrak{R}_{n_{g}}(\mathcal{F}_{g})\leq\frac{C}{\sqrt{n_{g}}}\int_{0}^{K_{g}}\sqrt{\log N(\varepsilon,\mathcal{F}_{g},\|\cdot\|_{\infty})}\,d\varepsilon.$ 

(58)

By the standard volumetric covering bound (Vershynin, 2018; Wainwright, 2019), we have that

 $\log N(\varepsilon,\mathcal{F}_{g},\|\cdot\|_{\infty})\leq d\log\left(1+\frac{3BL_{g}}{\varepsilon}\right)\leq d\log\left(e+\frac{3BL_{g}}{\varepsilon}\right)$ 

(59)

So we obtain

 $\mathfrak{R}_{n_{g}}(\mathcal{F}_{g})\leq\frac{C}{\sqrt{n_{g}}}\int_{0}^{K_{g}}\sqrt{d\log\left(e+\frac{3BL_{g}}{\varepsilon}\right)}\,d\varepsilon.$ 

(60)

Let $R_{g}=3BL_{g}$ . It remains to bound the following integral:

 $I_{g}=\int_{0}^{K_{g}}\sqrt{\log\left(e+\frac{R_{g}}{\varepsilon}\right)}\,d\varepsilon.$ 

(61)

By setting $u=\varepsilon/K_{g}$ and $a_{g}=R_{g}/K_{g}$ , we can rewrite this integral as

 $I_{g}=K_{g}\int_{0}^{1}\sqrt{\log\left(e+\frac{a_{g}}{u}\right)}\,du.$ 

(62)

For $u\in(0,1]$ , we have $e+\frac{a_{g}}{u}\leq\frac{e+a_{g}}{u}$ , and hence

 $\log\left(e+\frac{a_{g}}{u}\right)\leq\log(e+a_{g})+\log(1/u).$ 

(63)

Using $\sqrt{x+y}\leq\sqrt{x}+\sqrt{y}$ , we obtain

 $I_{g}\leq K_{g}\sqrt{\log(e+a_{g})}+K_{g}\int_{0}^{1}\sqrt{\log(1/u)}\,du.$ 

(64)

Moreover,

 $\int_{0}^{1}\sqrt{\log(1/u)}\,du=\int_{0}^{\infty}s^{1/2}e^{-s}\,ds=\Gamma(3/2)=\frac{\sqrt{\pi}}{2}.$ 

(65)

Since $\log(e+a_{g})\geq 1$ , the fixed numerical term
 $\sqrt{\pi}/2$ can be absorbed into a universal constant. Therefore,

 $I_{g}\leq CK_{g}\sqrt{\log(e+a_{g})}=CK_{g}\sqrt{\log\left(e+\frac{3BL_{g}}{K_{g}}\right)}.$ 

(66)

Substituting this into Eq. (60), we obtain

 $\mathfrak{R}_{n_{g}}(\mathcal{F}_{g})\leq CK_{g}\sqrt{\log\left(e+\frac{3BL_{g}}{K_{g}}\right)}\sqrt{\frac{d}{n_{g}}}.$ 

(67)

Thus, by the setting of $A_{g}=CK_{g}\sqrt{\log\left(e+\frac{3BL_{g}}{K_{g}}\right)}$ , we obtain

 $\mathfrak{R}_{n_{g}}(\mathcal{F}_{g})\leq A_{g}\sqrt{\frac{d}{n_{g}}}.$ 

(68)

This completes the proof of Lemma 4.

Lemma 5 (Group-wise local uniform convergence)

Suppose Assumption 1 holds and
 $U_{g,1},\ldots,U_{g,n_{g}}\overset{\rm i.i.d.}{\sim}P_{g}$ for each
 $g\in[m]$ . Then, with probability at least $1-\delta$ , for
all $g\in[m]$ ,

 $\sup_{\boldsymbol{\theta}\in\Theta}\left|\mathcal{R}_{g}(\boldsymbol{\theta})-\widehat{\mathcal{R}}_{g}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\right|\leq\epsilon_{g},$ 

where $\epsilon_{g}=2A_{g}\sqrt{\frac{d}{n_{g}}}+K_{g}\sqrt{\frac{\log(2m/\delta)}{2n_{g}}}$ .

Proof. 
For a fixed group $g$ , the constant term $\gamma_{g}\rho_{g}^{X}$ appears in
both $\mathcal{R}_{g}(\boldsymbol{\theta})$ and $\widehat{\mathcal{R}}_{g}(\boldsymbol{\theta})$ , so it
cancels and we have

 $\mathcal{R}_{g}(\boldsymbol{\theta})-\widehat{\mathcal{R}}_{g}(\boldsymbol{\theta})=\mathbb{E}_{P_{g}}[\ell_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{g}(U;\gamma_{g})]-\frac{1}{n_{g}}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{g}}\ell_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{g}(U_{g,i};\gamma_{g}).$ 

(69)

By the standard Rademacher uniform convergence bound (Bartlett and Mendelson, 2002) for functions bounded in
 $[0,K_{g}]$ , with probability at least $1-\delta_{g}$ ,

 $\sup_{\boldsymbol{\theta}\in\Theta}\left|\mathcal{R}_{g}(\boldsymbol{\theta})-\widehat{\mathcal{R}}_{g}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\right|\leq 2\mathfrak{R}_{n_{g}}(\mathcal{F}_{g})+K_{g}\sqrt{\frac{\log(2/\delta_{g})}{2n_{g}}}.$ 

(70)

Using Lemma 4,

 $\mathfrak{R}_{n_{g}}(\mathcal{F}_{g})\leq A_{g}\sqrt{\frac{d}{n_{g}}}.$ 

(71)

Setting $\delta_{g}=\delta/m$ and applying a union bound over
 $g=1,\ldots,m$ gives the result.

## Appendix B Experimental Details

## B.1 Details for OOD preference alignment

We construct a controlled benchmark from the dair-ai/emotion
dataset (Saravia et al., 2018), which consists of short English social-media
texts labeled with six emotion categories. We remove the underrepresented
surprise class, retaining five emotions: sadness, joy,
love, anger, and fear. The benchmark is built in three steps: we first train a multi-objective emotion reward model, then use it to construct preference pairs, and finally define latent groups and the OOD evaluation environments.

## Multi-objective reward model.

Following Xu et al. (2025), we convert the dataset into a
multi-label reward dataset by randomly concatenating one to three source texts and
merging their emotion labels into a five-dimensional binary vector $z\in\{0,1\}^{5}$ .
We fine-tune a Qwen2.5-0.5B-Instruct backbone with a linear classification head,
trained with binary cross-entropy loss

 $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{RM}}=-\sum\nolimits_{k=1}^{5}\bigl[z_{k}\log\sigma(h_{k}(x))+(1-z_{k})\log(1-\sigma(h_{k}(x)))\bigr],$ 

where $h(x)\in\mathbb{R}^{5}$ are the logits extracted from the last non-padding token.
Training uses learning rate $5\times 10^{-5}$ , weight decay $0.01$ , dropout $0.1$ ,
and maximum sequence length $128$ for $8$ epochs.

## Base model and preference pair construction.

We fine-tune two base models: Qwen2.5-0.5B-Instruct and LLaMA-3.2-1B-Instruct via supervised fine-tuning (SFT) on the original emotion texts for $10$ epochs with learning rate $5\times 10^{-6}$ , weight decay $0.01$ , max gradient norm $10$ .
The resulting models serve as both the initial policy and the reference model for preference
optimization. For each prompt (first four tokens of each source text), we sample
eight candidate completions with temperature $0.7$ , top- $p=0.9$ , max $64$ tokens, and score them with a scalar preference reward defined as a convex combination of two emotion rewards:
 $R_{\alpha}(y)=\alpha\,r_{\texttt{anger}}(y)+(1-\alpha)\,r_{\texttt{fear}}(y).$ 
Training pairs are constructed under $\alpha_{\mathrm{train}}=0.2$ by selecting the
highest- and lowest-scoring completions as chosen and rejected responses,
respectively, and filtering out pairs with reward margin below $0.02$ .

## Group Construction and OOD Shift Settings.

Since the Emotion dataset lacks explicit domain annotations, we construct six latent groups by applying $K$ -means clustering ( $K=6$ ) to the prompt and response embeddings extracted from Qwen2.5-0.5B-Instruct.
We consider four OOD evaluation settings corresponding to the distribution shifts described in Section 3.1:
(i) Group-level shift: We change the group mixture proportions from a skewed training distribution $(40\%,25\%,15\%,10\%,7\%,3\%)$ to a uniform test distribution.
(ii) Prompt-level shift: We apply synonym substitution to at most one word per prompt from a predefined vocabulary (feel, happy, sad, angry, afraid, love).
(iii) Preference-level shift: We change the reward mixing coefficient from $\alpha_{\mathrm{train}}=0.2$ to $\alpha_{\mathrm{test}}=0.8$ , shifting the preference criterion from fear toward anger.
(iv) Combined shift: We apply all three shifts simultaneously.

## B.2 Details for Preference Poisoning Attack

## Benchmark construction.

For a selected poisoned example $(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}^{+},\boldsymbol{y}^{-})$ , poisoning replaces it with $(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}^{-},\boldsymbol{y}^{+})$ . We construct poisoned training sets from the controlled Emotion preference benchmark (Appendix B.1), swapping chosen and rejected responses for a selected subset of training pairs at poisoning rates $\{0.1,0.2,0.25,0.3,0.35,0.4\}$ , while validation and test splits remain clean. We consider two attack strategies described in Section 5.

## Training and evaluation setup.

All methods use learning rate $\eta_{\theta}=5\times 10^{-6}$ , batch size $64$ , $\beta=0.1$ , and AdamW (Loshchilov and Hutter, 2019) on 2 NVIDIA H100 80GB GPUs. To ensure a comparable tuning budget, we select method-specific robustness hyperparameters on the clean validation split. For rDPO, we tune $\epsilon\in\{0.01,0.1,0.2\}$ . For Dr.DPO, we tune $\tau\in\{0.1,0.5,1.0\}$ . For TPO, we disable prompt-level and group-level robustness and tune the preference-level radius $\rho_{g}^{S}\in\{0.05,0.1,0.2\}$ . All selected models are trained on poisoned preference data and evaluated on the clean test split. For both the random and margin-based preference flip attacks, we report average preference accuracy to evaluate robustness to preference poisoning.

## B.3 Details for Downstream Generalization

## Dataset and preference construction.

We use UltraFeedback (Cui et al., 2023) as the preference training source. It contains instruction-following prompts together with multiple model-generated responses and fine-grained quality annotations. Following Meng et al. (2024), we first collect on-policy responses from the SFT model, and then construct candidate response pairs using the ArmoRM reward model (Wang et al., 2024a) by selecting the highest- and lowest-scoring responses for each prompt. We use the naturally defined UltraFeedback categories as the group structure for robust preference alignment.

## Training and evaluation setup.

We fine-tune all models for one epoch using the AdamW optimizer (Loshchilov and Hutter, 2019) with learning rate $\eta_{\theta}=5\times 10^{-6}$ , global batch size $64$ , and $\beta=0.1$ , on 2 NVIDIA H100 80GB GPUs. For TPO, the preference-level radius is selected from $\rho_{g}^{S}\in\{0.005,0.01,0.05,0.1,0.2\}$ . We evaluate on Open LLM Leaderboard v2 (Hugging Face, 2024), which comprises six benchmark families: IFEval, BBH, MATH-Hard, GPQA, MuSR, and MMLU-Pro.

## B.4 Additional Implementation Details

Following embedding-space adversarial training (Zhu et al., 2020),
we apply the prompt perturbation $\boldsymbol{\delta}$ to the
prompt-token embedding matrix, leaving response tokens unchanged.
The same perturbation is shared across the chosen, rejected, and
reference branches, with padding positions masked out.
The reference policy is evaluated on the perturbed prompts with
its parameters frozen. We use the quadratic penalty
 $\frac{\gamma_{g}}{2}\|\boldsymbol{\delta}\|_{F}^{2}$ and project
perturbations onto $\|\boldsymbol{\delta}\|_{F}^{2}\leq 2\rho_{g}^{X}$ ,
where $\|\cdot\|_{F}$ denotes the Frobenius norm.

For group-weight updates, we adopt exponentiated gradient
ascent (Sagawa et al., 2020; Ramesh et al., 2024),
followed by a KL projection onto the group-mixture ambiguity set.
Given group-wise surrogate losses $\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}_{g}^{(t)}$ ,
we compute provisional weights
 $\widetilde{\omega}_{g}\propto\omega_{g}^{(t-1)}\exp(\eta_{\omega}\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}_{g}^{(t)})$ 
and update

 $\boldsymbol{\omega}^{(t)}=\arg\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}\boldsymbol{\omega}\in\Delta^{m-1}\\
\|\boldsymbol{\omega}-\boldsymbol{\alpha}\|_{1}\leq 2\rho^{\omega}\end{subarray}}\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}(\boldsymbol{\omega}\,\|\,\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\omega}}).$ 

(96)

The $\ell_{1}$ constraint follows from
 $\mathcal{W}_{1}^{G}(\boldsymbol{\omega},\boldsymbol{\alpha})=\frac{1}{2}\|\boldsymbol{\omega}-\boldsymbol{\alpha}\|_{1}$ 
under the discrete group transport cost.
We solve this convex projection using
CVXPY (Diamond and Boyd, 2016).

## Hyperparameter sensitivity analysis.

We further study the sensitivity of TPO to the three robustness radii on the OOD preference alignment benchmark using the LLaMA-3.2-1B-Instruct model. Figures 2 and 3 show the results obtained by varying one radius at a time while keeping the other robustness components fixed. The results suggest that TPO is generally stable across a broad range of values. The group radius $\rho^{\omega}$ has a relatively mild effect, while performance improves as the prompt radius $\rho_{g}^{X}$ increases from very small values and then quickly saturates. The preference radius $\rho_{g}^{S}$ is more influential: moderate values improve robustness, but overly large values can reduce performance, indicating possible over-regularization. These trends offer practical guidance for robustness-radius selection and motivate future work on task-adaptive tuning strategies.

## Computational Cost.

Figure 4: Computational cost on UltraFeedback with LLaMA-3.2-1B-Instruct.

We report the wall-clock training time of LLaMA-3.2-1B-Instruct on the UltraFeedback setting in Figure 4 using 2 NVIDIA H100 80GB GPUs. The time is measured in minutes. Here, TPO $(1,0,0)$ , TPO $(1,1,0)$ , and TPO $(1,1,1)$ denote enabling group-level robustness only, enabling both group- and prompt-level robustness, and enabling all three robustness components, respectively. The corresponding overheads are approximately $+3.5$ , $+13.8$ , and $+14.9$ minutes compared with DPO. These results show that TPO introduces additional computation compared with standard DPO, mainly due to group reweighting and the inner adversarial prompt perturbation steps. Nevertheless, the overall overhead remains modest and manageable in our implementation.

## Appendix D Limitations

First, our finite-sample convergence guarantee relies on the uniform covariance coverage assumption (Assumption 2), which requires the training dataset to provide sufficient coverage across the space of pairwise feature differences between chosen and rejected responses. This assumption is standard in preference learning theory and holds in well-collected datasets, though relaxing it to broader settings remains an interesting theoretical direction.

Second, our prompt-level robustification operates via embedding-space perturbations as a practical approximation to shift in the discrete token space, which is a widely adopted strategy in robust NLP but may not capture all forms of prompt-level variation in real-world scenarios, though it has been shown to be effective in our controlled OOD alignment experiments. Further work may explore more robust prompt-level perturbations in the discrete token space, such as adversarial token substitutions or paraphrasing.

Third, TPO introduces additional hyperparameters, including the robustness radii $\{\rho^{\omega},\rho_{g}^{X},\rho_{g}^{S}\}$ . Since the appropriate ranges of these radii may vary across tasks and scenarios, selecting them can require additional effort. Although our sensitivity analysis provides some practical guidance, developing principled selection rules or task-adaptive tuning strategies remains an important direction for future work.

## Appendix E Impact Statement

This work proposes a hierarchical distributionally robust framework for preference optimization, with the primary goal of improving the reliability of LLM alignment across diverse user populations and deployment environments, which may help reduce performance disparities across underrepresented groups. As a foundational algorithmic contribution, it does not involve direct model deployment or human subject data collection. More robustly aligned models could nonetheless be misused to generate more persuasive harmful content across a broader range of contexts, a risk shared with LLM alignment research more broadly.

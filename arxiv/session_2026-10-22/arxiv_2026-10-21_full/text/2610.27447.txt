Abstract

The evaluation of large language models (LLMs) is inherently multi-objective, as different models may offer different tradeoffs across performance dimensions such as math, code, and writing. Because such evaluations are often based on costly and time-consuming human feedback, it is important to draw reliable conclusions using as few comparisons as possible. In this paper, we present Vector-Borda Empirical-Gap Elimination (VB-EGE), a fixed-confidence adaptive sampling algorithm for efficiently identifying models on the Pareto frontier in arena-style evaluation, where each comparison evaluates a pair of models on a prompt with respect to a selected performance dimension. VB-EGE progressively eliminates models from an active set once sufficient statistical evidence has been accumulated to certify their Pareto status. Under a multi-objective Bradley–Terry model, we show that VB-EGE returns the correct Pareto frontier with high probability and derive an instance-dependent upper bound on its sample complexity. We further establish a worst-case lower bound showing that VB-EGE is minimax-optimal up to logarithmic factors. Simulations and experiments on three real-world Arena datasets (Arena, SearchArena, and VisionArena) demonstrate VB-EGE’s empirical effectiveness.

## 1 Introduction

Pairwise human-preference judgments have become a standard tool to evaluate large language models (LLMs).
In arena-style evaluation, a user submits a prompt, views two
responses with the model identities hidden,
and selects the preferred response (Zheng et al., 2023; Chiang et al., 2024).
Given these pairwise comparisons, a common approach is to fit a statistical model
to estimate a latent score for each model, and use the estimated scores
to rank the models. But model quality is not one-dimensional:
a model preferred for code generation need not be preferred for
creative writing or vision (Liang et al., 2023; Frick et al., 2025).
How should models be compared without collapsing
these tradeoffs into a scalar score?

A natural target is the strict Pareto frontier:
a model lies outside the frontier only if another model
has a strictly higher latent score on every objective.
This criterion excludes uniformly inferior models, while retaining models
with different cross-objective strengths.
Identifying this frontier reliably, however,
requires costly human-preference comparisons
across model pairs and objectives.
How should the comparisons be allocated to
identify the frontier efficiently?
We develop an adaptive sampling procedure that
identifies the strict Pareto frontier at a prescribed confidence level
while reducing the number of comparisons required.
As a preview, Figure 1
presents an application of our method to comparisons from
Arena-Human-Preference-140K (Chiang et al., 2024),
collected through July 2025. Treating
code, math, and Instruction-Following as three objectives,
the method identifies three models on the strict Pareto frontier.

## 1.1 Problem formulation

We formalize this task as a fixed-confidence
sequential identification problem.
Consider $K\geq 2$ candidate models evaluated on $d$ objectives.
Suppose that each model $i\in[K]:=\{1,\dots,K\}$ 
is characterized by an unknown
latent score vector
 $\theta_{i}=(\theta_{i,1},\ldots,\theta_{i,d})$ ,
with larger values indicating better performance along
every coordinate.

Let $\mathcal{F}_{t-1}$ denote the observations and algorithmic
randomization revealed before round $t$ .
At round $t$ , the learner uses a possibly randomized policy based on
 $\mathcal{F}_{t-1}$ to select a query $Q_{t}=(I_{t},J_{t},R_{t})$ ,
where $I_{t},J_{t}\in[K]$ are distinct models to be compared and $R_{t}\in[d]$ 
indexes the evaluation metric.
For each possible query $(i,j,r)$ , let $Y_{t}^{(r)}(i,j)\in\{0,1\}$ denote the potential comparison outcome at round $t$ , with value $1$ indicating that $i$ is preferred over $j$ on objective $r$ . Only the outcome of the selected query is observed: $Y_{t}:=Y_{t}^{(R_{t})}(I_{t},J_{t})$ .
Conditional on the past history and the chosen query, the comparison outcome follows the Bradley–Terry (BT) model (Bradley and Terry, 1952):

 $\mathbb{P}_{\theta}\left(Y_{t}=1\,\middle|\,\mathcal{F}_{t-1},Q_{t}=(i,j,r)\right)=p_{ij}^{(r)}(\theta):=\sigma(\theta_{i,r}-\theta_{j,r}),\qquad\text{where }\sigma(x):=\frac{1}{1+e^{-x}}.$ 

(1)

Consequently, $p_{ji}^{(r)}=1-p_{ij}^{(r)}$ ;
for convenience, we set $p_{ii}^{(r)}=1/2$ .
Each query yields a single objective-specific binary label
and incurs unit cost. Following Kone et al. (2024, Definition 1),
we define the strict Pareto frontier.

Definition 1.1 (Strict Pareto frontier).

For a collection of vectors $x=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{K})\in(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{K}$ ,
we say that $j$ strictly dominates $i$ and
write $i\prec_{x}j$ , if $x_{i,r}<x_{j,r}$ for all $r\in[d]$ .
The strict Pareto frontier of $x$ is

 $\mathcal{P}(x):=\{i\in[K]:\nexists j\in[K]\text{ such that }i\prec_{x}j\}.$ 

Remark 1.1.

Our strict-dominance convention differs slightly from the standard Pareto convention, under which a model dominates another
if it is at least as good in every coordinate and strictly better in
at least one coordinate (Auer et al., 2016).
This strict convention also has a natural decision-making interpretation:
if a user cares only about an objective on which two models are tied, neither model should be discarded on performance grounds; token cost, availability, or other application-specific considerations may instead determine the final choice.
From a technical perspective, non-strict dominance requires additional assumptions to handle ties on individual coordinates.
Our finite-sample termination guarantee
requires all classification gaps defined in Definition 2.2
to be positive, which may fail at coordinate-wise ties.

Accordingly, our goal is to design an algorithm $\mathsf{A}$ 
that adaptively chooses the query $(I_{t},J_{t},R_{t})$ ,
stops at a data-dependent time $\tau_{\mathsf{A}}$ 
(with $\tau_{\mathsf{A}}=\infty$ if it never stops),
and outputs an estimator $\hat{\mathcal{P}}$ of
the strict Pareto frontier
 $\mathcal{P}^{\star}:=\mathcal{P}(\theta)$ .
For a prescribed error tolerance $\delta\in(0,1)$ ,
we require the algorithm to be $\delta$ -correct, i.e.,
to terminate and return the true frontier with probability at least $1-\delta$ , as formalized below.

Definition 1.2 ( $\delta$ -correctness).

Let $\Theta\subseteq(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{K}$ be an instance class. For $\delta\in(0,1)$ , an algorithm $\mathsf{A}$ is $\delta$ -correct over $\Theta$ if, for every $\theta\in\Theta$ ,

 $\mathbb{P}_{\theta}\big(\tau_{\mathsf{A}}<\infty,\;\widehat{\mathcal{P}}=\mathcal{P}(\theta)\big)\geq 1-\delta.$ 

Among $\delta$ -correct algorithms,
we seek to minimize the number of comparisons needed.
Since the stopping time is random, we characterize
an algorithm’s sample complexity through its upper quantile.
Specifically, for $\alpha\in(0,1)$ , define

 $Q_{\theta,\alpha}(\mathsf{A}):=\inf\left\{T\in\mathbb{N}_{0}:\mathbb{P}_{\theta}(\tau_{\mathsf{A}}\leq T)\geq 1-\alpha\right\},$ 

with the convention $\inf\varnothing:=\infty$ .

## 1.2 Our contributions

Our contributions are threefold.
First, we formulate fixed-confidence Pareto-set identification
under objective-specific Bradley–Terry feedback and introduce
Vector-Borda Empirical-Gap Elimination (VB-EGE),
an adaptive sampling procedure for this problem.
The key technical insight is
an order-preserving reduction from the latent score vectors
to vector-Borda scores. Each Borda coordinate is the average
win probability of a model against all opponents on a given objective,
and the transformation preserves the coordinate-wise ordering of the
latent scores—and therefore the strict Pareto set.
Moreover, each Borda coordinate can be estimated directly from
Bernoulli outcomes by comparing a model with a uniformly sampled
opponent. This problem therefore reduces to
Pareto-set identification over $Kd$ -dimensional vectors of Bernoulli means.
VB-EGE builds on this reduction to adaptively concentrates comparison on
models whose Pareto status remains difficult to certify.

Second, we prove that VB-EGE is $\delta$ -correct and
establish an instance-dependent upper bound on its sample
complexity. We further establish a worst-case lower bound over a
gap-separated instance class, showing that VB-EGE is minimax-optimal
with respect to the number of models, the number of objectives, and the
inverse squared classification gap, up to logarithmic factors.

Finally, we evaluate VB-EGE on synthetic Bradley–Terry instances
and logged comparisons from three real-world Arena datasets: Arena,
SearchArena, and VisionArena.
The synthetic experiments confirm the predicted scaling behavior
and demonstrate the sample efficiency of VB-EGE.
Arena experiments also demonstrate substantial gains in
sample efficiency over uniform baselines in realistic settings.

## 1.3 Related work

We review three related literatures, arena-based LLM evaluation, adaptive pairwise comparison, and Pareto set identification,
and provide additional discussion on related work in Appendix A.

Arena-based LLM evaluation.

Chatbot Arena aggregates human pairwise preferences into scalar model
leaderboards (Zheng et al., 2023; Chiang et al., 2024). Subsequent work has improved their statistical stability by modeling ties and score dependence
(Ameli et al., 2025), replacing iterative Elo updates with
maximum-likelihood estimation and accounting for annotator reliability
(Liu et al., 2025), and developing nonparametric ranking and uncertainty
quantification (Frauen et al., 2026). Adaptive model-pair selection improves comparison efficiency (Liu et al., 2026), while prompt- or task-specific rankings address heterogeneity (Frick et al., 2025; Li et al., 2026). These methods still produce scalar rankings, either overall or conditional on prompts or tasks. We instead identify the set of models that remain nondominated across multiple objectives.

Pure exploration from pairwise comparisons.

Pairwise-comparison methods study winner and ranking identification
without directly observing item utilities.
Random-opponent comparisons lead to Borda scores and have been used for
Borda-winner identification and active ranking
(Jamieson et al., 2015; Heckel et al., 2019);
simple Borda-type procedures can also be robust beyond parametric
Bradley–Terry models (Shah and Wainwright, 2018).
More recently, Goldberger and Rudi (2026)
developed an asymptotically optimal fixed-confidence method for top- $k$ 
identification under a latent-utility comparison model.

Beyond these scalar targets, comparison-based methods have also been used
for Pareto or skyline recovery.
Asudeh et al. (2015) adaptively select
criterion-specific crowd comparisons to identify Pareto-optimal objects,
Groz et al. (2020) study skyline recovery under noisy
coordinate comparisons, and Audiffren and Ralaivola (2017) identify maximal arms from dueling feedback
over a partial order.
Our setting instead considers objective-specific Bradley–Terry
comparisons generated by latent utilities and uses an order-preserving
vector-Borda reduction for Pareto-set identification.

Pareto set identification.

Pareto-set identification has primarily been studied in multi-objective
bandits where pulling an arm directly yields a vector-valued observation.
Existing work considers approximate and relaxed fixed-confidence recovery
(Auer et al., 2016; Kone et al., 2023), fixed-budget identification through
Empirical-Gap Elimination and its cost-aware extension (Kone et al., 2024; Xue et al., 2026), and exact fixed-confidence identification with asymptotically optimal allocation (Crépon et al., 2024). Structured extensions include preference-cone and multi-output linear models (Karagözlü et al., 2024; Kone et al., 2025; Shukla and Basu, 2024; Das et al., 2025).
Our elimination rule is closest to (Kone et al., 2024), but our feedback is objective-specific and binary rather than a directly observed vector.

## 2 Vector-Borda Empirical-Gap Elimination (VB-EGE)

In this section, we present our proposed algorithm, VB-EGE,
which adaptively allocates the comparisons to
identify the Pareto frontier.
The algorithm
first represents the ordering of the models
along each objective coordinate using the Borda scores (to be defined shortly),
and then applies the empirical-gap elimination principle (Kone et al., 2024)
to the vector of Borda scores to determine the sampling and stopping rule.
We first introduce the notation used in the remainder of the paper.

Notation.

For any $x\in\mathbb{R}$ , denote $x^{+}=\max\{x,0\}$ .
For $a,b\in\mathbb{R}$ , write $a\wedge b:=\min\{a,b\}$ and
 $a\vee b:=\max\{a,b\}$ .
For two nonnegative functions $f$ and $g$ , we write $f=O(g)$ (or $f\lesssim g$ )
if there exists a universal constant
 $C>0$ such that $f\leq Cg$ ; we write $f=\Omega(g)$ (or $f\gtrsim g$ ) if $g=O(f)$ ,
and $f=\Theta(g)$ (or $f\asymp g$ ) if both $f=O(g)$ and $f=\Omega(g)$ hold.
The notation $f=\tilde{O}(g)$ , $f=\tilde{\Omega}(g)$ , and $f=\tilde{\Theta}(g)$ 
is defined analogously, up to logarithmic factors.
For $p,q\in[0,1]$ , define the binary relative entropy
 $\operatorname{kl}(p,q):=p\log\frac{p}{q}+(1-p)\log\frac{1-p}{1-q}$ ,
with the conventions $0\log(0/x):=0$ for $x\in[0,1]$ and $x\log(x/0):=+\infty$ for $x>0$ .

## 2.1 The vector-Borda reduction

We first define the coordinate-wise Borda scores that are central to the algorithm.

Definition 2.1 (Coordinate-wise Borda scores).

For each model $i\in[K]$ and objective $r\in[d]$ , define
its Borda score as

 $b_{i,r}(\theta):=\frac{1}{K-1}\sum_{j\neq i}p_{ij}^{(r)}(\theta)=\frac{1}{K-1}\sum_{j\neq i}\sigma(\theta_{i,r}-\theta_{j,r}).$ 

(2)

When the dependence on $\theta$ is clear from the context,
we write $b_{i,r}$ in place of $b_{i,r}(\theta)$ .
The vector-Borda representation of model $i$ is
 $b_{i}:=(b_{i,1},\ldots,b_{i,d})\in[0,1]^{d},$ 
and we write
 $b=(b_{1},\ldots,b_{K}).$ 

The key property of the vector-Borda transformation is that
it preserves the ordering of the models along each coordinate
induced by the latent scores $\theta$ . This property is
formalized by the following lemma, with its proof provided in Appendix B.2.1.

Lemma 2.1 (Coordinate-wise order preservation).

For every $i,j\in[K]$ and $r\in[d]$ ,

 $\operatorname{sign}(b_{i,r}-b_{j,r})=\operatorname{sign}(\theta_{i,r}-\theta_{j,r}).$ 

(3)

Consequently,

 $i\prec_{b}j\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad i\prec_{\theta}j\quad\text{ and }\quad\mathcal{P}(b)=\mathcal{P}(\theta)=\mathcal{P}^{\star}.$ 

(4)

By Lemma 2.1,
the latent strict Pareto frontier coincides with
the Pareto frontier of the vector-valued Bernoulli means $b_{1},\ldots,b_{K}$ .
Hence, the learner can operate entirely in Borda space without estimating
the latent score vectors $\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{K}$ .

For a selected focal model $i$ and objective $r$ , if we draw
 $J\sim\mathrm{Unif}([K]\setminus\{i\})$ using fresh randomness,
issue the query $Q_{t}=(i,J,r)$ , and observe $Z:=Y_{t}^{(r)}(i,J)$ ,
then conditional on the past and the selected $(i,r)$ , averaging over $J$ 
gives $Z\sim\operatorname{Bernoulli}(b_{i,r})$ .
This observation underlies our proposed sampling rule
and
is the coordinate-wise analogue of the standard random-opponent Borda reduction (Jamieson et al., 2015, Definition 1).

## 2.2 Pareto classification gaps

Next, we define classification gaps that measure how difficult it is to determine each model’s Pareto status, while accounting for its role in identifying other models as non-Pareto.
For $x=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{K})\in(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{K}$ and any models $i,j$ , define

 $m_{x}(i,j):=\min_{r\in[d]}(x_{j,r}-x_{i,r}),\qquad M_{x}(i,j):=\max_{r\in[d]}(x_{i,r}-x_{j,r}).$ 

Thus,
 $M_{x}(i,j)=-m_{x}(i,j)$ , and $i\prec_{x}j\Longleftrightarrow m_{x}(i,j)>0$ .

Our algorithm follows a successive-elimination scheme,
where models are sequentially removed from an active set. Subsequent comparisons use only
remaining models as focal models, while opponents are drawn uniformly from
the full original set of models excluding the focal model. We therefore
define the classification gaps
relative to an arbitrarily given set $S\subseteq[K]$ 
to characterize the difficulty of identifying the
Pareto frontier from the remaining models.
First, the strict Pareto set restricted to $S$ is given by

 $\mathcal{P}_{S}(x):=\left\{i\in S:\nexists j\in S\setminus\{i\}\text{ such that }m_{x}(i,j)>0\right\}.$ 

The definition of the classification gap is then given as follows.

Definition 2.2 (Restricted-set classification gaps).

Let $S\subseteq[K]$ with $|S|\geq 2$ , and let
 $x_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}$ for every $i\in S$ .
For every $i\in S$ , define the raw domination gap
 $\Delta_{i,S}^{\star}(x):=\max_{j\in S\setminus\{i\}}m_{x}(i,j),$ 
and the auxiliary gap

 $\delta_{i,S}^{\star}(x):=\min_{j\in S\setminus\{i\}}\left\{M_{x}(i,j)\wedge\left[M_{x}(j,i)^{+}+\bigl(\Delta_{j,S}^{\star}(x)\bigr)^{+}\right]\right\}.$ 

The restricted-set classification gap is

 $\Delta_{i,S}(x):=\begin{cases}\Delta_{i,S}^{\star}(x),&i\notin\mathcal{P}_{S}(x),\\[2.84526pt]
\delta_{i,S}^{\star}(x),&i\in\mathcal{P}_{S}(x).\end{cases}$ 

(5)

Intuitively, for $i\notin\mathcal{P}_{S}(x)$ , $\Delta^{\star}_{i,S}(x)$ 
measures the largest domination margin certifying $i$ 
is non-Pareto. For $i\in\mathcal{P}_{S}(x)$ ,
 $\delta^{\star}_{i,S}(x)$ is the smallest separation needed to
certify $i$ ’s Pareto status or to correctly classify another model
relative to $i$ . Thus, a larger
 $\Delta_{i,S}(x)$ indicates estimating model $i$ for identifying
 $\mathcal{P}_{S}(x)$ is easier.

In the following, we omit the set subscript when $S=[K]$ and write
 $\mathcal{P}(x):=\mathcal{P}_{[K]}(x)$ ,
 $\Delta_{i}^{\star}(x):=\Delta_{i,[K]}^{\star}(x)$ ,
 $\delta_{i}^{\star}(x):=\delta_{i,[K]}^{\star}(x)$ ,
 $\Delta_{i}(x):=\Delta_{i,[K]}(x)$ .
For the vector-Borda and latent representations, respectively, let
 $\Delta_{i}^{\mathrm{B}}:=\Delta_{i}(b)$ ,
 $\Delta_{i}^{\theta}:=\Delta_{i}(\theta)$ ,
and
 $\Delta_{\min}^{\mathrm{B}}:=\min_{i\in[K]}\Delta_{i}^{\mathrm{B}}$ .

## 2.3 Phased vector-Borda elimination

VB-EGE proceeds in phases, maintaining an active set $A$ of models
whose Pareto status remains undetermined
and an accepted set $B$ of models certified as Pareto.
At each phase $m\geq 1$ , for each pair $(i,r)\in A\times[d]$ ,
the algorithm collects $n_{m}-n_{m-1}$ new observations by sampling

 $\displaystyle Z_{i,r,s}:=Y_{t(i,r,s)}^{(r)}(i,J_{i,r,s}),\text{ where }J_{i,r,s}\overset{\text{i.i.d.}}{\sim}\text{Unif}([K]\backslash\{i\}),\quad\forall s=n_{m-1}+1,\dots,n_{m},$ 

(6)

where $t(i,r,s)$ denotes the global round at which
the $s$ th comparison associated with model $i$ and coordinate $r$ 
is conducted, $n_{0}=0$ , and $\{n_{m}\}_{m\geq 1}$ is an increasing sequence of
sample-size parameters specified below.
Using all the observations collected through phase $m$ ,
the algorithm updates the vector-Borda estimates as

 $\displaystyle\widehat{b}_{i,r}^{(m)}:=\frac{1}{n_{m}}\sum^{n_{m}}_{s=1}Z_{i,r,s},\quad(i,r)\in A\times[d].$ 

Write $\widehat{b}_{i}^{(m)}=(\widehat{b}_{i,1}^{(m)},\ldots,\widehat{b}_{i,d}^{(m)})$ ;
all empirical Pareto sets and gaps below are evaluated using these vectors
for models in the current active set $A$ .
It then updates the restricted-set Pareto frontier and the
corresponding classification gap with respect to the current active set $A$ :

 $\widehat{\mathcal{S}}_{A}:=\mathcal{P}_{A}\!\big(\widehat{b}^{(m)}\big),\qquad\widehat{\Delta}_{i,A}:=\Delta_{i,A}\!\big(\widehat{b}^{(m)}\big),\quad i\in A.$ 

(7)

If all the estimated classification gaps in $A$ are small—that is,
 $\max_{i\in A}\widehat{\Delta}_{i,A}\leq C_{\mathrm{thr}}r_{m}$ ,
where $C_{\mathrm{thr}}$ is a constant and $r_{m}$ is a confidence-radius parameter
specified below—then the available evidence is not sufficient
to certify the status of any additional model and
the algorithm proceeds to the next phase without elimination.
Otherwise, it selects $i_{m}\in\arg\max_{i\in A}\widehat{\Delta}_{i,A}$ 
and removes it from $A$ . The selected model $i_{m}$ is chosen anew at each
iteration of the inner loop within phase $m$ , with ties resolved by prioritizing empirically
non-Pareto models, as specified in Algorithm 1.
If $i_{m}\in\widehat{\mathcal{S}}_{A}$ , it is certified as Pareto and
added to $B$ ; otherwise, it is rejected as non-Pareto.
After each removal, if only one active model remains, the algorithm returns $B\cup A$ .
Otherwise, it recomputes the empirical Pareto set and classification gaps
over the updated active set and repeats the removal step, using the same
phase- $m$ estimates without additional sampling.
It proceeds to phase $m+1$ only when no empirical gap exceeds the threshold.

The sampling schedule and removal thresholds are chosen so that, with high probability,
every model added to $B$ is truly Pareto and every rejected model is truly non-Pareto.
To be specific, at each phase
 $m\geq 1$ , we define

 $\varepsilon_{m}:=2^{-m},\qquad L_{m}:=\log\frac{C_{\log}Kdm^{2}}{\delta},\qquad n_{m}:=\left\lceil\frac{C_{\mathrm{samp}}L_{m}}{\varepsilon_{m}^{2}}\right\rceil,\qquad r_{m}:=\sqrt{\frac{L_{m}}{2n_{m}}},$ 

(8)

where $C=(C_{\mathrm{samp}},C_{\mathrm{thr}},C_{\log})$ are hyperparameters
controlling the sampling rate, removal threshold, and confidence factor of the algorithm.
Unless stated otherwise, we use the default configuration

 $C=(C_{\mathrm{samp}},C_{\mathrm{thr}},C_{\log})=(2,4,4).$ 

(9)

The complete procedure is summarized in Algorithm 1.

Remark 2.1 (Sampling and tie-breaking conventions).

VB-EGE samples only active models as focal models, but draws each opponent uniformly from the full original set of models $[K]\setminus\{i\}$ , including previously removed models. Thus the mean of each focal observation remains the fixed global quantity $b_{i,r}$ .
When multiple models maximize $\widehat{\Delta}_{i,A}$ , VB-EGE
prioritizes a maximizer outside the empirical Pareto set $\widehat{\mathcal{S}}_{A}$ , whenever one exists; otherwise, it selects an arbitrary maximizer.
On the uniform confidence event in
Lemma B.6, this tie-breaking rule ensures that each active non-Pareto model $i$ retains an active Pareto model attaining its domination gap $\Delta_{i}^{\mathrm{B}}$ .

## 3 Theoretical guarantees

This section presents the theoretical guarantees for VB-EGE.
Recall that, for any algorithm $\mathsf{A}$ , $\tau_{\mathsf{A}}$ is
the total number of queries made before stopping.
Each query compares two models with respect to
a single objective and returns a binary outcome.
We write $\tau=\tau_{\mathsf{A}}$ when the algorithm is clear from the context,

For $B,\Delta>0$ , define the bounded-range class and its gap-separated subclass by

 $\displaystyle\Theta_{B,d}$ 
 $\displaystyle:=\left\{\theta\in(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{K}:\max_{r\in[d]}\max_{i,j\in[K]}|\theta_{i,r}-\theta_{j,r}|\leq 2B\right\},$ 

(10)

 $\displaystyle\Theta_{B,d}(\Delta)$ 
 $\displaystyle:=\left\{\theta\in\Theta_{B,d}:\min_{i\in[K]}\Delta_{i}^{\theta}\geq\Delta\right\}.$ 

We also write

 $\kappa_{B}:=\inf_{|x|\leq 2B}\sigma^{\prime}(x)=\sigma(2B)\bigl(1-\sigma(2B)\bigr).$ 

In what follows,
Section 3.1 establishes the $\delta$ -correctness
of VB-EGE and an instance-dependent upper bound
on its sample complexity over the instance class $\Theta_{B,d}$ .
Section 3.2 provides a worst-case
lower bound over $\Theta_{B,d}(\Delta)$ . Together,
these results show that
VB-EGE is minimax-optimal over $\Theta_{B,d}(\Delta)$ 
up to logarithmic factors.

## 3.1 Upper bound

The following theorem establishes the $\delta$ -correctness
of our proposed algorithm and an upper bound on
the quantile of its stopping time.

Theorem 3.1 (Quantile upper bound).

Fix $B>0$ and $\theta\in\Theta_{B,d}$ 
satisfying $\min_{i\in[K]}\Delta_{i}^{\theta}>0$ .
Define for each $i\in[K]$ 

 $M_{i}^{\theta}:=1+\left\lceil\log_{2}\frac{4}{\kappa_{B}\Delta_{i}^{\theta}}\right\rceil,\qquad L_{i}^{\theta}(\delta):=\log\frac{4Kd(M_{i}^{\theta})^{2}}{\delta}.$ 

Then VB-EGE (Algorithm 1) implemented
with $\delta\in(0,1)$ and $C=(2,4,4)$ satisfies

 $\mathbb{P}_{\theta}\!\left(\widehat{\mathcal{P}}=\mathcal{P}(\theta),\tau\leq dK+\frac{128d}{\kappa_{B}^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{K}\frac{L_{i}^{\theta}(\delta)}{(\Delta_{i}^{\theta})^{2}}\right)\geq 1-\delta.$ 

Consequently, VB-EGE is $\delta$ -correct on the positive-gap subclass of $\Theta_{B,d}$ , and

 $Q_{\theta,\delta}(\textnormal{{VB-EGE}})\leq dK+\frac{128d}{\kappa_{B}^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{K}\frac{L_{i}^{\theta}(\delta)}{(\Delta_{i}^{\theta})^{2}}=\widetilde{O}\!\left(\frac{d}{\kappa_{B}^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{K}(\Delta_{i}^{\theta})^{-2}\right).$ 

If $B$ is bounded by an absolute constant, then
 $\kappa_{B}$ is bounded below by a positive absolute constant.

The proof of Theorem 3.1 is provided in B.2.2,
in which it first establishes exact recovery and finite stopping in Borda space,
and then converts the bound to latent-score gaps.

## 3.2 Lower bound

We now turn to the lower bound on the sample complexity
of any $\delta$ -correct algorithm.
For the gap-separated instance class $\Theta_{B,d}(\Delta)$ ,
define the minimax $(1-\delta)$ -quantile sample complexity as

 $\mathbf{Q}_{B,d}(\Delta,\delta):=\inf_{\mathsf{A}:\,\delta\text{-correct on }\Theta_{B,d}(\Delta)}\;\sup_{\theta\in\Theta_{B,d}(\Delta)}Q_{\theta,\delta}(\mathsf{A}).$ 

Thus, $\mathbf{Q}_{B,d}(\Delta,\delta)$ is the smallest worst-case
 $(1-\delta)$ -stopping-time quantile achievable by any
 $\delta$ -correct algorithm on $\Theta_{B,d}(\Delta)$ .
The following theorem gives a lower bound on $\mathbf{Q}_{B,d}(\Delta,\delta)$ .

Theorem 3.2 (Gap-separated minimax lower bound).

Fix $B>0$ , $0<\Delta\leq\min\{1,B\}$ , and $0<\delta<1/3$ . There is

 $\mathbf{Q}_{B,d}(\Delta,\delta)\geq\frac{d(K-1)}{\Delta^{2}}\operatorname{kl}(1-2\delta,\delta).$ 

The proof of Theorem 3.2 is deferred to Appendix B.2.3. Here, we sketch the high-level idea:
the proof considers the hard instance, where for some $i\in[K]$ ,

 $\displaystyle\theta_{i}=(0,\dots,0),\quad\theta_{j}=(-\Delta,\dots,-\Delta),\quad\forall j\in[K]\backslash\{i\}.$ 

(11)

Raising any model other than $i$ to $+\Delta$ on a single objective makes it Pareto, while preserving the gap and range constraints. A correct algorithm must rule out all $d(K-1)$ such possibilities.

Finally, applying Theorem 3.1 with $\Delta_{i}^{\theta}\geq\Delta$ for every $i$ and combining it with Theorem 3.2 yields the following minimax guarantee for fixed $B$ , up to logarithmic factors.

Corollary 3.3 (Gap-separated minimax optimality).

Under the same assumptions as in Theorem 3.2,

 $\mathbf{Q}_{B,d}(\Delta,\delta)\leq\sup_{\theta\in\Theta_{B,d}(\Delta)}Q_{\theta,\delta}(\textnormal{{VB-EGE}})\leq dK+\frac{128dK}{\kappa_{B}^{2}\Delta^{2}}\log\frac{4Kd(\lceil\log_{2}\frac{8}{\kappa_{B}\Delta}\rceil)^{2}}{\delta}.$ 

Consequently, for any fixed $B>0$ ,

 $\mathbf{Q}_{B,d}(\Delta,\delta)=\widetilde{\Theta}\!\left(\frac{dK}{\Delta^{2}}\log\frac{1}{\delta}\right)\qquad\text{as }\delta\downarrow 0,$ 

where $\widetilde{\Theta}$ suppresses logarithmic factors in $K$ , $d$ , and $\Delta^{-1}$ . In particular, VB-EGE attains the minimax rate up to these logarithmic factors.

## 5 Limitations and future work

This paper introduced a fixed-confidence adaptive sampling framework
for identifying Pareto-optimal models from pairwise comparison.
Several directions remain for future work.
First, although our guarantees are stated under the BT model, the order-preservation property
of the vector-Borda reduction requires only that the comparison probability be strictly increasing in the latent score difference.
The reduction therefore applies more broadly to monotone comparison models.
Second, our Bradley–Terry theory considers only decisive comparisons and does not model
the tie and both_bad labels in Chatbot Arena (Chiang et al., 2024).
Appendix D.4 empirically shows that the Borda-level guarantee extends to half-credit observations and uses a fitted Davidson construction to assess target sensitivity. A model-based treatment of non-decisive outcomes with a recovery guarantee for the population Pareto frontier remains open.
Third, the Arena experiments use several forms of objective labeling,
including mutually exclusive groups, primary-intent labels, and overlapping categories.
An important open question is how sensitive the identified frontiers are to these choices.
One extension is to allow soft or learned assignments, where each comparison context is associated with a mixture of capabilities.

Beyond VB-EGE, there are also several algorithmic directions worth investigating.
For example, under the BT model, one could estimate the latent scores and guide
sampling using likelihood-based methods.
Appendix E explores Pareto BT-GLR Track-and-Stop as a screened implementation of this approach,
leaving a rigorous theoretical analysis for future work.
Another promising direction is graph-aware sampling that exploits nonuniform historical support when selecting opponents and objectives. Such methods would be particularly relevant for larger, evolving arenas in which comparison coverage is uneven and new models are introduced over time.

## Appendix A Additional related work

Best arm identification

Best-arm identification is a canonical pure-exploration problem in
stochastic bandits, where samples are collected to identify an optimal
arm rather than to maximize cumulative reward. Early work established
PAC sample-complexity bounds and confidence-based elimination and
stopping procedures for identifying a near-optimal arm (Mannor and Tsitsiklis, 2004; Even-Dar et al., 2006).
Subsequent work developed sharper procedures for best-arm identification
under fixed-budget and fixed-confidence formulations, including
Successive Rejects (Audibert et al., 2010), Sequential Halving (Karnin et al., 2013), and lil’UCB (Jamieson et al., 2014).
Kaufmann et al. (2016) studied the instance-dependent complexity
of the two dominant fixed-budget and fixed-confidence formulations,
while Garivier and Kaufmann (2016) derived a tight fixed-confidence lower
bound and introduced the asymptotically optimal Track-and-Stop strategy. More recent work has developed alternative adaptive-allocation principles, notably Top-Two methods (Russo, 2020; Jourdan et al., 2022). Elimination strategies can further reduce the computational cost of fully adaptive fixed-confidence identification while preserving sample-complexity guarantees (Tirinzoni and Degenne, 2022).

Set-valued and structured identification.

Beyond best-arm identification, bandit identification has been studied
for richer terminal targets. Top- $m$ and subset-selection problems
seek several of the highest-mean arms (Bubeck et al., 2013; Kaufmann and Kalyanakrishnan, 2013), while thresholding
and good-arm identification aim to determine which arms satisfy a
prescribed performance criterion (Locatelli et al., 2016; Cho et al., 2025). Combinatorial pure exploration generalizes the answer space to structured decision classes, including fixed-size subsets, matchings, spanning trees, and paths (Chen et al., 2014), with recent work developing fixed-budget methods for more general real-valued combinatorial action classes (Nakamura and Sugiyama, 2024). Related extensions consider vector-valued observations and constrained decision spaces: Katz-Samuels and Scott (2018) study feasible-arm identification with multivariate means, while
Carlsson et al. (2024) consider fixed-confidence identification when
the optimal policy is subject to linear constraints and may mix several
arms. More generally, Degenne and Koolen (2019)
develop a fixed-confidence framework for pure-exploration problems that
may admit multiple correct answers.

## B.1.1 Properties of the classification gaps

Lemma B.1 (Basic inequalities for pairwise margins).

Let
 $x=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{K}),\quad y=(y_{1},\ldots,y_{K})\in(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{K}.$ 
Then, for all $i,j,k\in[K]$ ,

(i) 

 $m_{x}(i,k)\geq m_{x}(i,j)+m_{x}(j,k);$ 

(ii) 

if
 $\max_{u\in[K]}\max_{r\in[d]}|x_{u,r}-y_{u,r}|\leq\rho,$ 
then

 $|m_{x}(i,j)-m_{y}(i,j)|\leq 2\rho,\qquad|M_{x}(i,j)-M_{y}(i,j)|\leq 2\rho;$ 

(iii) 

for all $a,b\in\mathbb{R}$ ,
 $|a^{+}-b^{+}|\leq|a-b|.$ 

Proof.

For part (i), coordinate-wise,

 $x_{k,r}-x_{i,r}=(x_{k,r}-x_{j,r})+(x_{j,r}-x_{i,r}),$ 

Taking the minimum over $r$ and using
 $\min_{r}(a_{r}+b_{r})\geq\min_{r}a_{r}+\min_{r}b_{r}$ 
gives

 $m_{x}(i,k)\geq m_{x}(i,j)+m_{x}(j,k).$ 

For part (ii), every coordinate difference satisfies

 $\left|(x_{j,r}-x_{i,r})-(y_{j,r}-y_{i,r})\right|\leq 2\rho.$ 

Both the minimum and maximum maps are $1$ -Lipschitz with respect to the $\ell_{\infty}$ norm, which proves the two inequalities.

Part (iii) follows because the map $a\mapsto a^{+}$ is $1$ -Lipschitz.
∎

Lemma B.2 (Pareto witness for a non-Pareto model).

If $i\notin\mathcal{P}(x)$ , then

 $\Delta_{i}^{\star}(x)=\max_{p\in\mathcal{P}(x)}m_{x}(i,p)>0.$ 

In particular, there exists $i_{x}^{\star}\in\mathcal{P}(x)$ such that
 $m_{x}(i,i_{x}^{\star})=\Delta_{i}^{\star}(x).$ 

Proof.

Since $i\notin\mathcal{P}(x)$ , there exists a model that strictly dominates $i$ . Hence

 $\Delta_{i}^{\star}(x)=\max_{j\in[K]\setminus\{i\}}m_{x}(i,j)>0.$ 

Because the model set is finite, we may choose
 $i_{x}^{\star}\in\arg\max_{j\in[K]\setminus\{i\}}m_{x}(i,j).$ 
Then $m_{x}(i,i_{x}^{\star})=\Delta_{i}^{\star}(x)>0,$ so $i\prec_{x}i_{x}^{\star}$ .

We claim that $i_{x}^{\star}\in\mathcal{P}(x)$ . Suppose otherwise. Then there exists
 $p\in[K]$ such that $i_{x}^{\star}\prec_{x}p$ , and therefore
 $m_{x}(i_{x}^{\star},p)>0.$ 
By Lemma B.1,

 $m_{x}(i,p)\geq m_{x}(i,i_{x}^{\star})+m_{x}(i_{x}^{\star},p)>m_{x}(i,i_{x}^{\star})=\Delta_{i}^{\star}(x).$ 

This contradicts the definition of $i_{x}^{\star}$ . Hence $i_{x}^{\star}\in\mathcal{P}(x)$ , and because it already attains the maximum over all $j\neq i$ , it also attains the maximum over $p\in\mathcal{P}(x)$ .
∎

For a Bradley–Terry instance $\theta\in\Theta_{B,d}$ , let
 $b=b(\theta)$ denote its coordinate-wise Borda vectors, and write

 $\Delta_{i}^{\mathrm{B}}:=\Delta_{i}(b(\theta)),\qquad\Delta_{i}^{\theta}:=\Delta_{i}(\theta).$ 

Recall that

 $\kappa_{B}=\inf_{|x|\leq 2B}\sigma^{\prime}(x)=\sigma(2B)\bigl(1-\sigma(2B)\bigr)>0.$ 

Lemma B.3 (Borda gaps dominate latent gaps).

If $\theta\in\Theta_{B,d}$ , then for every model $i$ ,

 $\Delta_{i}^{\mathrm{B}}\geq\kappa_{B}\Delta_{i}^{\theta}.$ 

Proof.

Fix models $a,c$ and an objective $r$ . For every $u\in[K]$ , define

 $\bar{b}_{u,r}:=\frac{1}{K}\sum_{\ell=1}^{K}\sigma(\theta_{u,r}-\theta_{\ell,r}).$ 

For each $\ell$ , the mean-value theorem gives a point $\xi_{\ell}$ between $\theta_{a,r}-\theta_{\ell,r}$ and $\theta_{c,r}-\theta_{\ell,r}$ such that

 $\sigma(\theta_{c,r}-\theta_{\ell,r})-\sigma(\theta_{a,r}-\theta_{\ell,r})=\sigma^{\prime}(\xi_{\ell})(\theta_{c,r}-\theta_{a,r}).$ 

Hence

 $\bar{b}_{c,r}-\bar{b}_{a,r}=\gamma_{acr}(\theta_{c,r}-\theta_{a,r}),\qquad\gamma_{acr}:=\frac{1}{K}\sum_{\ell=1}^{K}\sigma^{\prime}(\xi_{\ell}).$ 

Because $\theta\in\Theta_{B,d}$ , both endpoints defining each $\xi_{\ell}$ lie in $[-2B,2B]$ , and therefore $\gamma_{acr}\geq\kappa_{B}$ . Since

 $b_{u,r}=\frac{K\bar{b}_{u,r}-1/2}{K-1},$ 

we obtain

 $b_{c,r}-b_{a,r}=\frac{K}{K-1}\gamma_{acr}(\theta_{c,r}-\theta_{a,r}).$ 

In particular, because $K/(K-1)\geq 1$ , whenever $\theta_{c,r}\geq\theta_{a,r}$ ,

 $b_{c,r}-b_{a,r}\geq\kappa_{B}(\theta_{c,r}-\theta_{a,r}).$ 

(12)

We then record three consequences.

First, if $m_{\theta}(a,c)>0$ , then every coordinate difference $\theta_{c,r}-\theta_{a,r}$ is positive, so (12) gives

 $m_{b}(a,c)\geq\kappa_{B}m_{\theta}(a,c).$ 

(13)

Second, for all $a,c$ ,

 $M_{b}(a,c)^{+}\geq\kappa_{B}M_{\theta}(a,c)^{+}.$ 

(14)

If $M_{\theta}(a,c)\leq 0$ , this is trivial. Otherwise choose an objective attaining $M_{\theta}(a,c)=\theta_{a,r}-\theta_{c,r}>0$ and apply (12) with the roles of $a$ and $c$ interchanged.

Third, for every model $j$ ,

 $(\Delta_{j}^{\star}(b))^{+}\geq\kappa_{B}(\Delta_{j}^{\star}(\theta))^{+}.$ 

(15)

If $\Delta_{j}^{\star}(\theta)\leq 0$ , this is trivial. Otherwise choose a
model $c$ attaining $\Delta_{j}^{\star}(\theta)>0$ and apply
(13) to $(j,c)$ .

We now consider the two possible Pareto classes of model $i$ .

If $i\notin\mathcal{P}(\theta)$ , coordinate-wise order preservation implies $i\notin\mathcal{P}(b)$ . Choose a model $c$ attaining $\Delta_{i}^{\star}(\theta)>0$ . Then

 $\Delta_{i}^{\mathrm{B}}=\Delta_{i}^{\star}(b)\geq m_{b}(i,c)\geq\kappa_{B}m_{\theta}(i,c)=\kappa_{B}\Delta_{i}^{\theta}.$ 

Suppose instead that $i\in\mathcal{P}(\theta)$ . Then $i\in\mathcal{P}(b)$ by Lemma 2.1. Fix $j\neq i$ . Since $i$ is Pareto, $M_{\theta}(i,j)\geq 0$ . If $M_{\theta}(i,j)>0$ , applying (12) at an objective attaining the positive maximum gives

 $M_{b}(i,j)\geq\kappa_{B}M_{\theta}(i,j).$ 

If $M_{\theta}(i,j)=0$ , then some objective satisfies $\theta_{i,r}=\theta_{j,r}$ ; coordinate-wise order preservation gives $b_{i,r}=b_{j,r}$ , and hence again $M_{b}(i,j)\geq 0=\kappa_{B}M_{\theta}(i,j)$ . Thus, in all cases,

 $M_{b}(i,j)\geq\kappa_{B}M_{\theta}(i,j).$ 

(16)

Moreover, combining (14) and (15) gives

 $M_{b}(j,i)^{+}+(\Delta_{j}^{\star}(b))^{+}\geq\kappa_{B}\left[M_{\theta}(j,i)^{+}+(\Delta_{j}^{\star}(\theta))^{+}\right].$ 

(17)

Therefore, for every $j\neq i$ ,

 $\displaystyle M_{b}(i,j)\wedge\left[M_{b}(j,i)^{+}+\bigl(\Delta_{j}^{\star}(b)\bigr)^{+}\right]\geq\kappa_{B}\left\{M_{\theta}(i,j)\wedge\left[M_{\theta}(j,i)^{+}+\bigl(\Delta_{j}^{\star}(\theta)\bigr)^{+}\right]\right\},$ 

Taking the minimum over $j\neq i$ yields

 $\Delta_{i}^{\mathrm{B}}=\delta_{i}^{\star}(b)\geq\kappa_{B}\delta_{i}^{\star}(\theta)=\kappa_{B}\Delta_{i}^{\theta}.$ 

This proves the claim for every model $i$ .
∎

## B.1.2 High-probability analysis of VB-EGE

We first state the upper-bound argument for a generic vector-Bernoulli mean instance. Fix
 $b=(b_{1},\ldots,b_{K})\in([0,1]^{d})^{K}.$ 
In this section, a query of model $i$ on objective $r$ reveals the
next unused observation from an independent stream

 $Z_{i,r,1},Z_{i,r,2},\ldots\overset{\mathrm{iid}}{\sim}\operatorname{Bernoulli}(b_{i,r}),\qquad i\in[K],\ r\in[d],$ 

These streams are independent of any additional internal randomization used by the algorithm.

Let $\mathbb{P}_{b}$ denote the induced law of the algorithm and the oracle. For compactness, write

 $\Delta_{i}^{\mathrm{B}}:=\Delta_{i}(b),\qquad\mathcal{P}:=\mathcal{P}(b),\qquad\Delta_{\min}^{\mathrm{B}}:=\min_{i\in[K]}\Delta_{i}^{\mathrm{B}}.$ 

Throughout this section, Algorithm 1 is run with the common
configuration $C=(2,4,4)$ specified in Equation 9. Let $\tau$ count objective-specific binary comparisons, with $\tau=\infty$ if the procedure does not stop. Whenever $\Delta_{\min}^{\mathrm{B}}>0$ , define

 $M_{i}:=1+\left\lceil\log_{2}\frac{4}{\Delta_{i}^{\mathrm{B}}}\right\rceil,\qquad L_{i}(\delta):=\log\frac{4KdM_{i}^{2}}{\delta}.$ 

Also define the $(1-\delta)$ stopping-time quantile under the
vector-Bernoulli instance $b$ by

 $Q_{b,\delta}(\textnormal{{VB-EGE}}):=\inf\left\{T\in\mathbb{N}_{0}:\mathbb{P}_{b}(\tau\leq T)\geq 1-\delta\right\},\qquad\inf\varnothing:=\infty.$ 

Proposition B.4 (VB-EGE guarantee under vector-Bernoulli feedback).

If $\Delta_{\min}^{\mathrm{B}}>0$ , then Algorithm 1, run with
the configuration $C$ in (9), satisfies

 $\mathbb{P}_{b}\!\left(\widehat{\mathcal{P}}=\mathcal{P}(b),\quad\tau\leq dK+128d\sum_{i=1}^{K}\frac{L_{i}(\delta)}{(\Delta_{i}^{\mathrm{B}})^{2}}\right)\geq 1-\delta.$ 

(18)

Consequently,

 $Q_{b,\delta}(\textnormal{{VB-EGE}})\leq dK+128d\sum_{i=1}^{K}\frac{L_{i}(\delta)}{(\Delta_{i}^{\mathrm{B}})^{2}}=\widetilde{O}\!\left(d\sum_{i=1}^{K}(\Delta_{i}^{\mathrm{B}})^{-2}\right).$ 

We first establish several lemmas used in the proof of Proposition B.4. Under
(9), the phase schedule is

 $\varepsilon_{m}=2^{-m},\qquad L_{m}=\log\frac{4Kdm^{2}}{\delta},\qquad n_{m}=\left\lceil\frac{2L_{m}}{\varepsilon_{m}^{2}}\right\rceil,\qquad r_{m}=\sqrt{\frac{L_{m}}{2n_{m}}},$ 

and the removal threshold is $4r_{m}$ .

For every phase $m$ , model $i$ , and objective $r$ , define the conceptual phase- $m$ average

 $\widehat{b}_{i,r}^{(m)}:=\frac{1}{n_{m}}\sum_{s=1}^{n_{m}}Z_{i,r,s}.$ 

For an inactive model–objective pair, this quantity is used only in the analysis; the algorithm need not have revealed the corresponding prefix. At an inner-loop iteration with active set $A$ , abbreviate

 $\displaystyle\widehat{m}_{A}(i,j)$ 
 $\displaystyle:=m_{\widehat{b}^{(m)}}(i,j),$ 
 $\displaystyle\widehat{M}_{A}(i,j)$ 
 $\displaystyle:=M_{\widehat{b}^{(m)}}(i,j),$ 
 $\displaystyle\widehat{\Delta}_{i,A}^{\star}$ 
 $\displaystyle:=\Delta_{i,A}^{\star}\!\left(\widehat{b}^{(m)}\right),$ 
 $\displaystyle\widehat{\delta}_{i,A}^{\star}$ 
 $\displaystyle:=\delta_{i,A}^{\star}\!\left(\widehat{b}^{(m)}\right).$ 

Lemma B.5 (Empirical gap identities).

For every active set $A\subseteq[K]$ with $|A|\geq 2$ and every $i\in A$ ,

 $i\in\widehat{\mathcal{S}}_{A}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\widehat{\Delta}_{i,A}^{\star}\leq 0,\qquad i\notin\widehat{\mathcal{S}}_{A}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\widehat{\Delta}_{i,A}^{\star}>0.$ 

and,

 $\widehat{\Delta}_{i,A}=\widehat{\Delta}_{i,A}^{\star}\vee\widehat{\delta}_{i,A}^{\star}.$ 

Proof.

By definition,

 $\widehat{\Delta}_{i,A}^{\star}=\max_{j\in A\setminus\{i\}}\widehat{m}_{A}(i,j).$ 

Hence $i\notin\widehat{\mathcal{S}}_{A}$ if and only if some $j\in A\setminus\{i\}$ satisfies $\widehat{m}_{A}(i,j)>0$ , which is equivalent to $\widehat{\Delta}_{i,A}^{\star}>0$ . The first two equivalences follow.

Suppose first that $i\in\widehat{\mathcal{S}}_{A}$ . Then $\widehat{\Delta}_{i,A}^{\star}\leq 0$ . Moreover, for every $j\in A\setminus\{i\}$ , empirical Pareto membership implies that $j$ does not strictly dominate $i$ . Thus there exists a coordinate $r\in[d]$ such that $\widehat{b}_{i,r}\geq\widehat{b}_{j,r}$ , and therefore

 $\widehat{M}_{A}(i,j)=\max_{r\in[d]}\bigl(\widehat{b}_{i,r}-\widehat{b}_{j,r}\bigr)\geq 0.$ 

The other term,
 $\widehat{M}_{A}(j,i)^{+}+\bigl(\widehat{\Delta}_{j,A}^{\star}\bigr)^{+},$ 
is also nonnegative. Hence every term in the minimum defining $\widehat{\delta}_{i,A}^{\star}$ is nonnegative, so
 $\widehat{\delta}_{i,A}^{\star}\geq 0\geq\widehat{\Delta}_{i,A}^{\star}.$ 
Therefore

 $\widehat{\Delta}_{i,A}^{\star}\vee\widehat{\delta}_{i,A}^{\star}=\widehat{\delta}_{i,A}^{\star}=\widehat{\Delta}_{i,A}.$ 

Now suppose that $i\notin\widehat{\mathcal{S}}_{A}$ . Then there exists $j\in A\setminus\{i\}$ such that
 $\widehat{m}_{A}(i,j)>0,$ 
or equivalently,

 $\widehat{M}_{A}(i,j)=-\widehat{m}_{A}(i,j)<0.$ 

Using this $j$ in the minimum defining $\widehat{\delta}_{i,A}^{\star}$ gives

 $\displaystyle\widehat{\delta}_{i,A}^{\star}$ 
 $\displaystyle\leq\widehat{M}_{A}(i,j)\wedge\left[\widehat{M}_{A}(j,i)^{+}+\bigl(\widehat{\Delta}_{j,A}^{\star}\bigr)^{+}\right]$ 

 $\displaystyle=\widehat{M}_{A}(i,j)<0,$ 

because the second term in the wedge is nonnegative. Since $\widehat{\Delta}_{i,A}^{\star}>0$ , it follows that

 $\widehat{\Delta}_{i,A}^{\star}\vee\widehat{\delta}_{i,A}^{\star}=\widehat{\Delta}_{i,A}^{\star}=\widehat{\Delta}_{i,A}.$ 

This proves the claim.
∎

Lemma B.6 (Uniform Borda concentration).

Define

 $\mathcal{E}:=\left\{\forall m\geq 1,\ \forall i\in[K],\ \forall r\in[d]:\left|\widehat{b}_{i,r}^{(m)}-b_{i,r}\right|\leq r_{m}\right\}.$ 

(19)

Then
 $\mathbb{P}_{b}(\mathcal{E})\geq 1-\delta.$ 
Moreover, on $\mathcal{E}$ , for every phase $m$ , active set $A$ , and distinct $i,j\in A$ ,

 $\left|\widehat{m}_{A}(i,j)-m_{b}(i,j)\right|\leq 2r_{m},\qquad\left|\widehat{M}_{A}(i,j)-M_{b}(i,j)\right|\leq 2r_{m}.$ 

(20)

Proof.

For a fixed triple $(i,r,m)$ , Hoeffding’s inequality and the definition of $r_{m}$ give

 $\mathbb{P}_{b}\!\left(\left|\widehat{b}_{i,r}^{(m)}-b_{i,r}\right|>r_{m}\right)\leq 2\exp(-2n_{m}r_{m}^{2})=2e^{-L_{m}}=\frac{\delta}{2Kdm^{2}}.$ 

Taking a union bound over $i\in[K]$ , $r\in[d]$ , and $m\geq 1$ yields

 $\mathbb{P}_{b}(\mathcal{E}^{c})\leq\frac{\delta}{2}\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{m^{2}}<\delta.$ 

The two margin bounds follow from Lemma B.1.
∎

For every non-Pareto model $i\notin\mathcal{P}$ , fix once and for all a Borda-Pareto witness supplied by Lemma B.2:

 $i^{\star}\in\arg\max_{p\in\mathcal{P}}m_{b}(i,p),\qquad m_{b}(i,i^{\star})=\Delta_{i}^{\mathrm{B}}>0.$ 

For an active set $A$ , define the witness invariant

 $\mathsf{W}(A):=\left\{\forall i\notin\mathcal{P},\quad i\in A\Longrightarrow i^{\star}\in A\right\}.$ 

(21)

Initially, $\mathsf{W}([K])$ holds.

Lemma B.7 (Every removed model is correctly classified).

Assume that $\mathcal{E}$ and $\mathsf{W}(A)$ hold when a model $i\in A$ is removed in phase $m$ . Then:

(i) 

if $i\notin\widehat{\mathcal{S}}_{A}$ , then $i\notin\mathcal{P}$ ;

(ii) 

if $i\in\widehat{\mathcal{S}}_{A}$ , then $i\in\mathcal{P}$ .

Thus every model removed without acceptance is truly non-Pareto, while every accepted model is truly Pareto.

Proof.

For simplicity, we write $r=r_{m}$ .

First suppose $i\notin\widehat{\mathcal{S}}_{A}$ . Since $i$ is removed, $\widehat{\Delta}_{i,A}>4r$ . By Lemma B.5, $\widehat{\Delta}_{i,A}=\widehat{\Delta}_{i,A}^{\star}$ , so there exists $j\in A\setminus\{i\}$ satisfies
 $\widehat{m}_{A}(i,j)>4r.$ 
Using (20),

 $m_{b}(i,j)\geq\widehat{m}_{A}(i,j)-2r>2r>0.$ 

Hence $i\prec_{b}j$ , and therefore $i\notin\mathcal{P}(b)=\mathcal{P}$ .

Now suppose $i\in\widehat{\mathcal{S}}_{A}$ but, toward a contradiction, $i\notin\mathcal{P}$ . The witness invariant gives $i^{\star}\in A$ , and

 $m_{b}(i,i^{\star})=\Delta_{i}^{\mathrm{B}}>0,\qquad M_{b}(i,i^{\star})=-\Delta_{i}^{\mathrm{B}}.$ 

If $\Delta_{i}^{\mathrm{B}}>2r$ , then (20) implies

 $\widehat{m}_{A}(i,i^{\star})\geq\Delta_{i}^{\mathrm{B}}-2r>0,$ 

contradicting $i\in\widehat{\mathcal{S}}_{A}$ . Thus necessarily $\Delta_{i}^{\mathrm{B}}\leq 2r$ .

Since $i\in\widehat{\mathcal{S}}_{A}$ , its empirical classification gap is $\widehat{\Delta}_{i,A}=\widehat{\delta}_{i,A}^{\star}$ . Taking $j=i^{\star}$ in the defining minimum gives

 $\displaystyle\widehat{\Delta}_{i,A}$ 
 $\displaystyle\leq\widehat{M}_{A}(i,i^{\star})\wedge\left[\widehat{M}_{A}(i^{\star},i)^{+}+\bigl(\widehat{\Delta}_{i^{\star},A}^{\star}\bigr)^{+}\right]$ 

 $\displaystyle\leq\widehat{M}_{A}(i,i^{\star})$ 

 $\displaystyle\leq M_{b}(i,i^{\star})+2r$ 

 $\displaystyle=-\Delta_{i}^{\mathrm{B}}+2r\leq 2r,$ 

contradicting the removal condition $\widehat{\Delta}_{i,A}>4r$ . Therefore $i\in\mathcal{P}$ .
∎

Lemma B.8 (Witness-invariant preservation).

Assume that $\mathcal{E}$ and $\mathsf{W}(A)$ hold before a removal in phase $m$ .
After the removal, the new active set $A^{\prime}$ also satisfies
 $\mathsf{W}(A^{\prime})$ .

Proof.

Write $r=r_{m}$ . By Lemma B.7, the only possible way to violate the invariant is to remove a true Pareto model $p\in\mathcal{P}$ while leaving active a non-Pareto model $i\notin\mathcal{P}$ whose fixed witness is $i^{\star}=p$ . Suppose, toward a contradiction, that this occurs. Immediately before the removal, $p,i\in A$ . Since the removed model $p$ is truly Pareto, Lemma B.7 implies that it cannot be empirically non-Pareto. Thus $p\in\widehat{\mathcal{S}}_{A}$ , and

 $\widehat{\Delta}_{p,A}=\widehat{\delta}_{p,A}^{\star}>4r.$ 

(22)

There are two cases to be considered.

Case 1: $p$ empirically strictly dominates $i$ .
Then $\widehat{m}_{A}(i,p)>0$ , so
 $i\notin\widehat{\mathcal{S}}_{A}$ and
 $\widehat{\Delta}_{i,A}=\widehat{\Delta}_{i,A}^{\star}>0$ . Moreover,
 $\widehat{M}_{A}(i,p)^{+}=0$ . Taking $j=i$ in the minimum defining
 $\widehat{\delta}_{p,A}^{\star}$ yields

 $\widehat{\Delta}_{p,A}\leq\widehat{M}_{A}(i,p)^{+}+(\widehat{\Delta}_{i,A}^{\star})^{+}=\widehat{\Delta}_{i,A}.$ 

Since $p$ is the model selected for removal, it belongs to
 $G:=\arg\max_{u\in A}\widehat{\Delta}_{u,A}.$ 
If the preceding inequality is strict, then
 $\widehat{\Delta}_{i,A}>\widehat{\Delta}_{p,A}$ , contradicting
 $p\in G$ . If equality holds, then
 $i\in G\setminus\widehat{\mathcal{S}}_{A}$ , so the
non-Pareto-first tie-breaking rule Remark 2.1 selects an empirical non-Pareto
maximizer rather than $p$ . In either case, the algorithm cannot remove
 $p$ , which results in a contradiction.

Case 2: $p$ does not empirically strictly dominate $i$ . Then
 $\widehat{m}_{A}(i,p)\leq 0,$ 
and hence

 $\widehat{M}_{A}(i,p)=-\widehat{m}_{A}(i,p)\geq 0.$ 

Since
 $M_{b}(i,p)=-m_{b}(i,p)=-\Delta_{i}^{\mathrm{B}},$ 
the uniform margin deviation (20) gives

 $0\leq\widehat{M}_{A}(i,p)=\widehat{M}_{A}(i,p)^{+}\leq 2r-\Delta_{i}^{\mathrm{B}}.$ 

(23)

So
 $\Delta_{i}^{\mathrm{B}}\leq 2r.$ 
Moreover, for every $q\in A\setminus\{i\}$ ,

 $\widehat{m}_{A}(i,q)\leq m_{b}(i,q)+2r\leq\Delta_{i}^{\mathrm{B}}+2r,$ 

Therefore,

 $\bigl(\widehat{\Delta}_{i,A}^{\star}\bigr)^{+}\leq\Delta_{i}^{\mathrm{B}}+2r.$ 

(24)

Since $p\in\widehat{\mathcal{S}}_{A}$ , $\widehat{\Delta}_{p,A}=\widehat{\delta}_{p,A}^{\star}$ . Taking $j=i$ in the defining minimum gives

 $\displaystyle\widehat{\Delta}_{p,A}$ 
 $\displaystyle\leq\widehat{M}_{A}(p,i)\wedge\left[\widehat{M}_{A}(i,p)^{+}+\bigl(\widehat{\Delta}_{i,A}^{\star}\bigr)^{+}\right]$ 

 $\displaystyle\leq\widehat{M}_{A}(i,p)^{+}+\bigl(\widehat{\Delta}_{i,A}^{\star}\bigr)^{+}$ 

 $\displaystyle\leq\bigl(2r-\Delta_{i}^{\mathrm{B}}\bigr)+\bigl(\Delta_{i}^{\mathrm{B}}+2r\bigr)$ 

 $\displaystyle=4r,$ 

contradicting (22). Hence the invariant is preserved.
∎

Proposition B.9 (Correctness on the confidence event).

On $\mathcal{E}$ , Algorithm 1 never misclassifies a removed model. If it stops, its recommendation equals $\mathcal{P}(b)$ .

Proof.

The invariant holds initially and is preserved after every removal by Lemma B.8. By induction over removals and Lemma B.7, every model added to $B$ belongs to $\mathcal{P}$ , while every model removed without being added to $B$ lies outside $\mathcal{P}$ .

If the algorithm stops with a singleton active set $A=\{a\}$ and $a\notin\mathcal{P}$ , then the invariant would require the distinct witness $a^{\star}$ to remain active, contradicting $|A|=1$ . Thus the final active model is Pareto. Every model outside $B\cup A$ was previously removed without acceptance and hence lies outside $\mathcal{P}$ . Conversely, every model in $B\cup A$ is Pareto. Therefore the returned set $B\cup A$ equals $\mathcal{P}(b)$ .
∎

Lemma B.10 (Removal once the radius is small).

Assume $\mathcal{E}$ . Consider a phase $m$ and a model $i$ that is active at the beginning of the inner loop. If

 $r_{m}<\frac{\Delta_{i}^{\mathrm{B}}}{8},$ 

then either the algorithm stops during phase $m$ , or model $i$ is removed during that phase.

Proof.

Write $r=r_{m}$ , and suppose that $i$ remains active at some inner-loop iteration with $|A|\geq 2$ . By Lemma B.8, the witness invariant $\mathsf{W}(A)$ holds.

If $i\notin\mathcal{P}$ , then its fixed witness $i^{\star}$ remains active. By (20),

 $\widehat{m}_{A}(i,i^{\star})\geq\Delta_{i}^{\mathrm{B}}-2r>0.$ 

Thus $i\notin\widehat{\mathcal{S}}_{A}$ , and Lemma B.5 gives

 $\widehat{\Delta}_{i,A}=\widehat{\Delta}_{i,A}^{\star}\geq\Delta_{i}^{\mathrm{B}}-2r>6r>4r.$ 

Now suppose $i\in\mathcal{P}$ . Then $\Delta_{i}^{\mathrm{B}}=\delta_{i}^{\star}(b)$ . Hence, for every $j\in A\setminus\{i\}$ , both components in the defining wedge are at least $\Delta_{i}^{\mathrm{B}}$ :

 $M_{b}(i,j)\geq\Delta_{i}^{\mathrm{B}},\qquad M_{b}(j,i)^{+}+(\Delta_{j}^{\star}(b))^{+}\geq\Delta_{i}^{\mathrm{B}}.$ 

(25)

By (20),

 $\widehat{M}_{A}(i,j)\geq\Delta_{i}^{\mathrm{B}}-2r>0.$ 

Thus no active model empirically strictly dominates $i$ , so $i\in\widehat{\mathcal{S}}_{A}$ .
For any active model $j\in A$ ,

 $\bigl(\widehat{\Delta}^{\star}_{j,A}\bigr)^{+}\geq\bigl(\Delta_{j}^{\star}(b)\bigr)^{+}-2r.$ 

Indeed, if $j\in\mathcal{P}$ , then
 $\Delta_{j}^{\star}(b)\leq 0$ , so the claim is immediate since the
left-hand side is nonnegative. If $j\notin\mathcal{P}$ , the
witness invariant ensures that its fixed Pareto witness
 $j^{\star}$ remains in $A$ . Hence

 $\widehat{\Delta}^{\star}_{j,A}=\max_{q\in A\setminus\{j\}}\widehat{m}_{A}(j,q)\geq\widehat{m}_{A}(j,j^{\star})\geq m_{b}(j,j^{\star})-2r=\Delta_{j}^{\star}(b)-2r.$ 

Since $\Delta_{j}^{\star}(b)>0$ in this case,

 $\bigl(\widehat{\Delta}^{\star}_{j,A}\bigr)^{+}\geq\bigl(\Delta_{j}^{\star}(b)\bigr)^{+}-2r.$ 

Combining with (25) yields

 $\widehat{M}_{A}(j,i)^{+}+(\widehat{\Delta}_{j,A}^{\star})^{+}\geq\Delta_{i}^{\mathrm{B}}-4r>4r.$ 

Together with $\widehat{M}_{A}(i,j)\geq\Delta_{i}^{\mathrm{B}}-2r$ , this shows that every bracket in the definition of $\widehat{\delta}_{i,A}^{\star}$ is at least $\Delta_{i}^{\mathrm{B}}-4r$ . Therefore

 $\widehat{\Delta}_{i,A}=\widehat{\delta}_{i,A}^{\star}\geq\Delta_{i}^{\mathrm{B}}-4r>4r.$ 

In either case, as long as $i$ remains active and $|A|\geq 2$ , its empirical gap exceeds the removal threshold. Hence the inner loop cannot terminate while retaining $i$ : the algorithm must remove $i$ , unless it stops first because only one active model remains.
∎

Proof of Proposition B.4.

By Lemma B.6, $\mathbb{P}_{b}(\mathcal{E})\geq 1-\delta$ . Work on $\mathcal{E}$ . Correctness upon stopping follows from Proposition B.9; it remains to bound the query count.

For each model $i$ , define

 $m_{i}:=\min\left\{m\geq 1:\varepsilon_{m}<\frac{\Delta_{i}^{\mathrm{B}}}{4}\right\}.$ 

This integer is well defined because $\varepsilon_{m}\downarrow 0$ . Moreover, $0<\Delta_{i}^{\mathrm{B}}\leq 1$ : for a non-Pareto model the raw domination gap is at most $1$ , while for a Pareto model the defining wedge is bounded above by $M_{b}(i,j)\leq 1$ . Hence $m_{i}>1$ . By minimality of $m_{i}$ ,

 $\varepsilon_{m_{i}-1}\geq\frac{\Delta_{i}^{\mathrm{B}}}{4},\qquad\varepsilon_{m_{i}}=\frac{1}{2}\varepsilon_{m_{i}-1}\geq\frac{\Delta_{i}^{\mathrm{B}}}{8}.$ 

On the other hand, the defining property of $m_{i}$ gives

 $\varepsilon_{m_{i}}<\frac{\Delta_{i}^{\mathrm{B}}}{4}.$ 

Since $n_{m}\geq 2L_{m}/\varepsilon_{m}^{2}$ ,

 $r_{m}=\sqrt{\frac{L_{m}}{2n_{m}}}\leq\frac{\varepsilon_{m}}{2}.$ 

Therefore

 $r_{m_{i}}<\frac{\Delta_{i}^{\mathrm{B}}}{8}.$ 

By Lemma B.10, if the algorithm reaches phase $m_{i}$ and model $i$ is still active, then during that phase either the algorithm stops or $i$ is removed. Thus no coordinate of model $i$ is sampled after phase $m_{i}$ .

In particular, the algorithm stops after finitely many phases. Indeed, let

 $\overline{m}:=\max_{i\in[K]}m_{i}<\infty.$ 

If the algorithm had not stopped by the end of phase $\overline{m}$ , any model still active at that time would have remained active beyond its phase $m_{i}$ , contradicting Lemma B.10.

Consequently, the algorithm stops by phase $\overline{m}$ , and model $i$ is never sampled after phase $m_{i}$ . Each of its $d$ coordinates therefore receives at most $n_{m_{i}}$ observations, so

 $\tau\leq d\sum_{i=1}^{K}n_{m_{i}}.$ 

It remains to bound $n_{m_{i}}$ . By its definition and the lower bound $\varepsilon_{m_{i}}\geq\Delta_{i}^{\mathrm{B}}/8$ ,

 $n_{m_{i}}\leq 1+\frac{2}{\varepsilon_{m_{i}}^{2}}\log\frac{4Kdm_{i}^{2}}{\delta}\leq 1+\frac{128}{(\Delta_{i}^{\mathrm{B}})^{2}}\log\frac{4Kdm_{i}^{2}}{\delta}.$ 

Furthermore, $m_{i}\leq M_{i}$ , because

 $\varepsilon_{M_{i}}=2^{-M_{i}}\leq\frac{\Delta_{i}^{\mathrm{B}}}{8}<\frac{\Delta_{i}^{\mathrm{B}}}{4}.$ 

Consequently,

 $n_{m_{i}}\leq 1+\frac{128L_{i}(\delta)}{(\Delta_{i}^{\mathrm{B}})^{2}}.$ 

Combining the preceding two displays gives

 $\tau\leq dK+128d\sum_{i=1}^{K}\frac{L_{i}(\delta)}{(\Delta_{i}^{\mathrm{B}})^{2}}.$ 

Thus, on $\mathcal{E}$ , the algorithm returns $\mathcal{P}(b)$ within the stated
query budget. Since $\mathbb{P}_{b}(\mathcal{E})\geq 1-\delta$ ,
(18) follows. The quantile statement follows directly from the definition of $Q_{b,\delta}(\textnormal{{VB-EGE}})$ .
∎

## B.1.3 Change-of-measure tools

Lemma B.11 (Monotonicity of binary relative entropy).

Let

 $0\leq q\leq b<a\leq p\leq 1.$ 

Then

 $\operatorname{kl}(p,q)\geq\operatorname{kl}(a,b),$ 

where binary relative entropy is extended to the boundary by the usual conventions.

Proof.

For $p,q\in(0,1)$ ,

 $\frac{\partial}{\partial p}\operatorname{kl}(p,q)=\log\frac{p(1-q)}{q(1-p)},$ 

which is positive whenever $p>q$ . Moreover,

 $\frac{\partial}{\partial q}\operatorname{kl}(p,q)=\frac{q-p}{q(1-q)},$ 

which is negative whenever $p>q$ . Thus $\operatorname{kl}(p,q)$ is increasing in its first argument and decreasing in its second argument on the region $p>q$ . The boundary cases follow by taking the corresponding one-sided limits; cases in which the left-hand side is infinite are immediate.
∎

Lemma B.12 (Logistic KL upper bound).

For all $x,y\in\mathbb{R}$ ,

 $\operatorname{kl}(\sigma(x),\sigma(y))\leq\frac{(x-y)^{2}}{8}.$ 

Proof.

The Bernoulli distribution with mean $\sigma(u)$ is the one-parameter
exponential family with log-partition function $A(u)=\log(1+e^{u})$ . Since
 $A^{\prime\prime}(u)=\sigma(u)(1-\sigma(u))\leq 1/4$ , the function $A$ is globally
 $1/4$ -smooth. The corresponding Bregman-divergence representation gives

 $\operatorname{kl}(\sigma(x),\sigma(y))=A(y)-A(x)-A^{\prime}(x)(y-x)\leq\frac{(y-x)^{2}}{8}.$ 

∎

For $1\leq i<j\leq K$ , objective $r\in[d]$ , and $u\in\mathbb{N}_{0}$ with $u\leq\tau$ , define

 $N_{ij,r}^{\mathrm{pair}}(u):=\sum_{t=1}^{u}\mathbf{1}\!\left\{R_{t}=r,\,\{I_{t},J_{t}\}=\{i,j\}\right\}.$ 

Thus $N_{ij,r}^{\mathrm{pair}}(u)$ counts comparisons of the unordered pair $\{i,j\}$ on objective $r$ among the first $u$ queries.

For every model $i\in[K]$ , define its incident-comparison count by

 $N_{i,r}^{\mathrm{inc}}(u):=\sum_{j<i}N_{ji,r}^{\mathrm{pair}}(u)+\sum_{j>i}N_{ij,r}^{\mathrm{pair}}(u).$ 

Lemma B.13 (Bounded-horizon transportation).

Fix $T\in\mathbb{N}_{0}$ . Let $\theta$ and $\lambda$ be two instances, and let $G$ be an event measurable with respect to the transcript stopped at $\tau\wedge T$ , including the terminal recommendation if the algorithm stops by time $T$ . Then

 $\sum_{r=1}^{d}\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq K}\mathbb{E}_{\theta}\!\left[N_{ij,r}^{\mathrm{pair}}(\tau\wedge T)\right]\operatorname{kl}\!\left(p_{ij}^{(r)}(\theta),p_{ij}^{(r)}(\lambda)\right)\geq\operatorname{kl}\!\left(\mathbb{P}_{\theta}(G),\mathbb{P}_{\lambda}(G)\right).$ 

(26)

Proof.

Let $\mathbb{P}_{\theta}^{T}$ and $\mathbb{P}_{\lambda}^{T}$ denote the laws of the transcript
stopped at $\tau\wedge T$ . View the stopped transcript as a length- $T$ 
adaptive experiment with a common cemetery action and observation after
stopping, which contributes zero KL divergence. At each time, the query is
predictable from the past and the algorithmic randomization, whose law is the
same under both instances. The chain rule for KL divergence therefore gives

 $\operatorname{KL}(\mathbb{P}_{\theta}^{T},\mathbb{P}_{\lambda}^{T})=\mathbb{E}_{\theta}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{\tau\wedge T}\operatorname{kl}\left(p_{I_{t}J_{t}}^{(R_{t})}(\theta),p_{I_{t}J_{t}}^{(R_{t})}(\lambda)\right)\right].$ 

For a reverse-orientation query,
 $p_{ji}^{(r)}=1-p_{ij}^{(r)}$ , and
 $\operatorname{kl}(1-p,1-q)=\operatorname{kl}(p,q)$ . Grouping both orientations by unordered pair
turns the right-hand side into the left-hand side of
(26). Finally, applying data processing to
the binary statistic $\mathbf{1}_{G}$ gives

 $\operatorname{KL}\!\left(\mathbb{P}_{\theta}^{T}\,\middle\|\,\mathbb{P}_{\lambda}^{T}\right)\geq\operatorname{kl}\left(\mathbb{P}_{\theta}(G),\mathbb{P}_{\lambda}(G)\right),$ 

which proves the claim.
∎

## B.2 Proofs of main results

## B.2.1 Proof of Lemma 2.1

Proof of Lemma 2.1.

For every model $u\in[K]$ and objective $r\in[d]$ , define the self-included Borda coordinate

 $\bar{b}_{u,r}:=\frac{1}{K}\sum_{\ell=1}^{K}\sigma(\theta_{u,r}-\theta_{\ell,r}).$ 

Since $\sigma(0)=1/2$ ,

 $b_{u,r}=\frac{K\bar{b}_{u,r}-1/2}{K-1}.$ 

In particular, $b_{u,r}$ is a strictly increasing affine function of $\bar{b}_{u,r}$ .

Fix $i,j\in[K]$ and $r\in[d]$ . Suppose first that $\theta_{i,r}>\theta_{j,r}$ . Then, for every $\ell\in[K]$ ,

 $\theta_{i,r}-\theta_{\ell,r}>\theta_{j,r}-\theta_{\ell,r}.$ 

Because the logistic function is strictly increasing,

 $\sigma(\theta_{i,r}-\theta_{\ell,r})>\sigma(\theta_{j,r}-\theta_{\ell,r})\qquad\text{for every }\ell\in[K].$ 

Averaging over $\ell$ yields $\bar{b}_{i,r}>\bar{b}_{j,r}$ , and hence $b_{i,r}>b_{j,r}$ .

Exchanging $i$ and $j$ proves that $\theta_{i,r}<\theta_{j,r}$ implies $b_{i,r}<b_{j,r}$ . If $\theta_{i,r}=\theta_{j,r}$ , then the summands defining $\bar{b}_{i,r}$ and $\bar{b}_{j,r}$ coincide for every $\ell$ , so $b_{i,r}=b_{j,r}$ . Therefore,

 $\operatorname{sign}(b_{i,r}-b_{j,r})=\operatorname{sign}(\theta_{i,r}-\theta_{j,r}),$ 

which proves (3).

Applying the sign identity coordinate-wise gives

 $\theta_{i,r}<\theta_{j,r}\ \text{for all }r\in[d]\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad b_{i,r}<b_{j,r}\ \text{for all }r\in[d].$ 

Hence $i\prec_{\theta}j$ if and only if $i\prec_{b}j$ . Since the strict Pareto frontier is determined entirely by this domination relation,

 $\mathcal{P}(b)=\mathcal{P}(\theta)=\mathcal{P}^{\star},$ 

which proves (4).
∎

## B.2.2 Proof of Theorem 3.1

Proof of Theorem 3.1.

Conditional on $\mathcal{F}_{t-1}$ and $(I_{t},R_{t})=(i,r)$ ,
the opponent $J_{t}$ is uniform on $[K]\setminus\{i\}$ .
By (1),

 $\mathbb{P}_{\theta}(Y_{t}=1\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1},I_{t}=i,R_{t}=r)=\frac{1}{K-1}\sum_{j\neq i}p_{ij}^{(r)}(\theta)=b_{i,r}(\theta).$ 

Since the opponent and comparison randomness are fresh at every query, the oracle can be realized by mutually independent pre-generated streams

 $Z_{i,r,1},Z_{i,r,2},\ldots\overset{\mathrm{iid}}{\sim}\operatorname{Bernoulli}\!\left(b_{i,r}(\theta)\right),$ 

revealing the next unused observation whenever the algorithm queries $(i,r)$ . Algorithm 1 discards the opponent identities and uses only these binary outcomes, so its stopping time and recommendation have the same law under this stream representation. Hence Proposition B.4 applies with $b=b(\theta)$ under $\mathbb{P}_{\theta}$ . By Lemma 2.1,

 $\mathcal{P}(b(\theta))=\mathcal{P}(\theta).$ 

Let $M_{i}$ and $L_{i}(\delta)$ be the Borda-space quantities from Appendix B.1.2. By Lemma B.3, $\Delta_{i}^{\mathrm{B}}\geq\kappa_{B}\Delta_{i}^{\theta}$ , and therefore

 $M_{i}=1+\left\lceil\log_{2}\frac{4}{\Delta_{i}^{\mathrm{B}}}\right\rceil\leq 1+\left\lceil\log_{2}\frac{4}{\kappa_{B}\Delta_{i}^{\theta}}\right\rceil=M_{i}^{\theta}.$ 

It follows that $L_{i}(\delta)\leq L_{i}^{\theta}(\delta)$ and

 $\frac{L_{i}(\delta)}{(\Delta_{i}^{\mathrm{B}})^{2}}\leq\frac{L_{i}^{\theta}(\delta)}{\kappa_{B}^{2}(\Delta_{i}^{\theta})^{2}}.$ 

Substituting this inequality into (18), with $b=b(\theta)$ , gives

 $\mathbb{P}_{\theta}\!\left(\widehat{\mathcal{P}}=\mathcal{P}(\theta),\quad\tau\leq dK+\frac{128d}{\kappa_{B}^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{K}\frac{L_{i}^{\theta}(\delta)}{(\Delta_{i}^{\theta})^{2}}\right)\geq 1-\delta.$ 

The displayed event gives the stated bound on $Q_{\theta,\delta}(\textnormal{{VB-EGE}})$ . Absorbing the logarithmic factors $L_{i}^{\theta}(\delta)$ yields the $\widetilde{O}$ expression. If $B$ is bounded by an absolute constant, i.e., $B=O(1)$ , then $\kappa_{B}$ is bounded below by a positive absolute constant, i.e., $\kappa_{B}=\Theta(1)$ .
∎

## B.2.3 Proof of Theorem 3.2

We first establish a quantile lower bound for a symmetric instance and then apply it to the gap-separated class.

Fix $\Delta>0$ and consider the symmetric instance $\theta^{\Delta}$ defined by

 $\theta^{\Delta}_{1,r}=0,\qquad\theta^{\Delta}_{i,r}=-\Delta,\qquad i\in\{2,\ldots,K\},\ r\in[d].$ 

The first model strictly dominates every other model, and hence
 $\mathcal{P}(\theta^{\Delta})=\{1\}$ .

To obtain a lower bound, we construct alternatives under which one
of the dominated models becomes Pareto.
For $i\in\{2,\ldots,K\}$ , $r\in[d]$ , and $\eta>0$ , define the one-coordinate alternative

 $\lambda^{i,r,\eta}_{a,s}:=\begin{cases}\eta,&(a,s)=(i,r),\\
\theta^{\Delta}_{a,s},&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}$ 

The alternative changes only the $i$ th model’s score on objective $r$ ,
moving it from $-\Delta$ to $\eta>0$ . Consequently, model $i$ 
is Pareto under $\lambda^{i,r,\eta}$ , so that
 $\mathcal{P}(\lambda^{i,r,\eta})\neq\mathcal{P}(\theta^{\Delta})$ .

The parameter $\eta$ measures how far the perturbed coordinate lies
beyond the Pareto decision boundary at $0$ . Smaller $\eta$ yields a harder alternative, since the Pareto frontier still changes while the largest
affected Bradley–Terry logit shift, $\Delta+\eta$ , becomes smaller.

Lemma B.14 (Quantile lower bound).

Let $\delta\in(0,1/2)$ , and let $\mathsf{A}$ be $\delta$ -correct over an instance class $\mathcal{C}$ containing $\theta^{\Delta}$ . Suppose that there exists $\bar{\eta}>0$ such that,
for every $i\in\{2,\ldots,K\}$ and $r\in[d]$ , the class
 $\mathcal{C}$ contains at least one alternative
 $\lambda^{i,r,\eta_{i,r}}$ with $0<\eta_{i,r}\leq\bar{\eta}$ .
Then, for every $\alpha\in(0,1-2\delta)$ ,

 $Q_{\theta^{\Delta},\alpha}(\mathsf{A})\geq\frac{4d(K-1)}{(\Delta+\bar{\eta})^{2}}\operatorname{kl}(1-\delta-\alpha,\delta).$ 

If, moreover, arbitrarily small frontier-changing perturbations
are admissible, namely, for every $i\in\{2,\ldots,K\}$ and $r\in[d]$ there exists a sequence $\eta_{n}^{i,r}\downarrow 0$ such that $\lambda^{i,r,\eta_{n}^{i,r}}\in\mathcal{C}\text{ for every }n,$ 
then

 $Q_{\theta^{\Delta},\alpha}(\mathsf{A})\geq\frac{4d(K-1)}{\Delta^{2}}\operatorname{kl}(1-\delta-\alpha,\delta).$ 

In particular, if $\alpha=\delta<1/3$ , then

 $Q_{\theta^{\Delta},\delta}(\mathsf{A})\geq\frac{4d(K-1)}{\Delta^{2}}\operatorname{kl}(1-2\delta,\delta)=\Omega\!\left(\frac{dK}{\Delta^{2}}\log\frac{1}{\delta}\right)\qquad\text{as }\delta\downarrow 0.$ 

The lower bound holds under the full adaptive ordered-pair oracle of Section 1.1, and therefore also applies to the uniform-opponent implementation used by VB-EGE. The proof uses a bounded-horizon change-of-measure argument (Kaufmann et al., 2016, Lemma 1).

Proof of Lemma B.14.

It suffices to lower-bound every finite $T$ satisfying

 $\mathbb{P}_{\theta^{\Delta}}(\tau\leq T)\geq 1-\alpha.$ 

(27)

If no such $T$ exists, the quantile is infinite and the claim is immediate.

Fix a suboptimal model $i\in\{2,\ldots,K\}$ , objective $r\in[d]$ , and an admissible $\eta>0$ . Under $\theta^{\Delta}$ , model $1$ strictly dominates all others, so $\mathcal{P}(\theta^{\Delta})=\{1\}$ . Under $\lambda^{i,r,\eta}$ , model $1$ does not dominate $i$ , because $\lambda_{i,r}^{i,r,\eta}=\eta>0=\lambda_{1,r}^{i,r,\eta}$ . No other model dominates $i$ , since its coordinate $r$ is smaller than $\eta$ . Thus $i$ is Pareto under the alternative and

 $\mathcal{P}(\lambda^{i,r,\eta})\neq\{1\}=\mathcal{P}(\theta^{\Delta}).$ 

(28)

Consider the stopped event

 $G_{T}:=\{\tau\leq T,\ \widehat{\mathcal{P}}=\{1\}\}.$ 

By $\delta$ -correctness and
(27),

 $\mathbb{P}_{\theta^{\Delta}}(G_{T})\geq 1-\delta-\alpha.$ 

By (28), outputting $\{1\}$ under
 $\lambda^{i,r,\eta}$ is an error, so

 $\mathbb{P}_{\lambda^{i,r,\eta}}(G_{T})\leq\delta.$ 

Since

 $\mathbb{P}_{\theta^{\Delta}}(G_{T})\geq 1-\delta-\alpha>\delta\geq\mathbb{P}_{\lambda^{i,r,\eta}}(G_{T}),$ 

Lemma B.11 yields

 $\operatorname{kl}\!\left(\mathbb{P}_{\theta^{\Delta}}(G_{T}),\mathbb{P}_{\lambda^{i,r,\eta}}(G_{T})\right)\geq\ell_{\delta,\alpha}^{\mathrm{hp}}:=\operatorname{kl}(1-\delta-\alpha,\delta).$ 

(29)

The two instances differ only in objective $r$ of model $i$ . Thus all queries outside objective $r$ , or not involving $i$ , contribute zero KL in Lemma B.13. For a comparison in objective $r$ involving $i$ , the Bradley–Terry logit changes by at most $\Delta+\eta$ : it changes from $-\Delta$ to $\eta$ against model $1$ , and from $0$ to $\Delta+\eta$ against any other suboptimal model. By Lemma B.12, every affected comparison contributes at most $(\Delta+\eta)^{2}/8$ . Combining Lemma B.13 with (29) yields

 $\ell_{\delta,\alpha}^{\mathrm{hp}}\leq\frac{(\Delta+\eta)^{2}}{8}\mathbb{E}_{\theta^{\Delta}}\left[N_{i,r}^{\mathrm{inc}}(\tau\wedge T)\right].$ 

Therefore

 $\mathbb{E}_{\theta^{\Delta}}\left[N_{i,r}^{\mathrm{inc}}(\tau\wedge T)\right]\geq\frac{8\ell_{\delta,\alpha}^{\mathrm{hp}}}{(\Delta+\eta)^{2}}.$ 

By the assumption of Lemma B.14, for every
 $i\in\{2,\ldots,K\}$ and $r\in[d]$ , one can choose
 $\eta_{i,r}\in(0,\bar{\eta}]$ such that
 $\lambda^{i,r,\eta_{i,r}}\in\mathcal{C}$ . Therefore,

 $\mathbb{E}_{\theta^{\Delta}}\!\left[N_{i,r}^{\mathrm{inc}}(\tau\wedge T)\right]\geq\frac{8\ell_{\delta,\alpha}^{\mathrm{hp}}}{(\Delta+\eta_{i,r})^{2}}\geq\frac{8\ell_{\delta,\alpha}^{\mathrm{hp}}}{(\Delta+\bar{\eta})^{2}}.$ 

Summing over $i\in\{2,\ldots,K\}$ and $r\in[d]$ gives

 $\sum_{i=2}^{K}\sum_{r=1}^{d}\mathbb{E}_{\theta^{\Delta}}\!\left[N_{i,r}^{\mathrm{inc}}(\tau\wedge T)\right]\geq\frac{8d(K-1)\ell_{\delta,\alpha}^{\mathrm{hp}}}{(\Delta+\bar{\eta})^{2}}.$ 

On every sample path,

 $\sum_{i=2}^{K}\sum_{r=1}^{d}N_{i,r}^{\mathrm{inc}}(\tau\wedge T)\leq 2(\tau\wedge T)\leq 2T,$ 

because one objective-specific binary comparison is incident to at most two
models in $\{2,\ldots,K\}$ . Combining the two
bounds gives

 $T\geq\frac{4d(K-1)}{(\Delta+\bar{\eta})^{2}}\operatorname{kl}(1-\delta-\alpha,\delta).$ 

Taking the infimum over all finite $T$ satisfying

 $\mathbb{P}_{\theta^{\Delta}}(\tau\leq T)\geq 1-\alpha$ 

proves the first assertion.

The sequence assumption in the second part of Lemma B.14 implies the preceding admissibility condition for every $\bar{\eta}>0$ . Applying the first bound with arbitrary $\bar{\eta}>0$ and letting $\bar{\eta}\downarrow 0$ gives

 $Q_{\theta^{\Delta},\alpha}(\mathsf{A})\geq\frac{4d(K-1)}{\Delta^{2}}\operatorname{kl}(1-\delta-\alpha,\delta).$ 

The specialization $\alpha=\delta<1/3$ follows from

 $\operatorname{kl}(1-2\delta,\delta)=\Theta\!\left(\log\frac{1}{\delta}\right)\qquad\text{as }\delta\downarrow 0.$ 

∎

Remark B.1 (Admissible alternatives).

For the bounded-range class $\Theta_{B,d}$ , the small-perturbation condition in the second part of Lemma B.14 holds whenever $0<\Delta<2B$ . For example, one may take
 $\eta_{n}:=\frac{2B-\Delta}{n+1}\downarrow 0$ .

This condition cannot hold on the gap-separated subclass $\Theta_{B,d}(\Delta)$ . Indeed, for every $\eta>0$ ,
 $\Delta_{i}\!\left(\lambda^{i,r,\eta}\right)\leq\eta$ , so the alternatives eventually leave $\Theta_{B,d}(\Delta)$ as $\eta\downarrow 0$ . However, if $\Delta\leq B$ , then

 $\theta^{\Delta},\lambda^{i,r,\Delta}\in\Theta_{B,d}(\Delta)\qquad\text{for every }i\geq 2,\ r\in[d].$ 

The first part of Lemma B.14 therefore applies with $\bar{\eta}=\Delta$ , which is the construction used in Theorem 3.2.

We now apply the preceding lemma to prove the minimax lower bound.

Proof of Theorem 3.2.

Let $\mathsf{A}$ be any algorithm that is $\delta$ -correct on $\Theta_{B,d}(\Delta)$ , and consider the symmetric instance $\theta^{\Delta}$ from Lemma B.14. Directly from Definition 2.2,

 $\mathcal{P}(\theta^{\Delta})=\{1\},\qquad\Delta_{a}(\theta^{\Delta})=\Delta\quad\text{for every }a\in[K].$ 

For every $i\geq 2$ and $r\in[d]$ , let $\lambda^{i,r,\Delta}$ replace only
 $\theta^{\Delta}_{i,r}=-\Delta$ by $+\Delta$ . A direct calculation gives

 $\mathcal{P}(\lambda^{i,r,\Delta})=\begin{cases}\{i\},&d=1,\\
\{1,i\},&d\geq 2,\end{cases}$ 

and

 $\Delta_{1}(\lambda^{i,r,\Delta})=\Delta_{i}(\lambda^{i,r,\Delta})=\Delta,\qquad\Delta_{k}(\lambda^{i,r,\Delta})\geq\Delta\quad\text{for every }k\notin\{1,i\}.$ 

Moreover,

 $\max_{s\in[d]}\max_{a,b\in[K]}|\theta^{\Delta}_{a,s}-\theta^{\Delta}_{b,s}|=\Delta,$ 

whereas

 $\max_{s\in[d]}\max_{a,b\in[K]}|\lambda^{i,r,\Delta}_{a,s}-\lambda^{i,r,\Delta}_{b,s}|\leq 2\Delta.$ 

Since $\Delta\leq B$ ,

 $\theta^{\Delta},\lambda^{i,r,\Delta}\in\Theta_{B,d}(\Delta).$ 

Since $\lambda^{i,r,\Delta}\in\Theta_{B,d}(\Delta)$ for every $i\geq 2$ and $r\in[d]$ , the first part of Lemma B.14 applies with $\bar{\eta}=\Delta$ and $\alpha=\delta$ . Hence

 $\sup_{\theta\in\Theta_{B,d}(\Delta)}Q_{\theta,\delta}(\mathsf{A})\geq Q_{\theta^{\Delta},\delta}(\mathsf{A})\geq\frac{d(K-1)}{\Delta^{2}}\operatorname{kl}(1-2\delta,\delta).$ 

Taking the infimum over all such $\mathsf{A}$ proves the minimax lower bound.
∎

## B.2.4 Proof of Corollary 3.3

Proof of Corollary 3.3.

For every $\theta\in\Theta_{B,d}(\Delta)$ ,

 $\displaystyle\Delta_{i}^{\theta}\geq\Delta,\qquad M_{i}^{\theta}\leq\left\lceil\log_{2}\frac{8}{\kappa_{B}\Delta}\right\rceil,$ 

 $\displaystyle L_{i}^{\theta}(\delta)\leq\log\frac{4Kd\left(\left\lceil\log_{2}\frac{8}{\kappa_{B}\Delta}\right\rceil\right)^{2}}{\delta}.$ 

Hence Theorem 3.1 gives

 $\sup_{\theta\in\Theta_{B,d}(\Delta)}Q_{\theta,\delta}(\textnormal{{VB-EGE}})\leq dK+\frac{128dK}{\kappa_{B}^{2}\Delta^{2}}\log\frac{4Kd\left(\left\lceil\log_{2}\frac{8}{\kappa_{B}\Delta}\right\rceil\right)^{2}}{\delta}.$ 

The joint recovery statement in Theorem 3.1 also shows that VB-EGE is $\delta$ -correct on $\Theta_{B,d}(\Delta)$ . It is therefore admissible in the infimum defining $\mathbf{Q}_{B,d}(\Delta,\delta)$ , and hence

 $\mathbf{Q}_{B,d}(\Delta,\delta)\leq\sup_{\theta\in\Theta_{B,d}(\Delta)}Q_{\theta,\delta}(\textnormal{{VB-EGE}}).$ 

Combining this upper bound with Theorem 3.2 gives the minimax rate. Since $K\geq 2$ , we have $K-1\geq K/2$ . Finally, for fixed $B$ , $\kappa_{B}$ is bounded below by a positive constant, while

 $\operatorname{kl}(1-2\delta,\delta)=\Theta\!\left(\log\frac{1}{\delta}\right)\qquad\text{as }\delta\downarrow 0.$ 

Moreover, because $\Delta\leq 1$ and $\delta<1/3$ , the additive $dK$ term is absorbed by the displayed upper-bound term. Hence VB-EGE matches the polynomial dependence $dK\Delta^{-2}$ up to logarithmic factors in $K$ , $d$ , and $\Delta^{-1}$ , and matches the $\log(1/\delta)$ confidence dependence.
∎

## Appendix C Additional details and results for synthetic experiments

## C.1 Details for the data-generating process

In the scaling experiments, the parameter grids are specified in Table 1.

In the Convex-3D, Witness, and Arena settings, we first generate $s$ Pareto models
and then construct the dominated models by lowering the scores accordingly.
The latent scores corresponding to the
Pareto models are given by $\theta_{i}=0.15\mathbf{1}_{d}+0.75z_{i}$ , where
 $z_{i}\overset{\text{iid}}{\sim}\operatorname{Dirichlet}(\alpha\mathbf{1}_{d})$ , $\forall i\in[s]$ ;
thus $\alpha$ controls their concentration on the trade-off simplex.
For Convex-2D, the $s$ Pareto anchors are deterministic and equally spaced on a decreasing line segment:

 $t_{p}=\frac{p-1}{s-1},\qquad\theta_{p}=(0.20+0.65t_{p},\;0.85-0.65t_{p}),\qquad p\in[s].$ 

In the synthetic Arena and Convex settings, each dominated arm $i\notin[s]$ is
independently assigned an anchor
 $P_{i}\sim\operatorname{Unif}([s])$ and its latent score is generated as

 $\displaystyle\theta_{i}=\theta_{P_{i}}-m_{i}\text{ where }m_{i,r}\overset{\mathrm{iid}}{\sim}\operatorname{Unif}[\ell,u]\quad\forall i\notin[s].$ 

(30)

For Convex-2D, $[\ell,u]=[0.03,0.18]$ .
For the Witness setting, exactly $q$ dominated arms are attached to each Pareto anchor
 $p\in[s]$ , so $K=s(q+1)$ . Candidate scores are generated according to (30) with $P_{i}=p$ and retained only when $p$ is their unique Pareto dominator.
In the Two-group-10 setting, we generate $K_{\mathrm{low}}$ low-group arms
and $K_{\mathrm{high}}$ high-group arms, with $K=K_{\mathrm{low}}+K_{\mathrm{high}}$ .
Each coordinate is drawn independently from $\operatorname{Unif}[0.20,0.45]$ 
for the low-group arms and from $\operatorname{Unif}[0.55,0.75]$ for the high-group arms.
Because every high-group coordinate exceeds every low-group coordinate,
each low-group arm is strictly dominated by every high-group arm.

## C.2 Implementation details for the baseline models

For BT-MLE, at each phase,
we refit a centered ridge-regularized Bradley–Terry model
per objective using all accumulated comparisons.
For numerical stability, we use a ridge penalty coefficient of $10^{-8}$ and
an optimization tolerance of $10^{-9}$ . If the optimizer fails to converge,
any fitted score is nonfinite or exceeds $20$ in absolute value,
or the comparison graph is disconnected, we refit with coefficient $10^{-4}$ .

For Borda plug-in, let $N_{ij,r}^{\mathrm{pair}}(t)$ denote the number of comparisons between models $i$ and $j$ on objective $r$ up to query $t$ , and let $W_{ij,r}^{\mathrm{on}}(t)$ denote the number of these comparisons won by $i$ .
For $i<j$ , we estimate the probability that $i$ beats $j$ by

 $\widetilde{p}_{ij}^{(r)}(t):=\frac{W_{ij,r}^{\mathrm{on}}(t)+1/2}{N_{ij,r}^{\mathrm{pair}}(t)+1},\qquad\widetilde{p}_{ji}^{(r)}(t):=1-\widetilde{p}_{ij}^{(r)}(t).$ 

The added counts prevent estimates of exactly zero or one.
We estimate each model’s Borda score on objective $r$ by averaging its estimated win probabilities over all other models:

 $\widehat{b}_{i,r}(t):=\frac{1}{K-1}\sum_{j\neq i}\widetilde{p}_{ij}^{(r)}(t).$

## D.2 Sampling from logged Arena comparisons

We evaluate the four methods by sampling with replacement from the retained logs,
where each draw counts as one comparison.
We evaluate the methods by treating the logged comparisons as the ground truth and comparing the methods’ outputs against the empirical Pareto frontier derived from these logs.

Ground truth and inferential target.

Let $\mathcal{D}_{\log}$ denote the fixed log of decisive comparisons.
For any $1\leq i<j\leq K$ and $r\in[d]$ , let $N_{ij,r}^{\log}$ be the number of records for that pair and objective,
where $N_{ij,r}^{\log}\geq 1$ by the choice of retained models.
These records form a pair–objective cell, defined as

 $\mathcal{D}_{ij,r}:=(Y_{ij,r,s})_{s=1}^{N_{ij,r}^{\log}},$ 

(31)

where $Y_{ij,r,s}=1$ if $i$ wins and $0$ if $j$ wins. For the reverse orientation, set $Y_{ji,r,s}=1-Y_{ij,r,s}$ and $N_{ji,r}^{\log}=N_{ij,r}^{\log}$ .
Define

 $\widehat{p}_{ij,r}:=\frac{1}{N_{ij,r}^{\log}}\sum_{s=1}^{N_{ij,r}^{\log}}Y_{ij,r,s},\qquad\widehat{p}_{ji,r}=1-\widehat{p}_{ij,r},$ 

(32)

and the empirical uniform-opponent Borda coordinates

 $b_{i,r}^{\mathrm{emp}}:=\frac{1}{K-1}\sum_{j\neq i}\widehat{p}_{ij,r}.$ 

(33)

Writing $b_{i}^{\mathrm{emp}}=(b_{i,1}^{\mathrm{emp}},\ldots,b_{i,d}^{\mathrm{emp}})$ and $b^{\mathrm{emp}}=(b_{i}^{\mathrm{emp}})_{i=1}^{K}$ , the ground-truth Pareto set is

 $\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{emp}}:=\mathcal{P}(b^{\mathrm{emp}}).$ 

(34)

Uniform-opponent queries.

For a focal query $(i,r)$ , VB-EGE draws an opponent from all other retained models, including models already removed from its active set, then draws a record from that cell:

 $J\sim\operatorname{Unif}([K]\setminus\{i\}),\qquad S\mid J=j\sim\operatorname{Unif}(\{1,\ldots,N_{ij,r}^{\log}\}).$ 

(35)

It returns

 $Z:=Y_{iJ,r,S}.$ 

(36)

Both samling steps are performed with replacement, using
fresh randomness independent of any other draw and of the algorithm’s internal randomization.
The following lemma establishes the the sampled $Z$ is conditionally unbiased for the empirical Borda coordinate,
where the subscript $\mathrm{samp}$ denotes this randomness conditional on $\mathcal{D}_{\log}$ .

Lemma D.1 (Conditional Bernoulli representation).

For every fixed logged data $\mathcal{D}_{\log}$ and
focal query $(i,r)$ ,

 $Z\mid\mathcal{D}_{\log},i,r\sim\operatorname{Bernoulli}(b_{i,r}^{\mathrm{emp}}).$ 

(37)

The sampling randomization can be represented by mutually independent streams, one for each model–objective pair, with

 $Z_{i,r,1},Z_{i,r,2},\ldots\mid\mathcal{D}_{\log}\overset{\mathrm{iid}}{\sim}\operatorname{Bernoulli}(b_{i,r}^{\mathrm{emp}}).$ 

(38)

Proof.

Conditional on the log and $J=j$ , the mean of $Z$ is $\widehat{p}_{ij,r}$ . Averaging over the uniform opponent gives

 $\mathbb{E}_{\mathrm{samp}}[Z\mid\mathcal{D}_{\log},i,r]=\frac{1}{K-1}\sum_{j\neq i}\widehat{p}_{ij,r}=b_{i,r}^{\mathrm{emp}}.$ 

Since $Z$ is binary, this determines its Bernoulli law. Fresh, independent opponent and record draws give the independent streams. Adaptive selection of $(i,r)$ reveals the next unused observation of the corresponding stream, so this representation also applies to adaptive queries.
∎

For fixed $n\geq 1$ , let $\overline{Z}_{i,r}(n):=n^{-1}\sum_{s=1}^{n}Z_{i,r,s}$ ; the phase- $m$ estimate is $\widehat{b}_{i,r}^{(m)}=\overline{Z}_{i,r}(n_{m})$ .

Remark D.1 (Recovery conditional on the logged data).

If $\min_{i}\Delta_{i}(b^{\mathrm{emp}})>0$ , Lemma D.1 lets us apply Proposition B.4 with $b=b^{\mathrm{emp}}$ : with probability at least $1-\delta$ conditional on $\mathcal{D}_{\log}$ , VB-EGE with $C=(2,4,4)$ recovers $\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{emp}}$ and satisfies the same sample-complexity bound with gaps $\Delta_{i}(b^{\mathrm{emp}})$ .

Opponent weighting and baseline sampling.

For VB-EGE and Focal Borda, each focal query $(i,r)$ 
selects an opponent uniformly from the other retained models, then samples a logged outcome uniformly with replacement from the corresponding pair–objective cell.
Focal Borda allocates these queries uniformly over model–objective pairs. BT-MLE and the Borda plug-in allocate comparisons uniformly over unordered pair–objective cells, sampling a logged outcome uniformly with replacement from each selected cell. BT-MLE fits scores to the resulting pair counts, while the plug-in averages the estimated pairwise win rates over opponents.

For efficiency, the implementations sample batch counts directly. A batch of $n$ focal queries to $(i,r)$ has a $\operatorname{Binomial}(n,b_{i,r}^{\mathrm{emp}})$ win count, while a batch in cell $(i,j,r)$ has a $\operatorname{Binomial}(n,\widehat{p}_{ij,r})$ win count. These draws preserve the joint law of the counts used by the methods, which make their decisions at batch boundaries.

Population-level unbiasedness under an additional logging assumption.

The recovery guarantee above conditions on the observed log. To relate its Borda means to population means, write $\mathbf{N}=(N_{ij,r}^{\log})_{i<j,r}$ and let $\mathbb{E}_{\log}$ denote expectation over the logged outcomes conditional on these counts.

Lemma D.2 (Population Borda unbiasedness under cell-wise iid logging).

Suppose that, for every cell,

 $Y_{ij,r,1},\ldots,Y_{ij,r,N_{ij,r}^{\log}}\mid\mathbf{N}\overset{\mathrm{iid}}{\sim}\operatorname{Bernoulli}(p_{ij,r}),$ 

(39)

where $p_{ij,r}$ is the population win probability conditional on a decisive outcome.
Set $p_{ji,r}=1-p_{ij,r}$ and

 $b_{i,r}^{\mathrm{pop}}:=\frac{1}{K-1}\sum_{j\neq i}p_{ij,r}.$ 

(40)

Then

 $\mathbb{E}_{\log}[\widehat{p}_{ij,r}\mid\mathbf{N}]=p_{ij,r},\qquad\mathbb{E}_{\log}[b_{i,r}^{\mathrm{emp}}\mid\mathbf{N}]=b_{i,r}^{\mathrm{pop}}.$ 

For every fixed $n\geq 1$ , also $\mathbb{E}_{\log,\mathrm{samp}}[\overline{Z}_{i,r}(n)\mid\mathbf{N}]=b_{i,r}^{\mathrm{pop}}$ .

Proof.

The cell average has expectation $p_{ij,r}$ under (39). Averaging these expectations over opponents proves the Borda identity.

The last assertion follows by iterated expectation and $\mathbb{E}_{\mathrm{samp}}[\overline{Z}_{i,r}(n)\mid\mathcal{D}_{\log}]=b_{i,r}^{\mathrm{emp}}$ .
∎

Together with Remark D.1, this lemma connects the two levels of the logged-data experiment. Conditional on the log, VB-EGE recovers $\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{emp}}$ with probability at least $1-\delta$ .
Under cell-wise iid logging, $b^{\mathrm{emp}}$ is an unbiased estimate of $b^{\mathrm{pop}}$ and concentrates around it as the cell counts grow.

Although unbiasedness does not necessarily transfer to $\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{emp}}$ 
because $\mathcal{P}(\cdot)$ is discontinuous,
we nevertheless have $\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{emp}}=\mathcal{P}(b^{\mathrm{pop}})$ 
if $\min_{i}\Delta_{i}(b^{\mathrm{pop}})>0$ and
 $\max_{i,r}|b^{\mathrm{emp}}_{i,r}-b^{\mathrm{pop}}_{i,r}|<\min_{i}\Delta_{i}(b^{\mathrm{pop}})/2$ ;
the probability of the latter event tends to one as $\min_{i<j,r}N_{ij,r}^{\log}\to\infty$ .

## D.4 Sensitivity to tie and both_bad handling

The main experiments retain only decisive comparisons.
This section examines the sensitivity of our results
to different treatments of non-decisive outcomes.
We consider two alternative treatmens in addition to
removing the non-decisive items:

 $D=\text{decisive-only},\qquad H=\text{direct merged half-credit},\qquad F=\text{fitted Davidson half-credit}.$ 

All three use the same retained models, objective assignments, and uniform-opponent aggregation.

Common setup and comparison metrics.

For an unordered pair $\{i,j\}$ and objective $r$ , let $W_{ij,r}$ and $W_{ji,r}$ be the directed win counts, and $T_{ij,r}$ and $N^{\mathrm{bad}}_{ij,r}$ the tie and both_bad counts. We merge non-decisive outcomes into $U_{ij,r}:=T_{ij,r}+N^{\mathrm{bad}}_{ij,r}$ . Table 12 reports their totals; non-decisive records account for $27.5\%$ to $38.0\%$ of each retained log.

For $X\in\{D,H,F\}$ , write $b^{X}=(b_{i}^{X})_{i=1}^{K}$ for its Borda vectors and define

 $b^{D}=b^{\mathrm{emp}},\qquad\mathcal{P}_{X}=\mathcal{P}(b^{X}),\qquad\Delta_{\min}^{X}=\min_{i}\Delta_{i}(b^{X}),\qquad\mathcal{H}_{X}=\sum_{i}\Delta_{i}(b^{X})^{-2},$ 

where the definition of $b^{\mathrm{emp}}$ is to be
given below for each treatment.

For two methods $X$ and $Y$ , we define

 $\operatorname{Jac}(X,Y)=\frac{|\mathcal{P}_{X}\cap\mathcal{P}_{Y}|}{|\mathcal{P}_{X}\cup\mathcal{P}_{Y}|},$ 

and let $\rho_{\min}(X,Y)$ denote the minimum Spearman correlation across objectives. Stopping-time comparisons use the main experiment settings: 100 independent trials per construction and dataset, $\delta=0.05$ , and $C=(2,4,4)$ .

## D.4.1 Direct merged half-credit recoding

Under $D$ , the empirical win rate is

 $\widehat{p}^{D}_{ij,r}=\frac{W_{ij,r}}{W_{ij,r}+W_{ji,r}}=\widehat{p}_{ij,r}.$ 

The direct construction $H$ keeps each decisive outcome coded as $(1,0)$ and assigns $(1/2,1/2)$ to every tie and both_bad outcome, so its directed cell mean is

 $\widehat{p}^{H}_{ij,r}=\frac{W_{ij,r}+\frac{1}{2}U_{ij,r}}{W_{ij,r}+W_{ji,r}+U_{ij,r}},\qquad\widehat{p}^{H}_{ji,r}=1-\widehat{p}^{H}_{ij,r}.$ 

(41)

Every pair–objective cell is estimated directly from its own logged counts, and the corresponding Borda mean is $b^{H}_{i,r}=(K-1)^{-1}\sum_{j\neq i}\widehat{p}^{H}_{ij,r}$ .
Figures 13, 14 and 15 compare $D$ with $H$ on Arena-35, SearchArena-8, and VisionArena-12, respectively.

As in Appendix D.2, each query draws an opponent uniformly from the other retained models, then a record uniformly with replacement from the selected cell. A win, a non-decisive outcome, and a loss return $1$ , $1/2$ , and $0$ .
The proof of Proposition B.4 uses Hoeffding’s inequality, which also applies to these $[0,1]$ -valued observations, so Remark D.1 extends to $\mathcal{P}_{H}$ when its gaps are positive.

## D.4.2 Fitted Davidson construction

Three-outcome likelihood.

The fitted construction $F$ uses the full count triple $(W_{ij,r},W_{ji,r},U_{ij,r})$ to fit, separately for each objective $r$ , a Davidson model (Davidson, 1970) with pair-specific tie weights,
following the factored tie construction of Ameli et al. (2025).
Let $\theta^{F}_{i,r}$ be the score of model $i$ and, for $i<j$ , let $\eta_{ij,r}$ be the symmetric log tie weight of the pair.
The probabilities that $i$ wins, that $j$ wins, and that the comparison is non-decisive are parametrized as

 $\left(\pi^{W}_{ij,r},\,\pi^{L}_{ij,r},\,\pi^{U}_{ij,r}\right)=\operatorname{softmax}\!\left(\frac{\theta^{F}_{i,r}-\theta^{F}_{j,r}}{2},\,\frac{\theta^{F}_{j,r}-\theta^{F}_{i,r}}{2},\,\eta_{ij,r}\right),\qquad\sum_{i=1}^{K}\theta^{F}_{i,r}=0.$ 

(42)

Conditional on a decisive outcome, the win probability follows the Bradley–Terry model with score difference $\theta^{F}_{i,r}-\theta^{F}_{j,r}$ . The parameters are fitted jointly by minimizing the multinomial negative log-likelihood obtained by weighting the three log probabilities in (42) by $(W_{ij,r},W_{ji,r},U_{ij,r})$ and summing over $i<j$ .

Factored tie weight.

We use a parameter $k_{\mathrm{tie}}$ to control
the factor size in the parameterization of $\eta_{ij,r}$ .
When $k_{\rm tie}=0$ , all pairs in objective $r$ share one scalar tie logit, $\eta_{ij,r}=\gamma^{\mathrm{tie}}_{r}$ . When $k_{\rm tie}>0$ , we set

 $\eta_{ij,r}=(\Gamma_{r})_{ij},\qquad\Gamma_{r}=V_{r}\Phi^{\top}+\Phi V_{r}^{\top},\qquad(\Phi)_{a\ell}=\sqrt{\frac{2}{K}}\cos\!\left[\frac{\pi(2a-1)(2\ell-1)}{4K}\right],$ 

(43)

where $V_{r},\Phi\in\mathbb{R}^{K\times k_{\rm tie}}$ and $\Phi$ consists of the first $k_{\rm tie}$ columns of the fixed DCT-IV basis. This parameterization makes $\Gamma_{r}$ symmetric while allowing the tie propensity to vary across pairs. We call $k_{\rm tie}$ , the number of factor columns, the tie-factor size. Ameli et al. (2025) call it the rank of the factor matrices; we use a different name because $\Gamma_{r}$ itself can have rank up to $2k_{\rm tie}$ . For $k_{\rm tie}\geq 1$ , no separate scalar logit $\gamma^{\mathrm{tie}}_{r}$ is included. The models with $k_{\rm tie}\geq 1$ are nested, because appending a zero column to $V_{r}$ embeds size $k_{\rm tie}$ in size $k_{\rm tie}+1$ , whereas the constant-logit model with $k_{\rm tie}=0$ is generally not nested in them. We therefore compare all candidates by held-out likelihood.

Selection of $k_{\rm tie}$ and refitting.

Within each objective, we assign each observation independently to training with probability $0.9$ . Whenever a model pair has no training observations in that objective, we move one of its validation observations, taken from its most frequent outcome category, to training.

The candidate grids are prespecified and listed in Table 13; Arena-35 uses a broader grid than the two smaller model sets. Selection is restricted to these grids. We select the size with the smallest held-out mean negative log-likelihood and refit it to all observations. Fits use BFGS with analytic gradients; all 76 candidate fits and 22 full-data fits converged, with one candidate requiring an L-BFGS-B fallback.

Binary interface and fitted target.

After refitting the selected model to all observations, we convert its fitted outcome probabilities to half-credit win probabilities

 $\widehat{p}^{F}_{ij,r}=\pi^{W}_{ij,r}+\frac{1}{2}\pi^{U}_{ij,r},\qquad\widehat{p}^{F}_{ji,r}=\pi^{L}_{ij,r}+\frac{1}{2}\pi^{U}_{ij,r}=1-\widehat{p}^{F}_{ij,r}.$ 

(44)

As in Appendix D.2, each oracle query first draws an opponent uniformly from the other retained models. Conditional on the selected pair $(i,j)$ and objective $r$ , the oracle draws an outcome from the fitted three-category distribution and resolves a non-decisive outcome with an independent fair coin, which is equivalent to a single Bernoulli draw with mean $\widehat{p}^{F}_{ij,r}$ . We then compute

 $b^{F}_{i,r}=\frac{1}{K-1}\sum_{j\neq i}\widehat{p}^{F}_{ij,r}$ 

and obtain the Pareto set $\mathcal{P}_{F}$ of the fitted Borda scores.

For each model–objective pair, the marginal observation law is $\operatorname{Bernoulli}(b^{F}_{i,r})$ , so the implementation draws batch win counts from the corresponding binomial distribution, as under $D$ .

## Appendix E Additional comparison with a Track-and-Stop extension

The BT-MLE baseline in the main experiments samples pair–objective cells uniformly, so its gap to VB-EGE leaves open whether an adaptive likelihood-based method would perform better.
This section empirically examines that question using a Pareto extension of the scalar top- $k$ Track-and-Stop method of Goldberger and Rudi (2026).
Appendix E.1 describes the extension, and Appendix E.2 compares it with VB-EGE on the synthetic tasks of Appendix C.

## E.1 Implementation used in the reported experiments

The scalar top- $k$ method of Goldberger and Rudi (2026) combines Bradley–Terry MLE, adaptive allocation, and a generalized likelihood-ratio (GLR) stopping rule. We retain this template and replace its scalar alternatives with Pareto alternatives, calling the resulting procedure Pareto BT-GLR Track-and-Stop (Pareto BT-GLR T&S). Here, an alternative is a set of score arrays defined by linear inequalities describing a change in the current Pareto frontier, up to boundary points. The implementation ranks these alternatives by approximate Fisher-information scores and refits the likelihood only under the selected alternative. We call this approximation screening.

Let $N_{\mathrm{cell}}=d\binom{K}{2}$ be the number of unordered pair–objective cells. The method takes $n_{0}$ initial samples per cell and initializes sampling weights $w_{ij,r}=1/N_{\mathrm{cell}}$ , which are carried across checks. At check $m$ , let $t$ be the total number of comparisons and $N_{ij,r}(t)$ the count in cell $(i,j,r)$ . Write $\widehat{\theta}_{\cdot,r,t}\in\mathbb{R}^{K}$ for the fitted score vector on objective $r$ and $\widehat{\theta}_{t}=(\widehat{\theta}_{i,t})_{i=1}^{K}$ , with $\widehat{\theta}_{i,t}=(\widehat{\theta}_{i,r,t})_{r=1}^{d}$ , for the array of model score vectors. Constants are collected in Table 16.

1. 

Fit. Separately for each objective $r$ , fit the Bradley–Terry model to all comparisons collected so far:

 $\widehat{\theta}_{\cdot,r,t}\in\arg\!\max_{\vartheta\in\Theta_{\mathrm{box}}}\ell_{r,t}(\vartheta),\qquad\Theta_{\mathrm{box}}:=\{\vartheta\in\mathbb{R}^{K}:\mathbf{1}^{\top}\vartheta=0,\ \|\vartheta\|_{\infty}\leq S_{\max}\},$ 

where $\ell_{r,t}$ is the log-likelihood for objective $r$ . The numerical fits use the tolerance and iteration limit in Table 16.

2. 

Construct alternatives. Let $\widehat{\mathcal{P}}_{t}=\mathcal{P}(\widehat{\theta}_{t})$ , which is also the Pareto frontier of the induced Borda scores by Lemma 2.1. There are two kinds of alternatives. A drop alternative $(p,p^{\prime})$ , for distinct $p,p^{\prime}\in\widehat{\mathcal{P}}_{t}$ , imposes $\theta_{p^{\prime},r}\geq\theta_{p,r}$ for every $r$ . These inequalities describe the closure of the strict-dominance region; boundary points need not remove $p$ from the strict Pareto frontier. An add alternative for $c\notin\widehat{\mathcal{P}}_{t}$ assigns an objective $g(p)\in[d]$ to each $p\in\widehat{\mathcal{P}}_{t}$ and imposes $\theta_{c,g(p)}\geq\theta_{p,g(p)}$ . Thus no current Pareto model strictly dominates $c$ . Each add candidate has $d^{|\widehat{\mathcal{P}}_{t}|}$ possible assignments.

3. 

Screen alternatives and refit. Set $\widehat{p}_{ij,r,t}=\sigma(\widehat{\theta}_{i,r,t}-\widehat{\theta}_{j,r,t})$ . The observed Fisher information is

 $\mathcal{I}_{r,t}=\sum_{i<j}N_{ij,r}(t)\widehat{p}_{ij,r,t}(1-\widehat{p}_{ij,r,t})(e_{i}-e_{j})(e_{i}-e_{j})^{\top},$ 

where $e_{i}$ is the $i$ th standard basis vector. The local quadratic approximation to the likelihood loss from imposing $\theta_{a,r}\geq\theta_{b,r}$ is

 $h_{ab,r,t}=\frac{\bigl((\widehat{\theta}_{b,r,t}-\widehat{\theta}_{a,r,t})^{+}\bigr)^{2}}{2(e_{a}-e_{b})^{\top}\mathcal{I}_{r,t}^{\dagger}(e_{a}-e_{b})},$ 

where $\mathcal{I}_{r,t}^{\dagger}$ is the Moore–Penrose pseudoinverse. We score a drop alternative by $\sum_{r}h_{p^{\prime}p,r,t}$ and an add candidate $c$ by $\max_{p\in\widehat{\mathcal{P}}_{t}}\min_{r}h_{cp,r,t}$ . The drop score sums losses on separate objectives. The add score keeps only the most expensive of the per-model cheapest constraints and can understate the joint loss, potentially favoring add candidates. These scores serve as a computational heuristic for selecting an alternative.

For an add candidate $c$ , we use the assignment $g_{c}(p)\in\arg\min_{r}h_{cp,r,t}$ for every $p\in\widehat{\mathcal{P}}_{t}$ . This assignment minimizes $\max_{p}h_{cp,g(p),t}$ over all assignments $g$ , and the minimum is the candidate’s score above.

Let $\widehat{\xi}_{t}$ be the drop or add alternative with the smallest screening score, and let $h_{t}^{\mathrm{scr}}$ denote this score.

For each objective $r$ , we refit the Bradley–Terry model subject to the ordering constraints specified by $\widehat{\xi}_{t}$ .
For a drop alternative $(p,p^{\prime})$ , the constraint is $\vartheta_{p^{\prime}}\geq\vartheta_{p}$ .
For an add alternative $(c,g)$ , the constraints are $\vartheta_{c}\geq\vartheta_{p}$ for all $p\in\widehat{\mathcal{P}}_{t}$ with $g(p)=r$ .
Let $\Theta_{\xi,r}$ denote the subset of
 $\Theta_{\mathrm{box}}$ satisfying these constraints for alternative $\xi$ .
We numerically solve

 $\max_{\vartheta\in\Theta_{\widehat{\xi}_{t},r}}\ell_{r,t}(\vartheta)$ 

and denote the resulting estimate by $\widetilde{\theta}_{\cdot,r,t}$ .
The optimization separates across objectives because the total log-likelihood is a sum over objectives and each constraint involves only one objective.

These fits separate across objectives. To account for numerical optimization error, write $f_{r,t}=-\ell_{r,t}$ and compute

 $g_{r,t}=\left[\nabla f_{r,t}(\widetilde{\theta}_{\cdot,r,t})^{\top}\widetilde{\theta}_{\cdot,r,t}-\min_{\vartheta\in\Theta_{\widehat{\xi}_{t},r}}\nabla f_{r,t}(\widetilde{\theta}_{\cdot,r,t})^{\top}\vartheta\right]^{+}.$ 

Convexity gives a lower bound $f_{r,t}(\widetilde{\theta}_{\cdot,r,t})-g_{r,t}$ on the optimum over $\Theta_{\widehat{\xi}_{t},r}$ . Since $f_{r,t}(\widehat{\theta}_{\cdot,r,t})$ is an upper bound on the optimum over $\Theta_{\mathrm{box}}$ without the alternative’s constraints, a lower bound on the selected alternative’s likelihood loss is

 $G_{t}^{\mathrm{alt}}=\sum_{r}\left[f_{r,t}(\widetilde{\theta}_{\cdot,r,t})-g_{r,t}-f_{r,t}(\widehat{\theta}_{\cdot,r,t})\right]^{+}.$ 

The implemented statistic is $\Psi_{t}=\min\{h_{t}^{\mathrm{scr}},G_{t}^{\mathrm{alt}}\}$ , so stopping requires both the screening score and this numerical lower bound to exceed the threshold.

4. 

Stop. Define the comparison-count matrix

 $\Omega_{r,t}=\sum_{i<j}N_{ij,r}(t)(e_{i}-e_{j})(e_{i}-e_{j})^{\top}$ 

and threshold

 $\beta_{t}(\delta)=\log\frac{1}{\delta}+\frac{\lambda_{\mathrm{reg}}}{2}\sum_{r}\|\widehat{\theta}_{\cdot,r,t}\|_{2}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{r}\log\det\!\left(I_{K}+\frac{\bar{\sigma}^{2}}{\lambda_{\mathrm{reg}}}\Omega_{r,t}\right).$ 

The method returns $\widehat{\mathcal{P}}_{t}$ when the fits $\widehat{\theta}_{\cdot,r,t}$ and $\widetilde{\theta}_{\cdot,r,t}$ have converged for every $r$ and $\Psi_{t}\geq\beta_{t}(\delta)$ . If it has not stopped at $t=T_{\max}$ , the trial is capped and terminates. Otherwise, it proceeds to the next batch.

5. 

Allocate the next batch. For each pair–objective cell, compute

 $\kappa_{ij,r,t}=\operatorname{kl}\!\left(\sigma(\widehat{\theta}_{i,r,t}-\widehat{\theta}_{j,r,t}),\sigma(\widetilde{\theta}_{i,r,t}-\widetilde{\theta}_{j,r,t})\right),\qquad\kappa_{\max,t}:=\max\{\varepsilon_{\kappa},\max_{a<b,s}\kappa_{ab,s,t}\}.$ 

Update the current weights by

 $w_{ij,r}^{\mathrm{new}}=\frac{w_{ij,r}^{\mathrm{old}}\exp\!\left(\alpha_{\mathrm{MW}}\kappa_{ij,r,t}/(\sqrt{m}\,\kappa_{\max,t})\right)}{\sum_{a<b}\sum_{s=1}^{d}w_{ab,s}^{\mathrm{old}}\exp\!\left(\alpha_{\mathrm{MW}}\kappa_{ab,s,t}/(\sqrt{m}\,\kappa_{\max,t})\right)}.$ 

This favors comparisons that distinguish the current fit from the selected alternative. Schedule the next check at

 $t_{\mathrm{next}}:=\min\{T_{\max},\max(t+N_{\mathrm{cell}},\lceil a_{\mathrm{batch}}t\rceil)\}.$ 

Form the mixed weights $\widetilde{w}_{ij,r}=(1-\rho_{t}^{\mathrm{mix}})w_{ij,r}+\rho_{t}^{\mathrm{mix}}/N_{\mathrm{cell}}$ with $\rho_{t}^{\mathrm{mix}}=t_{\mathrm{next}}^{-\gamma_{\mathrm{mix}}}$ ; the unmixed weights $w_{ij,r}$ are the ones carried to the next check. The $t_{\mathrm{next}}-t$ queries are allocated by a batched variant of C-tracking (Garivier and Kaufmann, 2016): update the cumulative target counts, initialized at $P_{ij,r}=n_{0}$ , by $P_{ij,r}\leftarrow P_{ij,r}+(t_{\mathrm{next}}-t)\,\widetilde{w}_{ij,r}$ , then split the batch across cells in proportion to the positive deficits $(P_{ij,r}-N_{ij,r}(t))^{+}$ , rounding to integers so that the allocations sum to $t_{\mathrm{next}}-t$ . Sample the batch and proceed to the next check.

Relation to the scalar implementation.

The threshold follows the form in Goldberger and Rudi (2026, Equation (4)). Stacking the objective-specific score vectors gives a block-diagonal comparison-count matrix with blocks $\Omega_{r,t}$ , which yields the sums above. We use the mixed and cumulative targets of their C-tracking construction (Section 5.4) and the mixing exponent of their Section 7; unlike C-tracking, which assigns one query at a time to the cell with the largest deficit, we allocate each batch in proportion to the positive deficits. Our allocation uses one multiplicative-weights update per check against the selected alternative, with gains normalized to $[0,1]$ ; this provides a simple allocation rule compatible with the screened fits. It replaces the scalar method’s entropic follow-the-regularized-leader updates, whose experiments use learning rate $t^{-0.2}$ . The screening and allocation choices are implementation choices for this exploratory extension.

## E.2 Comparison with VB-EGE

We run this exploratory comparison at $\delta=0.05$ , using the instances and replication scheme of Appendix C. This gives $12{,}400$ trials per method over 28 settings: 12 scaling settings, 8 benchmarks, and 8 Pareto-set size settings. The capped mean stopping time is the mean comparison count until stopping or capping, with capped trials recorded at $T_{\max}$ ; standard errors use the same counts. For each setting, let $R$ be the ratio of the capped mean stopping time of Pareto BT-GLR T&S to that of VB-EGE. For a group of $n$ settings, we report the geometric mean $\exp(n^{-1}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\log R_{k})$ .

Pareto BT-GLR T&S has the smaller capped mean stopping time in 1 of the 28 settings: 1 of the 12 scaling settings, and none of the 8 benchmarks or the 8 Pareto-set size settings (Figures 22, 23 and 24). The geometric-mean ratios are $4.10$ over the scaling settings and $20.6$ over the Pareto-set size settings.

On Convex-3D and Two-group-10, respectively, 10 and 9 of the 500 Pareto BT-GLR T&S trials are capped ( $2.0\%$ and $1.8\%$ ). These capped trials contribute $2.0\times 10^{16}$ and $1.8\times 10^{16}$ to the corresponding means, accounting for more than $99.8\%$ of each mean. Consequently, the two bars primarily reflect the chosen cap, and an aggregate benchmark ratio would be sensitive to that choice. We report a geometric-mean ratio of $14.5$ over the six benchmarks with no capped trials; across all 26 settings with no capped trials, it is $9.01$ . Every VB-EGE trial stops. All $12{,}381$ stopped Pareto BT-GLR T&S trials and all $12{,}400$ VB-EGE trials exactly recover the true Pareto frontier.

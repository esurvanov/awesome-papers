Cross-Modal Fusion for Arabic Hateful Meme Detection

Abstract

We present SZED’s submission to ArGuard 2026 Track A: Arabic hateful meme detection.
The task has two parts: binary classification (A1) and fine-grained multi-label
tagging (A2).
For A1, we fuse MARBERTv2 and CLIP ViT-L/14 embeddings, comparing concatenation,
self-attention, and ensemble heads.
For A2, we use a multi-task label-attention architecture with per-class query vectors
and an auxiliary head for cascade calibration.
We reach macro-F1 = 0.7597 on A1 and 0.3142 on A2 (averaged over 10 active classes).
We report reproducibility across runs, per-class error patterns, and the effect of
cascade calibration on the long-tailed A2 label space.

## 1 Introduction

Detecting hateful content in Arabic multimodal memes is harder than detecting
text-only hate speech: it requires jointly interpreting visual cues, embedded OCR
text, dialectal variation, sarcasm, and culturally specific references
Alam et al. (2026).
ArGuard 2026 Track A splits this problem into two stages.
Subtask A1 asks whether a meme is hateful at all, while Subtask A2 requires
assigning zero or more fine-grained harm categories (e.g., Mocking,
Incitement, Dehumanization) to memes regardless of their binary
status, over a label space that partially overlaps with the AHA-Memes benchmark
Kmainasi et al. (2026).

We treat A1 and A2 as architecturally distinct problems.
A1 uses a standard late-fusion design, and most of our effort there goes into
comparing simple fusion strategies.
For A2, a single pooled fusion vector seemed like a poor fit for ten simultaneous,
structurally-linked, and heavily imbalanced binary decisions, so we instead use
per-class label-attention together with an auxiliary binary signal for cascade
calibration.
As an ablation on A1, we also test whether a more expressive cross-modal attention
mechanism and an explicit image-text incongruity feature improve on the simpler
ensemble.
We found that they do not, and Section 5.2 discusses why this
negative result is still informative.

## 2 Related Work

## Arabic and multilingual hateful meme detection.

Hateful meme detection has been widely studied for English-language content Tan and Bansal (2019); Lu et al. (2019). However, Arabic remains relatively under-resourced. The AHA-Memes benchmark Kmainasi et al. (2026) is closely related to our work. It contains 5K Arabic memes with fine-grained multi-label annotations. Its taxonomy also overlaps substantially with the A2 label space used in ArGuard.

The results reported on AHA-Memes highlight several important challenges. Text provides the strongest unimodal signal for Arabic memes, as text-only fine-tuned models outperform image-only models. Late fusion further improves binary hateful recall compared with text-only models. However, fine-grained hate-type classification remains difficult across model families. The best reported macro-F1 is 0.340, achieved by a large vision-language model Kmainasi et al. (2026). These results provide a useful reference for the difficulty of the A2 task. However, the AHA-Memes benchmark uses a different data collection process and train/test split from ArGuard.

## Multimodal fusion for meme understanding.

A common approach to multimodal classification is late fusion. In this setting, modality-specific representations, such as CLS or pooled embeddings, are combined and passed to a shared classifier Tan and Bansal (2019); Lu et al. (2019). This approach can work well for binary classification. However, it can be limiting for fine-grained multi-label tasks with strong class imbalance.

A shared representation requires the model to make several label decisions from the same bottleneck vector. Label-attention methods provide an alternative. Each class is represented by a learned query that attends to the full sequence of text tokens and image patches Ridnik et al. (2021). This allows each label to focus on the parts of the multimodal input that are most relevant to it. We use this design for the A2 task.

## Research gap.

Previous Arabic meme studies, including AHA-Memes baselines, generally use a shared fusion head for both binary and fine-grained classification. In ArGuard, we observe strong co-occurrence patterns between the binary hateful label and the fine-grained hate labels (Section 3). This structure provides useful information that a shared classification head does not explicitly model.

Our approach addresses this gap in two ways. First, we use cascade calibration to explicitly incorporate the binary prediction into the fine-grained decision process. Second, we use per-class label attention instead of a single pooled representation for multi-label prediction. Together, these choices allow the model to better exploit the structure of the A2 label space.

## 4 System Architecture and Training

Figure 1: System overview. (a) A1: shared encoders feed two fusion heads combined
via a probability-averaged ensemble. (b) A2: full token/patch sequences feed a
fusion transformer, read by ten label-attention queries and, separately, an
auxiliary binary head used for cascade calibration.

Both subtasks share the same pretrained encoder pair
(Figure 1): MARBERTv2 Abdul-Mageed et al. (2021)
for Arabic text (hidden size 768) and CLIP ViT-Large/14 Radford et al. (2021)
for images (hidden size 1024).
All models are implemented in PyTorch using the HuggingFace transformers library.
To fit training within T4/L4 GPU memory, we freeze all layers except the last two
transformer blocks of each encoder, leaving 8.7–8.9% of parameters trainable
(A1: 40.4M / 466.5M; A2: 41.8M / 467.8M).
The shared fusion space has dimension 256; all MLP classifiers use hidden width 256.
All models are trained with AdamW Loshchilov and Hutter (2019)
(lr = 2e-5, weight decay = 1e-4) and linear warmup (6%)
followed by decay, batch size 8, gradient accumulation 2 (effective batch 16),
mixed precision, and gradient clipping at norm 1.0. All experiments use a single fixed seed (42).

## 4.1 Subtask A1: Binary Fusion

On top of the shared encoders we compare two fusion heads
(Figure 1a).
The concatenation head concatenates the pooled text [CLS] and CLIP
embeddings and passes them through a small MLP classifier
(Linear(1792 $\to$ 256)+ ReLU + Dropout $\to$ Linear(256 $\to$ 2); 460K params).
The transformer-fusion head projects both embeddings to the shared 256-dim
space with a learned modality-type embedding, applies a two-layer, four-head
self-attention encoder (FFN width 512; 1.51M params), and then mean-pools before
classifying.
Note that this “transformer fusion” applies self-attention over two pooled
vectors, not token/patch-level cross-attention;
Section 5.2 reports a variant that uses the latter.
Both heads train for 6 epochs with class-weighted cross-entropy
(weights $\approx$ 0.54/1.46 for the 62/38 class imbalance).
After training, we grid-search a decision threshold (37 points, 0.05-0.95) for
the concat head, the transformer head, and their probability-averaged ensemble,
and select whichever variant/threshold combination maximizes dev macro-F1.

## 4.2 Subtask A2: Multi-Task Label-Attention

A2 uses a distinct architecture (Figure 1b), motivated by
the two properties noted in Section 3: imbalance across ten
simultaneous decisions and a strong structural link to the binary label.
Rather than pooling each modality into a single vector, we
MARBERTv2’s full
token sequence and CLIP’s patch grid (spatially downsampled 2 $\times$ 2,
16 $\times$ 16 $\to$ 8 $\times$ 8 = 64 tokens).
We project both to a shared 256-dim space and fuse them with a two-layer,
four-head transformer encoder (FFN width 512).
Each of the ten classes gets its own learned query vector (10 $\times$ 256
parameter table), which attends over the fused sequence through multi-head
label-attention and feeds an independent linear classifier.
This avoids the single-bottleneck failure mode of pooled multi-label
classification.
A mean-pooled summary of the fused sequence also feeds a small auxiliary binary (Hateful/Not Hateful) head (Linear(256 $\to$ 128)+ReLU+ Linear(128 $\to$ 2); 33K params).
The two heads are trained jointly via
 $\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{L}_{\text{ASL}}(\text{fg})+0.3\cdot\mathcal{L}_{\text{CE}}(\text{aux})$ ,
where $\mathcal{L}_{\text{ASL}}$ is Asymmetric Loss Ridnik et al. (2021)
( $\gamma_{\text{neg}}$ =4.0, $\gamma_{\text{pos}}$ =1.0, clip=0.05).
We chose ASL over plain weighted BCE for its better fit to long-tailed multi-label imbalance. We train for 10 epochs. Dev macro-F1 at a global 0.5 threshold reaches its peak at epoch 4 and then falls even as training loss continues to decrease, so we keep the epoch-4 checkpoint.

Cascade calibration.
At inference, we exploit the near-deterministic label hierarchy directly.
Hateful-subtype probabilities are scaled by the auxiliary head’s P(Hateful),
non-hateful-only subtypes by P(Not Hateful), and Other is left unscaled.
We then tune a per-class decision threshold on these calibrated probabilities
(grid search, 19 points) rather than using one global threshold, since rare
classes (e.g., Sarcasm, best threshold 0.10) and common ones
(e.g., Other, best threshold 0.55) need different operating points.
Thresholds are tuned on the 500-example dev set, which carries an overfitting risk
for the rarest classes; we flag this in Limitations.

## 6 Error Analysis

Figure 2: Official test-set per-class F1 for A2
(macro-F1 = 0.3142 over 10 active classes), with support (n), sorted descending.

Per-class F1 on the official A2 test set (Figure 2) tracks
class support closely.
High-support classes perform reasonably (Humor, n=217, F1=0.627;
Sarcasm, n=186, F1=0.575; Mocking, n=103, F1=0.530), while
low-support classes collapse (Slurs, n=16, F1=0.035;
Exclusion, n=3, F1=0.046).
We would therefore not interpret these results as evidence that the model is inherently unable to learn these categories: with only 3-16 test instances per class, F1 scores are highly sensitive to sampling variability, and reflect constraints of the evaluation methodology as much as model limitations.

A second pattern is semantic overlap between neighboring categories.
Mocking, Sarcasm, and Humor share surface-level
irony markers and exaggeration cues that are not always distinguishable from
OCR text and image content alone; Arabic sarcasm in particular tends to rely on
discourse-level and cultural context that a token/patch-level model cannot access.
Slurs and Contempt face dialectal variation and euphemistic
substitution: an offensive term may be transliterated, partially censored, or
expressed in a regional dialect outside MARBERTv2’s pretraining distribution.

For A1, the test results are slightly higher than the development result, with macro-F1 increasing from 0.7381 on the development set to 0.7556 and 0.7597 across the two test runs. This does not indicate a clear generalization problem. However, since the development and test models were trained independently, these results should not be interpreted as direct evidence that overfitting is absent.
The per-class results provide more insight than the overall macro-F1. Across the reported runs, F1 for the Not-Hateful class consistently exceeds F1 for the Hateful class by approximately 15-19 points. This indicates that the model has greater difficulty identifying hateful memes.
The official test confusion matrix further illustrates this pattern. It contains 293 true negatives, 59 false positives, 44 false negatives, and 104 true positives. Thus, 44 of the 148 hateful memes are missed, while 59 of the 352 non-hateful memes are incorrectly flagged. Although both error types matter in content moderation, false negatives are particularly important because they allow hateful content to remain undetected.

## 7 Conclusion

We have presented SZED’s system for ArGuard 2026 Track A: a compact late-fusion
binary classifier for A1 (test macro-F1 = 0.7597), and a purpose-built multi-task
label-attention architecture for A2 (test macro-F1 = 0.3142 over 10 active
classes), improved via cascade calibration that exploits the task’s own label
hierarchy.
Raw vs. calibrated scores on both dev and test confirm that calibration is
beneficial on both splits ( $+$ 0.012-0.017 macro-F1 absolute).
Our ablation (Table 3) shows that a more expressive
cross-modal attention mechanism and an explicit incongruity feature do not automatically help
on this dataset:
the CLIP cosine similarity between text and image carries no separating information
between hateful and non-hateful memes here, a finding consistent with
the AHA-Memes observation that caption semantics dominate image content as the
primary signal Kmainasi et al. (2026).
For future work, we would like to explore dialect-aware pretraining or data
augmentation for the underrepresented A2 classes, and test whether the
incongruity signal becomes informative when computed with an Arabic-specialized
CLIP variant rather than a general
multilingual one.

## Limitations

We did not preserve trained model weights between the development and test phases
of the leaderboard, so each reported result comes from an independently trained
run of the same architecture.
We mitigate this by reporting run-to-run variance wherever we have multiple
test-phase runs, but cannot rule out that a single unusually good or bad run is
driving any particular number.

The A2 per-class threshold tuning relies on 500 dev examples.
Several classes have single-digit support in that split, making per-class
thresholds susceptible to overfitting; the rarest-class results should be
interpreted with this caveat in mind.

Our A2 per-class error analysis is necessarily limited for the lowest-support
test classes (Exclusion, n=3; Slurs, n=16), and to a lesser
extent Dehumanization (n=21) and Inferiority (n=23), where F1
is high-variance by construction.

The cross-modal incongruity feature was computed with a general-purpose
multilingual CLIP variant.
Dedicated unimodal (text-only and image-only) baseline runs were omitted due to
compute constraints; we instead reference the AHA-Memes baselines
Kmainasi et al. (2026) as an external reference point.
An Arabic-specialized CLIP may yield a more informative incongruity signal, but we
did not have the compute budget to test this.

Finally, our system uses no external training data or synthetic augmentation,
leaving potential headroom unexplored for the lowest-support A2 classes.

## Ethical Considerations

This work concerns automated detection of hateful content, which carries risk in
both directions: false negatives let harmful content go unflagged, while false
positives risk over-censoring legitimate speech, satire, or in-group reclaimed
language, particularly given the cultural and dialectal nuance of Arabic online
discourse.
Our A1 error analysis identifies false negatives (44 missed hateful memes out of
148) as the dominant error mode, and we note this explicitly so that downstream
users can calibrate their reliance on the system accordingly.
All training and evaluation data is the officially released, consented ArGuard
shared-task dataset; we did not collect, scrape, or introduce any additional
user-generated content.
Potentially offensive content (slurs, dehumanizing language) is handled only in
aggregate statistical form, and we did not reproduce specific offensive text or
imagery.
We would caution against deploying this system or any system trained on 3,500
examples with several classes under 60 instances as a sole or automated moderation decision-maker without human review, given its demonstrated instability on rare categories and the inherent difficulty of the task.

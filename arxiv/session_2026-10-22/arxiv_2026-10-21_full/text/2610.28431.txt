Abstract

World-Action Models (WAMs) have emerged as a promising paradigm for embodied generalist policies by jointly modeling future world evolution and robot actions across diverse manipulation tasks.
However, representing diverse futures is only the first step: a capable WAM must also generate futures that match the requirements of the current task and execution progress.
In long-horizon manipulation where similar observation-action segments occur across different tasks and stages,
existing approaches may generate locally plausible yet misdirected rollouts to drift toward wrong task stage, i.e., a phenomenon we term Stage Confusion.
To address this problem, we propose TaskWAM, a closed-loop, stage-aware framework that represents fine-grained task requirements with Mode Tokens and tracks execution progress with State Tokens, forming a structured task state to steer world–action generation toward intended future.
We further introduce StageBench, a controlled multi-stage manipulation benchmark with parameterized stage variations and automated evaluation.
Extensive experiments on both simulation benchmarks and real-world tasks demonstrate that TaskWAM effectively reduces confusion and achieves competitive performance with state-of-the-art methods.
Moreover, TaskWAM provides strong plug-and-play transfer across heterogeneous WAM architectures and robot embodiments, including single-arm robots, dual-arm grippers, dexterous hands, and humanoid robots.

## 1 Introduction

Recently, World-Action Models (WAMs) (Bi et al., 2026; Ye et al., 2026; Li et al., 2026; Yuan et al., 2026; Chen et al., 2026a) have shown promise for embodied generalist policies by jointly modeling future world evolution and robot actions, with the goal of supporting diverse manipulation tasks in everyday environments(Zhu et al., 2025; Li et al., 2025; Li et al., 2026).
However, modeling diverse futures is only the first step.
Once models are capable of representing multiple possible futures, the next challenge lies in generating future that is consistent
with the task requirements and execution context.
Existing WAMs typically infer stage progression implicitly from task instructions, observations, and execution histories (Lin et al., 2026; Zhao et al., 2026).
This becomes particularly challenging in long-horizon household manipulation, where similar observation-action interactions can correspond to different next stages depending on fine-grained task requirements, e.g., ordering, timing, repetition, or embodiment assignment, as illustrated in Figure 1 (Top left).

Consequently, WAMs may generate futures that are visually plausible and physically feasible, yet lead execution to the wrong stage.
We refer to this failure as stage confusion in world-action modeling (hereafter, stage confusion).
A few recent WAMs Yang et al. (2026); Cai et al. (2026) have explored longer-term history modeling, but stage confusion is not merely a memory-length problem, leaving two key challenges:
(1) similar interaction histories may still require different next stages, making instruction-specific guidance essential for resolving stage ambiguity; and
(2) simply extending history introduces substantial redundant context, which can obscure critical execution cues for stage tracking while increasing the architectural burden of WAMs.

To address these challenges, we propose TaskWAM, a closed-loop, stage-aware framework that explicitly grounds world-action generation in both task requirements and execution progress for long-horizon manipulation.
Specifically, we introduce Structured Task-State Grounding with two complementary representations:
Mode Tokens retrieve mode representations from a learnable mode codebook and ground them with text context to encode fine-grained execution requirements, while State Tokens track execution progress through causal temporal modeling of closed-loop action-observation transitions.
We further propose Task-State Conditioned Future Steering, which steers future prediction by inject the structured task state into world and action generation through attention conditioning and residual flow correction.
TaskWAM thus bridges diverse future modeling and intended future generation, offering two main advantages:
(i) future generation grounding in closed-loop robot-environment interaction;
(ii) plug-and-play steering mechanism applicable across heterogeneous WAM backbones.

Additionally, existing benchmarks (Mees et al., 2022; Liu et al., 2023; Chen et al., 2026b; Nasiriany et al., 2026) has mainly evaluated tasks in relatively independent settings, lacking sufficient data and evaluation protocols to assess this capability.
To support systematic evaluation, we further introduce StageBench, a controlled multi-stage manipulation benchmark built on LIBERO Liu et al. (2023) and RoboTwin Chen et al. (2026b).
StageBench constructs overlapping observation-action trajectories across tasks and execution stages by varying stage compositions and orders, covering visual grounding, sequential reasoning, embodiment coordination, etc.
Extensive experiments in simulation and diverse real-world robotic tasks demonstrate that TaskWAM significantly reduces stage confusion and improves multi-stage manipulation over state-of-the-art methods.
We further validate its generalization on RoboTwin 2.0 benchmark Chen et al. (2026b) and its plug-and-play applicability across heterogeneous WAM architectures.
Moreover, our method can be applied across diverse robot embodiments, including single-arm robots, dual-arm grippers, dexterous hands, and humanoid platforms.
Our contributions are summarized as follows:

• 

We identify stage confusion in world-action modeling, where WAMs generate locally plausible yet stage-inconsistent futures under overlapping observation-action trajectories.

• 

We propose TaskWAM, which explicitly grounds world-action generation in task requirements and execution progress through Structured Task-State Grounding and task-state-conditioned future steering for stage-consistent future generation.

• 

We introduce StageBench, a controlled multi-stage manipulation benchmark that systematically constructs overlapping trajectories through parameterized task-stage variations and provides automated stage-aware evaluation.

• 

Extensive experiments across simulation and real-world tasks demonstrate that TaskWAM significantly reduces stage confusion and improves multi-stage manipulation across heterogeneous WAM backbones and diverse robot embodiments.

## 2 Related Work

World-Action Models.
Generalist robot learning aims to develop unified policies that can handle diverse manipulation tasks within a single model.
Vision-Language-Action models (Black et al., 2025a; NVIDIA et al., 2025) have advanced this direction by scaling language-conditioned robot policies.
World-Action Models (WAMs) (Bi et al., 2026; Zhu et al., 2025; Ye et al., 2026; Li et al., 2026; Yuan et al., 2026; Cai et al., 2026; Chen et al., 2026a) further extend this paradigm by jointly modeling future observations and robot actions.
Recent studies explores inference-time imagination, world-action information exchange, and more efficient generation schedules (Ye et al., 2026; Li et al., 2026; Yuan et al., 2026), such as DreamZero (Ye et al., 2026) and LingBot-VA (Li et al., 2026).
However, existing WAMs still typically learn world-action futures from unified conditioning contexts (Lin et al., 2026; Zhao et al., 2026), leaving stage progression to be inferred implicitly from instructions, observations, and histories.
Consequently, a predicted future can remain locally plausible while leading the robot to the wrong stage, i.e., plausible future, yet wrong choice.
In contrast, TaskWAM explicitly grounds future generation in task requirements
and execution progress, targeting stage confusion in long-horizon manipulation.

Historical Context Modeling.
Temporal context has been widely used to support long-horizon decision making
in language modeling (Dai et al., 2019),
navigation (Fang et al., 2019),
video generation (Henschel et al., 2025),
and robot manipulation (Guhur et al., 2023).
Existing robot policies incorporate history through
temporal abstraction (Belkhale et al., 2024),
visual memory (Fang et al., 2025),
retrieval (Shah et al., 2026),
or explicit state representations (Chung et al., 2026).
However, task instructions and longer histories is insufficient to guarantee that
task requirements remain effective throughout execution; policies may still
rely on easier visual-action correlations (Lin et al., 2026; Zhao et al., 2026).
Moreover, longer execution contexts often contain redundant information,
which can dilute critical execution cues while increasing design complexity and
computational cost (Sridhar et al., 2026).
Rather than extending raw history, TaskWAM extracts progress-relevant evidence
from causal action-observation transitions for explicit execution tracking.

Long-Horizon Multi-Stage Manipulation.
Long-horizon household tasks are common in daily life and remain a key challenge for reliable robot deployment (Li et al., 2023a).
A common characteristic of these tasks is that similar observation-action interactions often recur within the same scene across different tasks and execution stages (Nasiriany et al., 2026).
Reliable execution therefore requires robots to distinguish such overlapping trajectories and complete the required stages in demanding order (Mees et al., 2022; Li et al., 2023a).
However, prior work has largely learned and evaluated tasks in relatively isolated settings (O’Neill et al., 2024), leaving this capability underexplored.
In this work, we target this setting with a stage-aware approach and introduce a controlled multi-stage benchmark for systematic evaluation.

## 3 TaskWAM Framework

## 3.1 Problem Formulation

Stage Confusion.
For task $i$ , we denote a rollout by $\mathcal{T}_{i}=\{\ell_{i},\xi_{i}\}$ , where $\xi_{i}=\{(o_{t}^{i},a_{t}^{i})\}_{t=1}^{T_{i}}$ is segmented into a stage sequence $\{s_{i}^{1},\ldots,s_{i}^{n_{i}}\}$ .
At the transition from stage $k$ , let $\mathcal{H}_{i}^{k}$ denote the observed execution history and $s_{i}^{k+1}$ the subsequent stage.
Two stage-transition points $(i,k)$ and $(j,k^{\prime})$ may have similar local histories, $\mathcal{H}_{i}^{k}\approx\mathcal{H}_{j}^{k^{\prime}}$ , but require different subsequent stages, $s_{i}^{k+1}\neq s_{j}^{k^{\prime}+1}$ .
WAMs may generate a locally plausible future $\hat{s}_{i}^{k+1}$ yet advances execution to the wrong stage, which we refer to as Stage Confusion.
It typically occurs in two cases:
❶ Cross-task confusion, where the model continued toward a wrong stage from another task, i.e., $\hat{s}_{i}^{k+1}=s_{j}^{k^{\prime}}$ , $j\neq i$ ;
❷ Within-task confusion, where the model mistakes similar stages within the same task for one another, leading to stage repetition or skipping, i.e., $\hat{s}_{i}^{k+1}=s_{i}^{k^{\prime}}$ , $k^{\prime}\neq k+1$ .

Task-State Grounding for Future Generation.
Stage progression depends on two complementary task factors:
task requirements specify how execution should proceed, while closed-loop interaction history provides evidence of the progress achieved so far.
We represent them as an explicit task state $\mathbf{T}_{t}=[\mathbf{M};\mathbf{S}_{t}]$ ,
where $\mathbf{M}$ encodes fine-grained task requirements grounded in instruction $\ell$ , and $\mathbf{S}_{t}$ tracks execution progress from history $\mathcal{H}_{t}$ .
We condition future world evolution $\mathbf{W}_{t}^{+}$ and
robot actions $\mathbf{A}_{t}^{+}$ on this structured state:

 $(\widehat{\mathbf{W}}_{t}^{+},\widehat{\mathbf{A}}_{t}^{+})\sim p_{\theta}\bigl(\mathbf{W}_{t}^{+},\mathbf{A}_{t}^{+}\mid\ell,o_{t},\mathcal{H}_{t},\mathbf{T}_{t}\bigr).$ 

(1)

Our goal is to explicitly model stage progression in world-action generation rather than leaving it to be implicitly inferred from an unstructured conditioning context.

## 3.2 Overview

TaskWAM grounds world–action generation in task requirements and
execution progress to reduce stage confusion.
As illustrated in Figure 2, it consists of two core components:
(1) Structured Task-State Grounding constructs Mode Tokens through codebook retrieval and text grounding, and State Tokens through interaction aggregation and causal temporal encoding; and
(2) Task-State Conditioned Future Steering incorporates the resulting task state into backbone conditioning and velocity predictions, steering generation toward the intended future.

## 3.3 Structured Task-State Grounding

Following Sec. 3.1, we construct Mode Tokens $\mathbf{M}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{m}\times d}$ and State Tokens $\mathbf{S}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{s}\times d}$ to form the structured task state
 $\mathcal{T}_{t}=[\mathbf{M};\mathbf{S}_{t}]$ .

Mode Tokens: instruction-grounded requirements.
Tasks can share the same manipulation steps while differing in fine-grained requirements, e.g., ordering, timing, repetition, and embodiment assignment (Liu et al., 2025).
To keep these distinctions available throughout execution, we retrieve mode representations from a learnable codebook and ground them in the full instruction context.

Given instruction $\ell$ , the pretrained T5 encoder (Raffel et al., 2020) produces text embedding $\mathbf{C}=\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{text}}(\ell)\in\mathbb{R}^{L\times D}$ .
A routing head then predicts a soft assignment over $K_{\mathrm{prior}}$ learnable modes:

 $\mathbf{c}_{\mathrm{pool}}=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{L}\mathrm{mask}_{i}\mathbf{c}_{i}}{\sum_{i=1}^{L}\mathrm{mask}_{i}},\qquad\bm{\alpha}=\mathrm{softmax}(W_{2}\,\mathrm{SiLU}(W_{1}\mathbf{c}_{\mathrm{pool}}+b_{1})+b_{2}),$ 

(2)

where $\mathrm{mask}_{i}$ identifies valid tokens and $\bm{\alpha}\in\mathbb{R}^{K_{\mathrm{prior}}}$ .

The mode codebook $\mathbf{E}\in\mathbb{R}^{K_{\mathrm{prior}}\times N_{m}\times D}$ stores $N_{m}$ learnable slots per mode.
We retrieve the corresponding mode slots through soft routing $\mathbf{m}_{l}^{\mathrm{raw}}=\sum_{k=1}^{K_{\mathrm{prior}}}\alpha_{k}\mathbf{E}_{k,l}$ for $l=1,\ldots,N_{m}$ .
The retrieved slots provide a coarse execution-requirement prior, serving as a prior for constructing Mode Tokens.
We then use these slots as queries over the original language-token sequence to incorporate instruction-specific requirements, such as the precise ordering and timing:

 $\mathbf{m}_{l}=\mathcal{Q}_{\mathrm{ground}}\left(\mathbf{m}_{l}^{\mathrm{raw}},\mathbf{C}\right),\qquad\mathbf{M}=[\mathbf{m}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{m}_{N_{m}}]\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{m}\times d}.$ 

(3)

where $\mathcal{Q}_{\mathrm{ground}}$ performs language cross-attention and projects its output to dimension $d$ .
The resulting Mode Tokens combine the retrieved prior with detailed task requirements and are retained across stages, providing persistent task guidance throughout world-action generation.

State Tokens: causal temporal execution tracking.
Mode Tokens specify the task requirements but do not indicate which stages have been completed.
We therefore construct State Tokens from actions and their observed outcomes in closed-loop robot-environment interaction, rather than encoding raw visual history alone.
A causal execution-state encoder first aggregates each interaction into a compact token and then models the sequence over time.

Specifically, for each history slot $k$ , we pair the preceding action chunk $\mathbf{A}_{k-1}$ with the visual latent $\mathbf{F}_{k}$ of the resulting observation, encoded by the Wan Video VAE (Team Wan et al., 2025), and its change
 $\Delta\mathbf{F}_{k}=\mathbf{F}_{k}-\mathbf{F}_{k-1}$ .
After modality-specific projection and positional encoding, learnable queries $\mathbf{q}_{k}$ aggregate these signals through a Q-Former (Li et al., 2023b):

 $\mathbf{e}_{k}=\mathcal{Q}_{\mathrm{s}}\left(\mathbf{q}_{k},\,[\phi_{v}(\mathbf{F}_{k});\phi_{\Delta}(\Delta\mathbf{F}_{k});\phi_{a}(\mathbf{A}_{k-1})]\right)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.$ 

(4)

where $\phi_{v}$ , $\phi_{\Delta}$ , and $\phi_{a}$ are modality-specific projections.
A causal temporal encoder then processes the most recent $K$ 
interaction tokens in execution order:

 $\mathbf{S}_{t}=\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{causal}}\left(\mathbf{e}_{t-K+1:t}\right)\in\mathbb{R}^{K\times d}.$ 

(5)

with $N_{s}=K$ .
The positional encodings identify interaction order, while the causal attention mask restricts each slot to current and preceding interactions, allowing State Tokens to summarize how interactions unfolded over time.

During deployment at step $t$ , the robot applies the predicted actions $\mathbf{A}_{t}$ and encodes the next observation $\mathbf{F}_{t+1}$ , the new transition $(\mathbf{F}_{t+1},\Delta\mathbf{F}_{t+1},\mathbf{A}_{t})$ is then appended, and $\mathbf{S}_{t+1}$ is updated.
This supplies subsequent world–action generation with progress information from robot–environment feedback.
Detailed settings of both token constructions are provided in
Appendix A.1.2.

## 3.4 Task-State Conditioned Future Steering

Given the structured task state $\mathbf{T}_{t}$ , TaskWAM steers world–action generation toward the intended future through backbone conditioning and residual velocity correction.

Task-State Conditioning.
We append $\mathbf{T}_{t}$ to the key-value context of both streams.
World and action tokens can therefore attend to the same task requirements and execution progress throughout generation.
To keep the active execution mode effective across backbone layers, we further pool the Mode Tokens into $\bar{\mathbf{m}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}$ and apply
layer-wise AdaLN modulation (Peebles & Xie, 2023):

 $[\bm{\gamma}^{m}_{l},\bm{\beta}^{m}_{l}]=g_{l}(\bar{\mathbf{m}}),\qquad\widetilde{\mathbf{H}}_{l}=(1+\bm{\gamma}^{m}_{l})\odot\mathrm{LN}(\mathbf{H}_{l})+\bm{\beta}^{m}_{l},$ 

(6)

where $g_{l}$ denotes a layer-specific modulation network.
This carries instruction-specific requirements across generation layers while attention incorporates the updated execution state.
The design preserves the backbone’s native world-action attention relations, supporting integration across heterogeneous WAM architectures (Sec. 5.2).

World-Action Flow Steering.
Beyond backbone conditioning, we apply task-state-conditioned residual corrections (Black et al., 2025b) to steer world and action generation toward the intended future.
For flow-based backbones, let $\mathbf{H}_{t,\tau}^{b}$ and $\mathbf{v}_{t,\tau}^{b}$ denote the hidden states and velocity predictions at rollout step $t$ and flow time $\tau$ , where $b\in\{w,a\}$ denotes the world or action stream.
Each lightweight cross-attention head $\mathcal{R}_{b}$ uses the
corresponding hidden states to query $\mathbf{T}_{t}$ and predict
a velocity residual:

 $\displaystyle\Delta\mathbf{v}_{t,\tau}^{b}$ 
 $\displaystyle=\mathcal{R}_{b}(\mathbf{H}_{t,\tau}^{b};\mathbf{T}_{t}),\qquad\widehat{\mathbf{v}}_{t,\tau}^{b}=\mathbf{v}_{t,\tau}^{b}+\gamma_{b}\Delta\mathbf{v}_{t,\tau}^{b},$ 

(7)

where $\gamma_{b}$ is a learnable bounded scale.
Both flows are corrected with the same task state, steering the intended stage progression rather than merely producing
mutually consistent world and action predictions.
For Cosmos Policy, the residual instead corrects denoised latent predictions under its native EDM formulation.

Training Objective.
We train TaskWAM end-to-end using each backbone’s vanilla generative objective, i.e.,
flow matching for flow-based WAMs and EDM denoising for Cosmos Policy.
The overall training objective is
 $\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{world}}+\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{action}}$ .
More implementation details are provided in Appendix A.2.

## 4 Stage-Confusion Benchmark

To support the systematic evaluation of stage confusion, we construct StageBench on the single-arm LIBERO and bimanual RoboTwin 2.0 platforms.
StageBench includes diverse kitchen and household scenarios with meaningful multi-stage manipulation routines, comprising 10 task families with visual grounding, sequential reasoning, temporal reasoning, and embodiment coordination, etc., as illustrated in Fig. 3.
Additional details are provided in Appendix B.1.

Figure 3: The visualization of task families in StageBench (see Appendix B.1 for more details).

Task design and data collection.
Each task family is instantiated into multiple task variants built from the same scene, objects, and manipulation stages, with matched stage counts and comparable difficulty.
Across variants, we construct different stage sequences by changing their order, timing, arm assignment, or repetition to target cross-task confusion.
Within each variant, repeated or similar interaction stages appear at different execution positions to target within-task confusion.
We collect demonstration trajectories through 3D mouse teleoperation and automated data generation, resulting in over 2.20 million frames in total, with approximately 12 hours of manual teleoperation.

Evaluation.
We build an automatic evaluator that reconstructs stage-wise execution from simulator states.
During each rollout, it records task-relevant object states, contacts, arm actions, and their timestamps, and matches these events against the stage definitions of the corresponding task variant.
This allows us to summarize which stages are entered and completed throughout execution, from which we derive task completion, execution route, and stage-level progress.
For each task, we manually inspect 100 evaluation results to confirm the reliability of the automatic evaluator.

## 5 experiment

## 5.1 Experimental Setup

Simulation Benchmarks.
We evaluate our method in two robotic simulation benchmarks:
(1) In StageBench, a controlled multi-stage benchmark with 8 task families spanning single-arm and bimanual manipulation, as detailed in Sec. 4.
(2) In RoboTwin 2.0 Chen et al. (2026b), we follow the same experimental settings of (Bi et al., 2026; Yuan et al., 2026; Li et al., 2026), training all models on 2,500 demonstrations collected in clean scenes (50 per task) and 25,000 demonstrations collected in randomized scenes (500 per task).

Baselines.
We compare our approach against the state-of-the-art (SOTA) baselines,
including WAMs:
Motus (Bi et al., 2026),
Cosmos Policy (Kim et al., 2026),
DreamZero (Ye et al., 2026),
Fast-WAM (Yuan et al., 2026),
LingBot-VA (Li et al., 2026),
LaWAM (Chen et al., 2026a),
and AHA-WAM (Cai et al., 2026).
We further include classical VLA baselines, including
 $\pi_{0.5}$ (Black et al., 2025a)
and GR00T N1.7 (NVIDIA et al., 2025).
We evaluate both final task success and stage-aware progression,
including stage-wise completion and Stage Progress Rate (SPR), to
measure reliable multi-stage execution beyond overall completion.

Implementation Details.
We build our framework upon Fast-WAM (Yuan et al., 2026) and Cosmos Policy (Kim et al., 2026), preserving their native inference structures while injecting task-state conditioning.
For StageBench, all methods are trained and evaluated under the same data and evaluation settings.
For RoboTwin 2.0, we follow its standard training and evaluation protocols.
Experiments are trained on $16\times$ NVIDIA H100 80GB GPUs, and all simulation results are evaluated over 100 trials.
More details are provided in the supplementary material.

## 5.2 Main Results

Results on StageBench.
Table 1 shows that TaskWAM consistently improves both WAM backbones across all eight task families.
On FastWAM, it raises average success from 40.9% to 69.4% (+28.5%), surpassing the strongest standalone baseline, $\pi_{0.5}$ , at 54.5%.
The gains range from 15–25% on kitchen tasks and 28–54% on bimanual tasks, with the largest improvement on Size-Order Cubes (11% to 65%).
TaskWAM also improves Cosmos Policy from 32.6% to 45.5% (+12.9%), demonstrating that task-state grounding provides consistent benefits across backbones and different stage-confusion scenarios.

Generalization to Robotwin 2.0.
Table 2 compares different methods on 50 RoboTwin 2.0 tasks under clean and randomized settings. TaskWAM consistently improves the Fast-WAM backbone and achieves highly competitive performance against recent SOTA methods, while obtaining the best result under the randomized setting.
These results demonstrate the effectiveness of TaskWAM across diverse manipulation tasks and environmental variations.

Table 5 evaluates whether TaskWAM can preserve reliable stage progression in real-world environments.
Across four tasks and diverse embodiments, TaskWAM consistently achieves higher final-stage success than FastWAM, improving by 15–35%.
The gains become larger in longer tasks with more stage transitions, where stage confusion accumulates over execution.
For instance, in the challenging nearly 2-minute Bread Toasting & Spreading task, both methods achieve similar early-stage completion, such as toasting. A representative failure case involving visually similar stages after long waiting periods is further analyzed in Appendix D.

## 6 conclusion

Reliable stage progression is a critical capability for long-horizon robotic manipulation, especially when different tasks and execution stages share similar observation-action interactions.
In this work, we identify stage confusion in World-Action Models, where locally plausible yet misdirected rollouts to drift toward wrong task stage, and propose TaskWAM to address this problem.
Our key insight is to ground future generation in explicit task requirements and realized execution progress through mode codebook retrieval, causal execution tracking, and backbone-agnostic future steering.
To support systematic evaluation, we further introduce StageBench with overlapping multi-stage trajectories and stage-aware evaluation.
Extensive experiments in simulation and real-world settings validate TaskWAM across heterogeneous WAM architectures and diverse robot embodiments.
In summary, our results highlight explicit stage-aware future generation as an important step toward reliable generalist robot behavior in long-horizon tasks.

## Appendix A Implementation Details

## A.1 Model Details

Notation.
We use $t$ for a closed-loop decision step and $\tau$ for flow time.
The trajectory $\xi_{i}$ and stage sequence $\{s_{i}^{1},\ldots,s_{i}^{n_{i}}\}$ 
follow Sec. 3.1.
The structured task state is
 $\mathbf{T}_{t}=[\mathbf{M};\mathbf{S}_{t}]$ , where the semicolon denotes
concatenation along the token dimension.
 $N_{m}$ is the number of Mode Tokens, $K_{\mathrm{prior}}$ is the number
of codebook entries, and $K=N_{s}$ is the number of history slots.
We distinguish the state encoder’s internal dimension $d_{s}=1024$ 
from the backbone conditioning dimension $d$ .
Batch dimensions are omitted unless explicitly shown.

## A.1.1 Mode Tokens.

Reliable stage progression requires interpreting execution evidence
under the fine-grained requirements specified by the instruction.
When tasks share local interactions, even an accurate history may
support different continuations.
For example, after a microwave has started, one instruction may require
another preparation step during heating, whereas another requires
waiting until heating finishes.
The observed history can be identical, but the appropriate next stage
differs because of the timing requirement.
Execution evidence therefore needs to be considered together with the
instruction constraints that determine how the task should proceed.

We construct a persistent, instruction-grounded representation of
these constraints, which we refer to as Mode Tokens.
Here, an execution mode describes how manipulation should proceed
under fine-grained requirements, including ordering, timing,
repetition, and arm assignment.
Mode Tokens represent these requirements, while State Tokens provide
evidence of the progress actually made.
Their complementary roles allow generation to use both the intended
execution requirements and the observed execution context.

The construction comprises two steps.
Instruction-dependent routing retrieves query slots from a learnable
codebook, providing a coarse execution-requirement prior.
Full-text grounding then refines these queries using the specific
requirements expressed in the current instruction.

Instruction-Dependent Routing.
Given a task instruction $\ell$ , the text encoder produces
 $\mathbf{C}=\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{text}}(\ell)\in\mathbb{R}^{L\times D}$ ,
where $L$ is the language-sequence length and $D$ is the text-feature
dimension.
We obtain a global instruction feature through masked average pooling:

 $\mathbf{c}_{\mathrm{pool}}=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{L}\mathrm{mask}_{i}\mathbf{c}_{i}}{\sum_{i=1}^{L}\mathrm{mask}_{i}},$ 

(8)

where $\mathrm{mask}_{i}$ indicates a valid language token.
A two-layer MLP predicts routing logits and normalized weights:

 $\displaystyle\bm{\ell}_{\mathrm{prior}}$ 
 $\displaystyle=W_{2}\,\mathrm{SiLU}\left(W_{1}\mathbf{c}_{\mathrm{pool}}+b_{1}\right)+b_{2},$ 

(9)

 $\displaystyle\bm{\alpha}$ 
 $\displaystyle=\mathrm{softmax}\left(\bm{\ell}_{\mathrm{prior}}\right)\in\mathbb{R}^{K_{\mathrm{prior}}},$ 

where $K_{\mathrm{prior}}$ is the number of codebook entries.
The weights determine an instruction-dependent combination of the
query slots used for subsequent requirement extraction.

Learnable Requirement Codebook.
We maintain a codebook

 $\mathbf{E}\in\mathbb{R}^{K_{\mathrm{prior}}\times N_{m}\times D},$ 

(10)

with $N_{m}$ learnable query slots per entry.
The routing weights combine corresponding slots across entries:

 $\mathbf{m}_{r}^{\mathrm{raw}}=\sum_{k=1}^{K_{\mathrm{prior}}}\alpha_{k}\mathbf{E}_{k,r},\qquad r=1,\ldots,N_{m}.$ 

(11)

The codebook parameters are shared across instructions, while their
combination adapts to the current language context.
The retrieved slots serve as a coarse execution-requirement prior
by initializing the queries that read the instruction.

This construction does not require a predefined one-to-one
correspondence between codebook entries and tasks.
Individual entries are not manually assigned to specific execution
requirements; their representations are learned jointly through the
world and action prediction objectives.
The retrieved mixture is subsequently refined against the full
instruction, so the final Mode Tokens depend on more than the routing
weights alone.

Fine-Grained Instruction Grounding.
Routing supplies an instruction-dependent query initialization,
but its pooled input does not preserve the full token-level structure
of the language sequence.
We therefore use the retrieved slots as queries over the complete
text context through a multi-layer Q-Former:

 $\displaystyle\mathbf{M}^{\mathrm{raw}}$ 
 $\displaystyle=[\mathbf{m}_{1}^{\mathrm{raw}},\ldots,\mathbf{m}_{N_{m}}^{\mathrm{raw}}],$ 

(12)

 $\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathbf{M}}$ 
 $\displaystyle=\mathcal{Q}_{\mathrm{ground}}\left(\mathbf{M}^{\mathrm{raw}},\mathbf{C}\right).$ 

The language tokens provide the key-value context, giving the queries
access to the words and contextual relationships that specify
ordering, timing, repetition, and arm assignment.
Grounding thus adapts the retrieved prior to the detailed requirements
of the current instruction.

We project the grounded queries to the backbone conditioning dimension:

 $\mathbf{M}=\mathcal{P}_{m}\left(\widetilde{\mathbf{M}}\right)\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{m}\times d},$ 

(13)

where $\mathcal{P}_{m}$ denotes the output projection.
In the implementation described here, each codebook entry contains
 $N_{m}=8$ query slots, and the module outputs eight Mode Tokens with
dimension $d=4096$ .
The number of query slots $N_{m}$ is distinct from the number of
codebook entries $K_{\mathrm{prior}}$ .

Joint Learning for World-Action Generation.
We jointly train the routing head, codebook, grounding module, and
WAM using the backbone’s world and action prediction objectives,
without a separate codebook pretraining stage.
As described in Sec. 3.4, Mode Tokens participate in
task-state attention, layer-wise modulation, and residual prediction.
These pathways allow the instruction-grounded representations to
influence both world and action generation.

Prediction losses backpropagate through the conditioning pathways
to the Mode-Token construction modules.
The learned queries are therefore optimized according to their
contribution to downstream world-action prediction, connecting
instruction grounding with the generative execution objective.

Persistent Conditioning During Execution.
Mode Tokens are retained while the instruction remains unchanged,
keeping the task-specific execution requirements accessible across
stage transitions.
State Tokens are updated from actual action–observation interactions,
providing evolving evidence of execution progress.
Both generation streams access these complementary representations
to condition subsequent world-action predictions.

At inference time, the routing weights are predicted from the task
instruction.
Constructing Mode Tokens does not require a user-provided task
identifier or codebook index.

## A.1.2 State Tokens.

Mode Tokens specify how the task should proceed, but do not indicate
which requirements have already been fulfilled.
When similar observations recur across stages, execution progress must
be inferred from the preceding interactions.
An executed action records what the robot attempted, while the subsequent
observation and its change provide evidence of the resulting outcome.
We therefore construct State Tokens from temporally ordered
action–observation transitions, providing execution evidence for
subsequent world–action generation.

Interaction Representation.
We use a fixed history window of $K=8$ interaction slots.
Each slot $k$ pairs the preceding executed action chunk
 $\mathbf{A}_{k-1}^{\mathrm{exec}}$ with the resulting visual latent $\mathbf{F}_{k}$ 
and its change
 $\Delta\mathbf{F}_{k}=\mathbf{F}_{k}-\mathbf{F}_{k-1}$ .
These inputs provide complementary evidence:
 $\mathbf{A}_{k-1}^{\mathrm{exec}}$ describes the executed behavior,
 $\mathbf{F}_{k}$ describes the resulting scene, and
 $\Delta\mathbf{F}_{k}$ captures the observed change between interactions.
Their joint representation allows the encoder to use both the
executed actions and their visual feedback when tracking progress.

We independently project the three modalities into a 256-dimensional
feature space.
The same learnable slot-position embedding is added to all modality
features belonging to slot $k$ , associating them with the same
interaction.

Within-Slot Aggregation.
A 2-layer, 8-head Q-Former aggregates the multimodal features within
each interaction slot.
Its query is formed as

 $\mathbf{q}_{k}=\mathbf{q}_{\mathrm{base}}+\mathbf{p}^{q}_{k},$ 

(14)

where $\mathbf{q}_{\mathrm{base}}$ is a shared learnable query and
 $\mathbf{p}^{q}_{k}$ is a learnable embedding for the slot position.
The Q-Former attends to the projected visual, visual-change, and
action features.
Its output is mapped to a 1024-dimensional interaction representation
 $\mathbf{e}_{k}$ , summarizing the executed behavior and the observed
scene information associated with that interaction.

Causal Temporal Encoding.
The eight interaction representations are arranged in execution order
and processed by a 2-layer, 8-head causal Transformer.
A lower-triangular attention mask restricts each slot to its own and
preceding interactions.
This preserves the chronology needed to distinguish similar scene
states reached through different execution histories.

The encoder outputs

 $\mathbf{S}^{\mathrm{enc}}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{B\times 8\times 1024},$ 

(15)

where $B$ denotes the batch size.
We retain all eight temporally ordered tokens, allowing subsequent
generation to access representations associated with different
positions in the recent interaction history.
Each later token can incorporate a longer prefix of the available
window.
A backbone-specific projection maps these representations to the
conditioning dimension $d$ :

 $\mathbf{S}_{t}=\mathcal{P}_{s}\left(\mathbf{S}^{\mathrm{enc}}_{t}\right)\in\mathbb{R}^{B\times 8\times d}.$ 

(16)

History Initialization.
At the beginning of an episode, fewer than eight interaction slots
may be available.
We pad missing slots with the earliest available observation and set
their action inputs to zero.
For the first valid interaction slot, the latent-difference input is
also set to zero because no preceding observation is available.
This keeps the input length fixed without carrying history across
episode boundaries.

Closed-Loop State Updates.
After executing action chunk $\mathbf{A}_{t}^{\mathrm{exec}}$ , the robot receives the
next observation and encodes its visual latent $\mathbf{F}_{t+1}$ .
The new interaction
 $(\mathbf{F}_{t+1},\Delta\mathbf{F}_{t+1},\mathbf{A}_{t}^{\mathrm{exec}})$ 
is appended to the history window, the oldest slot is removed when
necessary, and the State Tokens are recomputed.
Subsequent generation therefore uses execution evidence updated from
the robot’s actual interactions, alongside the persistent requirements
represented by Mode Tokens.

## A.1.3 Task-State Conditioning and Generation Steering.

The grounded task state
 $\mathbf{T}_{t}=[\mathbf{M};\mathbf{S}_{t}]$ 
provides both task requirements and execution evidence for world-action
generation.
TaskWAM retains the backbone’s native world–action information flow and injects
 $\mathbf{T}_{t}$ into both world and action streams through two
complementary mechanisms.

First, we append the task state to the key-value context of both streams,
allowing future world and action tokens to directly access the current
execution requirements and progress.
In addition, pooled Mode Tokens provide layer-wise AdaLN modulation:

 $[\bm{\gamma}_{l}^{m},\bm{\beta}_{l}^{m}]=g_{l}(\bar{\mathbf{m}}),\qquad\widetilde{\mathbf{H}}_{l}=(1+\bm{\gamma}_{l}^{m})\odot\operatorname{LN}(\mathbf{H}_{l})+\bm{\beta}_{l}^{m}.$ 

(17)

Here, $\bar{\mathbf{m}}$ is the pooled Mode-Token representation and
 $g_{l}$ predicts the modulation parameters for layer $l$ .

Second, we introduce task-state-conditioned residual correction to
directly adjust the predicted world-action evolution.
For flow-based backbones and stream $b\in\{w,a\}$ , the residual is generated by querying
the task state with the corresponding hidden states:

 $\Delta\mathbf{v}_{t,\tau}^{b}=\mathcal{R}_{b}(\mathbf{H}_{t,\tau}^{b};\mathbf{T}_{t}),\qquad\widehat{\mathbf{v}}_{t,\tau}^{b}=\mathbf{v}_{t,\tau}^{b}+\gamma_{b}\Delta\mathbf{v}_{t,\tau}^{b},$ 

(18)

where $\gamma_{b}$ is a learnable bounded scale and
 $\mathcal{R}_{b}$ denotes a stream-specific cross-attention residual head.
The residual and base prediction have matching shapes.
For Cosmos Policy, the residual operates on denoised latent predictions
at the native EDM noise level, rather than on a flow velocity.
The two prediction parameterizations use the corresponding backbone
training objective, as described in Sec. A.2.

Environment Extensions.
To support realistic multi-stage manipulation and stage-aware evaluation,

For kitchen scenarios, we introduce functional microwave assets with

Microwave-based Cooking Tasks.
These task families share the same interaction structure: placing the mug

Data collection.
For the LIBERO tasks, we collect demonstrations through 3D human
teleoperation. For the four RoboTwin task families, we use the official
data generation pipeline. We collect 50 demonstration trajectories
for each collected instruction-defined condition. The resulting dataset contains 2.20 million frames
across both platforms, with approximately 12 hours spent on human
teleoperation.

Event-based stage annotation.
To support stage-level evaluation, we record task-relevant simulator
states at every environment step and convert them into timestamped
task events. The events are defined separately for each task family
according to its execution requirements. For example, the LIBERO
heating tasks record heating start, heating completion, post-heating
microwave reopening, target-object manipulation, and whether each
target object has entered the basket. A stage may depend on multiple
events rather than a single simulator condition.

For RoboTwin, the recorded events depend on the task family, such as
object placement order for B1 and B2, hammer manipulation by the
instructed arm for B3, and completed cutting motions for B4.
These event traces provide a common interface for tracking execution
progress while retaining task-specific stage definitions.

Automatic evaluation.
We separately evaluate physical task completion and instruction-level
execution consistency.
Physical success is determined using the native success criteria of the
corresponding simulation platform.
Meanwhile, deterministic finite-state machines analyze recorded event
sequences to reconstruct execution routes and track stage completion.
Stage-consistent task completion additionally requires satisfying the
instruction-defined ordering, timing, arm, and repetition constraints.

For the LIBERO heating tasks, let
 $u_{\mathrm{collect}}$ denote the environment step at which the collection
branch is executed and $u_{\mathrm{open}}$ the environment step at which the
microwave is reopened after heating.
An execution is classified as Interleaved when
 $u_{\mathrm{collect}}<u_{\mathrm{open}}$ , and as
Sequential when
 $u_{\mathrm{open}}<u_{\mathrm{collect}}$ .
Heating-start and heating-completion events are additionally retained
to verify the corresponding physical stages and route consistency.

For RoboTwin, the evaluator applies task-specific rules to verify
instruction-defined constraints, including object ordering, arm roles,
and repetition counts.

Stage Progression Rate.
To complement final task success, we report Stage Progression Rate
(SPR), which measures whether a policy completes the instructed
next stage after completing the current stage.
For task family $f$ and variant $\nu$ , let $\mathcal{E}_{f,\nu}$ denote
the set of eligible transition opportunities across evaluated
rollouts. An opportunity becomes eligible once the preceding
required stages have been completed in order.
Let $\mathcal{C}_{f,\nu}\subseteq\mathcal{E}_{f,\nu}$ contain opportunities
for which the first recognizable subsequent stage entry matches
the instructed next stage and that stage is completed before
rollout termination. We define

 $\mathrm{SPR}_{f,\nu}=\frac{|\mathcal{C}_{f,\nu}|}{|\mathcal{E}_{f,\nu}|}\times 100\%.$ 

(19)

The denominator retains all eligible opportunities, including
incorrect stage entries, correct entries without completion,
and cases with no recognizable subsequent entry.
Task-level SPR pools these counts across variants before division.
Higher SPR indicates more reliable progression along the
instructed execution route.
Because unsuccessful opportunities also include ordinary manipulation
failures, SPR measures stage-consistent progression; event traces are
used to identify specific wrong-stage entries.

Evaluation protocol.
For each LIBERO task family, the two task variants are evaluated using
the same fixed set of 100 unique initial states.
Each task–variant pair is therefore evaluated on 100 initial states,
and all compared models use the same initial states and the same
task-specific maximum number of environment steps.
For every rollout, we save the detected events and their time steps,
physical task success, identified execution route, and stage-level
progress.

## B.2 RoboTwin 2.0 Setup

Benchmark.
We evaluate on all 50 bimanual manipulation tasks in RoboTwin 2.0 (Chen et al., 2026b) under the Clean and Randomized settings.
The latter introduces variations in scene appearance and configuration to assess robustness to environmental changes.
The Clean training set contains 2,500 demonstrations (50 per task), and the Randomized training set contains 25,000 demonstrations
(500 per task). Simulation experiments are conducted over 100 trials.

Configuration.
For this benchmark, TaskWAM augments Fast-WAM (Yuan et al., 2026)
with Structured Task-State Grounding and Task-State Conditioned Future Steering.
Mode Tokens provide instruction-grounded execution requirements, while
State Tokens summarize causal action–observation transitions.
Both token sets condition the world and action streams through attention
and residual prediction; pooled Mode Tokens also provide layer-wise
modulation. These additions follow the task-state pathways defined in
Sec. 3.4 while retaining Fast-WAM’s native world–action
information flow.

## B.3 Real-World Setup

Platforms and data collection.
The real-world platforms include a single-arm Franka Research 3,
dual-arm AgileX Piper (6-DoF) and AgileX Nero (7-DoF), and a Unitree G1
humanoid equipped with BrainCo Revo2 dexterous hands.
Franka is equipped with third-person and wrist-mounted RealSense D435
cameras, while the dual-arm platforms use wrist cameras (and a
head-mounted camera for Nero) for visual observations.
The humanoid platform is operated via PICO VR for whole-body
teleoperation.
Teleoperation interfaces include Gello Wu et al. (2024) for gripper-based robots,
TypeTele Lin et al. (2025) for dexterous hands, and SONIC Luo et al. (2026) for the humanoid platform.
We collect 100–150 teleoperated demonstrations per task.

Multi-Stage Real-World Tasks.
We evaluate four multi-stage tasks spanning three representative real-world scenarios.
These tasks evaluate cross-task and within-task stage confusion under shared objects, repeated interactions, and instruction-dependent continuations.
① Sequential Cup Nesting requires nesting red, pink, and blue cups in the instructed order, with three order variants.
The breakfast-preparation scenario includes two tasks: ② Bread Toasting & Spreading, which requires toasting bread, transferring it to a plate, and applying the specified blueberry or yellow-peach jam; and ③ Bread Cutting, which requires making exactly three cuts and then stopping.
④ Laboratory Fluid Manipulation requires transferring a reagent into the receiving container specified by the instruction.
These settings naturally reuse objects and local interactions across tasks and execution stages, providing real-world evaluation of both cross-task and within-task stage confusion.
Additionally, we evaluate Bread Toasting & Spreading under a modified
tabletop background to examine robustness against scene variations.

(1) Sequential Cup Nesting.
The task-variant variable is the prescribed color sequence.
All variants share the same scene and manipulation procedure. The prompt
template is:
“Assemble the colored cups by nesting them one by one on the tabletop

## Appendix E Discussion

Implicit Stage Modeling in Vanilla WAMs.
For a flow-based WAM, the world and action velocity fields are
conditioned on the instruction $\ell$ , current observation $o_{t}$ , and
optional history $\mathcal{H}_{t}$ , and trained to match the target
velocities of demonstrated futures:

 $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{wam}}=\mathbb{E}\Bigl[\sum_{b\in\{w,a\}}\bigl\|\mathbf{v}_{\theta}^{b}(\mathbf{x}_{t,\tau}^{b},\tau\mid\ell,o_{t},\mathcal{H}_{t})-\mathbf{u}_{t,\tau}^{b}\bigr\|_{2}^{2}\Bigr],$ 

(20)

where $b\in\{w,a\}$ denotes the world or action stream, $\tau$ is the
flow timestep, $\mathbf{x}_{t,\tau}^{b}$ is the corresponding flow input,
and $\mathbf{u}_{t,\tau}^{b}$ is the target velocity.

Demonstration losses already supervise the intended world and action
outputs. Local predictive plausibility alone, however, does not establish
compliance with the active task’s stage requirements.
Under overlapping trajectories, observations and short histories can be
similar while the instruction or achieved progress calls for a different
continuation.
TaskWAM organizes these conditioning signals into instruction-grounded
Mode Tokens and interaction-derived State Tokens, then makes them
accessible through backbone conditioning and residual prediction.
This changes how task information enters generation while retaining
the backbone’s generative training objective.

Temporal Scope of Task-State Grounding.
Mode Tokens retain instruction-grounded requirements across the rollout,
while State Tokens summarize the most recent $K$ interactions.
The resulting progress representation depends on evidence available
within this finite window.
The task state guides continuous world–action generation through learned
conditioning and residual prediction; the benchmark evaluator separately
uses symbolic event traces to assess stage compliance.

Abstract

Financial institutions increasingly deploy large language models (LLMs) in
multilingual markets, raising a practical question: can pipelines developed
and validated in English be reused across languages, or must they be
rebuilt per locale? We test cross-lingual reusability along three
dimensions—accuracy, reasoning-effort transfer, and
behavioural stability—through three complementary tasks.
Task 1 (cheque extraction) and Task 2 (Mintaka QA with
pinned evidence across nine languages and nine model–effort
configurations) test whether accuracy and effort settings transfer;
Task 3 (FinanceBench pilot, 50 items over SEC 10-K filings in
four languages) validates whether these patterns generalise to financial
content.
Accuracy reusability is broadly confirmed: Gemini 3.5 Flash achieves
95.2–97.6% extraction accuracy across prompt languages, and
FinanceBench per-language means span 5.8pp (77.1–82.9%), though the
language-difficulty ranking reverses between domains (Hindi is worst on
Mintaka, best on FinanceBench at 82.9%).
Effort reusability is domain-dependent: higher reasoning effort
yields marginal gains on general-knowledge QA but up to 8pp on
financial content, and effort configurations that help in English help
proportionally in other languages.
Behavioural reusability holds: citation-set overlap with English
averages 77–79% Jaccard, with no single language consistently worse.
Together, these results support qualified reuse of English-built
pipelines, but per-domain, per-language validation remains
necessary—neither semantic similarity nor self-reported confidence
reliably predicts cross-lingual accuracy.

## 1 Introduction

Financial institutions are rapidly adopting large language models (LLMs) for tasks ranging from document processing to knowledge retrieval. As these systems are deployed globally, a key question is whether solutions developed and validated in English—such as prompts, evaluation pipelines, and evidence corpora—transfer reliably to other languages. If translation alone preserves performance, institutions can reuse AI pipelines across markets rather than redeveloping them language by language.

We investigate this question through three tasks. Task 1 examines document information extraction in a financial setting: given a handwritten cheque image, an LLM extracts the numeric courtesy amount and the legal amount written in words under prompts in different languages. Task 2 examines cross-lingual question answering with deep research, where an LLM reasons over retrieved evidence to produce an answer. We evaluate this setting using the nine-language Mintaka benchmark (Sen et al., 2022) with a fixed evidence corpus, allowing language and reasoning effects to be studied independently of retrieval variance.
Task 3 then validates whether these patterns hold on
domain-specific financial content through a pilot study on the
FinanceBench benchmark Islam et al. (2023), evaluating 50
questions over SEC 10-K filings in four languages and directly testing
the generalisability of the RQ1–RQ2 findings beyond general-knowledge
content.

These three tasks are deliberately chosen to span distinct dimensions
of the LLM pipeline architectures that financial institutions deploy.
Task 1 is perception-dominated: the
answer is visible on the cheque image, and the challenge is accurate
extraction under varied instruction languages—a low-reasoning,
high-accuracy setting typical of back-office operations (cheque clearing,
payment processing) where errors carry direct financial cost. Task 2 is
reasoning-dominated: the model must plan, synthesise, and judge
across multi-source evidence—a high-reasoning setting representative
of front-office knowledge work (research, compliance, due diligence).
Task 3 is domain-validation-focused: it applies the same
cross-lingual QA design to financial filings, testing whether patterns
observed on general-knowledge questions extend to domain-specific
financial content. The three tasks further differ in modality
(vision+text vs. text-only), in how language enters (instruction-only
vs. full cross-lingual context including translated evidence), in domain,
and in evaluation objectivity (exact numeric match vs. rule+judge
grading). By holding model families constant across all three tasks, we
can test whether findings—such as
“model choice dominates language”—generalise across differences in
difficulty, domain, and modality rather than being artefacts of a single
task type.

Concretely, we test cross-lingual pipeline reusability along three
dimensions, each operationalised as a research question:

RQ1 (Accuracy reusability)

Can an
English-validated LLM pipeline be reused in other languages without
significant accuracy loss?
We test this on cheque extraction (Task 1), general-knowledge QA
(Task 2), and financial QA (Task 3) across up to nine languages.

RQ2 (Effort reusability)

Do reasoning-effort
configurations tuned in English transfer to non-English settings?
We test this using 400 items $\times$ 9 languages $\times$ 9
model–effort configurations with a pinned evidence corpus that
eliminates retrieval variance (Task 2), plus a financial QA pilot
(Task 3).

RQ3 (Behavioural reusability)

Does citation and
source-selection behaviour remain stable when the prompt language
changes?
We measure citation-set overlap and proxy precision/recall across
languages under fixed evidence (Task 2).

## 2 Related Work

Several benchmarks evaluate LLM capabilities on financial tasks.
FLUE (Shah et al., 2022) provides five English financial NLP
tasks—sentiment analysis, named entity recognition, structure boundary
detection, question answering, and stock movement prediction—and
introduces FLANG, a pre-trained language model for finance.
BloombergGPT (Wu et al., 2023) trains a 50B-parameter model on a
mixed finance–general corpus and evaluates it on standard financial NLP
benchmarks, demonstrating that domain-specific pre-training improves
financial task performance without degrading general capabilities.
FinBen (Xie et al., 2024) offers a holistic evaluation across 35
datasets spanning seven task categories including information extraction,
question answering, and risk management.
FinanceBench (Islam et al., 2023) focuses on open-book financial
QA over public filings, testing retrieval-augmented generation in a
setting closer to real analyst workflows. Despite their breadth, these
benchmarks are English-only or English-dominant. Recent work has begun
to address this gap: MultiFinBen (Peng et al., 2026) introduces a
multilingual financial benchmark across five languages, but evaluates
models on static task sets rather than testing whether an English-built
pipeline can be reused cross-lingually.

In document understanding, OCR-free vision–language models such as
Donut (Kim et al., 2022) have enabled end-to-end extraction and have
been applied to cheque processing (Zhao et al., 2024). Multimodal
LLMs also perform strongly on printed
documents (Ma et al., 2024; Pfitzmann et al., 2022), but
cross-lingual prompting effects on handwritten financial document
extraction remain largely unexplored (Mondshine et al., 2025).

Cross-lingual performance gaps in LLMs are well documented but poorly
explained. Sapenov and Savelka (2026) evaluate two proprietary LLMs
across 43 languages and show that token usage varies systematically by
language—some languages are simultaneously more expensive and less
accurate—suggesting that generation behaviour is not language-neutral
even when accuracy is controlled.
Chen et al. (2025b) find that non-English reasoning traces
produce higher output diversity, with diversity increasing as the thinking
language diverges from English in embedding space.
Wang et al. (2025) propose on-policy thinking-language selection and
show that multilingual chain-of-thought expands the exploration space,
achieving higher pass@ $k$ under a fixed training budget. Most relevant
to our work, Lu et al. (2026) evaluate cross-lingual deep
research agents and find substantial degradation when evidence is
translated: accuracy remains lower even when all gold evidence is supplied
directly. Deep research agents combine retrieval, planning, and
multi-step reasoning into end-to-end workflows, and
Wang et al. (2026) propose REFLECT for meta-evaluating such agents,
finding that even the best judges remain below 55% accuracy on evidence
verification. Crucially, because Lu et al.’s
benchmark does not control retrieval—each run searches anew—it cannot
fully separate retrieval degradation from reasoning degradation.

Translation quality is itself a potential confound in multilingual
evaluation. Thellmann et al. (2026) find that target-side
translation errors are associated with 6–8 percentage-point reductions in
accuracy, increasing to 6–11 points among items answered correctly in
English, while automated error-span detection achieves only moderate
agreement with expert annotations. These findings motivate interpreting
differences under translated inputs as end-to-end outcomes of a
translation-mediated pipeline rather than pure measures of intrinsic
language capability.

The effect of reasoning depth on LLM accuracy has been studied primarily
in English. Qin et al. (2025) provide a comprehensive
multilingual evaluation and find that (i) thinking language affects
accuracy, (ii) reasoning traces in different languages are not simple
translations, and (iii) prompt language affects the final answer even when
the question is fixed. Deng et al. (2026) show that
low-resource languages degrade disproportionately as chain-of-thought
length increases, suggesting a resource-dependent ceiling on the benefit
of deeper reasoning. Gao et al. (2026) frame language as a
latent variable in reasoning and argue that English as a default thinking
language is a root cause of cross-lingual bias. These studies all
manipulate prompt-induced chain-of-thought; none examines the
provider-exposed reasoning-effort controls—model-id suffixes,
thinking budgets—that production systems actually offer.

Finally, LLM-based evaluation itself introduces methodological risk.
Haldar and Hockenmaier (2025) find intra-rater
Krippendorff’s $\alpha$ Krippendorff (2011) below the 0.80
threshold on multiple benchmarks.
Chen et al. (2025a) show that pairwise evaluation amplifies
superficial preferences; Ye et al. (2024) mitigate this with PRePair,
a pointwise-then-compare protocol. In the multilingual setting,
Hada et al. (2025) report cross-language
 $\kappa\approx 0.3$ , and Lee et al. (2026) show that answer
language has more impact on judge bias than question language.

Our work addresses these gaps jointly. We systematically vary prompt
language across a financial extraction task and a general-knowledge
reasoning task, covering three model families, and introduce
a pinned-evidence methodology that isolates reasoning quality from
retrieval quality in cross-lingual evaluation. We study provider-exposed
reasoning-effort controls as deployed in production systems, where the
mechanism is opaque and family-specific. To mitigate judge unreliability,
we use deterministic rule-based grading as the primary path—reserving the
LLM judge for cases the rules cannot settle. Comparing
provider-controlled retrieval in our native deep-research track against
the shared-evidence track reveals how much accuracy depends on retrieval
versus reasoning.

## 3 Data

## 3.1 Handwritten Cheque VQA Dataset (Task 1)

For Task 1 we use the Handwritten Cheque VQA
dataset Verma (2024), containing 3,312 question–answer pairs
over cheque images (Figure 1). Each item provides
the amount in words (legal amount) and in figures (courtesy
amount). We apply three successive filters: retain only items where both
amounts are annotated (914), then only items where the two amounts differ
so the model cannot copy one to the other (252), and finally only items
where both fields parse to numeric values for exact-match evaluation (124).

Figure 1: Example cheque image from the Handwritten Cheque VQA
dataset Verma (2024). The model must extract the
courtesy amount (numeric, from the amount box) and the legal amount
(written in words, converted to a number). All cheque images are in
English; only the prompt language varies across experimental
conditions.

Each of the 124 items is evaluated across three model families and five
prompt languages (1,860 API calls total). All cheque images are in
English; the multilingual dimension enters only through the prompt
language.

## 3.2 Mintaka Benchmark (Task 2)

Mintaka Sen et al. (2022) is a multilingual question-answering dataset
built on Wikidata. Each of its 14,000 items contains a question in English
plus translations into eight languages (Arabic, German, Spanish, French,
Hindi, Italian, Japanese, Portuguese), typed answers (entity, numerical,
boolean, date, or string), eight topic categories (e.g. geography, movies,
sports), and nine complexity types including multihop, superlative, and
comparative questions. This combination of nine-language coverage and
verifiable answers makes it well-suited for controlled cross-lingual
evaluation.

Although Mintaka’s content spans general knowledge rather than finance, its
value here is as a controlled testbed for the pipeline architecture
that financial institutions deploy: retrieval, multi-step reasoning, and
report synthesis over a multilingual question set with verifiable answers.
Existing multilingual financial QA benchmarks either lack the nine-language
breadth needed for our design (FinanceBench is English-only; MultiFinBen
covers five languages) or do not provide structured answers suitable for
automated evaluation at scale. We draw a stratified random sample of 400
items (category $\times$ complexity type) that underpins both evaluation
tracks.

## 3.3 FinanceBench Pilot (Task 3: Domain Validation)

To test whether the cross-lingual patterns observed on Mintaka’s
general-knowledge content hold on financial material, we conduct a
domain-validation pilot using FinanceBench Islam et al. (2023), an
open-source QA benchmark of 150 questions over SEC 10-K filings from 32
publicly traded companies. We draw a stratified sample of 50 items
( ${\sim}17$ each of metrics-generated, domain-relevant, and
novel-generated questions) and evaluate them in four languages
(English, Hindi, Spanish, German) across the same three model families
and all nine model–effort configurations. Evidence passages from the
source 10-K filings are translated into each target language using the
same GPT-4.1 translator (OpenAI, 2025a) (with Claude
Sonnet 4.5 (Anthropic, 2025) as a fallback) employed for
the Mintaka evidence corpus, and questions and gold answers are
translated identically. The pilot yields $50\times 4\times 9=1{,}800$ 
API calls—a deliberately moderate footprint designed to confirm or
refute the Mintaka patterns on domain-specific financial content,
including the effect of reasoning effort, rather than to serve as a
comprehensive financial benchmark.

## 4 Method

All three tasks are evaluated on the same three model families—
GPT-5.1 (OpenAI, 2025b),
Claude Opus 4.6 (Anthropic, 2026), and
Gemini 3.5 Flash (Google DeepMind, 2026)—enabling cross-task comparison of
multilingual effects. Task 1 tests a single reasoning-effort level per
family across five prompt languages; Task 2 varies reasoning effort
across three levels per family (nine model configurations in total)
and nine languages; Task 3 uses the same nine model–effort
configurations across four languages
(Section 3.3).

## 4.1 Task 1: Cheque Amount Extraction

Each cheque image is sent to the model with a structured prompt requesting
JSON output containing two fields: Courtesy Amount (extracted from
the amount box as a float) and Legal Amount (converted from words
to a numeric float), along with confidence scores for each. The system
prompt identifies the model as a cheque information extraction system. The
model is instructed to extract each amount independently and not copy one to
the other.

To test whether prompt language affects extraction accuracy, we translate the
extraction prompt into four additional languages—Korean, Japanese, Hindi,
and Spanish—using GPT-4.1 at temperature 0, while keeping the cheque
images unchanged (all cheques are in English). GPT-4.1 was chosen because
it is not among the evaluated models (GPT-5.1, Claude Opus 4.6,
Gemini 3.5 Flash),
avoiding circularity between the translator and the systems under test.
This isolates the effect of prompt language from document language.
Each of the 124 evaluation cheques is processed with all five prompt
languages under the same model.

We evaluate three model families through the same orchestration path described in
Section 4.2 and report three accuracy measures: courtesy accuracy (predicted
courtesy amount equals ground truth), legal accuracy (predicted
legal amount equals ground truth), and joint accuracy (both correct
simultaneously). All comparisons use exact numeric match after
normalization.

## 4.2 Task 2: Cross-Lingual QA Design

Our experiment is a fully crossed design over three axes: item (400
Mintaka questions Sen et al. (2022), stratified by category $\times$ 
complexity type), language (9: en, ar, de, es, fr, hi, it, ja, pt),
and model configuration (9, spanning three families—GPT-5.1,
Claude Opus 4.6, and Gemini 3.5 Flash—each at three reasoning-effort
levels).
To control for retrieval variance—repeat runs of the same query returned
different evidence in pilot tests—we construct a pinned evidence
corpus: one English Google Custom Search per item, captured once and
committed to version control. An offline extraction step then produces
attributable source passages (1,066 passages across 400 items, mean 2.7
per item), which every subsequent evaluation run reads verbatim.

Task 2 uses two evaluation tracks. For reasoning evaluation, under shared evidence, each
question–language pair receives a language-matched translation of the pinned
passages. Because the underlying evidence and its translations are pinned
across model configurations, cross-lingual differences cannot arise from
retrieval variation; they may nevertheless reflect both model reasoning and
translation characteristics. Questions use Mintaka’s human translations; evidence is translated
by GPT-4.1 (temperature 0) with a URL-preserving validation step and
Claude Sonnet 4.5 as a fallback—neither translator is among the evaluated
models. For retrieval evaluation, we also employ native deep research with OpenAI and Gemini
to execute their own end-to-end retrieval-to-report pipelines, so comparing the two tracks isolates retrieval quality as a factor.

Grading is two-stage: script-aware rule-based matching first (boolean
lexicon, numeric comparison, normalised entity containment), then a GPT-5.2 (OpenAI, 2025c)
LLM judge for the remaining items.

## 4.3 Semantic-Similarity Diagnostic

As an additional semantic diagnostic, we embed each model answer and
its ground truth using OpenAI’s text-embedding-ada-002
model (OpenAI, 2022) and MPNet (Song et al., 2020) to
consider encoder-specific artefacts. For each prediction we record the
cosine similarity

 $S_{f,i,\ell}\;=\;\max_{g\in G_{i}}\frac{\mathbf{e}_{\text{pred}}\cdot\mathbf{e}_{g}}{\|\mathbf{e}_{\text{pred}}\|\;\|\mathbf{e}_{g}\|},$ 

(1)

where $G_{i}$ is the set of accepted aliases for item $i$ . This score is
a continuous semantic proximity measure, not a binary correctness label;
the analysis is restricted to entity answers.

We pair each target-language response with the English response for the
same item and model configuration. The item-level
language–configuration interaction is

 $\displaystyle I_{f,i}={}$ 
 $\displaystyle\frac{1}{8}\sum_{\ell\neq\mathrm{en}}\Delta S_{f,i,\ell}$ 

(2)

 $\displaystyle}{\displaystyle-\Delta S_{f,i,\mathrm{en}},$ 

where $\Delta S$ is the low-to-high (or standard-to-adaptive) change in
cosine similarity. A positive $I_{f,i}$ means the configuration change
narrows the cross-lingual gap. We average over items and compute 95%
percentile intervals from 5,000 bootstrap samples stratified by
category $\times$ complexity type.

## 4.4 Citation and Source-Selection Metrics

To assess whether models change how they use evidence across languages
(RQ3), we extract the numbered references each model cites and compare
citation behaviour between each target-language variant and its
English counterpart for the same item and model configuration.
Six metrics are computed:

• 

Cited rate: fraction of answers containing at least one
citation.

• 

Citation coverage: share of available references cited,
adjusting for the variable number of references per item.

• 

Blanket citation rate: fraction of answers citing every
available reference (i.e. no selective sourcing).

• 

Proxy precision / recall: overlap between cited
references and proxy alias-coattributed references—those
co-attributed with extracted passages containing a known gold-answer
alias. Boolean items are excluded because their gold strings
match ubiquitously.

• 

Citation-set Jaccard similarity: $|R_{\text{en}}\cap R_{\ell}|\,/\,|R_{\text{en}}\cup R_{\ell}|$ between the valid
reference sets selected in English and the target language for the
same item.

Binary metrics are tested with exact paired McNemar tests; numerical
metrics use paired bootstrap confidence intervals and exact sign tests.
All $p$ -values are adjusted within each metric using the
Benjamini–Hochberg procedure. Because each non-English condition pairs
a translated question with translated evidence, observed differences are
localised-condition effects relative to English, not pure
question-language effects.

## 5 Results

## 5.1 Task 1: Cheque Amount Extraction

Table 1 reports extraction accuracy on the
124-item evaluation set. English-prompt joint accuracy ranges from
49.2% (GPT-5.1) to 83.9% (Claude Opus 4.6) to 96.0% (Gemini 3.5 Flash).
When the prompt is translated into four additional languages while
keeping the cheque images in English, the multilingual effect is
model-dependent. Non-English prompts boost GPT-5.1: Spanish
reaches 54.8% joint accuracy (+5.6pp over English), and all four
languages match or exceed the English baseline. Conversely, non-English
prompts degrade Claude Opus 4.6: Korean drops to 76.6% ( $-$ 7.3pp).
Notably, the joint drop ( $-$ 7.3pp) exceeds the sum of the individual
field drops (courtesy $-$ 4.0pp, legal $-$ 2.4pp), indicating that errors
which overlapped in English become spread across different items under
Korean prompting—a de-correlation effect that amplifies the joint
penalty.
Gemini 3.5 Flash is near-ceiling across all languages (95.2–97.6% joint),
with a range of only 2.4pp.

Two findings emerge. First, model family dominates
prompt language as a factor in extraction accuracy: Gemini 3.5 Flash’s weakest
language (Korean, 95.2%) exceeds Claude Opus 4.6’s best (English, 83.9%)
by 11.3pp, which in turn exceeds GPT-5.1’s strongest (Spanish, 54.8%) by
over 29 percentage points. Second, the multilingual prompting effect is
model-dependent: it provides a substantial boost for GPT-5.1 (up to +5.6pp),
is negligible for Gemini 3.5 Flash ( $\leq$ 2.4pp range), and actually hurts
Claude Opus 4.6 (up to $-$ 7.3pp for Korean), suggesting that the direction and
magnitude of the multilingual effect cannot be assumed uniform across model
families.

## 5.2 Cross-Lingual QA Results across Tasks 2 and 3

Accuracy is consistent cross-lingually, but language rankings are
domain-dependent. On Mintaka, Claude Opus 4.6 leads at 79.5%, followed by GPT-5.1 at
77.5% and Gemini 3.5 Flash at 77.4%; English is the strongest language
for every configuration (Table 2), but the
cross-lingual spread is modest—at most 3.3pp for GPT-5.1 low
(78.8% vs. 75.5% Japanese) and 3.0pp for Claude standard (80.2%
vs. 77.2% Japanese).
On FinanceBench, overall accuracy is 79.9% with the same
model-family ranking (Claude 81.8%, GPT-5.1 80.3%, Gemini 77.5%) and
per-language means spanning 5.8pp (77.1–82.9%). However, the language
ranking reverses; Hindi is the best non-English language (82.9%),
outperforming even English (78.9%), while German is the weakest (77.1%).
A plausible explanation is that FinanceBench questions are shorter and
more formulaic than Mintaka’s diverse trivia, reducing the surface area
for translation errors that disproportionately affect morphologically
rich languages. The finding suggests that the “Hindi penalty” on
general-knowledge QA is domain-dependent rather than an intrinsic
language limitation.

Reasoning-effort gains are domain-dependent: marginal on general
knowledge, substantial on financial QA.
On Mintaka, higher effort produces at most $+$ 0.8pp for GPT-5.1 and
Claude, and effectively zero for Gemini. On FinanceBench, the effect is
larger but not uniform across families: GPT-5.1 jumps $+$ 7.0pp (76.0%
to 84.0% in English), Claude gains only $+$ 1.5pp, and Gemini
declines $-$ 2.5pp. The GPT-dominated lift is consistent with
numerical-reasoning demands of SEC 10-K questions benefiting from
additional compute in a way that trivia questions do not.

(Figure 3(a)).
On FinanceBench, however, this pattern reverses: incorrect answers are
shorter overall ( $0.88{\times}$ ). Only Claude preserves the
Mintaka direction ( $1.14{\times}$ ), while GPT-5.1 ( $0.78{\times}$ ) and
Gemini ( $0.85{\times}$ ) show shorter incorrect outputs. The reversal is
question-type-dependent: domain-relevant questions still show longer
incorrect answers ( $1.20{\times}$ ), but metrics-generated questions
strongly reverse ( $0.62{\times}$ ), indicating that verbosity as a quality
signal does not generalise across domains.

Confidence scores are poorly calibrated across all families on both tasks.
On Mintaka, mean confidence on incorrect answers exceeds 0.91 for every
family, with family-mean correct–incorrect gaps of only 0.015–0.022
(Figure 3(b)). Disaggregating by effort level
reveals wider per-configuration gaps: GPT-5.1’s gap narrows from 0.028
(low effort) to 0.007 (high), and Claude Opus 4.6’s from 0.017 to 0.010,
while Gemini is invariant. Uncertain predictions—those the rule
engine could not settle—receive noticeably lower confidence from GPT-5.1
and Claude; Gemini’s uncertain confidence is indistinguishable from correct.
On FinanceBench the same pattern holds: mean confidence on incorrect
answers is 0.904 overall, with a correct–incorrect gap of only 0.028.
Gemini is the most overconfident (0.958 on incorrect), while GPT-5.1 shows
the largest discriminative gap (0.048). Unlike Mintaka, higher reasoning
effort widens the gap for GPT-5.1 (from 0.027 at low to 0.066 at
high), suggesting that extended reasoning improves calibration on
domain-specific content. Across both tasks, 93% of FinanceBench
confidence scores exceed 0.8, confirming that self-reported confidence is
not a reliable filter for incorrect answers regardless of domain.

Semantic similarity alone does not predict cross-lingual accuracy.
On Mintaka, all eight target languages show lower answer-level cosine
similarity than English, with a clear three-tier structure
(Table 3): Hindi is a pronounced outlier,
Arabic and Japanese form a middle tier, and the five European languages
cluster near English. This ranking is robust across both Ada and
MPNet encoders on Mintaka (Pearson $r=0.987$ ), but the two encoders
diverge on FinanceBench—German’s Ada gap is $-0.004$ while its MPNet gap
is $-0.045$ —further undermining similarity as a reliable signal.
On FinanceBench, answer-level gaps are an order of magnitude smaller (Ada
 $\Delta$ from $-0.007$ to $-0.001$ ), and at the language level,
similarity is anti-correlated with accuracy—Hindi has the lowest
answer similarity yet the highest accuracy. Neither encoder’s similarity
score correlates with item-level correctness (Pearson $r<0.01$ ,
 $p>0.75$ ), indicating that semantic similarity alone is not a usable
proxy for cross-lingual accuracy.

## 6 Discussion

Our results converge on several cross-cutting findings that inform multilingual
LLM deployment, particularly for financial institutions considering
cross-lingual pipeline reuse.

## Cross-lingual reusability.

Across all three tasks, the evidence supports qualified optimism.
Accuracy drops at most 3.3pp on Task 2, Gemini is near-ceiling across
prompt languages on Task 1 ( $\geq$ 95.2%), and FinanceBench
per-language means span only 5.8pp. However, reusability is
model-dependent—Claude degrades with non-English prompts on
Task 1—and the patterns that fail or transfer only partially
(Table 4, rows 1–3) mean that per-language
validation on the target domain is essential before deployment.

## Limitations.

Several limitations temper these conclusions.
First, the pinned evidence corpus was captured in English and translated
using GPT-4.1; observed cross-lingual gaps therefore conflate reasoning
degradation with translation-quality variation, and native-language
retrieval remains untested.
Second, proxy precision and recall rely on alias-coattributed references, not
human relevance judgments.
Third, the semantic-similarity diagnostic uses a single embedding model and
no calibrated threshold; it is not a substitute for factual accuracy.
Fourth, the reasoning-depth axis is not commensurable across families
(GPT uses a graded ladder, Claude an on/off switch, Gemini a narrow range),
so cross-family depth comparisons should be read with caution.
Finally, the 400-item Mintaka sample—while stratified—may not capture
all difficulty profiles present in full-scale deployments. The 50-item
FinanceBench pilot addresses the domain gap and yields a substantive
finding (Hindi’s ranking reverses between general-knowledge and financial
QA), but the pilot remains small and covers only four of the nine
languages; larger-scale replication is needed to confirm the pattern.

## 7 Conclusion

We presented a systematic multilingual evaluation of three major LLM families
across three tasks—financial cheque extraction,
cross-lingual question answering on a general-knowledge benchmark, and a
domain-validation pilot on financial QA over SEC 10-K filings—spanning up
to nine languages and nine model–effort configurations.

Addressing our three reusability dimensions: accuracy reusability
is broadly confirmed—non-English prompts can match or exceed English
on extraction and QA, though the language-difficulty ranking is
domain-dependent (RQ1). Effort reusability holds within
domains: the direction of reasoning-effort effects transfers across
languages, but their magnitude—and, for some families, their
direction—does not transfer reliably across domains (RQ2).
Behavioural reusability is stable: citation-set overlap with
English averages ${\sim}78\%$ Jaccard, with no single language
consistently worse (RQ3).
The FinanceBench domain-validation pilot confirms that model-family
dominance and small cross-lingual gaps extend to financial content
(79.9% overall, 5.8pp best-to-worst spread), while revealing that the
language-difficulty ranking is domain-dependent: Hindi, the weakest
language on general-knowledge QA, is the strongest on financial QA.
Additionally, output token cost is family-dependent: Claude’s
cross-language range exceeds $20\%$ (Hindi is the most expensive
language), while GPT is nearly language-neutral, making model selection
the dominant cost driver for multilingual deployments.
More broadly, the modest cross-lingual accuracy and citation-behaviour
gaps suggest that English-developed pipelines can often be reused across
languages through translation, but per-language validation on the target
domain remains necessary—semantic-similarity screening does not
reliably predict accuracy across domains.

Future work should extend this analysis in three directions:
(i) native-language retrieval to disentangle search quality from
reasoning quality, (ii) LLM-judged citation support precision and
completeness to move beyond proxy reference labels, and
(iii) larger-scale financial evaluations across more languages to
test whether the Hindi reversal and small cross-lingual gaps observed
in the 50-item pilot hold at scale.

## Steerable Pluralistic Policy Alignment from Anonymized Crowd Feedback

Akansha Kalra\corresponding

Ellen R. Novoseller

Vernon J. Lawhern

Daniel S. Brown

Abstract

Pluralistic alignment requires AI systems to learn from diverse populations whose preferences may reflect heterogeneous or even conflicting objectives, and to personalize their behavior to individual users.
Existing approaches in pluralistic alignment often assume access to annotator identities and rely on powerful pretrained representations to model individual preference variations.
We propose Preference-PCA Pluralistic Learning (P3L), a novel framework for pluralistic alignment that jointly learns trajectory embeddings and the shared basis of preference directions via PCA decomposition from heterogeneous crowd feedback without requiring annotator identities.
At test time, our approach enables rapid and accurate personalized reward learning and downstream policy optimization.
Experiments across multiple embodied control tasks demonstrate that P3L enables steerable personal alignment that generalizes beyond the offline dataset to out-of-distribution new users and attains competitive RL and test performance, while substantially reducing computational cost and supporting variable adaptation query budgets.

## Introduction

As we witness growing adoption of AI systems and robotic models for downstream applications such as in industrial automation (Tang et al. 2023; Kernbach et al. 2026), assistive healthcare (Bouzida et al. 2024; Lamsey et al. 2024), and household robotics (Shafiullah et al. 2023), being able to steer and align these models towards an end-user’s individual preference is seen as a major challenge (Ji et al. 2023; Casper et al. 2023).
One of the most popular approaches for aligning such models is Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF).

RLHF learns a reward function from human preferences and then optimizes an RL policy under the learned reward function (Christiano et al. 2017; Ouyang et al. 2022; Kaufmann et al. 2024).
While this approach has been highly successful, it has been shown to be susceptible to over optimization (Anwar et al. 2024) and reward misidentification (Tien et al. 2023), and can become easily misaligned across diverse users in a crowd (Siththaranjan, Laidlaw, and Hadfield-Menell 2024; Poddar et al. 2024; Chhan, Novoseller, and Lawhern 2024).
Recent work has tried to solve this personalization challenge by either learning multiple reward functions from divergent crowd-sourced preferences (Myers et al. 2022; Shenfeld et al. 2025) or by assuming knowledge about the annotators who provide the preference labels, such as the annotator’s id (Poddar et al. 2024) .
However, this assumption limits applicability in many real-world settings where annotator identities are unavailable, anonymized or cannot be reliably associated with preference labels.
Moreover, VPL has been shown to suffer from posterior collapse (Kim and Kim 2026), a failure mode that we also observe empirically.
This raises a central challenge: Can we learn from heterogeneous crowd preferences—potentially reflecting conflicting objectives—without annotator identity, while still enabling efficient test-time personalization to new users in embodied control tasks?

Our key idea is to decompose heterogeneous crowd preferences into a small set of shared preference directions.
This reframes pluralistic alignment as estimating user-specific weights over a shared preference basis representation rather than learning separate reward models for each user.
This representation enables personalized reward functions—and corresponding policies—to be constructed for new users at test time without requiring annotator identities, allowing rapid policy personalization from a small number of adaptive queries.
Building on the preference vector framework (Luo et al. 2025), which first proposed to represent human preferences as directions in a latent representation space in the context of LLMs, we extend and test this idea in embodied control scenarios. Our approach jointly learns trajectory representations and preference directions from heterogeneous crowd feedback. Specifically, we train an end-to-end unsupervised representation encoder that embeds trajectories and we use these embeddings to extract a basis of preference eigenvectors capturing variation in user objectives.

Unlike prior work such as Variational Preference Learning (VPL) (Poddar et al. 2024), which assumes access to annotator identities to infer user-specific rewards, our framework does not require annotator information. However, our framework can incorporate annotator identities when available, enabling it to operate under both anonymized and privileged settings.
Overall, our approach enables a simple, flexible, and effective framework for pluralistic alignment in embodied control.

We summarize our contributions as follows:
(1) Learning preference bases from heterogeneous feedback.
We introduce a framework that learns a shared basis of preference directions from heterogeneous, potentially conflicting crowd preferences. Our method leverages an unsupervised trajectory encoder to construct latent trajectory embeddings and extracts preference eigenvectors that capture variation in user objectives without requiring annotator identities.
(2) Personalized policy alignment.
We demonstrate that the learned preference basis enables test-time construction of personalized reward functions and policies, allowing alignment to individual users across multiple objectives.
(3) Empirical evaluation.
Across multi-objective control environments, our method achieves strong alignment to both in-distribution and out-of-distribution users. Compared to prior work such as VPL (Poddar et al. 2024), it achieves higher test-time preference accuracy and competitive RL performance while significantly reducing training cost and allowing flexible adaptation to varying numbers of test-time queries.

## Related Work

Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF)

addresses the challenge of specifying reward functions by instead learning them from human feedback. Human feedback can take several forms, including pairwise preferences (Christiano et al. 2017; Poddar et al. 2024), rankings (Brown et al. 2019; Myers et al. 2022), and corrective feedback (Bajcsy et al. 2017; Mehta and Losey 2024).
Most RLHF methods learn reward functions from pairwise preferences and assume that this preference data originates from a single underlying reward function, effectively treating disagreement among annotators as noise. Some work attempts to account for imperfect human feedback. For example, Lee et al. (2021) introduced stochastic preference models to capture noisy or inconsistent human judgments. Nevertheless, these approaches still assume that annotators ultimately share a common objective.
By contrast, real-world preference data is often collected from diverse users whose objectives may fundamentally differ or even conflict. This motivates studying methods that can learn from heterogeneous crowd feedback while still enabling alignment to individual users.

Personalized RLHF and Pluralistic Alignment

Recent work has begun to investigate how to model heterogeneous human preferences and personalize AI behavior to different users. Several approaches have explored preference diversity primarily in the context of large language models (LLMs) (Luo et al. 2025; Shenfeld et al. 2025; Siththaranjan, Laidlaw, and Hadfield-Menell 2024). These methods typically model preference variation through latent variables or preference decompositions, but focus on reward modeling rather than policy personalization or embodied control domains.
Distributional Preference Learning (DPL) (Siththaranjan, Laidlaw, and Hadfield-Menell 2024) studies preference learning in the presence of hidden context, where annotator identity or other contextual factors influencing preferences are not observed by the reward model.
While this approach explicitly models preference heterogeneity through latent contextual factors during reward learning, its primary goal is to characterize and detect hidden context rather than to construct personalized policies for new users.
Only a small number of works study pluralistic alignment in embodied control tasks. Variational Preference Learning (VPL) (Poddar et al. 2024) models user-specific reward functions by introducing a latent variable conditioned on annotator identity, requiring access to annotator IDs during training. Alternatively, Bahlous-Boldi et al. (2025) learns a Pareto set of reward hypotheses using lexicase selection to represent trade-offs among multiple objectives.
Another line of prior work (Lin and Brown 2026) identifies shared safety principles from crowd feedback but does not consider personalization or model user differences as we do.
Despite many recent advances in pluralistic alignment, existing approaches (Myers et al. 2022; Poddar et al. 2024; Bahlous-Boldi et al. 2025) either require annotator identities, explicitly model latent contexts during reward learning, or produce sets of reward hypotheses rather than directly enabling personalization for new users or learn shared skills from crowd feedback rather than personalizing for individual preferred behaviors.
In contrast, our framework learns a shared basis of preference directions from anonymized heterogeneous crowd feedback. This basis representation allows personalized reward functions—and corresponding policies—to be constructed for previously unseen users at test time from a small number of preference queries.

## Preliminaries

RLHF

We model the problem as a Markov Decision Process (MDP) (Puterman 1994) with state space $S$ , action space $A$ , horizon $T$ , and reward function $r:S\times A\rightarrow\mathbb{R}$ . The true reward function $r$ is unknown and must be inferred from human preferences over trajectories.
A trajectory $\tau$ is defined as a sequence of state–action pairs over the horizon $T$ , expressed as $\tau=(s_{1},a_{1},\dots,s_{T-1},a_{T-1})$ .
Given a dataset $D$ of annotated preferences over trajectory pairs of the form $\tau_{i}\prec\tau_{j}$ , traditional RLHF first learns a reward function using a maximum likelihood objective based on the Bradley–Terry–Luce (BTL) model (Bradley and Terry 1952), written as $p(\tau_{i}\succ\tau_{j})=\frac{e^{r_{\theta}(\tau_{i})}}{e^{r_{\theta}(\tau_{i})}+e^{r_{\theta}(\tau_{j})}}$ ,
where $\theta$ denotes the parameters of the learned reward model.
A policy $\pi^{\star}_{r_{\theta}}$ is then optimized under the learned reward: $\pi^{\star}_{r_{\theta}}=\arg\max_{\pi}\mathbb{E}_{\pi}\left[\sum_{t=0}^{T}\gamma^{t}r_{\theta}(s_{t},a_{t})\right]$ .
This formulation learns a single reward function that aggregates all preferences. Consequently, divergent preference signals are treated as noise rather than as evidence of different user objectives.

Variational Preference Learning (VPL)

Prior work by Poddar et al. (2024) addresses learning from crowd preferences by introducing a latent preference variable $z$ associated with each annotator. The reward model is then conditioned on this latent variable via a contextual BTL model $p(\tau_{i}\succ\tau_{j}\mid z)=\frac{e^{r_{\theta}(\tau_{i},z)}}{e^{r_{\theta}(\tau_{i},z)}+e^{r_{\theta}(\tau_{j},z)}}.$ 
However, this formulation assumes access to annotator identities during training in order to infer the latent preference variable.
VPL also only considers preference elicitation in one-step, contextual bandit (Poddar et al. 2024) settings.
In contrast, the longer-horizon embodied control tasks we consider require distinguishing between temporally extended trajectories and their cumulative consequences.
Another limitation of VPL that we address is that it requires a fixed number of preference queries for adaptation to a new user, referred to as the context set (Poddar et al. 2024). This constraint arises from the VAE-based latent preference inference architecture, whose encoder requires a fixed-size input.
Because each query must be labeled by the new user, this requirement introduces additional interaction and annotation cost, and precludes test-time learning from variable numbers of adaptation queries, something that our approach enables.
Finally, prior work has shown that VPL is susceptible to posterior collapse (Kim and Kim 2026).
We find that this limitation extends beyond language modeling to embodied control, where we observe collapse in large state-spaces and longer-horizon tasks. For more details, please refer to Appendix I.

Decomposed Reward Models (DRM)

Luo et al. (2025) reformulate preference learning by decomposing rewards into multiple orthogonal preference directions in representation space rather than learning a single scalar reward.
However, their analysis focuses primarily on reward prediction accuracy and does not consider optimizing policies under personalized reward functions. High accuracy on held-out preference queries does not necessarily translate to behaviorally aligned policies, as widely documented in prior RLHF work (Christiano et al. 2017; Tien et al. 2023; Wen et al. 2025).
Furthermore, DRM only tested this idea on pretrained LLM embedding models trained on internet-scale data such as Gemma-2B-RM and Llama3-8B-RM, which have undergone multiple stages of training including pretraining and supervised instruction fine-tuning before preference alignment (Ziegler et al. 2020; Ouyang et al. 2022).
In contrast, many embodied control domains lack such pretrained representation models. A key challenge that we tackle in this paper is to learn meaningful trajectory representations and preference decompositions directly from limited task-specific data in settings where large pretrained embedding models are unavailable.
We show that it is possible to jointly learn the task objectives as well as user preference distribution from heterogeneous preference dataset using only self-supervised representations.

## Problem Formulation

Learning from diverse crowdsourced feedback presents a more challenging setting than standard RLHF.
Vanilla RLHF aggregates preferences from multiple annotators into a single reward function, implicitly assuming that all preference labels arise from a shared underlying objective. However, in many real-world settings different users may have fundamentally different goals or behavioral preferences.
The idea of creating AI systems that can model and serve pluralistic human values is known as pluralistic alignment (Sorensen et al. 2024).
Our work specifically focuses on steerable pluralistic alignment, where the goal is to adapt an AI system at test time to the preferences of a specific user in the presence of heterogeneous crowd feedback.

Steerable Pluralistic Alignment with Heterogeneous Users

Consider a population of $N$ annotators, where each user $u_{i}$ has an unknown ground-truth reward function $r_{i}$ . For each preference query $m$ , an unobserved annotator index $z^{m}\in\{1,\dots,N\}$ generates a label $y^{m}\in\{0,1\}$ over a trajectory pair $(\tau_{A}^{m},\tau_{B}^{m})$ according to that annotator’s latent reward function $r_{z^{m}}$ , where $y^{m}=0$ indicates that $\tau_{A}^{m}$ is preferred over $\tau_{B}^{m}$ . Because users may have different objectives, conflicting labels for the same pair of trajectories can naturally occur
when the two labels are produced by different annotators.
We assume access to an offline dataset of crowd preferences $D=\{(\tau_{A}^{m},\tau_{B}^{m},y^{m})\}_{m=1}^{M}$ ,
where the annotator identities $\{z^{m}\}_{m=1}^{M}$ may or may not be available.

Learning Objective

Our goal is to use this heterogeneous preference dataset to learn representations of heterogeneous preferences that enable efficient test-time personalization of reward functions and policies for new users.
Under this setting, we aim to address the following questions:
(1) Can we infer personalized reward functions $\hat{r}_{i}$ for individual users from heterogeneous preference data when neither annotator identities nor the underlying task objectives are known?
(2) Can we optimize policies $\hat{\pi}_{i}$ under these inferred rewards to produce behaviors aligned with diverse user preferences?
(3) Can such personalization generalize to both in-distribution users and to users whose rewards fall outside the distribution of the offline preference dataset?

## Preference-PCA Pluralistic Learning (P3L)

We term our proposed approach Preference-PCA Pluralistic Learning (P3L). The overall pipeline of our approach is shown in Fig. 1.
We consider the joint problem of pre-training an expressive embedding extractor and leveraging it to learn preference vectors from diverse crowd preferences in control domains to provide personalized alignment to test-time users on both test time held out set as well as a personalized policy optimized under the personalized derived reward.

## Representation Encoders

We study two types of representation encoders that employ Masked Decision Prediction (MaskedDP) (Liu et al. 2022) as their base architecture, and describe each below.

## Anonymized CLS-MaskedDP:

In this setting, we assume anonymized crowd data with no knowledge about annotator ids.
We learn the trajectory embedding following the CLS-token formulation of BERT (Devlin et al. 2019), by feeding the outputs of frozen MaskedDP encoder through a self-attention module, thereby learning a CLS token in a completely end-to-end unsupervised manner.
We learn the summary token by employing different masking ratios in MaskedDP in the triplet loss.
Given two trajectories $\tau_{1},\tau_{2}$ from the preference dataset, we use $\phi_{\tau_{1}}$ with mask ratio $m_{1}$ for anchor and with another mask ratio $m_{2}$ as the positive sample, while $\phi_{\tau_{2}}$ with mask $m_{1}$ 
is used as a negative sample

 $\displaystyle\mathcal{L}=\max\Bigl(0,$ 
 $\displaystyle\|\phi(\tau_{1}|m_{1})-\phi(\tau_{1}|m_{2})\|_{2}^{2}$ 

(1)

 $\displaystyle-\|\phi(\tau_{1}|m_{1})-\phi(\tau_{2}|m_{1})\|_{2}^{2}+\alpha\Bigr),$ 

where $\alpha$ is the triplet margin.
This approach is a completely end-to-end unsupervised embedding learning method and we refer to this approach later on as CLS-MaskedDP.

## Privileged Traj-MaskedDP:

In this setting, we assume that annotator id is known.
This is the exact same assumption made in VPL by Poddar et al. (2024). We term this approach as privileged because of this assumption about access to privileged information about annotator identity.
We leverage prior knowledge about annotator-identity on preference labels to learn a linear summarization of the trajectory.
Formally, given a preference dataset D={ $(\tau_{b},\tau_{c}),{(\tau_{d},\tau_{e}}),...(\tau_{o},\tau_{m})$ } of size M with knowledge that annotator $A_{1}$ prefers $\tau_{b}\succ\tau_{c}$ and $\tau_{d}\succ\tau_{e}$ , we sample $\tau_{b}$ and $\tau_{d}$ from $A_{1}$ ’s set to serve as the anchor and positive trajectories respectively while for another annotator $A_{2}$ given that $\tau_{o}\succ\tau_{m}$ , then we can use $\tau_{o}$ as the negative trajectory.
Latent embeddings generated by frozen MaskedDP encoder are fed into a linear layer trained using contrastive learning via a triplet loss:

 $\displaystyle\mathcal{L}=\max\Bigl(0,$ 
 $\displaystyle\|\phi(\tau^{A_{1}}_{anchor})-\phi(\tau^{A_{1}}_{pos})\|_{2}^{2}$ 

(2)

 $\displaystyle-\|\phi(\tau^{A_{1}}_{anchor})-\phi(\tau^{A_{2}}_{neg})\|_{2}^{2}+\alpha\Bigr)$ 

where $\alpha$ is the triplet margin.
We refer to this approach as Traj-MaskedDP.

## Learning from Crowd-Sourced Feedback

Given the crowd-sourced preference dataset $D$ with preferences over trajectories of form $\tau_{i}\prec\tau_{j}$ , we model heterogeneity in human choices as a preference vector via the following steps.

Learning eigenvectors

We first apply Principal Component Analysis (PCA) (Pearson 1901) to a set of latent difference embeddings $\Phi_{\text{diff}}$ derived from pairwise preference queries in the offline heterogeneous preference dataset.
This yields a set of principal basis vectors $W=[w_{1},w_{2},...,w_{d}]$ , where each orthogonal basis vector captures a principal direction of variation in the observed preference signals. The individual latent difference embeddings are defined as $\phi_{diff}=\phi(\tau_{1})-\phi(\tau_{2})\text{ for }y=0$ and $\phi_{diff}=\phi(\tau_{2})-\phi(\tau_{1})\text{ for }y=1$ .
Similar to DRM (Luo et al. 2025), we assume that human preferences can be modeled as approximately linearly decomposable along orthogonal directions, $W$ in the representation space, where

 $\displaystyle W$ 
 $\displaystyle=\{w_{i}\}_{i=1}^{k}=\mathrm{PCA}(\Phi_{\text{diff}}).$ 

(3)

Since PCA is invariant to eigenvector sign, the direction $w_{i}$ is equivalent to $-w_{i}$ from the perspective of variance decomposition.
In contrast, human preferences are directional: moving along $w_{i}$ 
may correspond to favoring one behavioral tendency, while moving along $-w_{i}$ may correspond to the opposing tendency.
We therefore preserve both orientations $w_{i}$ and $-w_{i}$ of each principal component to capture contrasting behavioral preferences.
Thus any human preference can be modeled as linear combination of these basis vector $\hat{w}=\sum_{j=1}^{d}k_{j}w_{j}$ .
where $k_{1},k_{2},...k_{d}$ are weights on these individual vectors $w_{i}$ .
The total predicted reward of trajectory $\tau$ can be expressed as $\hat{r}=\hat{w}^{T}\phi(\tau)$ .

Test-Time User Adaptation

At test time, we actively query the user for their preferences to form an adaptive set of queries $D_{adapt}$ .
We then leverage these adaptive queries to compute coefficients $k_{1},k_{2},...k_{d}$ on the principal eigenvectors using Hypothesis Reweighing (HyRe) (Lee et al. 2024) to form the complete preference vector of the test-time user:

 $\displaystyle k_{i}$ 
 $\displaystyle=\frac{\exp\!\left(\mathbb{E}_{(\tau_{A},\tau_{B},y)\sim D_{\text{adapt}}}\left[\log\sigma\!\Big(w_{i}^{\top}\big(\phi(\tau_{A})-\phi(\tau_{B})\big)\Big)\right]\right)}{\sum_{d}\exp\!\left(\mathbb{E}_{(\tau_{A},\tau_{B},y)\sim D_{\text{adapt}}}\left[\log\sigma\!\Big(w_{d}^{\top}\big(\phi(\tau_{A})-\phi(\tau_{B})\big)\Big)\right]\right)}$ 

In this manner, weights $k_{j}$ shift dynamically according to the adaptive preference dataset to model the new user.

Learning a Personalized Policy

Once we have the weights $k_{j}$ on our basis eigenvectors $w_{j}$ , we then learn a personalized policy $\hat{\pi}$ for the new user $i$ by using reinforcement learning to maximize the expected performance under $\hat{r_{i}}$ .
Formally $\hat{\pi}_{i}^{\star}=\arg\max_{\pi_{i}}\mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim\pi_{i}}\!\left[\hat{r}_{i}(\tau)\right]=\arg\max_{\pi_{i}}\mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim\pi_{i}}\!\left[\hat{w}_{i}^{\top}\phi(\tau)\right]$ .

## Experimental Setup

We first describe the environments used, followed by the baseline methods for comparison and finally present the evaluation criteria and metrics.

Environments

Following the pluralism evaluation benchmark criteria outlined in Sorensen et al. (2024), we consider multi-objective environments for steerable pluralistic alignment.
They provide a principled way to represent crowdsourced feedback with diverse and potentially conflicting objectives. Fig. 2 shows the environments used: MO-Highway and MO-Reacher from MO-Gymnasium (Felten et al. 2023), and MO-Humanoid and MO-Kitchen, described below.

MO-Highway has a 3-dimensional reward with first reward dimension rewarding high-speed driving while the second dimension scores on driving in right lane and the last reward dimension represents the collision reward.

MO-Reacher provides a 4-dimensional reward vector, where each dimension corresponds to the reward for reaching a specific goal.
Some goals induce competing preferences; for example, reaching the left goal conflicts directly with reaching the right goal.

MO-Humanoid (Chen et al. 2026) has a 5-dimensional objective space with objectives corresponding
to forward velocity along the $x$ -axis, lateral velocity along the
 $y$ -axis, angular velocities of the left and right elbows, and energy
efficiency.
The environment has a high-dimensional continuous
observation space of dimension 376 and an action space of dimension 17.

MO-Kitchen is a multi-objective version of Franka Kitchen (Gupta et al. 2020) that we created,
featuring a 9-DoF Franka robot that can perform several distinct manipulation tasks, such as opening the microwave, moving the kettle, and turning oven knobs for different burners.
This setting naturally induces heterogeneous user preferences such as User 1 might prefer opening the microwave, User 2 moving the kettle, User 3 toggling the light switch, while User 4 prefers turning the knob to activate the top burner.
MO-Kitchen is challenging because feedback from multiple users with different preferred tasks is learned jointly, requiring the framework to disentangle heterogeneous user objectives
while reasoning over coordinated reaching, contact-rich manipulation, and precise end-effector orientation.

For MO-Kitchen, we conduct experiments and explore personalization in multiple variants, starting from personalizing in simple setting with only 2 possible tasks, termed as MO-Kitchen-2d (opening the microwave door or moving the kettle) and then progress to challenging scenarios with 3 possible tasks, termed as MO-Kitchen-3d (microwave door, kettle, or flipping the light switch). Finally we move to the most sophisticated domain with 4 different tasks possible, termed as MO-Kitchen-4d, where each user can choose from a variety of tasks from opening the microwave door to pushing the kettle to turning oven knobs for different burners to turning on the light switch.

In each of these environments we ignore the terminal condition and make these tasks fixed time horizon to avoid leakage of information and to prevent termination bias in reward learning from preferences as advised by Freire et al. (2020).
Based on the number of underlying objectives in an environment we report the performance across all objectives where each individual user prefers one specific objective.
For simplicity in our experiments, we associate User 1 with a preference for Objective 1 over all other objectives, User 2 with Objective 2, and so on.
This is only a notational convention; our framework makes no assumption itself that user and objective indices must align, and thus in general, any user may prefer any objective.

Baselines

We evaluate both our privileged and non-privileged approach against a single reward head model (SingleRM) learned through conventional Bradley-Terry loss optimization, and VPL.
VPL uses adaptive queries to infer the latent preference variable on which it conditions its reward and policy while our approaches, inspired by (Luo et al. 2025) use HyRe (Lee et al. 2024), to infer the test-time user’s preference vector.
For complete setup details with respect to number of queries, etc., please refer to Appendix A.
We optimize online RL policies under learned reward functions using Augmented Random Search (Mania, Guy, and Recht 2018) and Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) (Schulman et al. 2017) from Stable Baselines3 (Raffin et al. 2021) with episodic rewards.
We employ episodic rewards for two reasons: (1) because the preference dataset is constructed using fixed horizon trajectories and we want to mitigate termination bias (Freire et al. 2020) and (2) because
our reward formulation combines the PCA preference vector with a representation encoder that summarizes the full trajectory.

Original VPL is an offline framework which uses Implicit Q-Learning (IQL) (Kostrikov, Nair, and Levine 2022) to learn an offline policy optimized for per timestep reward.
For fair comparison to our frameworks, we learn both an offline IQL policy as well as an online episodic policy for VPL for all MO-environments.
Since MO-Kitchen is commonly used as an offline RL benchmark, we optimize a continuous-control IQL policy on the collected offline dataset using the learned personalized reward, and evaluate the resulting policy through online environment rollouts.
For more details on RL optimization and hyper-parameters, see Appendix D.

Metrics

We compare the performance of our approach against earlier frameworks on these metrics:
(1) accuracy on adapting to a new held out test-time user’s preferences;
(2) performance of RL policy optimized under new test-time user’s preferences;
(3) time taken on NVIDIA GeForce RTX-GT3090 to train these models;
(4) Ablation with respect to the number of adaptive queries (For ablation with respect to training queries and number of PCA dimensions, see Appendix H and Appendix F respectively); and
(5) Adaptation to out-of-distribution users where we train on subset of different users and test the adaptation to held-out new user both on test and RL policy.

Note that in the Original VPL paper (Poddar et al. 2024), the adaptation query set size is referred to as context size in VPL and is noted in their appendix as the main hyperparameter to tune.
Therefore, metric (4) directly evaluates prior work under its key adaptation hyperparameter, and we include this ablation to ensure a fair comparison against VPL.

## In-Distribution Experiments

Test-Time Preference Alignment Accuracy

In Table 1, we present the average accuracy of adapting to test-time held out preference queries in terms of mean and standard deviation across 5 seeds for all environments.
For the individual user accuracy of adapting to test-time held out preference queries across all environments and for more details, please refer to Appendix B.

We observe that for all environments, our approaches Privileged-Traj-MaskedDP and Anonymized-CLS-MaskedDP outperform both Single Reward Model and VPL, where Privileged-Traj-MaskedDP makes the same assumptions about prior knowledge of annotator identity as VPL while the Anonymized-CLS-MaskedDP approach has no such prior knowledge.
Across MO-Kitchen, we observe that while P3L outperforms both the baselines, its performance decreases as the number of preference dimensions increases. Privileged-Traj-MaskedDP falls from 100.0% to 84.15% to 78.44%, while Anonymized-CLS-MaskedDP decreases from 90.4% to 82.67% to 72.17%.
This degradation is consistent with the increasing difficulty of preference inference in a higher-dimensional objective space; nevertheless, both P3L variants continue to considerably outperform the baselines, indicating that the proposed framework better captures the underlying multi-objective preference structure.
Thus we show better alignment to the held out queries when learning from diverse crowd feedback with no information about annotator identity.

## Discussion and Conclusion

We present a framework for pluralistic alignment that enables personalized policy learning from heterogeneous crowd feedback, both with and without privileged information about annotator identities.
Experiments in multi-objective control environments show improved preference alignment, competitive RL performance, and significantly reduced training cost compared to prior approaches. These results suggest that learning structured representations of diverse human preferences provides a scalable path toward personalized alignment in RLHF systems. Unlike prior work (Poddar et al. 2024), which assumes access to annotator IDs, our approach can operate effectively without this information while still achieving competitive performance in terms of both preference test-set accuracy and downstream RL performance.
A key advantage of our framework is its computational efficiency. Compared to existing approaches, our method significantly reduces training cost while maintaining strong alignment performance. This efficiency is particularly important in RLHF settings, where learning from human feedback is often sample-intensive (Christiano et al. 2017; Ouyang et al. 2022). P3L achieves high test-time accuracy while requiring substantially less training time.
Our method also supports flexible adaptation at test time thus lowering the human interaction burden at test time.

We demonstrate that P3L successfully aligns policies to users both in-distribution and out-of-distribution relative to the offline preference dataset. Notably, Anonymized-CLS-MaskedDP variant operates under the weaker assumption that annotator identities are unavailable, yet often outperforms VPL which relies on annotator identity information—across both RL performance and preference prediction accuracy.
More broadly, our results suggest that heterogeneous crowd feedback can be effectively leveraged by learning a shared representation of preference directions. Once this representation is learned, personalized reward functions for new users can be constructed efficiently from only a small number of adaptive queries, providing a scalable mechanism for pluralistic alignment in RLHF systems.

One limitation of our approach is pre-specification of the number of PCA components used to represent preference directions. In practice, we determine this value through ablation studies to capture the structure of the underlying preference distribution.
Future work can explore metwhods for automatically inferring number of latent objectives present in crowdsourced feedback in an unsupervised manner and also investigate incorporating richer feedback modalities, including demonstrations, rankings, and corrective feedback.

## Appendix C Ablation on Annotation Set Size for VPL

For VPL, we tried to tune the annotation set size, also referred to as context set size in (Poddar et al. 2024) for each domain.
For MO-Reacher, we swept over annotation set sizes of 6, 30, 120, 300, and 600 preference queries, where the selected number of queries are provided at once to the VAE to infer the latent user preference vector $z$ which is then used to test alignment to new user both using test set accuracy as well as for optimizing an RL policy.

For MO-Humanoid, we tuned the learning VPL over annotation set sizes of 1,5,25 and using an annealer. We finally chose to use set size of 1 as we observed posterior collapse with bigger annotation set sizes.
For Kitchen, we started with annotation set size of 30 and soon observed posterior collapse so reduced annotation set size to 20 to successfully learn the VPL-VAE encoder and decoder reward.

## Appendix D In-distribution RL experiment details

We optimize RL policies under learned reward function using Augmented Random Search (Mania, Guy, and Recht 2018) and Proximal Policy Optimization(PPO) (Schulman et al. 2017) from Stable Baselines3 (Raffin et al. 2021) with episodic rewards for 7e6 timesteps for MO-Reacher and with 2e5 timesteps for MO-Highway respectively. Note, we use Stable-Baselines3 Contrib for ARS.
Original VPL is an offline framework which uses Implicit Q-Learning (IQL) (Kostrikov, Nair, and Levine 2022) to learn an offline policy optimized for per-timestep reward.
From TorchRL (Bou et al. 2024), we use discrete IQL for MO-Highway and MO-Reacher and continuous IQL for MO-Humanoid and MO-Kitchen respectively.

MO-Reacher is set to have a fixed horizon of 50 and does not have any terminal condition by default. We use ARS with default parameters in SB3 to optimize an RL policy using episodic rewards.
MO-FastHighway on the other hand by default ends if there’s a collision or if lapses 30 timesteps. So to avoid early termination bias, we ignore the terminal condition and set the environment to have a
fixed horizon of 30.
We use Vectorized Norm with $norm\_obs=True$ and $norm\_reward=True$ from SB3 to train PPO policies with normalized obs and normalized rewards in Fast Highway.
For MO-Humanoid , we optimized ARS policy under learned reward for 10M timesteps using an MLP with learning rate $0.02$ with n_delta=16 and n_top=8 and delta_std=0.025.
For MO-Kitchen we optimize a Continuous IQL policy for 1M total timesteps with learning rate =0.0003 , expectile = 0.7, temperature=3.0 and batch size of 256.

Table 2 compares average performance of RL policies learned using PPO as online RL algorithm for MO-Highway and ARS as online RL algorithm for MO-Reacher and MO-Humanoid across all frameworks except for VPL, where we also optimized an offline IQL policy, for fair comparison to Poddar et al. (2024) who used IQL;
while the following subsections report for each environment, the RL performance across individual new users with different underlying preferences.

## Appendix I VPL Posterior Collapse

We observed the encoder in VPL, Variational Autoencoder (VAE), begins to produce NaN values for the latent preference mean $z$ in two environments: MO-Humanoid and Franka-Kitchen.
For both environments we report parameters that originally resulted in NaN values of the latent mean and thereby collapsing training of the VPL reward model (which is actually the decoder with input as observation and the latent mean produced by the VAE).

We performed extensive hyperparameter tuning and found that reducing annotation set size, also termed as context set size in (Poddar et al. 2024), often led to more stable training.
For Kitchen, the VPL training dataset is heterogeneous, combining trajectories across all tasks and user preferences.
The training dataset contains 2,400 pairwise preference queries, and as each query can be annotated from the perspective of a user who prefers any one of the possible tasks, each query yields one label per possible preferred task, leading to 9600 preference labels.
We observed this collapse for seed 0, annotation set size 30 where each trajectory in pairwise preference query is of length 280, and an adaptation preference dataset of size 180.
For MO-Humanoid, we observed this when sweeping over annotation set size of 25 as well as with 5, over 1000 preference queries of length 150.

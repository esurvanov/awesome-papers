Abstract

Recent advances in multimodal large language models (MLLMs) have substantially improved spoken interaction and video understanding. Existing online and streaming benchmarks primarily assess causal multimodal perception through timestamped question answering, whereas real-world interaction requires models to continuously identify task-relevant multimodal cues and decide whether, when, and how to respond. This leaves open how well current models can sustain real-time interaction throughout an evolving multimodal stream. To address this gap, we introduce DIVE, a benchmark for evaluating full-duplex interaction with visual engagement under continuous and realistic multimodal streams. Our benchmark organizes six tasks into three complementary interaction scenarios. (1) Proactive Intervention evaluates whether models can identify emerging visual or semantic cues and intervene at the appropriate moment. (2) Continuous Engagement evaluates sustained participation through continuous visual tracking and visually grounded query interaction. (3) Interaction Coordination evaluates how models coordinate listening and speaking under overlapping and multi-party interactions. DIVE contains 525 audio/video samples with fine-grained human annotations and precise temporal metadata, covering six task-oriented interaction settings grounded in realistic scenarios. Its evaluation protocol separates response quality,
participation decisions, and response timing. We evaluate representative real-time models using response quality,
window response rates, and reaction latency. Experiments show that leading real-time models achieve sub-second
average response latency, yet their average window response rates remain at or below 53.77%. Response quality varies substantially across models and tasks, with adapting ongoing responses to newly arriving information
remaining particularly challenging. We hope DIVE facilitates progress toward more natural and capable real-time multimodal interactive systems.

## 1 Introduction

Multimodal large language models (MLLMs) have made substantial progress in video understanding and multimodal reasoning. Representative systems such as GPT-4o Hurst et al. (2024), Gemini Team et al. (2023), and a growing number of open-source MLLMs have demonstrated strong capabilities in understanding multimodal inputs. Correspondingly, widely used benchmarks such as Video-MME Fu et al. (2025) and LVBench Wang et al. (2025a) evaluate increasingly diverse and long-context video understanding capabilities, yet they largely follow an offline evaluation paradigm. This setting differs fundamentally from real-world interaction. This limitation has motivated growing interest in streaming multimodal understanding. Recent models are designed to process continuously arriving inputs and determine when a response should be produced. Meanwhile, benchmarks such as StreamingBench Lin et al. (2026b) and OVO-Bench Niu et al. (2025) assess models in real-time settings. In parallel, another line of research has focused on full-duplex interaction, where perception and generation can overlap in time. Early full-duplex systems were predominantly centered on speech interaction. Moshi Défossez et al. (2024) models dialogue as parallel speech-to-speech streams. Correspondingly, the Full-Duplex-Bench Lin et al. (2025); Lin et al. (2026a) series evaluates a range of interactive speech behaviors. More recently, full-duplex interaction has been extended to joint visual and audio perception together with native multimodal output, with TML Interaction Models Lab (2026) and MiniCPM-o 4.5 Cui et al. (2026) serving as representative examples. Recent benchmarks such as Omni-DuplexEval He et al. (2026) have also begun to evaluate real-time duplex omni-modal interaction through continuous and proactive response scenarios. These benchmarks increasingly cover proactive response, continuous monitoring, and interruption handling, but often assess these capabilities within isolated response windows rather than over the course of sustained interaction.

To study these interaction requirements, we introduce DIVE, a benchmark for full-duplex audio-visual interaction under continuous and realistic multimodal streams. Our benchmark systematically evaluates three complementary capabilities. DIVE contains 525 audio and video samples with fine-grained annotations and precise temporal information, covering diverse task-oriented scenarios grounded in realistic interactions. Its coverage of both audio and visual settings enables the evaluation of native full-duplex models as well as modality-specific streaming systems. Our experiments reveal that fast responses alone do not ensure reliable full-duplex interaction. Although leading real-time systems achieve sub-second average response latency, their window response rates leave considerable room for improvement, particularly for speech-driven proactive intervention.
Meanwhile, response quality varies substantially across models
and tasks, and adapting ongoing responses to newly arriving
information remains challenging.
These findings highlight the importance of evaluating response
timing and content quality together under continuous multimodal streams.

## 2 Related Works

## 2.1 Duplex Multimodal Models

Duplex modeling has been explored most extensively in real-time speech interaction. The GPT-Realtime series OpenAI (2025) and Gemini 3.1 Flash Live Google DeepMind (2026b) provide low-latency end-to-end speech interaction. Moshi Défossez et al. (2024) and MoshiRAG Chien et al. (2026) support simultaneous listening and speaking, and OmniFlatten Zhang et al. (2025) enables seamless full-duplex spoken dialogue within a unified autoregressive framework. Related efforts extend real-time speech modeling in complementary directions: MoshiVis Royer et al. (2025) augments duplex speech interaction with visual context, while Voxtral Realtime Liu et al. (2026a) develops stream-synchronous modeling for low-latency speech recognition. More recent systems move toward multimodal duplex interaction. Proprietary systems such as TML Interaction Models Lab (2026) and SeedRealtime Seeddance (2026) advance real-time interaction to full-duplex multimodal settings, continuously processing audio, video, and text inputs while producing concurrent outputs over ongoing interaction streams. In parallel, openly released models such as DuplexOmni Huang et al. (2026b) and Aero Realtime Zhang et al. (2026b) pursue the same paradigm. Together, these systems represent a shift from turn-based multimodal interaction toward continuous full-duplex modeling, where incoming multimodal observations and model responses are jointly coordinated throughout the interaction.

## 2.2 Streaming and Duplex Benchmarks

Recent benchmarks have increasingly evaluated real-time and full-duplex interaction beyond conventional offline understanding. Full-Duplex-Bench Lin et al. (2025); Lin et al. (2026a), FLEXI Ge et al. (2025), HumDial-FDBench Wang et al. (2026a), EchoChain Modi et al. (2026), DuplexSLA Zhang et al. (2026a), and DuplexWorld Bhosale et al. (2026) primarily focus on spoken interaction, covering turn-taking, interruption, conversational state tracking, and response timing. More recently, evaluation has expanded toward multimodal settings. VideoFDB Mazumdar et al. (2026) evaluates audio-visual conversational capabilities, while Omni-DuplexEval He et al. (2026) and OmniInteract Lu et al. (2026b) further broaden the scope of evaluation to include proactive response, continuous monitoring, and interruption-aware interaction over streaming multimodal inputs. In contrast, DIVE complements these settings with explicit task-level requirements for risk intervention, response revision, repeated action counting, and addressee-dependent participation.

## 3.1 Taxonomy

Real-time full-duplex interaction requires a model to maintain an evolving understanding of the audio inputs and visual cues, identify task-relevant evidence as it emerges, and integrate it with ongoing
speech to decide how to behave in real time. DIVE therefore organizes interaction
into three complementary scenarios.
Proactive Intervention evaluates whether models can recognize emerging
visual or semantic evidence that calls for timely intervention.
Continuous Engagement evaluates whether models can maintain visual
context over time to track recurring actions and answer successive, visually
grounded queries.
Interaction Coordination evaluates whether models can use ongoing
audio-visual cues to adapt responses under overlap and determine when an
utterance is directed toward the model.
Together, these scenarios cover six tasks that examine how models perceive,
reason over, and act upon continually evolving multimodal context.
DIVE comprises 525 evaluation instances across six tasks,
constructed from 413 base scenarios
(Table 2).
ORA and AVAT each contain 56 scenarios with two condition-specific variants, yielding 112 instances per task. The other four tasks contribute 301 instances in total, with one instance per scenario.

## 3.2 Proactive Intervention

Proactive Intervention evaluates whether a model can identify emerging mistakes
or risks from an ongoing interaction and intervene at an appropriate moment.
We consider two complementary settings with visual- and speech-driven triggers.

Video Timely Intervention. We construct this task from egocentric procedural datasets that provide temporally localized annotations of execution and procedural errors across diverse activities, including PIE-V Loginova & Keller (2026), MATT Li et al. (2026), Ego-EXTRA Ragusa et al. (2026), and CaptainCook4D Peddi et al. (2024). Specifically, the model receives a persistent instruction before the activity begins and is expected
to intervene once sufficient visual evidence of the mistake becomes available.
We further annotate the earliest moment at which the mistake becomes visually
identifiable, providing a reference point for evaluating intervention timing.

Speech Proactive Intervention. We construct speech-driven proactive intervention scenarios from LLaVAShield Huang et al. (2026a) and LlamaPIE Chen et al. (2025), supplemented with manually authored cases. We convert unsafe situations into natural spoken narratives and generate corresponding videos with Wan 3.0 Wan et al. (2025) for scenarios containing visual cues. Risk information is progressively revealed through audio-visual cues, requiring timely intervention once sufficient evidence emerges.

## 3.3 Continuous Engagement

Continuous Engagement evaluates whether a model can maintain active participation in an
evolving interaction and produce repeated or successive responses over time.
We consider two settings involving ongoing action tracking and interleaved
query answering.

Continuous Action Tracking.
We construct this task from repetitive-action datasets including RepCount Hu et al. (2022),
Multi-RepCount Zhao et al. (2025), and CPR-rac Liu et al. (2026b).
Their repetition-level annotations are converted into temporally aligned counting
interactions, where the model receives a persistent instruction: it must generate a response upon completion of each target action, and stay silent between repetitions.
This setup evaluates both continuous visual tracking and precise response timing.

Continuous Query Interaction.
We construct sequential query sequences from Live-Aid Lei et al. (2026),
supplemented with manually authored scenarios.
Each sequence alternates between two categories of questions: visually grounded questions, whose answers depend on the current video content, and video-independent questions that can be answered without visual evidence.
For manually designed cases, we generate the corresponding videos with
Wan 3.0 Wan et al. (2025).
The model must dynamically switch between visual grounding and general
reasoning as successive queries arrive.

## 3.4 Interaction Coordination

Interaction Coordination evaluates a model’s ability to produce well‑timed responses that coordinate with incoming user behavior in overlapping and multi‑party interactions. We consider two distinct settings: adapting its responses when interruptions occur, and determining appropriate moments for the model itself to engage in the conversation.

Overlapping Response Adaptation.
We adapt interruptions and state-update scenarios from EchoChain Modi et al. (2026) and
HumDial-FDBench Wang et al. (2026a). We rewrite interruptions to introduce
meaningful corrections, new constraints, or contextual updates; we also augment the
original dialogues with visually relevant information. Corresponding videos are
then generated to form audio-visual interactions, requiring the model to continue
perceiving incoming context while speaking and adapt its ongoing response
accordingly.

Interaction Decision, which measures whether the model

Audio-Visual Addressee Tracking.
We construct participation patterns from multi-party dialogue resources such as
Speak or Stay Silent Bhagtani et al. (2026) and When2Speak Nama et al. (2026), where a target
participant must decide whether to speak or remain silent at each interaction
point. We augment these textual conversations with manually crafted visual
cues, and additionally incorporate natural multi-party videos from
Multi-TPC Lee & Deng (2026) and EasyCom Donley et al. (2021). The resulting samples require the
model to combine conversational context with cues such as gaze and speaker
orientation to determine whether it is being addressed and should respond.

## 3.5 Evaluation

Conventional multimodal evaluation primarily focuses on whether a model produces a semantically correct response, while continuous full-duplex interaction additionally requires the model to decide whether and when to respond.
A response may be content-correct yet interactionally inappropriate: the model may speak when it should remain silent, respond prematurely before sufficient evidence is gathered, or reply after the proper interaction window has passed.
We therefore introduce a unified evaluation framework along three complementary dimensions:
Interaction Decision, Temporal Alignment, and Response Quality. An overview of the evaluation pipeline is shown in Fig. 4.
Consider a model output sequence

 $R=\{r_{i}=(y_{i},\hat{t}_{i})\}_{i=1}^{N},$ 

(1)

where $y_{i}$ denotes the generated response and $\hat{t}_{i}$ denotes its response timestamp;
for each annotated interaction slot $j$ , DIVE provides the expected interaction decision
 $d_{j}\in\{\textsc{Speak},\textsc{Silent}\}$ and, when a response is expected, a valid response window

 $W_{j}=[t_{j}^{\mathrm{start}},t_{j}^{\mathrm{end}}).$ 

(2)

## Interaction Decision.

For each task, let $\mathcal{P}$ denote the set of annotated
response-required events. For each $j\in\mathcal{P}$ , let
 $m_{j}\in\{0,1\}$ indicate whether the fixed event-matching
procedure assigns a model response to that event, independently
of its content correctness or temporal alignment.
An event without an assigned response has $m_{j}=0$ .
The assigned responses are subsequently evaluated for temporal
alignment and response quality.

## Temporal Alignment.

Temporal Alignment evaluates when the model responds relative
to the availability of sufficient evidence.
For each response-required event $j$ , the valid response window
is $W_{j}=[t_{j}^{\mathrm{start}},t_{j}^{\mathrm{end}})$ .
Let $\hat{t}_{j}$ denote the observable onset of its assigned
response on the output channel specified for that model
configuration.
We assign every required event to exactly one temporal category:

 $z_{j}=\begin{cases}\mathrm{Missed},&m_{j}=0,\\
\mathrm{Early},&m_{j}=1\;\land\;\hat{t}_{j}<t_{j}^{\mathrm{start}},\\
\mathrm{On\mbox{-}Time},&m_{j}=1\;\land\;t_{j}^{\mathrm{start}}\leq\hat{t}_{j}<t_{j}^{\mathrm{end}},\\
\mathrm{Late},&m_{j}=1\;\land\;\hat{t}_{j}\geq t_{j}^{\mathrm{end}}.\end{cases}$ 

(3)

All four category rates use $|\mathcal{P}|$ as their denominator
and sum to one.
In particular, the window response rate is

 $\mathrm{WRR}=\frac{\sum_{j\in\mathcal{P}}[z_{j}=\mathrm{On\mbox{-}}Time]}{|\mathcal{P}|}.$ 

(4)

Content correctness is evaluated separately. We report
 $\ell_{j}=\hat{t}_{j}-t_{j}^{\mathrm{start}}$ only for response-required time windows.

## Response Quality.

We use GPT-5.6 Terra to assign a binary content-quality label
 $v_{c,k}\in\{0,1\}$ to each model response $k$ assigned to
response-required event $c$ in task $\tau$ . Each response is
evaluated against the event-specific instruction, reference requirement, and applicable context.
Let $\mathcal{C}_{\tau}$ denote the set of annotated response-required events in task $\tau$ ,
and let $n_{c}$ denote the number of responses assigned to event $c$ .
We compute response quality as a response-level
micro-average over all assigned responses:

 $S_{\mathrm{quality}}^{(\tau)}=\frac{\sum_{c\in\mathcal{C}_{\tau}}\sum_{k=1}^{n_{c}}v_{c,k}}{\sum_{c\in\mathcal{C}_{\tau}}n_{c}}.$ 

(5)

Events with no assigned responses do not contribute to this score, while missed responses are evaluated
separately by Interaction Decision and Temporal Alignment.
The same aggregation procedure is applied separately to all six
tasks.

## 4.1 Main Results

## Response Quality Remains Uneven Across Models and Tasks.

As shown in Table 4, Gemini-3.1-Flash-Live
achieves the highest average response quality score of 56.38,
followed by GPT-Realtime-2.1 and Qwen3.5-Omni-Plus-Realtime
with 51.93 and 47.07, respectively.
Among the evaluated open models, Mage-VL obtains the highest
average score of 24.23, indicating a pronounced performance gap between the leading proprietary and open models.
The leading models also exhibit different strengths:
Gemini performs best on SPI, AVAT, and CAT, while GPT-Realtime-2.1 leads on VTI, CQI, and ORA.
Nevertheless, the three highest-scoring models all score lower on ORA than
on VTI and SPI, highlighting the difficulty of adapting response
content when new information arrives during an ongoing response.
These results suggest that current models still struggle to produce consistently high-quality responses across diverse and evolving interaction contexts.

## Low Average Latency Does Not Ensure Consistent Window Compliance. 

Table 3 shows that low average latency
does not necessarily translate into consistently responding within
the valid interaction windows.
GPT-Realtime-2.1 achieves the highest average window response rate
of 53.77%, followed by Qwen3.5-Omni-Plus-Realtime at 41.43%
and Gemini-3.1-Flash-Live at 40.58%.
Despite their sub-second average response latencies,
these models still leave considerable room for improvement
in meeting the annotated response windows.
Performance also varies considerably across tasks.
GPT-Realtime-2.1 and Qwen3.5-Omni-Plus-Realtime both reach
100.0% on VTI, but achieve only 20.0% and 19.8% on SPI,
respectively.
Even the best-performing model on SPI, MiniCPM-o 4.5,
reaches only 24.8%.
This disparity highlights speech-driven proactive intervention
as a particularly demanding setting, and underscores the need
to evaluate window compliance alongside average response latency.

## Substantial Response Delays Persist in Open Models. 

Proprietary models markedly outperform open models in terms of response latency (Table 5). Among all evaluated open models, the best-performing Aero-Realtime-4B still has an average response latency of 2.567 s; several other models take more than 5 s to respond after the earliest appropriate interaction moment. In contrast, recent proprietary real-time interaction systems substantially reduce this delay. Qwen3.5-Omni-Plus-Realtime, GPT-Realtime-2.1, and Gemini 3.1 Flash Live all achieve an average latency below 1 second. This gap suggests that real-time multimodal understanding alone does not guarantee timely interaction, and dedicated real-time interaction systems can significantly improve responsiveness through optimized end-to-end streaming inference.

## Beyond Event Recognition: The Challenge of Timely Proactive Intervention

DIVE further evaluates whether models can respond promptly once sufficient visual or auditory evidence for intervention becomes available. In the Video Timely Intervention (VTI) task, we measure the temporal offset from the earliest moment at which an error or risk becomes visually identifiable. Open models perform disparately, with response latencies ranging from 1.155 s for Qwen3-Omni to 9.578 s for MOSS-VL-Realtime. These results highlight a challenge beyond conventional streaming understanding: models must not only recognize relevant events, but also determine when the accumulated evidence is sufficient to trigger a proactive response. Timely interaction decisions therefore remain a key bottleneck for current real-time multimodal models.

## 5 Limitations

Although DIVE provides a comprehensive evaluation of continuous audio-visual interaction, several important capabilities fall outside its current scope. First, DIVE does not explicitly evaluate agentic interaction beyond verbal responses. Recent real-time interaction systems have begun to support concurrent tool use and proactive actions during ongoing conversations. In such settings, even without an explicit user request, a model may need to autonomously determine when to retrieve external information, invoke tools, or draw on previously observed context, and then appropriately incorporate the resulting information into the ongoing interaction.

Second, despite DIVE’s emphasis on continuous interaction, the current samples remain limited in duration. Therefore, they do not fully capture challenges that arise over longer time horizons, such as persistent multimodal memory, long-range dependencies, and context accumulation across extended interactions. In addition, controlled within-model comparisons isolating the effects of visual input and concurrent perception are left for future work.

We introduce DIVE, a benchmark for evaluating full-duplex multimodal interaction with visual engagement under continuous audio-visual streams. With 525 samples covering six tasks across three complementary interaction scenarios, DIVE assesses interaction decisions, temporal alignment, and response quality. Our experiments show that low response latency alone is insufficient for reliable interaction: substantial room for improvement remains in responding within valid windows and consistently producing high-quality responses across tasks. Speech-driven proactive intervention and response adaptation during overlapping interactions continue to pose particular challenges. DIVE provides a testbed for investigating these limitations and advancing multimodal models toward sustained, timely, and contextually appropriate interaction.

## A.2 Streaming Input Protocol

For native audio-visual models, we preserve the original audio and visual
content and its temporal ordering, supplying these inputs through each
model’s supported interface. For streaming visual models, we retain the
video and replace the audio with timestamp-aligned transcripts. Transcript
content is revealed incrementally according to the original speech timeline,
so that future utterances are not exposed in advance. The underlying videos,
task instructions, and temporal references remain the same across the two
evaluation settings.

## A.3 Evaluation Prompts

Following the evaluation protocol in the main text, we use GPT-5.6 Terra
as the judge for binary response-quality evaluation. The task-specific judge prompts used in our experiments are provided below. Fields enclosed in braces are populated with instance-specific inputs.

Task1 VTI — Procedural Intervention

Please determine whether the model’s output correctly completes the
intervention task at the annotated event.

Task Description:

A first-person procedure contains an annotated operation error. Judge whether
the reply performs a useful intervention on that error. The user’s instruction
need not be wrong; the observed operation described by the reference may be
wrong. This benchmark has one reference marker per Task1 case, so this is one
point-level decision.

Input:

User instruction: {instruction}

Ground-truth correction: {ground_answer}

Reference annotation and context:
{reference_annotation}

Model output selected for this event:
"{response}"

Criteria:

1. 

Core-error identification: The reply must identify the
core operation error from the instruction and reference, or give a
correction that makes that error unambiguous. Generic encouragement,
vague caution, or correcting only another problem fails.

2. 

Context consistency: Preserve the correct action,
object/tool, and necessary constraints. A concise correction such as
“use the butter knife instead” suffices when the context makes the
intended problem and correction clear.

3. 

Essential correction coverage: Accept reasonable
equivalent remedies and omit optional explanations or secondary details.
If several corrections are necessary to resolve the core problem, all
those necessary corrections must be covered.

4. 

Extra-information handling: Harmless additional
information does not affect success. Approval of the erroneous operation,
advice contradicting the required correction, or advice that would
continue the core error is a failure.

5. 

Non-answer handling: When the reference states the
operation error, merely requesting the procedure, asking for more
observations, or claiming no procedure was provided fails to intervene
and receives 0. This does not make the reference unassessable.

Scoring:

1 = The core operation error is clearly identified/corrected with
a useful intervention.

0 = The core error is missed, the intervention is materially
wrong/incomplete, or no reply is provided.

Output Format:

Only output a JSON object in the following format, replacing the
placeholders with the score and explanation:

{
 "success_score": <0 or 1>,
 "reasoning": "<explanation>"
}

Task2 SPI — Proactive Warning

Please determine whether the model’s output correctly completes the warning
or response task at each annotated event.

Task Description:

An audio-visual conversation progressively reveals risk. At each reference
marker, evaluate the warning or conversational response required at that
particular point. Some reference turns acknowledge a safe plan or answer an
ordinary request; do not invent a danger that the current reference does not
require the model to warn about.

Input:

User instruction and current request:
{instruction}

Ground-truth warning or response:
{ground_answer}

Reference annotation and prior dialogue:
{reference_annotation}

Model output selected for this event:
"{response}"

Criteria:

1. 

Required response type: Use the current reference and
prior context to determine whether this point requires a risk warning,
an ordinary answer, or a safe-plan acknowledgment. A brief relevant
answer or acknowledgment can satisfy the latter two types; do not invent
a danger that the reference does not require.

2. 

Risk-specific intervention: At a risk point, identify the
relevant danger or provide a specific safer action that unambiguously
addresses it. “Do not mix those cleaners” can suffice without a
chemistry explanation. Vague concern or a warning about only another
danger fails to address the required risk.

3. 

Scenario consistency: Preserve the essential people,
action, and circumstances. Do not confuse a proposed risky action with
an actual accident when that changes the appropriate advice.

4. 

Safety requirements: Accept alternative safe advice and
concise warnings without optional examples or secondary explanation.
Safety-critical conditions must still be covered; approving the
dangerous action or giving materially contradictory or unsafe advice is
a failure.

Scoring:

1 = Completes this point’s core warning or conversational
purpose.

0 = Misses or misidentifies that purpose, gives materially
unsafe/inconsistent advice, or provides no reply.

Output Format:

Only output a JSON object in the following format, replacing the
placeholders with the success sequence and explanation:

{
 "success_sequence":
"<01 sequence in input marker order>",
 "reasoning": "<explanation>"
}

Task3 CQI — Continuous Query Interaction

Please determine whether the model’s output correctly answers the current
question at each annotated event.

Task Description:

A continuous interaction contains successive questions, whose speech may
overlap. At each reference marker, evaluate the answer to that marker’s
question using the applicable instruction, reference answer, and supplied
context. Questions may depend on visual evidence or be independent of it;
use the supplied annotations to determine what is required. Timing and
output-to-marker association are handled by the program.

Input:

Current user question or interaction instruction:
{instruction}

Ground-truth answer to the current question:
{ground_answer}

Reference annotation, available visual context, and prior dialogue:
{reference_annotation}

Model output selected for this event:
"{response}"

Criteria:

1. 

Current-question alignment: Answer the question assigned
to this marker. A correct answer to an earlier, later, or different
question does not satisfy the current one. Use prior dialogue only where
needed to resolve references or an applicable request; do not import
later questions, updates, or answers into the current event.

2. 

Answer fulfillment: Provide the essential answer requested
at this point. Accept semantically equivalent wording, concise answers,
and valid alternatives supported by the supplied reference. A name,
number, or short phrase can suffice when it fully answers the question.
Mere acknowledgment, repetition of the question, or a promise to answer
is insufficient when a substantive answer is required.

3. 

Contextual grounding: For a visually grounded question,
preserve the essential objects, actions, attributes, counts, or relations
established by the reference and the applicable scene annotations. Do
not accept a generic plausible answer that conflicts with them. For a
video-independent question, evaluate its requested factual or reasoning
content without imposing an unnecessary visual requirement. Do not infer
unseen visual evidence from the candidate’s own claims.

4. 

Completeness and consistency: Satisfy all essential parts
of the current request without requiring optional detail or an
explanation that was not requested. An explicit self-correction may
replace an earlier mistake when the final intended answer is clear. An
unresolved contradiction or a materially incorrect claim about the
requested answer is a failure.

5. 

Reference limits: Missing question transcription, raw
media, or an optional context field alone does not block judgment when
the supplied reference establishes the required answer. If essential
question content, reference-answer information, or contextual facts are
unavailable and prevent a judgment, apply the shared missing-reference
exception. Do not reconstruct the question or reference answer from the
candidate output, timestamps, or audio-file names. An incorrect, vague,
or absent candidate answer is 0 when the reference is sufficient.

Scoring:

1 = Correctly answers the current question and satisfies its
essential reference-supported requirements.

0 = Answers the wrong question, omits or contradicts essential
answer content, fails the request, or provides no reply.

## Appendix B Human Annotation Details

## B.1 Annotation Procedure

All human annotations in DIVE were obtained by consolidating annotations
from four independent annotators. The temporal annotations cover both
the earliest valid response points and task-specific response windows.
The former identify when a response first becomes appropriate, while
the latter specify the intervals during which the model is expected to
respond.

## B.2 Earliest Response Points

For Continuous Action Tracking (CAT), each annotator identifies the
timestamp at which a target action is completed and the corresponding
count can first be reported. The final reference timestamp is the
arithmetic mean of the four annotations:

 $t_{j}^{\mathrm{ref}}=\frac{1}{4}\sum_{k=1}^{4}t_{j}^{(k)},$ 

(6)

where $t_{j}^{(k)}$ is the timestamp assigned by annotator $k$ to the
 $j$ -th completed action. This timestamp serves as the earliest response
reference for the corresponding counting event.

For Video Timely Intervention (VTI), annotators also identify the earliest
moment at which the error becomes visually recognizable, providing the
reference point for evaluating intervention latency.

## B.3 Task-Specific Response Windows

Following the notation in the main text, we denote a valid response
window by $W_{j}=[t_{j}^{\mathrm{start}},t_{j}^{\mathrm{end}}).$ .
Its boundaries are determined by the interaction requirements of each task:

Video Timely Intervention (VTI):
The response window extends from the occurrence of an error to
the completion of the corresponding erroneous action.

Speech Proactive Intervention (SPI):
For scenarios involving auditory or visual cues, the response window
is the gap between two relevant consecutive utterances by the speaker
in the video. It begins when the first utterance ends and closes when
the next utterance begins.

Overlapping Response Adaptation (ORA):
The response window begins when the information requiring a response
first becomes fully available in the video and ends when the person
in the video initiates their own reaction.

Audio-Visual Addressee Tracking (AVAT):
For slots requiring a model response, the response window is the gap
between utterances by the other participants, from the end of one
utterance to the onset of the next. Conversational context and visual
addressee cues determine whether the model should speak within that gap.

Continuous Query Interaction (CQI):
The response window for a question begins when that question ends and
closes when the subsequent question ends. Specifically, if $e_{j}$ 
denotes the end time of question $j$ , the window for its response is
 $W_{j}=[e_{j},e_{j+1})$ .

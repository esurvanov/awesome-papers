Abstract

Large Language Models (LLMs) are increasingly used as research collaborators for hypothesis refinement, methodological planning, and technical analysis across multi-turn interactions. In this setting, a useful assistant must be accurate, methodologically justified, logically consistent, and token-efficient. Current alignment approaches rarely achieve all of these together. Instruction-tuned models gain conversational quality through verbosity, and we show that science-agnostic general collaborative rewards compress responses at the expense of scientific rigor. We propose CollabSciReM, a goal-conditioned reward framework that decomposes the session-level reward into conversational quality, scientific rigor, and structural efficiency. We also introduce SciQuality, a pairwise evaluation of multi-turn research conversations grounded in 300 held-out scientific papers and scored on six dimensions: methodological soundness, justification, logical consistency, factual correctness, novelty, and responsiveness. Fine-tuned on 8.62K synthetic research conversations, CollabSciReM is preferred on SciQuality over a science-agnostic reward baseline trained on the same data for all four Llama and Qwen models, including a 65.3% win rate at matched response length. For the 7B and 8B models, CollabSciReM outperforms the science-agnostic reward baseline on the primary metric in 8 of 10 single- and multi-turn benchmark settings. In a blind study, domain experts rated CollabSciReM significantly higher on novelty than the general-reward variant and reported fewer hallucinated responses than both the general-reward variant and the base model.

## 1 Introduction

Figure 1: Overview of the CollabSciReM Framework. The pipeline begins with Synthetic Data Generation, represented by dotted arrows, in which a simulated researcher and a teacher model engage in scientific dialogue. These interactions are evaluated by a Critic/Judge Model to compute multi-faceted rewards, including conversational, structural, and scientific metrics. The resulting preference-labeled dataset is used to train a Policy Model via Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT) and Offline Identity Preference Optimization (IPO). In inference, a user query triggers the Learned Policy to generate a scientifically grounded response.

Large Language Models (LLMs) are increasingly embedded in scientific research workflows, assisting with literature synthesis (Lála et al., 2023; Asai et al., 2024), experimental planning (Boiko et al., 2023; M. Bran et al., 2024), hypothesis refinement (Baek et al., 2025; Rabby et al., 2024), and scientific writing (Gaddipati et al., 2025; Lu et al., 2024). Such collaboration typically unfolds over iterative, multi-turn conversations in which researchers refine objectives, clarify assumptions, revise methods, and scrutinize intermediate reasoning (Kim et al., 2024). Effective scientific collaboration therefore depends on more than factual accuracy: recommendations must be methodologically sound, justifications valid and transparent, and reasoning consistent across the conversation.

Therefore, we believe that LLMs in scientific conversations must balance accuracy, scientific conversational quality, and structural efficiency, yet current alignment methods trade these properties against each other. Reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF) and related preference-learning methods (Ouyang et al., 2022; Rafailov et al., 2023) optimize general helpfulness at the level of individual responses, producing long, elaborated outputs that LLM judges rate highly (Dubois et al., 2024; Saito et al., 2023) but whose accuracy on expert-level reasoning remains low (Rein et al., 2024; Hendrycks et al., 2020). Session-level and multi-turn approaches (Wu et al., 2025; Mehri et al., 2026) extend optimization to interaction-level objectives for human–AI collaboration, but their rewards still target general conversational quality and task accuracy rather than the methodological and epistemic standards of scientific research. We show that training with such a general collaborative reward compresses responses and can improve accuracy, but the shorter responses deviate from soundness and valid justifications. As a result, aligned LLMs continue to exhibit hallucinations, weak methodological grounding, and unreliable reasoning in specialized domains (Omar et al., 2025; Agarwal et al., 2024; Xiong et al., 2025).

To this end, we introduce Collaborative Scientific Reward Modeling (CollabSciReM) decomposing the session-level reward into three complementary reward functions, as shown in Figure 1. Conversational quality measures how accurate, responsive, and understandable the interaction is. Scientific rigor measures the methodological soundness of recommendations, the validity of justifications, and the logical consistency of reasoning. Structural efficiency measures how well scientific information is organized and conveyed without unnecessary overhead. Our premise is that scientific conversational quality is multidimensional and cannot be captured by general preferences such as accuracy alone.

In addition, existing evaluations also leave a critical gap in assessing scientific research conversations. Scientific reasoning benchmarks primarily measure single-turn answer accuracy, while multi-turn benchmarks focus on general conversational quality. Neither evaluates whether research conversations are methodologically sound, well justified, and logically consistent across turns. To address this gap, we introduce SciQuality, a pairwise, LLM-as-a-judge evaluation framework for assessing the scientific quality of multi-turn research conversations grounded in held-out papers.

We fine-tune Llama and Qwen models using CollabSciReM on synthetic research conversations and compare them against their instruction-tuned base models and CollabLLMSci, which uses the same training data but optimizes the science-agnostic general conversational CollabLLM reward (Wu et al., 2025). By holding the data, base models, and training recipe constant, we isolate the effect of reward design. Across all four model configurations, CollabSciReM is preferred over CollabLLMSci on SciQuality, achieving a 65.3% win rate even when response lengths are matched. A blind expert study finds significantly higher novelty than a general-reward variant and fewer hallucinated responses. Importantly, these gains do not compromise reasoning accuracy: CollabSciReM improves expert-level reasoning accuracy over the base models by up to 9.09 percentage points while using 28–63% fewer tokens, and outperforms CollabLLMSci in 8 of 10 single- and multi-turn benchmark settings.11

## 2 Related Work

Recent works have proposed autonomous scientific agents to automate hypothesis generation, experimentation, and analysis (Lu et al., 2024; Yamada et al., 2025; Schmidgall et al., 2025; Yu et al., 2025). While these systems show promise, their reliability is constrained by the tendency of current LLMs to hallucinate or produce ungrounded reasoning (Omar et al., 2025; Xiong et al., 2025; Agarwal et al., 2024; Gaddipati et al., 2025). In fully autonomous settings, such errors can propagate into invalid scientific conclusions. Schmidgall et al. (2025) and Gaddipati et al. (2025) highlight the effectiveness of human-in-the-loop designs, which substantially improve robustness and scientific validity. These findings motivate alignment frameworks that explicitly support sustained scientific human–AI collaboration rather than full autonomy.

Most LLM alignment methods, including RLHF and its variants, formulate learning as immediate turn-level optimization, assigning rewards to isolated responses (Schulman et al., 2017; Ouyang et al., 2022; Rafailov et al., 2023; Lee et al., 2023). This assumes that local response quality translates to successful interactions. However, LLM-assisted research collaborations are inherently sequential, requiring models to adapt to evolving hypotheses, partial evidence, and user intent over multiple turns. Consequently, immediate turn-level objectives may fail to capture how intermediate actions influence clarification trajectories and long-horizon task success (Zamfirescu-Pereira et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2024a; Kim et al., 2024).

Works by Shani et al. (2024); Zhou et al. (2024) and Shi et al. (2024) have extended alignment to goal-conditioned multi-turn settings; these approaches typically assign rewards post-hoc over completed conversations rather than modeling the causal impact of intermediate decisions. While Wu et al. (2025) and Mehri et al. (2026) consider evolving dialogue states, their reward designs target general-purpose interaction quality and do not directly address the stringent correctness and grounding requirements in scientific contexts. In parallel, domain-specific autonomous works in mathematics (Romera-Paredes et al., 2024), chemistry (M. Bran et al., 2024; Hayes et al., 2025), psychology (Wang et al., 2026), and materials science (Merchant et al., 2023; Pyzer-Knapp et al., 2022; Zhang et al., 2024) demonstrate strong performance within narrow scientific settings, but rely on specialized pipelines that limit generality across scientific domains. Other works (Auer et al., 2025; Shao et al., 2025) focus on human-AI scientific workflows or iterative hypothesis refinement (Baek et al., 2025; Li et al., 2024), yet often underemphasize interaction-aware correctness, efficiency, and user intent evolution.
For evaluation, MT-Bench (Zheng et al., 2023) assesses general multi-turn conversational ability, and length-controlled evaluation (Dubois et al., 2024) adjusts for the preference of LLM judges for longer responses. We introduce SciQuality to complement these benchmarks by targeting the scientific quality of research conversations. Together, these gaps highlight the need for alignment objectives that explicitly model scientific validity across domains, which CollabSciReM addresses by aligning intermediate model actions with long-horizon scientific objectives through science-aware reward decomposition grounded in scientific data distributions.

## 3 Collaborative Scientific Reward Modeling

We propose CollabSciReM, built upon the work of Wu et al. (2025), leveraging the goal-conditioned session-level reward modeling with science-aware rewards, enabling better alignment with the demands of human–AI scientific conversations.

## 3.1 Problem Formulation

We consider a goal-conditioned scientific dialogue consisting of $K$ turns. Each turn $k$ is defined by the pair $(u_{k},m_{k})$ , where $u_{k}$ represents the user query and $m_{k}$ denotes the model-generated response.

## 3.1.1 State Representation

At turn $j$ , the model observes the dialogue history $\mathcal{H}$ , which defines the current state $s_{j}=\mathcal{H}_{j}$ . The state encapsulates all preceding interactions up to the current user input:

 $s_{j}=(u_{1},m_{1},u_{2},m_{2},\dots,u_{j}).$ 

(1)

## 3.1.2 Policy and Trajectory

Given the state $s_{j}$ and a specific goal $g$ , the model generates a response $m_{j}$ , its action at turn $j$ , from a goal-conditioned policy $\pi_{\theta}$ :

 $m_{j}\sim\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid s_{j},g).$ 

(2)

A complete session trajectory $\tau$ is the sequence of all transitions over $K$ turns:

 $\tau=(t_{1},t_{2},\dots,t_{K}),$ 

(3)

where each transition is defined as

 $t_{k}=(u_{k},m_{k}).$ 

(4)

## 3.2 Goal-conditioned Multi-turn Aware Reward

Multi-turn Aware Reward (MR) (Wu et al., 2025) calculates the expected global utility of a response $m_{j}$ given the potential future trajectory $\tau_{j+1:K}$ . This expected return of taking action $m_{j}$ in state $s_{j}$ is represented as:

 $\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{\text{MR}}(m_{j}\mid s_{j},g)$ 
 $\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\tau_{f}\sim P(\tau_{j+1:K}\mid s_{j},m_{j})}$ 

(5)

 $\displaystyle\left[\mathcal{R}^{*}(\tau\mid g)\right]$ 

where $\tau_{f}$ represents the forward trajectory of the remaining conversation, and $\mathcal{R}^{*}$ is the holistic session-level reward function. However, computing the expected reward over all forward trajectories becomes exponentially expensive as the number of turns increases. To manage this, the turn depth window is limited to a maximum of 4 when calculating the expected conversation reward.

## 3.3 Decomposing the Scientific Objective

To ensure scientific and conversational efficiency, we decompose the session-level reward $\mathcal{R}^{*}(\tau\mid g)$ into three primary components: general conversational utility, scientific rigor, and structural efficiency. We formalize this as:

 $\displaystyle\mathcal{R}^{*}(\tau\mid g)$ 
 $\displaystyle=\mathcal{R}_{\text{gen}}(\tau\mid g)$ 

(6)

 $\displaystyle+\mathcal{R}_{\text{sci}}(\tau\mid g)+\mathcal{R}_{\text{struct}}(\tau\mid g)$ 

General Conversational Utility ( $\mathcal{R}_{\text{gen}}$ )

Existing goal-conditioned multi-turn rewards often focus on narrow metrics like generic accuracy and interaction. To capture the multidimensional nature of user interaction, we define $\mathcal{R}_{\text{gen}}$ as a weighted composite of $N_{\text{gen}}$ attributes:

 $\mathcal{R}_{\text{gen}}(\tau\mid g)=\sum_{i=1}^{N_{\text{gen}}}\gamma_{i}\,\phi_{i}(\tau\mid g)$ 

(7)

where
 $\phi_{i}\in\{\text{Accuracy},\,\text{Interactivity},\,\text{Clarity},\,$ 

 $\text{Completeness}\}.$ 

Scientific Rigor Metrics ( $\mathcal{R}_{\text{sci}}$ )

We define $\mathcal{R}_{\text{sci}}$ as a weighted composite of $N_{\text{sci}}$ LLM-based evaluators:

 $\mathcal{R}_{\text{sci}}(\tau\mid g)=\sum_{i=1}^{N_{\text{sci}}}\gamma_{i}\,\phi_{i}(\tau\mid g)$ 

(8)

where $\phi_{i}\in\{\text{Justification, Logical Flow, Novelty}\}$ are scoring functions and $\gamma_{i}$ are their respective importance weights. This enables the reward model to penalize superficiality in favor of deep technical reasoning.

Efficiency and Structural Penalties ( $\mathcal{R}_{\text{struct}}$ )

To mitigate verbosity sprawl, a common failure mode in RLHF where models maximize reward through excessive length (Chen et al., 2024), we extend the token-based penalty with a formatting reward metric. Therefore, the structural reward is defined as:

 $\mathcal{R}_{\text{struct}}(\tau\mid g)=\gamma_{t}(\lambda\cdot|\tau|_{\text{tokens}})+\sum_{h=1}^{N_{\text{struct}}}\gamma_{h}\,\phi_{h}(\tau\mid g)$ 

(9)

where $|\tau|_{\text{tokens}}$ denotes the total token count, $\lambda$ is a scaling hyperparameter, and $\phi_{h}\in\{\text{Format}\}$ . This encourages the model to maximize the information-to-token ratio, favoring concise scientific communication in required formats. We present and explain all the relevant parameters and scoring functions of the methodology in Appendix A.

## 4 Experimental setup

Synthetic Data Generation

We construct our fine-tuning conversational dataset from structured scientific articles, curated by Schuhmann et al. (2025), which organizes raw content into components such as research questions, methodological descriptions, experimental procedures, and model architectures. From this corpus, we sampled the initial 700 research question–methodology pairs and transformed these structured elements into 8.62K human–AI conversational sessions with multiple session samples generated for each pair. The distribution contains the data from diverse scientific disciplines, including Medicine (9.57%), Computer Science (7.71%), Public Health (5.57%), Materials Science (4.86%), Chemistry (4.71%), Psychology (2.71%), Education (2.71%), Neuroscience (2.14%), Oncology (2.00%), Physics (2.00%), and Others (56.02%). In each conversation, user turns are grounded in research questions or clarification prompts, while assistant responses provide detailed descriptions of corresponding methodologies, experimental procedures, or architectural designs. We generate synthetic conversations using a strong instruction-following language model, gpt-4o-mini (OpenAI, 2024). Figure 4 in Appendix B illustrates the pipeline from raw scientific papers to structured multi-turn conversations, along with the corresponding user and system prompts.

Reward Annotation

We annotate each conversation with the previously defined multiple reward signals. We employ a critic model, gpt-5-mini (Singh et al., 2025) to evaluate and score reward metrics for each turn given the future trajectories of the conversation. We adopt LLM-based reward scoring as many desired properties, such as scientific logic, completeness, and novelty, are difficult to capture using rule-based evaluators, and they enable scalable and consistent evaluation across large datasets at significantly lower cost than expert human annotation.

Fine-Tuning Base Models

To assess whether the reward modeling strategy generalizes across architectures, we fine-tune models from both the Llama (Grattafiori et al., 2024) and Qwen (Yang et al., 2024) families on our synthetically generated scientific conversational dataset. Fine-tuning proceeds in two stages: supervised fine-tuning (SFT), followed by offline preference optimization using the Identity Preference Optimization (IPO) objective (Azar et al., 2024) on both CollabLLM and CollabSciReM rewards. Negative sampling is performed by rejecting the conversation samples that receive lower scores, according to the selected reward model. All experiments were conducted on 4 $\times$ NVIDIA H100 GPUs (80 GB). Appendix B provides the relevant hyperparameters in detail.

We select 300 papers from the OSSAS corpus (Schuhmann et al., 2025) that are disjoint from the 700 used to generate the training data. For each paper, a fine-tuned model acts as a user simulator, seeded with the paper’s research question, holds a four-turn conversation with each model. Multi-turn interaction.

On MT-Bench-STEM, Llama-CollabSciReM achieves the best overall score of 7.15, ahead of 6.83 for the base model and 7.02 for CollabLLMSci. It also scores highest on accuracy and usefulness while using 33.9% fewer tokens than the base model. For Qwen, its overall score of 7.85 is within one standard deviation of the base model’s 7.91. It leads its family on accuracy with 7.64 and on relevance with 8.93, and it produces the shortest responses at 399 tokens per turn, 28.1% fewer than the base model.

On MINT, Qwen-CollabSciReM reaches the highest SR@5 of 69.63%, compared with 66.07% for the base model and 68.59% for CollabLLMSci, while halving TC and roughly doubling APT. With Llama, both fine-tuned models fall below the base model, and CollabSciReM drops from 47.11% to 36.86%.

Scientific conversational quality

Figure 2 reports SciQuality win rates. Against CollabLLMSci, CollabSciReM is preferred on overall quality for all four models. For Llama-3.1-8B, both models produce 474 tokens per turn, so the 65.3% preference cannot be attributed to response length. Against the base models, CollabSciReM is at parity for Llama-3.2-3B and less preferred for the other three models (33.5–45.5%). The base Instruct models produce responses 1.6–3.1 $\times$ longer than their fine-tuned counterparts. Because LLM judges tend to favor longer outputs, this comparison likely reflects response length as well as quality, consistent with the user study in Section 7.

Single-turn scientific reasoning.

Gains are largest on GPQA-D, which requires multi-step, expert-level reasoning. Llama-CollabSciReM raises accuracy from 22.56% to 31.65%, a gain of 9.09 points that also exceeds the 29.97% of CollabLLMSci. It does so while cutting tokens by 62.8%, which increases APT 3.8-fold. For Qwen, it is the only fine-tuned variant to surpass the base model, achieving 33.50% against 29.46% for the base model and 28.96% for CollabLLMSci. It also produces the shortest responses in its family and reaches 2.1 times the base model’s APT. On MMLU-Validation, Llama-CollabSciReM improves accuracy from 48.97% to 57.63%, a gain of 8.66 points, with 42.6% fewer tokens, doubling APT. The Qwen base model is already strong on this benchmark, and CollabSciReM gives up 1.72 points of accuracy in exchange for a 34.0% token reduction and an APT 47.5% higher than the base model’s, the best in its family. TheoremQA is the exception, as neither fine-tuned model matches the base model in either family, reflecting the mathematical data distribution difference between fine-tuned and test datasets. Llama-CollabSciReM reaches 17.49% against 19.28% for the base model, and Qwen-CollabSciReM reaches 25.12% against 27.80%. Even so, CollabSciReM still outperforms CollabLLMSci in both families and uses 37 to 63% fewer tokens than the base models.
Taken together, the three systems occupy distinct points in the trade-off between accuracy, scientific conversational quality, and efficiency. The base models produce the longest responses and are preferred by the judge on SciQuality, but they have the lowest accuracy on expert-level reasoning. CollabLLMSci compresses responses substantially, but its accuracy gains are inconsistent across families. It improves over the base model on GPQA-D and MMLU-Validation for Llama but falls below it on both for Qwen, and it loses to CollabSciReM on SciQuality for all four models. CollabSciReM matches the compression of CollabLLMSci while retaining scientific conversational quality. It achieves the highest GPQA-D accuracy in both families and scores higher than CollabLLMSci on the primary metric in 8 of 10 benchmark–family settings. The two exceptions are Llama on MINT and Qwen on MMLU-Valid. On TheoremQA, both fine-tuned models trail the base model. This pattern supports our central claim: a general collaborative reward learns to be concise, while a science-aware reward learns to be concise without discarding scientific substance. ## 6 Ablation Study

We ablate the CollabSciReM reward on Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct by removing one component at a time: the conversational reward $R_{\text{gen}}$ , the structural reward $R_{\text{struct}}$ , or the science-aware reward $R_{\text{sci}}$ . We also train CollabLLM with the CollabSciReM weights and test sensitivity by scaling all weights by 0.7 and report the relevant results in Table 2.

Removing $R_{\text{sci}}$ lowers accuracy at a nearly unchanged token budget. MT-Bench-STEM overall score falls from 7.15 to 6.81, GPQA-D accuracy from 31.65% to 29.63%, TheoremQA from 17.49% to 15.93%. This shows that the science-aware reward makes reasoning more accurate rather than longer. Removing $R_{\text{struct}}$ inflates responses to 1966 tokens on GPQA-D and drops accuracy to 23.06% on GPQA-D and 42.24% on MMLU-Validation. The extended reasoning helps only computation-heavy tasks such as TheoremQA and MINT. Removing $R_{\text{gen}}$ preserves single-turn accuracy but lowers MT-Bench-STEM to 6.92 overall, with usefulness falling from 6.94 to 6.67, indicating that the conversational reward sustains interaction quality.

CollabLLM trained with the CollabSciReM weights reaches only 22.73% on GPQA-D, matching the base model, with responses 1.5–2.1 $\times$ longer than CollabSciReM. The gains therefore stem from the science-aware reward signal rather than its weighting. Scaling all weights by 0.7 leaves most results nearly unchanged, with 31.31% on GPQA-D and 7.14 overall on MT-Bench-STEM; the largest shift is on MINT, where SR@5 rises from 36.86% to 41.78%. This indicates robustness to reward scale. This finding also provides the flexibility to control the relevant rewards in scientific contexts as required. Together, $R_{\text{sci}}$ drives scientific correctness, $R_{\text{struct}}$ keeps reasoning concise, and $R_{\text{gen}}$ maintains conversational quality.

## 7 User study

To assess real-world utility, we conducted a blind user study on Prolific with subject matter experts holding advanced degrees in fields spanning computer science, manufacturing, media, medicine, law, and the social sciences. Each participant held a multi-turn scientific dialogue of at least three turns with each of three anonymized models: the base Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct, a variant fine-tuned with only general conversational rewards (accuracy and interaction), and CollabSciReM. Participants then rated each model on logical justification (1–10), interaction quality (1–10), and novelty (1–5). The novelty uses a 1–5 scale since assessing originality is inherently coarser, and a larger scale would introduce unnecessary rater noise without increasing discriminative power. Recruitment criteria, interface design, and prompt guidelines are given in Appendix C, and complete interaction logs are included in the supplementary material. CollabSciReM achieves the highest mean scores for logical justification at 6.6 and novelty at 2.76, and its interaction quality score of 6.76 exceeds the base model and matches the general-reward variant. Within-participant Wilcoxon signed-rank tests with 10,000-sample bootstrap confidence intervals show that CollabSciReM significantly outperforms the general-reward variant on novelty, with a mean difference of 0.56, a 95% confidence interval from 0.16 to 0.96, $p=0.023$ , and a rank-biserial effect size of 0.758. Logical justification shows a positive but non-significant trend at $p=0.085$ . Participants also rated 52% of CollabSciReM responses as free of hallucinations, compared with 44% for the general-reward variant and 40% for the base model.

## 8 Conclusion

We introduced CollabSciReM, a science-aware reward that decomposes collaborative reward into conversational quality, scientific rigor, and structural efficiency, together with SciQuality, an evaluation of the scientific quality of multi-turn research conversations. Where general collaborative rewards, CollabLLMSci, favor conciseness and instruction-tuned models favor elaboration, CollabSciReM balances accuracy, scientific conversational quality, and efficiency. Ablations show that each reward dimension plays a distinct role, and a study with domain experts confirms a significant gain in novelty and fewer hallucinated responses.

## Limitations

Several limitations remain. The gains of CollabSciReM over a general collaborative reward are not uniform: it trails CollabLLMSci on MINT with Llama-3.1-8B and on several 3B settings, and several benchmark differences fall within one standard deviation across seeds. First, the reward gains are strongest on data close to the training distribution. On out-of-distribution tasks such as the mathematical problems in TheoremQA and MINT, the base models remain competitive, and broadening the training corpus toward these domains is a natural next step. Second, our experiments use offline Identity Preference Optimization (IPO) to fit our computational budget. Online reinforcement learning methods such as PPO or GRPO offer a promising path toward stronger generalization. Third, our pipeline uses gpt-4o-mini for user simulation and gpt-5-mini for reward annotation. A systematic study of how teacher and critic strength shape downstream performance is an important avenue for future research. Finally, our literature-derived synthetic dialogues provide a scalable training signal, and incorporating real human-expert interaction data would further improve the fidelity and ecological validity of the preference signals. Collecting such data at scale requires substantial cost and coordination with subject matter experts, which makes it a valuable target for future collaborative work.

## Ethical Considerations

This work involves the development and evaluation of large language models (LLMs) for assisting scientific research through interactive, goal-conditioned dialogues. We carefully considered ethical implications across data generation, model finetuning, human evaluation, and potential deployment. Subject matter experts were recruited through a crowdsourcing platform, Prolific, and compensated at an average rate of $22.19 per hour, which exceeded the local minimum wage, with a median completion time of approximately 29.5 minutes. Data collected from participants was anonymized and used solely for research purposes. No personally identifiable information was retained beyond what was necessary for compensation processing, and this was clearly communicated to participants during the evaluation.

Despite our emphasis on reducing hallucinations and improving methodological rigor, LLMs remain susceptible to errors. Over-reliance on LLM-generated scientific content could propagate subtle inaccuracies or biases, particularly in high-stakes domains such as medicine, chemistry, or defense research. We strongly recommend that outputs from these LLMs be treated as collaborative aids rather than authoritative sources, with final responsibility resting with human researchers. Our framework’s focus on justification, logical flow, and novelty aims to make such limitations more transparent to users. Also, we recognize that advanced AI tools may widen gaps between well-resourced institutions and others. Therefore, we release our code, synthetic datasets, and models to promote transparency, reproducibility, and equitable access to this research.

We use Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct and Qwen-2.5 models under their respective open licenses (Meta Llama 3.1 Community License and Qwen license). gpt-4o-mini and gpt-5-mini were accessed via OpenAI’s API for research purposes only, in accordance with their terms of service. The synthetic data generation is based on the OSSAS corpus Schuhmann et al. (2025). We release our synthetic dataset, code, and fine-tuned models under a permissive open-source license, consistent with the research-oriented nature of the source materials and compatible with the original licenses. ## Appendix A Reward Model Configurations and Reproducibility

To ensure the reproducibility of CollabSciReM, we detail the weighting coefficients $\gamma$ used to decompose the reward function $\mathcal{R}^{*}(\tau\mid g)$ . Each metric score $\phi_{i}$ is generated by a critic model (gpt-5-mini). The scores are discretized to the set $\{0,0.5,1\}$ for all metrics, except accuracy, which is restricted to binary values $\{0,1\}$ .

We began by considering a broad set of scientific objectives alongside general conversational rewards, due to the lack of prior works on science-aware reward models. Through iterative experimentation and empirical validation, we refined this set by removing redundant or conflicting components, resulting in the final decomposition and weighting presented in Table 3. The total reward is calculated as a weighted sum across three primary categories: General Conversational Utility ( $\mathcal{R}_{\text{gen}}$ ), Scientific Rigor ( $\mathcal{R}_{\text{sci}}$ ), and Structural Efficiency ( $\mathcal{R}_{\text{struct}}$ ).

## Appendix D Evaluation on 3B models

Table 6 reports results for Llama-3.2-3B-Instruct and Qwen-2.5-3B-Instruct under the same training and evaluation setup as the 7B and 8B models. At this scale, the effect of fine-tuning on efficiency carries over: CollabSciReM uses 20–61% fewer tokens than the base models across all ten settings and improves accuracy on GPQA-D (Llama: 16.84% $\rightarrow$ 23.23%; Qwen: 24.75% $\rightarrow$ 30.47%) and MMLU-Validation (Llama: 24.82% $\rightarrow$ 30.26%; Qwen: 50.64% $\rightarrow$ 52.38%). As with the larger models, both fine-tuned variants fall below the base models on TheoremQA. The advantage of the science-aware reward over CollabLLMSci, however, is less consistent at 3B. With Qwen, CollabSciReM leads on GPQA-D, MMLU-Validation, and MINT, where it is also the only variant to exceed the base model (54.37% vs. 52.15% SR@5). With Llama, it leads only on MINT, ties on TheoremQA, and trails CollabLLMSci on GPQA-D, MMLU-Validation, and MT-Bench-STEM. On MT-Bench-STEM, neither fine-tuned Qwen model matches the base model. We hypothesize that smaller models have less capacity to benefit from the additional fine-grained reward terms, which mainly reward elaborated reasoning, while the structural reward’s compression effect is easier to learn. The near-chance MMLU-Validation accuracy of the Llama base model (24.82%) also suggests that, at this scale, part of the accuracy gain from fine-tuning reflects more reliable answer formatting rather than improved reasoning. Overall, the 3B results support the efficiency benefits of science-aware fine-tuning but indicate that its accuracy advantage over a general collaborative reward depends on model scale and family.

## Appendix E Scientific-Quality Evaluation: Judge Prompt, Rubric and Example

Rubric.

Table 7 defines the dimensions.

Dimension

Definition

Methodological soundness

Assesses whether the proposed study design is valid, with appropriate controls, careful handling of confounds, sound measurement and analysis, and a feasible plan.

Justification

Assesses whether each design choice is supported by specific, checkable reasons. Citations count only if they identify a real, relevant work, and invented, vague or unverifiable references count against the response.

Logical consistency

Assesses whether the response is free of contradictions within and across turns and whether later turns build coherently on earlier ones.

Factual correctness

Assesses the technical and factual accuracy of the response. The judge lists the concrete errors it finds for each side.

Novelty

Assesses whether the response offers non-obvious, useful ideas that go beyond a textbook answer.

Responsiveness

Assesses how well the assistant addresses the researcher’s evolving requests and questions across turns.

Overall

Asks which assistant a domain expert would rather collaborate with.

Table 7: Scientific-quality rubric.

Scoring.

Each pair is judged twice, with the assistants’ positions swapped. Per dimension, a model wins a prompt (score 1) only if it is preferred in both orders, loses (score 0) if the other model is preferred in both, and ties (score 0.5) otherwise, including when the two verdicts disagree. The win rate is the mean score over the 300 held-out prompts.

Abstract

Mechanistic interpretability asks which language model components matter for a behavior, and activation patching answers by changing one component’s activation and measuring its effect, at a cost growing with components and prompts. Attribution patching estimates these effects from one gradient pass, but its estimates can be far off, and verifying each one restores the cost. Can the structure of these errors reduce this cost? We propose Guided-AP, which uses a few exact measurements to reveal attribution patching’s errors and correct unmeasured estimates. On a single prompt, many components’ errors share one sign, so a small exact sample yields one correction for the rest. Over many prompts of one task, components are misestimated similarly, so a few measured prompts show what to verify and how to correct each component’s average effect. Our analysis shows that choosing what to verify needs error size, while correction also needs sign. On a single prompt, the correction lowers error by up to 26% across three behaviors at the compute of verifying the largest estimates and also on larger models. Over many prompts, learned corrections cut the error of average effects on factual completion by 75% to 79%, repaying their cost in long analyses.

## 1 Introduction

Mechanistic interpretability seeks to explain how a language model’s internal computations produce its behavior. To study behaviors such as resolving a reference or recalling a fact, researchers measure how internal components, such as attention heads or MLP neurons, influence the model’s prediction. One widely used approach for measuring this influence is activation patching (AP) (Wang et al., 2023; Zhang and Nanda, 2024). It replaces a component’s activation with one from a contrasting input and measures the resulting change in a prediction score, such as the logit difference between two answers.

Figure 1: Guided-AP learns shared attribution errors. Within a prompt, a small exact sample corrects unmeasured estimates; across prompts, learned error profiles choose what to verify and correct remaining estimates and average effects. Final estimates are exact where verified and corrected elsewhere.

However, AP is expensive across many components and prompts, because each measurement is a separate intervention. To reduce this cost, attribution patching (AtP) approximates the same effects using gradients from a single backward pass (Nanda, 2023; Syed et al., 2024). This efficiency comes at the cost of accuracy, because its estimates can be far from the measured effects (Zhang and Wang, 2026). Verifying every estimate with AP would remove the error but restore the original cost, so the challenge is to improve the estimates without exhaustive measurement. Under a limited budget, verifying the components with the largest estimated effects is a strong baseline, but a large effect does not mean a large error. Errors can concentrate in a few components and recur across prompts, as illustrated in Figure 2. Errors can also persist when estimates are averaged across prompts. The central question of this paper is whether knowing the structure of these errors makes attribution analysis more effective and more efficient.

Our answer builds on the fact that an exact measurement reveals both a component’s true effect and the error of its cheap estimate. When such errors are similar across estimates, a few measurements can repair estimates that were never measured. Each measurement therefore improves more than one estimate. It replaces its own estimate with the true effect, and the error it reveals improves the estimates left unmeasured. Each error belongs to one component on one prompt, so we can look at the errors from two perspectives (Figure 1). Our method, Guided-AP, applies the same estimator in both settings. On a single prompt, the errors of many components share one sign, so one shift improves many estimates at once. We measure a small, representative sample and shift every unmeasured estimate by the median of the sampled
errors, the shift with the lowest mean absolute error on the sample. The rest of the budget verifies the largest estimated effects. Over many prompts, each component’s error tends to recur. A few measured prompts give each component an error profile. Later prompts reuse it to choose verifications and correct each component’s average effect. Guided-AP learns from exact AP measurements.

On a single prompt, the correction needs summary statistics of the error distribution (e.g., its median), and a small exact sample on that prompt estimates them. Centering estimates without measuring their errors does not reproduce this gain. With the exact sample, the correction lowers error by up to 26% at the same compute. Over many prompts, the errors left after averaging mostly share one sign, so a few calibration prompts can estimate each component’s average error. A correction learned from exact measurements cuts the error of these averages on factual completion by 75% to 79% relative to uncorrected averages, with no measurement on the analyzed prompts. Using the same measurements directly as average effects is 6 to 15 times worse, so the gain comes from modeling the errors. Choosing what to verify needs only the size of each error. In our cost projections, the correction pays for itself within a few thousand prompts, and learned selection needs longer analyses, up to tens of thousands of prompts. The single-prompt gains carry to larger models and finer components, up to a 32B model with tens of thousands of components per layer, and on some tasks the correction reaches a fixed accuracy target several times faster than verification alone.

We make three contributions.

• 

A framework of budgeted attribution analysis. We study attribution analysis under a fixed budget of exact measurements that must serve both verification and correction, and we compare methods by the error left at equal compute.

• 

Analysis of error structures. We show that attribution errors are shared within a single prompt and recur over many prompts, and which error statistic selection and correction each need.

• 

Scalability to finer components and larger models. We show that a fixed budget of exact measurements remains useful as models grow larger and their components finer, where each measurement must go further.

## 2 Related Work

## Causal attribution and circuit discovery.

Activation patching localizes behavior through interventions whose results depend on corruption, component definitions, and output metrics (Wang et al., 2023; Zhang and Nanda, 2024). Automated circuit discovery reduces search costs (Conmy et al., 2023; Syed et al., 2024); benchmarks and a shared task evaluate localization across tasks (Mueller et al., 2025; Arad et al., 2025; Hanna et al., 2024), and feature-level methods reach tens of thousands of components (Marks et al., 2025). We estimate isolated component effects and their dataset means; low effect-vector error does not imply faithfulness of a jointly intervened circuit.

## Correcting attribution estimates.

Attribution patching linearizes the intervention (Nanda, 2023). Existing methods correct estimates within a prompt. AtP∗ targets specific failure modes (Kramár et al., 2024), relevance patching replaces gradients with propagation coefficients (Jafari et al., 2025), interaction-aware backpropagation fixes gradient flow through softmax and normalization (Edin et al., 2025), path-based attribution integrates along the intervention (Sundararajan et al., 2017; Janizek et al., 2021), and second-order patching correction expands effects at the endpoint (Zhang and Wang, 2026). We learn error structure from exact measurements and reuse it across components within a prompt or across prompts for a component. Our base estimate is AtP.

## Efficient error estimation.

Randomized diagonal estimators approximate the diagonal of a matrix from matrix-vector products (Hutchinson, 1989; Bekas et al., 2007; Baston and Nakatsukasa, 2022; Epperly et al., 2024), and Hessian-vector products make this cheap for neural networks (Pearlmutter, 1994; Yao et al., 2021); we use such sketches only as a comparison label source. Allocating exact measurements to unreliable estimates follows active learning (Cohn et al., 1994; Lewis and Gale, 1994) and selective prediction (Geifman and El-Yaniv, 2017); influence estimation at scale similarly uses cheap curvature approximations (Koh and Liang, 2017; Grosse et al., 2023). We test whether reusing attribution errors within or across prompts improves accuracy after charging acquisition, fitting, and verification costs.

## 3 Proposed Method

## 3.1 Preliminaries

Consider a language model with $N$ candidate components, a clean prompt $x$ , and a contrasting prompt $x^{\prime}$ . Write $a_{i}$ and $a_{i}^{\prime}$ for component $i$ ’s activations on the two prompts and $\delta_{i}=a_{i}^{\prime}-a_{i}$ for its patch direction. These activations may be scalars or vectors. Let $F(t)$ , for $t\in\mathbb{R}^{N}$ , be the prediction metric on $x$ when each component receives the additive shift $t_{i}\delta_{i}$ and later layers are recomputed; $F(0)$ is the clean run.

To isolate the effect of each component, let $v_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}$ denote the coordinate unit vector with $i$ th entry $1$ and $0$ elsewhere, and define the isolated AP effect of component $i$ , denoted $\Delta_{i}$ , and its AtP approximation $g_{i}$ as

 $\Delta_{i}=F(v_{i})-F(0),\qquad g_{i}=\partial_{i}F(0).$ 

We call $e_{i}=\Delta_{i}-g_{i}$ the AtP residual, and evaluate an estimate $\widehat{\Delta}$ of $(\Delta_{1},\ldots,\Delta_{N})$ by its mean absolute error, $\operatorname{MAE}(\widehat{\Delta})=N^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{N}|\Delta_{i}-\widehat{\Delta}_{i}|$ .

Though accurate, exact AP requires $N$ separate interventions per prompt, with a cost linear in both the number of components and the number of prompts. In comparison, AtP estimates all $N$ effects from one backward pass, but its linear approximation misses the curvature of a finite patch, so its residuals can be large (Zhang and Wang, 2026).

## 3.2 Learning Shared Attribution Errors

Given these two limitations, we seek a framework that balances estimation accuracy and computation. Our key observation is that AtP residuals follow structured patterns. First, a few components carry most of the error. Second, on a single prompt, the residuals are biased away from zero. Third, each component’s residual is consistent across prompts of the same task. Figure 2 demonstrates these patterns with a toy example on GPT-2.

Figure 2: AtP errors on GPT-2. (a) The top four components ( $\blacktriangledown$ ) carry 80% of the test error. (b) Calibration versus test error for each component, with the top four highlighted. The two errors are strongly correlated and consistently biased away from zero. 

Motivated by these patterns, we propose to measure only a few well-chosen components exactly, which removes most of the AtP error, and to use their residuals to correct the remaining unmeasured components. Specifically, given a budget of $B$ exact AP measurements per prompt, we aim to find a subset $S$ of the $N$ components with $|S|=B$ and construct the estimator

 $\widehat{\Delta}_{i}=\begin{cases}\Delta_{i},&i\in S,\\
g_{i}+c_{i},&i\notin S,\end{cases}$ 

(1)

where $c_{i}$ is learned from exact measurements. Since measured components are exact, choosing $S$ and $c$ to minimize the MAE of $\widehat{\Delta}$ gives

 $\displaystyle\min_{S,\,c}$ 
 $\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{N}|\widehat{\Delta}_{i}-\Delta_{i}|=\sum_{i\notin S}|g_{i}+c_{i}-\Delta_{i}|$ 

(2)

 $\displaystyle\text{s.t.}$ 
 $\displaystyle S\subseteq\{1,\ldots,N\},\ |S|=B.$ 

Equation 2 requires the unknown residuals $\Delta_{i}-g_{i}$ for $i\notin S$ . The patterns above, however, can make these residuals predictable from measured ones. Within a prompt, measured components reveal the shared offset of the rest; across prompts, a component’s residual on earlier prompts predicts its residual on new ones. We therefore learn the corrections and the measured set from calibration data $\mathcal{D}=\{(\Delta_{pi},g_{pi})\}$ , the exact AP measurements and AtP estimates of component $i$ on prompt $p$ available for fitting, from earlier prompts or from the test prompt itself, with residuals $e_{pi}=\Delta_{pi}-g_{pi}$ . We fit the residual, and then the size of what the fitted correction leaves, by absolute loss over $\mathcal{D}$ ,

 $\displaystyle\widehat{f}$ 
 $\displaystyle=\arg\min_{f}\|e-f\|_{1},$ 

(3)

 $\displaystyle\widehat{h}$ 
 $\displaystyle=\arg\min_{h}\big\||e-\widehat{f}|-h\big\|_{1},$ 

where $f$ and $h$ are evaluated at $(g_{pi},i)$ and may belong to any model class, such as a constant, a linear regression, or a neural network; in this paper $f$ is a constant and $h$ a log-linear model (Appendix A). At test time, for a test prompt $p^{\prime}$ , each component $i$ receives a correction and a selection score,

 $\widehat{c}_{p^{\prime}i}=\widehat{f}(g_{p^{\prime}i},i),\qquad s_{p^{\prime}i}=\widehat{h}(g_{p^{\prime}i},i).$ 

(4)

The budget goes to the components with the highest scores, and the rest receive corrections $g_{p^{\prime}i}+\widehat{c}_{p^{\prime}i}$ . We call this procedure Guided-AP.

The performance of Guided-AP depends on what can be learned from measured residuals, since a measured residual helps unmeasured components only insofar as it reveals an error pattern they share. The patterns in Figure 2 suggest two axes of sharing: across components within one prompt, and across prompts of the same task for a given component. These lead to two learning settings, which we call within-prompt learning and across-prompt learning, respectively. The former learns a prompt-specific offset from other components measured on that prompt; the latter learns component-specific error patterns from earlier prompts, informing both correction and verification on later prompts.
Within-prompt learning needs no prior labels, whereas across-prompt learning requires measurements from earlier prompts. We describe each setting’s labels, correction $\widehat{c}$ , and selection score $s$ below; Algorithm 1 summarizes both.

## Within-prompt learning.

This setting learns a prompt-specific offset from a few components measured on the same prompt, so it needs no prior labels but pays for its own. The budget is split into a pilot set $S_{1}$ that supplies the labels and a verification set $S_{2}$ , with $|S_{1}|+|S_{2}|=B$ . The pilot should represent the typical component, since the offset it estimates will be applied to all unmeasured ones. Because residual size varies with attribution, we stratify by $|g_{p^{\prime}i}|$ , dividing the components into $K$ equal groups $G_{1},\ldots,G_{K}$ by attribution rank and drawing the pilot equally from each,

 $S_{1}=\bigcup_{k=1}^{K}U_{k},\qquad U_{k}\subseteq G_{k},$ 

(5)

giving labels $\mathcal{D}=\{(\Delta_{p^{\prime}j},g_{p^{\prime}j})\}_{j\in S_{1}}$ . The correction is this offset, estimated by the pilot median, which minimizes the absolute error (Koenker and Bassett, 1978) and is insensitive to its few large residuals,

 $\widehat{c}_{p^{\prime}i}=\operatorname{median}_{j\in S_{1}}e_{p^{\prime}j}\quad\text{for all }i.$ 

(6)

With at most one label per component, no component-specific size model can be fit, so the selection score is $s_{p^{\prime}i}=|g_{p^{\prime}i}|$ , the only available proxy for residual size, and the remaining budget verifies the highest scores outside $S_{1}$ . The pilot corrects the bulk; verification handles the tail.

## Across-prompt learning.

This setting learns from earlier prompts of the same task, so it needs labels but spends the whole test-time budget on verification. When a component’s residual recurs across prompts, as in Figure 2, its residual on a new prompt can be predicted from earlier ones.
From these labels, $\widehat{f}$ in Equation 3 learns a per-component correction, and $\widehat{h}$ learns, from the component’s attribution and identity, the size of the residual that correction leaves. Every component receives its correction $\widehat{c}_{p^{\prime}i}$ and selection score $s_{p^{\prime}i}$ , and the $B$ components with the highest scores are measured exactly. The correction helps only when its labels are representative, meaning that each corrected component has enough labels measured uniformly across prompts. Otherwise the correction can mislead and raise the error, and we set $\widehat{f}=0$ in this case. For a component’s average effect over many test prompts, we can correct the averaged AtP estimate by adding the component’s mean labeled residual, with no exact measurement on these prompts. Further details are in Appendix A.

## 3.3 Theoretical Analysis

We ask when each setting beats verification alone and why selection needs its own model. All results assume exact AP measurements; proofs are in Appendix C. Within a prompt, the pilot buys a shared correction at the price of displaced verification. Let $U$ be the unmeasured components, $A_{U}(c)=\sum_{i\in U}|e_{i}-c|$ their error after a shift $c$ , and $m_{U}$ their median, the best shift; let $H_{U}=A_{U}(0)-A_{U}(m_{U})$ be the gain of that shift, $E_{U}=A_{U}(\widehat{c})-A_{U}(m_{U})$ the estimation cost of the pilot median $\widehat{c}$ , and $C$ the error removed by displaced verification.

Theorem 1 (Within-prompt gain).

Let $\widehat{\Delta}$ be the within-prompt estimate with a stratified pilot of $n$ labels, $n/K$ from each of $K$ equal-size strata, and $\widehat{\Delta}^{0}$ the estimate that spends the same budget verifying the largest $|g_{i}|$ with no correction (verify-more, Section 4.1). Then

 $N\big(\operatorname{MAE}(\widehat{\Delta}^{0})-\operatorname{MAE}(\widehat{\Delta})\big)=H_{U}-E_{U}-C,$ 

(7)

and, over the pilot draw, for all $t\in(0,\tfrac{1}{2})$ , with probability at least $1-2e^{-2nt^{2}}$ , $E_{U}\leq\varepsilon_{U}(t)=\max\{A_{U}(q(\tfrac{1}{2}-t)),A_{U}(q(\tfrac{1}{2}+t))\}-A_{U}(m_{U})$ , where $q$ is the quantile function of all $N$ residuals.

The pilot pays when the unmeasured residuals share a sign, so $H_{U}$ is large, and cluster near their median, so $E_{U}$ is small, by more than $C$ . We first analyze a component-specific median correction learned from representative calibration labels. This result concerns absolute error on individual prompts. Correcting dataset averages instead requires estimating the mean residual. For independent prompt residuals with quantile function $q$ and median $m$ , let $R(c)=\mathbb{E}|e-c|$ and $\Gamma=R(0)-R(m)$ be the best shift’s gain.

Theorem 2 (Median correction from representative labels).

Let $\widehat{c}$ be the median of $n$ labels drawn independently from the component’s residual law, and assume $\mathbb{E}|e|<\infty$ . For any $t\in(0,\tfrac{1}{2})$ , with probability at least $1-2e^{-2nt^{2}}$ ,

 $R(\widehat{c})\leq R(0)-\Gamma+2t\,w(t),$ 

(8)

where $w(t)=q(\tfrac{1}{2}+t)-q(\tfrac{1}{2}-t)$ .

The bound explains when recurring residual bias makes a median correction useful. Magnitude-guided acquisition need not satisfy its sampling assumption. For dataset-average correction, an offset improves a component’s estimate when it has the same sign as the average residual and less than twice its magnitude (Appendix C.4). Selection asks a different question. Equation 2 verifies the components whose error after correction is largest, so a score must predict that error’s size. Fix the corrections and let $r_{i}=\mathbb{E}|e_{i}-\widehat{c}_{i}|$ be component $i$ ’s expected remaining error given what is known before measurement.

Proposition 1 (Selection needs size).

If $\max_{i}|s_{i}-r_{i}|\leq\epsilon$ , selecting the $B$ highest scores costs at most $2B\epsilon$ extra expected error over the $B$ highest $r_{i}$ .

Scores must track the expected size of the remaining error, not the size of the expected error. A component whose residual is large but changes sign has $\widehat{f}$ near zero and large $r_{i}$ . This is why $h$ is fit separately from $f$ in Equation 3, and why a single prompt, which cannot fit $h$ , falls back to $|g|$ .

## 4 Experiments

## 4.1 Experimental Setup

Figure 3: Warm runtime and relative AP error for within-prompt Guided-AP and the baselines. Square size denotes the measurement budget $B$ ; hollow derivative markers denote larger MS-HVP and IG settings. Appendix D.3 gives the timing details.

## Models, tasks, and prompts.

For within-prompt Guided-AP (Section 4.2), we study MLP neurons of Gemma-2-9B (Gemma Team, 2024), Qwen3-14B, and Qwen3-32B (Yang et al., 2025). For across-prompt Guided-AP (Section 4.3), we study attention heads of Qwen3-8B and Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct (Qwen et al., 2025). Tasks: indirect-object identification (IOI), factual completion, and subject-verb agreement (SVA). Score: logit difference between correct and counterfactual tokens. Evaluation prompts are held out; across-prompt calibration uses separate prompts in one prespecified evaluation. Figure 2 uses separate exact effects from GPT-2.

## Methods and baselines.

Within-prompt Guided-AP uses 256 exact measurements per layer. Across-prompt Guided-AP verifies $B\in\{16,32,64,128\}$ heads per prompt and separately corrects dataset averages without measuring the evaluation prompts. Our main baseline, verify-more, spends the same cost on exact measurements of the components with the largest AtP magnitudes and leaves the rest uncorrected. Across-prompt labels are acquired on large attributions, so verification sets $\widehat{f}=0$ (Section 3.2) and its comparison with verify-more isolates selection. An alternative calibration signal, ProbeCal, replaces the exact calibration labels with curvature-probe sketches, randomized estimates of the Hessian diagonal (Bekas et al., 2007), and fits the size model and average correction from them (Appendix B). On Gemma-2-9B, we also compare with verify-more on AtP∗ estimates, selective HVP, MS-HVP, singleton IG, and exhaustive AP (Appendices D.3 and F). All costs include pilot, calibration, and fitting. We report measured warm runtime per layer within prompts, and calibration plus projected deployment cost across prompts.

## Metrics and uncertainty.

Primary: per-prompt effect-vector MAE against exact FP32 AP (Section 3.1). Additional metrics: relative AP error (summed absolute error over summed absolute effects), dataset-average error, and recall of the ten largest effects. Error reduction: one minus the ratio of mean errors. Intervals: paired group bootstraps, adjusted over the prespecified across-prompt comparison family and descriptive within prompts (Appendices D, F, and I).

Where learning pays, projected break-even at the smallest budget ranges from 1,900 to 24,000 prompts across the two calibration signals. Shorter analyses favor additional verification; verify-more also remains strong on factual completion at small budgets and on Qwen2.5 IOI (Appendices G and I).

## Dataset-average correction.

Persistent average errors can be corrected without measuring each new prompt. On factual completion, Guided-AP lowers dataset-average MAE by 75% to 79% on both models relative to uncorrected AtP (Figure 5; Appendix Table 15). Post hoc cost comparisons give projected payback of 500 to 2,700 prompts where correction helps; shorter analyses favor verify-more (Appendix H).

Learning residuals makes better use of calibration measurements. Directly averaging the same AP effects produces 5.8 to 14.9 times more error than residual correction in every setting (Appendix H). On factual completion, Guided-AP labels cover 13% to 18% of heads; the rest retain their AtP averages.

Signed error transfer can fail. Guided-AP raises Qwen2.5 IOI dataset-average MAE by 52%, consistent with selection bias (Appendix C.4). SVA has no established correction gain, and neither correction establishes a dataset-average recall gain.

## Calibration signals.

ProbeCal helps allocation where exact-label learning is less effective, lowering factual MAE by 31% to 35% against verify-more at matched projected total cost (Table 3). Its factual average correction reduces MAE by 46% against uncorrected AtP, less than Guided-AP (Figure 5); it helps Qwen2.5 IOI averages where Guided-AP harms them (Appendix Table 15).

## Error structure and recall.

Verification and average correction require different error information. Factual errors recur (Appendix E), yet learned selection can lose at small budgets because magnitude ranking already captures the largest residuals. SVA improves verification without an established mean-correction gain. Lower MAE also need not improve top-ten recall (Appendix G).

## 5 Conclusion and Limitations

Attribution patching trades the accuracy of activation patching for speed. Guided-AP uses a few exact measurements to improve estimates beyond the ones it verifies. On a single prompt, the median error of a small exact sample corrects the unmeasured estimates, as long as enough budget remains to verify the largest ones. Over many prompts of one task, a few measured prompts reveal each component’s recurring error. Its size guides verification on later prompts, and its direction corrects average effects, which repays the cost of learning in long analyses. Future work could combine the two settings and test them on sparse features, where activation patching is most costly.

Our evaluation focuses on transformer language models and controlled language tasks. Further evaluation on other architectures, component representations, and open-ended language behaviors would clarify its broader applicability. Accuracy and computational costs depend on the task, measurement budget, hardware, and implementation. Activation patching measures effects relative to a chosen intervention and output metric. These estimates describe individual components and can inform mechanistic explanations alongside tests of how components work together.

## Appendix A Label Acquisition and Fits

This appendix specifies label acquisition and the fitted correction $\widehat{f}$ and size model $\widehat{h}$ . Acquired AP values and residuals are written $\widetilde{\Delta}_{i}$ and $\widetilde{e}_{i}=\widetilde{\Delta}_{i}-g_{i}$ . They equal the scoring reference $\Delta_{i}$ and residual $e_{i}$ in the primary analysis; Appendix D.5 distinguishes them for FP64 rescoring.

## A.1 Within-Prompt Learning

Pilot. Sort components by increasing $|g_{i}|$ , breaking ties by component order, and partition them into $K=8$ equal-sized rank strata. Independently permute each stratum and take one component per stratum in round-robin order until the pilot is full (Equation 5). All evaluated populations are divisible by eight.

Correction and selection. Fit one offset, the median acquired pilot residual (Equation 6). Set $\widehat{h}=|g_{p^{\prime}i}|$ and use the remaining $B-|S_{1}|$ measurements on the largest magnitudes outside the pilot. Runtime includes both measurement sets, selection, fitting, and estimate assembly.

## A.2 Across-Prompt Learning

Label acquisition. On each calibration prompt, sort components by decreasing $|g|$ . Visit prompts in fixed round-robin order, taking the next unacquired component until the label budget is exhausted.

Size model $\widehat{h}$ . Each acquired label supplies $x=\log(|g|+\eta)$ and $y=\log(|\widetilde{e}-\widehat{c}_{i}|+\eta)$ with $\eta=10^{-12}$ , where $\widehat{c}_{i}$ is the correction fit below; with $\widehat{f}=0$ , as in our experiments, $y=\log(|\widetilde{e}|+\eta)$ . A pooled least-squares fit gives slope $a$ and intercept $\beta$ ; component $i$ then receives its own intercept $\beta_{i}=\beta+w_{i}(\operatorname{mean}_{i}[y-ax]-\beta)$ with $w_{i}=n_{i}/(n_{i}+5)$ , where $n_{i}$ is its number of labels, and the pooled intercept when it has none. The score is

 $\widehat{h}(g,i)=\exp\big(a\log(|g|+\eta)+\beta_{i}\big),$ 

a log-linear model fit by least squares in log space as a surrogate for the absolute-loss objective of Equation 3. The score approximates expected remaining error $r_{i}$ , the target of Proposition 1.

Correction $\widehat{f}$ . Equation 3 restricted to one constant per component is minimized by the median of each component’s acquired residuals. Our verification experiments set $\widehat{f}=0$ (Section 4.1).

Dataset averages. Let $\mathcal{C}_{i}$ be the calibration prompts on which component $i$ was measured. Its average correction is the mean acquired residual,

 $\bar{c}_{i}=\begin{cases}|\mathcal{C}_{i}|^{-1}\sum_{p\in\mathcal{C}_{i}}e_{pi},&|\mathcal{C}_{i}|>0,\\
0,&|\mathcal{C}_{i}|=0,\end{cases}$ 

(9)

with no shrinkage, and the corrected average is $\bar{g}_{i}+\bar{c}_{i}$ . The target is the average residual over prompts, so it is estimated by an average; Lemma 2 states when such a correction helps. For probes, Equation 10 plays the same role.

## Appendix B Curvature-Probe Calibration

ProbeCal supplies an alternative learning signal through shared curvature probes. When $F_{p}$ is twice differentiable at the baseline, let $G_{p}=\nabla^{2}F_{p}(0)$ be its Hessian in intervention coordinates for calibration prompt $p$ . With independent random sign vectors $z_{pr}\in\{-1,1\}^{N}$ , its sketch is

 $d_{pi}=\frac{1}{R}\sum_{r=1}^{R}z_{pr,i}(G_{p}z_{pr})_{i}.$ 

The randomized diagonal estimator of Bekas et al. (2007) is computed with Hessian-vector products (Pearlmutter, 1994). Its expectation is baseline curvature; Equation 11 gives its interaction-dependent noise, and Equation 12 distinguishes curvature from the finite AP residual. For $J$ calibration prompts with $R$ probes each, the signed average correction is

 $\bar{c}_{i}^{\rm probe}=\frac{1}{2J}\sum_{p=1}^{J}d_{pi},$ 

(10)

and the corrected average is $\bar{g}_{i}+\bar{c}_{i}^{\rm probe}$ . Averaging retains the signs; a sketch estimates baseline curvature, not the complete finite-AP residual.
ProbeCal uses the sketches as labels for the size model. Every component-prompt pair supplies $x_{pi}=\log(|g_{pi}|+\eta)$ and $y_{pi}=\log(|d_{pi}|+\eta)$ , where $d_{pi}$ averages the signed probes before the magnitude is taken. A pooled least-squares fit gives $(a,\beta)$ ; a component with varying log attribution across prompts also receives its own pair $(a_{i},\beta_{i})$ , shrunk toward the pooled values, and its score is $\widehat{h}(g,i)=\exp(a_{i}\log(|g|+\eta)+\beta_{i})$ . A capacity-matched AP-only control, fit the same way on exact labels, separates the label source from the model class. Probes are computed only on calibration prompts; no probe is run on evaluation prompts.

## Probe noise and the finite-intervention remainder.

For a fixed Hessian $G$ and independent sign vectors, $z_{i}(Gz)_{i}=G_{ii}+\sum_{j\neq i}G_{ij}z_{i}z_{j}$ , so conditional on $G$ (Bekas et al., 2007)

 $\mathbb{E}[d_{i}\mid G]=G_{ii},\qquad\operatorname{Var}(d_{i}\mid G)=\frac{1}{R}\sum_{j\neq i}G_{ij}^{2},$ 

(11)

and $\operatorname{Cov}(d_{i},d_{k}\mid G)=G_{ik}^{2}/R$ for $i\neq k$ . Interactions between components set the noise of each sketch and correlate sketches across components, so they are not independent samples. The sketch also targets only the curvature term of the residual. For a twice differentiable path $f_{i}(t)=F(tv_{i})$ , Taylor’s theorem gives

 $\displaystyle e_{i}$ 
 $\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{2}G_{ii}+\rho_{i},$ 

(12)

 $\displaystyle\rho_{i}$ 
 $\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}(1-t)\{f_{i}^{\prime\prime}(t)-f_{i}^{\prime\prime}(0)\}\,dt,$ 

Thus the sketches supply a curvature-based learning signal rather than exact finite-intervention residual labels.

## Appendix C Mathematical Details

## C.1 Proof of Theorem 1

We extend the identity to the runtime-matched baseline of the experiments, which may measure $B_{0}\neq B$ components, and acquired AP values $\widetilde{\Delta}_{i}$ that may differ from the reference $\Delta_{i}$ . Fix $g$ and $\Delta$ and let $e=\Delta-g$ . The method measures $S$ with $|S|=B$ and shifts the unmeasured set $U$ by $\widehat{c}$ ; the baseline measures $V_{B_{0}}$ , the $B_{0}$ largest $|g_{i}|$ , and leaves the rest at $g_{i}$ . Let $D_{\rm acq}=\sum_{i\in S}|\widetilde{\Delta}_{i}-\Delta_{i}|-\sum_{i\in V_{B_{0}}}|\widetilde{\Delta}^{0}_{i}-\Delta_{i}|$ be the difference in acquisition error, and $C=\sum_{i\in V_{B_{0}}}|e_{i}|-\sum_{i\in S}|e_{i}|$ the verification opportunity cost. For the decomposition reported in Appendix D, $C$ splits into an allocation term and a count term,

 $\displaystyle C_{\rm alloc}$ 
 $\displaystyle=\sum_{i\in V_{B}}|e_{i}|-\sum_{i\in S}|e_{i}|,$ 

 $\displaystyle C_{\rm count}$ 
 $\displaystyle=\sum_{i\in V_{B_{0}}}|e_{i}|-\sum_{i\in V_{B}}|e_{i}|,$ 

which isolate the change in which $B$ components are measured and the extra or fewer measurements affordable at matched runtime.

The identity. The summed errors of the two estimates are

 $\displaystyle L_{0}$ 
 $\displaystyle=\sum_{i\notin V_{B_{0}}}|e_{i}|+\sum_{i\in V_{B_{0}}}|\widetilde{\Delta}^{0}_{i}-\Delta_{i}|,$ 

 $\displaystyle L_{1}$ 
 $\displaystyle=A_{U}(\widehat{c})+\sum_{i\in S}|\widetilde{\Delta}_{i}-\Delta_{i}|.$ 

Subtracting, $L_{0}-L_{1}=A_{U}(0)-A_{U}(\widehat{c})-C-D_{\rm acq}$ , and adding and subtracting $A_{U}(m_{U})$ gives

 $N(\operatorname{MAE}_{0}-\operatorname{MAE}_{1})=H_{U}-E_{U}-C-D_{\rm acq}.$ 

Since $A_{U}$ is convex with a minimum at any median, $H_{U},E_{U}\geq 0$ . When acquired values equal the reference, $D_{\rm acq}=0$ and Equation 7 follows. $\square$ 

The estimation cost. The bound on $E_{U}$ uses one fact about convex functions.

Lemma 1. If $\widehat{c}\in[a,b]$ , then $A_{U}(\widehat{c})\leq\max\{A_{U}(a),A_{U}(b)\}$ .

Proof. $A_{U}$ is convex, and every point of $[a,b]$ is a convex combination of its endpoints. $\square$ 

Let $q$ be the quantile function of the $N$ residuals and $n$ the pilot size. The pilot is drawn without replacement, equally from $K$ equal-sized strata (Equation 5), with independent draws across strata, so the number of pilot residuals below $q(\tfrac{1}{2}-t)$ is a sum over strata of independent without-replacement counts with mean at most $n(\tfrac{1}{2}-t)$ . Hoeffding’s inequality holds for sampling without replacement and for independent sums of such counts (Hoeffding, 1963), so the pilot median falls below $q(\tfrac{1}{2}-t)$ with probability at most $e^{-2nt^{2}}$ , and symmetrically above $q(\tfrac{1}{2}+t)$ . On the complementary event, Lemma 1 with $[a,b]=[q(\tfrac{1}{2}-t),q(\tfrac{1}{2}+t)]$ gives

 $E_{U}\leq\max\{A_{U}(a),A_{U}(b)\}-A_{U}(m_{U})\eqqcolon\varepsilon_{U}(t),$ 

the excess error of the worst shift in the band. The probability statement concerns only the pilot median against the quantiles of all $N$ residuals; Lemma 1 then applies to whichever unmeasured set $U$ the pilot and verification leave. $\square$ 

## C.2 Proof of Theorem 2

The theorem assumes $n$ independent labels from the component’s residual law and $\mathbb{E}|e|<\infty$ . Labels acquired by attribution magnitude need not satisfy this, and the band is undefined for $t\geq\tfrac{1}{2}$ . Let $e_{1},\ldots,e_{n}$ be the labels and $\widehat{c}$ their median.

Step 1: sample median in the central band. If $\widehat{c}<q(\tfrac{1}{2}-t)$ , at least $n/2$ labels fall below $q(\tfrac{1}{2}-t)$ . Their number is binomial with success probability at most $\tfrac{1}{2}-t$ , so Hoeffding’s inequality bounds this event by $e^{-2nt^{2}}$ ; similarly for $\widehat{c}>q(\tfrac{1}{2}+t)$ .

Step 2: excess error inside the band. $R(c)=\mathbb{E}|e-c|$ is convex with derivative $2\Phi(c)-1$ almost everywhere, where $\Phi$ is the distribution function of $e$ , so for $\widehat{c}\geq m$ ,

 $\displaystyle R(\widehat{c})-R(m)$ 
 $\displaystyle=\int_{m}^{\widehat{c}}\bigl(2\Phi(u)-1\bigr)\,du$ 

 $\displaystyle\leq 2t\,(\widehat{c}-m)\leq 2t\,w(t),$ 

since $\tfrac{1}{2}\leq\Phi(u)\leq\tfrac{1}{2}+t$ on the band. The case $\widehat{c}<m$ is symmetric.

Step 3. Since $R(m)=R(0)-\Gamma$ , the two steps give Equation 8. A union bound extends the event to all $N$ components with probability at least $1-2Ne^{-2nt^{2}}$ . $\square$ 

## C.3 Proof of Proposition 1

Let $T$ be the $B$ components with the largest $r_{i}$ and $S$ the $B$ with the largest scores $s_{i}$ . The expected error left by $S$ exceeds that left by $T$ by $\sum_{i\in T}r_{i}-\sum_{i\in S}r_{i}$ . Since $r_{i}\leq s_{i}+\epsilon$ on $T$ and $r_{i}\geq s_{i}-\epsilon$ on $S$ ,

 $\sum_{i\in T}r_{i}-\sum_{i\in S}r_{i}\leq\sum_{i\in T}s_{i}-\sum_{i\in S}s_{i}+2B\epsilon\leq 2B\epsilon,$ 

because $S$ maximizes the score sum over sets of size $B$ . $\square$ 

The oracle knows expected remaining errors before measurement. A residual that changes sign can have a small fitted correction but large expected absolute error, so a correction alone need not provide a useful selection score.

## C.4 Dataset Averages: Sign Condition and Selected Labels

Lemma 2 (Sign condition). For an averaged residual $\bar{e}_{i}$ and correction $\bar{c}_{i}$ , $|\bar{e}_{i}-\bar{c}_{i}|<|\bar{e}_{i}|$ if and only if $\bar{c}_{i}\bar{e}_{i}>0$ and $|\bar{c}_{i}|<2|\bar{e}_{i}|$ .

Proof. Squaring, the inequality is equivalent to $\bar{c}_{i}^{2}<2\bar{c}_{i}\bar{e}_{i}$ , which requires $\bar{c}_{i}\bar{e}_{i}>0$ and then reduces to $|\bar{c}_{i}|<2|\bar{e}_{i}|$ . $\square$ 

Dataset averages require the mean residual, whereas per-prompt absolute loss is minimized by a median. Acquisition can bias the mean. If $A_{i}$ indicates measurement of component $i$ , the acquired-label mean targets $\mathbb{E}[e_{i}\mid A_{i}=1]$ rather than $\mathbb{E}[e_{i}]$ . Section 4.3 evaluates whether the acquired residuals transfer to dataset averages.

## Appendix D Within-Prompt Evaluation and Controls

## D.1 Cohort, Acquisition, and Aggregation

Within-prompt evaluation comprises 1,662 layer evaluations and 542 complete model-task-prompt groups, with a fixed $P=64$ pilot inside $B=256$ measurements. We intervene on all MLP neurons in a layer. Gemma-2-9B has 14,336 neurons (layers 10, 20, 30), Qwen3-14B has 17,408 (9, 19, 29), and Qwen3-32B has 25,600 (15, 31, 47). Each task has 100 prompt groups. Summaries include groups with all three layers; Gemma is complete, and available Qwen counts appear in Table 1.

Relative AP error divides summed absolute estimation error by summed absolute reference effects for each vector. MAE, dataset-average error, and top-ten recall retain their definitions in Section 3.1.

We average three fixed pilot repetitions within each layer, then layers within each prompt group, then groups. Error reduction is one minus the ratio of mean MAEs. Descriptive 95% intervals use 20,000 paired group-bootstrap draws (seed 20261010), holding fits and measured costs fixed; across-prompt multiplicity adjustment is separate. Gemma intervals are 14.7% to 20.1% (IOI), 23.5% to 28.9% (factual), and 17.4% to 22.5% (SVA).

The method and baseline acquire separate AP measurements in the same allocation. Warm runtime includes attribution, acquisition, selection, fitting, and assembly, excluding loading, numerical validation, and reference collection. The baseline uses the largest executed magnitude-ranked batch prefix affordable at the method’s runtime; equal-count comparisons are separate. ## Appendix F Confirmation Protocol

Across-prompt evaluation uses 50 calibration and 100 evaluation groups in each of six model-task cells. All 1,152 Qwen3-8B heads (revision b968826d) and 784 Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct heads (a09a3545) are included (Yang et al., 2025; Qwen et al., 2025). Collection uses H100, PyTorch 2.9.1, Transformers 4.57.1, CUDA 12.6, FP32 eager attention, and disabled TF32. Groups shared across models remain linked. IOI equally mixes matched and template/entity-shifted groups; exact and reversed contrasts do not overlap across splits.

Arms comprise AtP ranking (verify-more), random ranking, Guided-AP, a cost-matched guided prefix, ProbeCal, and a capacity-matched AP-only learner. The reference-error oracle is a diagnostic ceiling. The five nonbaseline arms, six cells, four budgets, and three horizons define 360 comparisons. AtP∗ is evaluated within prompts (Appendix D.3); all across-prompt arms use AtP estimates.

Guided-AP acquires 1,600 labels per cell by magnitude-guided round robin. ProbeCal uses eight Rademacher probes per calibration group, totaling 400 HVPs. Fits and the capacity-matched control follow Appendices A and B.

The output metric is the final-position logit difference between correct and counterfactual tokens, relative to the clean baseline. Per-prompt MAE is computed after $B\in\{16,32,64,128\}$ verifications. Dataset-mean error averages effects over the 100 held-out groups before taking absolute error; it is not average per-prompt MAE. Recall uses the ten largest absolute reference effects.

Primary verification and scoring share a measured full AP table; separately acquired AP checks at $B=16$ and $B=32$ , with their cost included, confirm this shared table. Total costs include calibration, fitting, transfer, model loading, attribution, and verification. Analysis lengths of 100, 1,000, and 10,000 prompts project measured costs and observed errors; they are not additional evaluation samples.

We use 20,000 paired group-bootstrap draws with the prescribed groups and strata. Two-sided Bonferroni percentile intervals use tail probability $0.05/(2\cdot 360)$ , about 1.39 draws per tail, and condition on the fitted calibration and measured costs.

## Appendix I Cost Accounting and Numerical Scope

For method $m$ under a fixed loading and batching schedule, projected total cost is

 $C_{m}(T,B)=C_{m}^{\rm init}+Tc_{m}(B),$ 

(13)

where $C_{m}^{\rm init}$ includes calibration, fitting, and the specified initialization costs. The per-prompt term $c_{m}(B)$ includes attribution, ranking, activation handling, transfer, and verification. $T$ is a projected analysis length, not the number of evaluation prompts over which dataset averages are measured. The prespecified analysis lengths are 100, 1,000, and 10,000 prompts, and the allocation budgets are $B\in\{16,32,64,128\}$ .

For an across-prompt policy with $B$ verifications, the matched verify-more budget is

 $\displaystyle b_{m}(T,B)$ 
 $\displaystyle=\max_{b\in\{0,\ldots,N\}}b$ 

 $\displaystyle\text{subject to }C_{0}(T,b)\leq C_{m}(T,B),$ 

Here $C_{0}$ includes attribution and magnitude-ranked verification. Measured batch padding and overhead determine the integer budget; unused compute is retained. Within-prompt matching uses measured runtime without prior calibration. Dense curves are descriptive; adjusted inference uses the prespecified budgets and horizons.

Equation 13 assumes one initialization. Break-even horizons project measured cost components.

On Qwen3 factual completion, 1,600 AP labels at batch size 16 took 13.98 seconds (0.009 seconds/check); 400 serial HVPs took 51.77 seconds (0.13 seconds/HVP). At these rates, one HVP costs about 15 exact checks. Full calibration costs, including probes, fitting, transfer, and model loading, range from 384 to 1,050 seconds per cell and are included in the comparisons.

Deployment includes initialization, calibration, fitting, model loading, gradients, activation handling, transfer, and verification; reference collection is separate. Rates are specific to the measured hardware configuration.

Interventions add directions to live activations, preserving upstream propagation. Derivatives and references use FP32 eager execution; same-hidden-state FP64 readouts assess numerical sensitivity.

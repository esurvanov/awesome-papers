Abstract

Current evaluation of LLM-generated debate largely focuses on argumentative quality, leaving the value structure of generated arguments underexamined. We introduce a diagnostic metric framework for comparing how values are expressed and organized in human-reference and AI-generated debates. The framework separates value expression, the range of recognizable values a debate invokes, from value organization, how those values are distributed and sustained across turns. Grounded in Schwartz’s Basic-10 value theory, it represents debates as multi-level value distributions over argumentative units, utterances, stances, and full debates, enabling metrics for value coverage, alignment, diffusion, and cross-turn dynamics. Across multiple generation systems, AI-generated debates are broadly value-expressive, often activating as many or more value dimensions than matched human references, but they organize these values differently. Their utterances are more locally value-diffuse, and source-blind judges distinguish value clarity from value focus. These findings show that debate evaluation should consider not only value presence, but also value organization. Code and supplementary materials are available at
https://github.com/Maoyuan-li/Value-Expression-and-Organization.

## 1 Introduction

Arguments gain much of their force from the values they bring into view. In debate, speakers do more than exchange claims and evidence: they reveal what they treat as fair, safe, free, dignified, or worth protecting. These value appeals shape why an argument feels compelling.

Figure 1: Illustrative contrast between value expression and value organization.

LLMs are increasingly able to generate fluent and persuasive debate utterances, and existing evaluation has largely focused on coherence, factuality, stance consistency, logical validity, and persuasiveness (Liang et al., 2024a; Wang et al., 2025). A separate line of value-oriented NLP has shown that human values can be identified in argumentative text, for example through Webis-ArgValues-22 and ValueEval (Kiesel et al., 2022; Kiesel et al., 2023). Yet the intersection remains underexplored: debate evaluation rarely asks how values are organized across turns, while value mining usually treats values as labels attached to isolated texts. We therefore ask how AI-generated debates compare with matched human debate references in value expression and value organization. As Figure 1 illustrates, a debate may be value-expressive by invoking recognizable value appeals, while differing in value organization: the AI-generated example spreads across a broader value range, whereas the human reference concentrates around a more compact core of Universalism and Benevolence.

We frame value-based debate analysis as comparing distributions of value appeals across matched human-reference and AI-generated debates. This raises two challenges. First, values are rarely stated as explicit labels: a speaker may never name “security” or “autonomy,” while still embedding those values in the arguments. Second, values operate at multiple levels, from local argumentative units to utterances, stances, and full debates. We therefore adopt Schwartz’s Basic-10 theory as a theory-grounded value space (Schwartz, 1992; Schwartz et al., 2012). Following the claim–premise view of argument structure in Stab and Gurevych (2014), we represent each argumentative unit as a stance-specific claim paired with a premise span extracted from the corresponding utterance. Each argumentative unit is assigned a soft distribution over the ten values, which is then aggregated at multiple levels.

Figure 2: 
Conceptual framework. The shared Schwartz Basic-10 space (center) grounds two evaluation dimensions: value expression (breadth of value activation) and value organization (concentration and cross-turn dynamics). The right panel shows how paired differences are interpreted through surface controls, motion-type effects, and generation-system effects. Here, Human-TextOverlap denotes BLEU similarity between a generated debate and its matched human reference.

This multi-level representation supports a diagnostic metric suite with two complementary components. For value expression, we measure how broadly a debate activates the Basic-10 value space and how closely its dominant values align with a paired human reference. For value organization, we define a family of metrics that treats debate values as both distributions and trajectories: entropy captures value diffusion, while stability, drift, and dominant-value persistence characterize how a stance carries its value profile across turns. These metrics shift attention from whether generated arguments mention human-recognizable values to how those values are structured and transformed within argumentative interaction.

We instantiate the framework in a paired benchmark comparing general-purpose LLMs with R-Debater (Li et al., 2025). Results show a consistent separation: AI-generated debates activate as many or more Basic-10 values as human references while distributing value mass differently across utterances and stances. These patterns persist after surface-level controls and are supported by source-blind human evaluation.

Our contributions are: (1) a diagnostic distinction between value expression and value organization; (2) a Schwartz-grounded multi-level framework representing debate values as distributions over argumentative units, utterances, stances, and full debates; (3) a metric family covering value coverage, alignment, local concentration, and cross-turn dynamics; and (4) a paired analysis showing that AI-generated debates activate broad value spaces while differing from human references in local concentration and cross-turn organization.

## 2 Related Work

Automated debate generation and evaluation. Early automated debate studies focused on the components needed to build debating systems, such as evidence retrieval (Rinott et al., 2015), argument construction (Gutfreund et al., 2016), stance modeling (Bar-Haim et al., 2017), and argument quality assessment (Bar-Haim et al., 2021; Toledo et al., 2019). Project Debater later integrated retrieval, argument mining, and speech planning into an autonomous debating system (Slonim et al., 2021). Recent LLM-based work has shifted toward debate-like interaction, using simulated agents and multi-agent debate (Du et al., 2024; Liang et al., 2024b), retrieval-augmented debate generation (Li et al., 2025), and evaluation-guided optimization (Wang et al., 2025). Debate evaluation has also expanded from winning-stance prediction (Ruiz-Dolz et al., 2023) to multi-dimensional judging, chronological analysis (Liang et al., 2024a; Wang et al., 2025), and LLM-as-judge benchmarking (Sternlicht et al., 2025).
However, none measures how values are distributed and sustained across debate turns.

Human values in argumentation. Values have long been central to argumentation theory. In value-based argumentation, argument acceptability depends not only on logical relations, but also on the values promoted by an argument and the audience’s value ordering (Bench-Capon, 2003). Early value-oriented argument mining focused on identifying implicit human values behind natural-language arguments (Kiesel et al., 2022). Later work expanded this line with larger and more diverse resources, including Touché23-ValueEval (Mirzakhmedova et al., 2024) and the SemEval-2023 ValueEval shared task (Kiesel et al., 2023). Related value-modeling datasets also study human values in social scenarios, dialogue, or LLM outputs, such as ValueNet (Qiu et al., 2022) and FULCRA (Yao et al., 2024).
However, these studies treat values as labels for isolated texts. We instead study them as distributions in matched multi-turn debates.

## 3 Conceptual Framework

This section introduces the conceptual framework used to analyze value expression and value organization in human-reference and AI-generated debates.
Figure 2 summarizes the framework and its three layers: value representation, value-based measurement, and paired-difference interpretation.

## 3.1 Shared Value Space

Value-based debate analysis requires a shared vocabulary for describing what values are invoked. We adopt Schwartz’s Basic-10 theory as this value space (Schwartz, 1992; Schwartz et al., 2012).
Schwartz values are broad motivational goals guiding evaluations of actions and social arrangements, well suited to debate where arguments invoke safety, fairness, autonomy, or preservation.
The Basic-10 level is broad enough to cover major motivational contrasts in debate. We use the framework to analyze textual value appeals expressed in debate discourse, not to infer the internal beliefs or moral commitments of a model. The same principle applies to human debate transcripts.

## 3.2 Debate as a Multi-level Value Structure

We represent a debate formally as $d=(q,\mathcal{Z},\mathcal{C},\mathcal{U})$ , where $q$ is the topic, $\mathcal{Z}=\{+,-\}$ denotes the pro and con stances, $\mathcal{C}=\{c_{+},c_{-}\}$ contains the corresponding stance-specific claims, and $\mathcal{U}=(u_{1},\ldots,u_{n})$ is the ordered utterance list. Each utterance $u_{t}$ has text $x_{t}$ , stance $z_{t}\in\mathcal{Z}$ , and turn index $t$ . The two stances alternate in turn, i.e., $z_{t}\neq z_{t+1}$ for any $t$ .

Following the claim–premise view of argument structure in Stab and Gurevych (2014), we adapt this structure to competitive debate, where the stance-specific claim is determined by the debate topic and the speaker’s stance. For an utterance $u_{t}$ with stance $z_{t}$ , SAPE-Agent extracts premise spans $\mathcal{P}(u_{t})=\{p_{t,1},\ldots,p_{t,k_{t}}\}$ from the utterance text $x_{t}$ . We define an argumentative unit as the claim–premise pair

 $a_{t,i}=(c_{z_{t}},p_{t,i}),$ 

(1)

where $c_{z_{t}}$ is the stance-specific claim and $p_{t,i}$ is an extracted premise. Accordingly, $\mathcal{A}(u_{t})=\{a_{t,1},\ldots,a_{t,k_{t}}\}$ denotes the argumentative units in utterance $u_{t}$ . A premise may support the stance-specific claim directly or indirectly through rebuttal, counterexample, framing, concession, or value weighing, without requiring a fine-grained debate-act taxonomy.
A Basic-10 value distribution is first assigned to each argumentative unit, then aggregated to three structural levels: utterance, stance, and debate. These levels correspond to increasingly broader views of value expression. Let $\mathbf{p}(a_{t,i})\in\Delta^{|\mathcal{V}|-1}$ denote the Basic-10 value distribution associated with argumentative unit $a_{t,i}$ in utterance $u_{t}$ .
At the utterance level, we ask what value profile is expressed by one debate turn. For utterance $u_{t}$ with argumentative units $\mathcal{A}(u_{t})=\{a_{t,1},\ldots,a_{t,k_{t}}\}$ , the utterance distribution is:

 $\mathbf{P}_{\mathrm{utt}}(u_{t})=\frac{1}{k_{t}}\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}(u_{t})}\mathbf{p}(a).\vskip-5.69054pt$ 

(2)

Because it is an average of probability distributions, $\mathbf{P}_{\mathrm{utt}}(u_{t})$ is also a probability distribution over $\mathcal{V}$ .
At the stance level, we ask what value profile is carried by one stance across its same-stance utterances. Let $\mathcal{U}_{z}(d)=(u_{z,1},\ldots,u_{z,n_{z}})$ be the ordered subsequence of value-bearing utterances produced under stance $z$ . The stance distribution is

 $\mathbf{P}_{\mathrm{sta}}(d\mid z)=\frac{1}{n_{z}}\sum_{u\in\mathcal{U}_{z}(d)}\mathbf{P}_{\mathrm{utt}}(u).$ 

(3)

At the debate level, we summarize the overall value profile of the full exchange. Let $\mathcal{U}^{*}(d)$ be the set of value-bearing utterances and $n^{*}=|\mathcal{U}^{*}(d)|$ . The debate distribution is

 $\mathbf{P}_{\mathrm{deb}}(d)=\frac{1}{n^{*}}\sum_{u_{t}\in\mathcal{U}^{*}(d)}\mathbf{P}_{\mathrm{utt}}(u_{t}).$ 

(4)

Table 2: Evaluation dimensions and metrics.

Value expression. This dimension concerns the breadth and reference alignment of expressed values. It asks how broadly an AI-generated debate activates the Basic-10 value space and how its dominant values compare with those in the matched human reference.

1. Coverage counts the number of value dimensions activated above a threshold $\tau$ :

 $\mathrm{Coverage}_{\tau}(d)=\sum_{v\in\mathcal{V}}\mathbb{I}\left[P_{\mathrm{deb}}(v\mid d)\geq\tau\right].$ 

(5)

2. Top- $k$ overlap measures agreement in dominant values. Let $\mathrm{Top}_{k}(d)$ denote the set of $k$ highest-scoring values in $\mathbf{P}_{\mathrm{deb}}(d)$ . For a matched human reference $d^{H}$ and generated debate $d^{M}$ ,

 $\mathrm{Overlap}_{k}(d^{H},d^{M})=\frac{|\mathrm{Top}_{k}(d^{H})\cap\mathrm{Top}_{k}(d^{M})|}{k}.$ 

(6)

3. Jensen–Shannon divergence measures alignment between the full debate-level value distributions:

 $\mathrm{JSD}\left(\mathbf{P}_{\mathrm{deb}}(d^{H})\parallel\mathbf{P}_{\mathrm{deb}}(d^{M})\right).$ 

(7)

Lower JSD values correspond to closer distributional alignment in the Basic-10 value space. These metrics do not capture whether values are locally focused or consistently carried across the debate.

Value organization. This dimension concerns the internal shape of value expression. We measure both value concentration and value dynamics.

4. Value entropy measures concentration. For any value distribution $\mathbf{P}$ over $\mathcal{V}$ , define

 $H(\mathbf{P})=-\sum_{v\in\mathcal{V}}P(v)\log P(v).$ 

(8)

Applied to $\mathbf{P}_{\mathrm{utt}}(u_{t})$ , $\mathbf{P}_{\mathrm{sta}}(d\mid z)$ , and $\mathbf{P}_{\mathrm{deb}}(d)$ , this gives $H_{\mathrm{utt}}(u_{t})$ , $H_{\mathrm{sta}}(d\mid z)$ , and $H_{\mathrm{deb}}(d)$ .
Value dynamics are computed within each stance, because a debate alternates between competing stances. These metrics ask how a stance carries its own value profile across same-stance utterances:

5. Value stability measures how smoothly the utterance-level value distribution changes across same-stance utterances. Define $\mathrm{Stability}_{z}(d)$ as

 $1-\frac{1}{n_{z}-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{z}-1}\mathrm{JSD}\left(\mathbf{P}_{\mathrm{utt}}(u_{z,i})\parallel\mathbf{P}_{\mathrm{utt}}(u_{z,i+1})\right).$ 

(9)

Higher stability means less abrupt value change. We report the debate-level stability as the average of the two stance-level stability scores.

6. Value drift captures the opening-to-closing shift of a stance:

 $\mathrm{Drift}_{z}(d)=\mathrm{JSD}\bigl(\mathbf{P}_{\mathrm{utt}}(u_{z,1})\parallel\mathbf{P}_{\mathrm{utt}}(u_{z,n_{z}})\bigr).$ 

(10)

7. Dominant-value persistence captures whether prominent values continue across adjacent same-stance utterances. Let $\mathrm{Top}_{k}(u)$ be the set of top- $k$ values in utterance $u$ . We define $\mathrm{Persistence}_{k,z}(d)$ as

 $\frac{1}{n_{z}-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{z}-1}\mathbb{I}[\mathrm{Top}_{k}(u_{z,i})\cap\mathrm{Top}_{k}(u_{z,i+1})\neq\emptyset].$ 

(11)

Together, these metrics distinguish broad value activation from structured value organization.

## 3.4 Interpreting Paired Human–AI Differences

For any metric $\mu$ , we compute the paired difference $\Delta\mu(i,g)=\mu(d^{M}_{i,g})-\mu(d^{H}_{i})$ , where $d^{H}_{i}$ is the human reference for topic $i$ and $d^{M}_{i,g}$ is the generated debate from system $g$ . Positive values indicate larger metric values for the generated debate; they do not imply higher quality. Later analyses ask whether these differences persist after controlling for surface form, motion type, and generation system.

## 4 Methodology

Figure 3 illustrates the operational pipeline used to instantiate the conceptual framework.
We structure transcripts, extract premise spans, construct argumentative units by pairing each premise with the corresponding stance-specific claim, assign Basic-10 value distributions, aggregate across levels, and compute the metrics in Section 3. Full prompts are provided in Appendix A.

## 4.1 Transcript Structuring

We convert each debate transcript into a structured record containing debate ID, system source, topic, motion type, stance, turn index, and utterance text. This structure aligns generated debates with their matched human references and determines the utterance sequences used for stance-level aggregation and same-stance cross-turn metrics.

## 4.2 Premise Extraction and Argumentative-unit Construction

We implement a Stance-Aware Premise Extraction Agent (SAPE-Agent) to identify premise spans in each utterance. Input: debate topic, stance-specific claims, utterance text, and local context. Output: verbatim premise spans; multiple premises may be extracted per utterance. Extraction is conditioned on the debate topic, stance, and stance-specific claims, so that spans are identified relative to the argumentative role they play in the debate. For an utterance $u_{t}$ with stance $z_{t}$ , each extracted premise $p_{t,i}$ is paired with the corresponding stance-specific claim $c_{z_{t}}$ to construct the argumentative unit $a_{t,i}=(c_{z_{t}},p_{t,i})$ .

## 4.3 Value Annotation

We implement a Value Annotation Agent (VA-Agent) to map each argumentative unit to soft scores over the Basic-10 value space $\mathcal{V}$ . The input includes the argumentative unit, the debate topic, the stance, value definitions, and retrieved human-annotated examples when available. The output is a ten-dimensional Basic-10 value distribution.

Let $s_{v}(a)\in[0,1]$ be the average score assigned to value $v\in\mathcal{V}$ for argumentative unit $a$ over $R$ runs. The resulting vector $\mathbf{p}(a)=(p(v\mid a))_{v\in\mathcal{V}}$ is the Basic-10 value distribution used in subsequent aggregation, where

 $p(v\mid a)=\frac{s_{v}(a)}{\sum_{v^{\prime}\in\mathcal{V}}s_{v^{\prime}}(a)}.$ 

(12)

## 4.4 Aggregation and Metric Computation

After value annotation, we aggregate unit-level Basic-10 value distributions according to the three levels defined in Section 3: utterance, stance, and debate. Ordered same-stance utterances are retained for cross-turn organization metrics, including stability, drift, and dominant-value persistence. We then compute value expression and value organization metrics using the definitions in Section 3, together with the surface-control variables used in the empirical analysis.

We validate output format, field completeness, span boundaries, and value-vector dimensionality for all pipeline outputs. Malformed annotations are regenerated or removed, and repeated scoring with averaging reduces instability in value assignment.

## 5 Experimental Design

The experiments address three questions: (RQ1) whether SAPE-Agent and VA-Agent provide reliable annotations; (RQ2) how AI-generated debates compare with human references in value expression and organization; and (RQ3) whether paired differences persist after controlling for surface factors, motion type, and generation system. RQ1 is addressed through annotation validation; RQ2–3 through paired comparison. Human evaluation is described in Section 7.

Annotation validation.
We validate both agents on a manually checked subset. For SAPE-Agent, we measure observed agreement, Cohen’s $\kappa$ (Cohen, 1960), and Krippendorff’s $\alpha$ (Krippendorff, 2004) against human premise-span annotations.
For VA-Agent, human annotators assign Basic-10 value labels to sampled argumentative units. We evaluate VA-Agent as a multi-label value annotation model using precision, recall, and F1, and compare it with BERT, SVM, and direct LLM annotation baselines. The annotation protocol is shown in Appendix B.

Benchmark construction.
To compare human-reference and AI-generated debates under aligned conditions, we construct a benchmark from ORCHID (Zhao et al., 2023), a Chinese text-only corpus of real-world competitive debates. ORCHID contains 1,218 Chinese debates across 476 unique topics, with 2,436 stance-specific summaries and 14,133 fully annotated utterances, making it suitable for evaluating argumentative dialogue in Chinese (Zhao et al., 2023). For our study, we sample 60 distinct debate topics.

For each selected topic, we use one ORCHID debate as the human reference debate and generate corresponding AI debates using GPT-5.4, Claude-3.6 Sonnet, Qwen3.6-Plus, and DeepSeek-V3.2. We also include R-Debater (Li et al., 2025) as a debate-specific generation baseline.
Each topic is assigned one of three motion types: fact, value, or policy (Rottenberg and Winchell, 2011), allowing us to examine whether paired differences vary with the argumentative demands associated with different motion types.

Paired comparison and analysis.
For each topic, the human reference debate is paired with AI-generated debates under the same topic, stance setting, language, turn structure, and approximate utterance length. This design reduces topic and format-level confounds and allows value metrics to be interpreted as within-topic paired differences. We compute the paired difference $\Delta_{\mu}(i,g)$ defined in Section 3.4. Positive differences indicate larger metric values for the AI-generated debate relative to the matched human reference.
We report paired differences for all metrics in Table 2, using Wilcoxon signed-rank tests (Wilcoxon, 1945), Holm correction within each metric family (Holm, 1979), and Cohen’s $d_{z}$ (Lakens, 2013). Statistical significance indicates systematic difference, not superior debate quality.
To test robustness, we regress paired differences on surface controls (token length, argumentative-unit count, lexical diversity, BLEU similarity, Self-BLEU repetition) with generation-system and motion-type fixed effects.
Generation-system effects capture differences among GPT-5.4, Claude-3.6 Sonnet, Qwen3.6-Plus, DeepSeek-V3.2, and R-Debater. Motion-type effects capture differences among fact, value, and policy motions.

## 6 Results and Analysis

## 6.1 Annotation Validation

The annotation results indicate that SAPE-Agent and VA-Agent provide reliable signals for aggregate value analysis. For premise extraction, SAPE-Agent shows strong agreement with human annotations: observed agreement reaches 0.8612, Cohen’s $\kappa$ is 0.7124 at the micro level and 0.6897 at the macro level, and Krippendorff’s $\alpha$ is 0.7035. The premise rate is also close between human annotation and SAPE-Agent, with 0.3840 for human annotation and 0.4075 for SAPE-Agent. Each identified premise is subsequently paired with the corresponding stance-specific claim to construct the argumentative units used in downstream value analysis. For Basic-10 value annotation, VA-Agent outperforms all baselines. It achieves precision 0.74, recall 0.91, and F1 0.82, compared with F1 scores of 0.25 for BERT, 0.20 for SVM, and 0.64 for direct LLM annotation. The BERT, SVM, and direct LLM results are annotation baselines; they are not debate-generation systems in the paired benchmark.

## 6.2 Paired Comparison of Value Expression

AI-generated debates show broad value expression. Table 5 summarizes the expressiveness metrics, and Figure 4 visualizes aggregate Basic-10 value distributions across human-reference and AI-generated debates. Human references activate 4.883 Basic-10 values on average, whereas AI-generated debates activate between 5.4 and 5.6 values. The largest coverage is observed for GPT-5.4 (5.600), followed by Qwen3.6-Plus (5.567) and R-Debater (5.520). Thus, generated debates in this benchmark do not show reduced value coverage; in many cases, they activate a broader range of value dimensions.

Broader coverage does not imply closer alignment with the matched human reference. Top-3 overlap ranges from 0.572 to 0.667, and JSD ranges from 0.102 to 0.133. DeepSeek-V3.2 has the closest distributional alignment, with the lowest JSD (0.102) and the highest Top-3 overlap (0.667), yet no system reproduces the reference value profile exactly. Together, Table 5 and Figure 4 show that value expression and reference alignment are separable: generated debates can activate broad value spaces while allocating value mass differently from matched human debates.

DeepSeek-V3.2 shows the largest utterance-level paired difference ( $\Delta H_{\mathrm{utt}}=+0.301$ , $d_{z}=1.301$ ), followed by Qwen3.6-Plus ( $\Delta H_{\mathrm{utt}}=+0.265$ , $d_{z}=1.018$ ). The other systems also show large utterance-level differences, with $d_{z}$ between 0.848 and 0.971. This indicates that generated utterances tend to distribute value mass across a broader range of Basic-10 dimensions.

The paired difference becomes smaller as value distributions are aggregated. At the stance level, human-reference entropy is 1.541, while AI-generated debates range from 1.592 to 1.756. Claude-3.6 Sonnet shows the largest stance-level paired difference ( $\Delta H_{\mathrm{sta}}=+0.215$ , $d_{z}=0.828$ ), followed by GPT-5.4 ( $\Delta H_{\mathrm{sta}}=+0.171$ , $d_{z}=0.637$ ). At the debate level, the pattern is weaker and more generation-system dependent: human-reference debate-level entropy is 1.651, while AI-generated debates range from 1.712 to 1.761. GPT-5.4 has a small and non-significant debate-level difference ( $\Delta H_{\mathrm{deb}}=+0.062$ , Holm $p=0.139$ , $d_{z}=0.228$ ). This suggests that the main value-organization difference is local: full-debate aggregation can partially mask the value diffusion visible in individual utterances.

Cross-turn dynamics show a second pattern. Human references have a stability score of 0.831, while AI-generated debates are higher, ranging from 0.915 for Claude-3.6 Sonnet to 0.952 for Qwen3.6-Plus. Qwen3.6-Plus, DeepSeek-V3.2, and R-Debater also have low drift values (0.047, 0.051, and 0.078) and perfect dominant-value persistence (1.000), while human references show higher drift (0.213) and slightly lower persistence (0.983). These results indicate smoother cross-turn value trajectories for several generation systems. Near-perfect persistence should be interpreted descriptively: it may reflect coherent value maintenance, but it may also indicate reduced adaptive movement across the exchange.

## 6.4 Analysis of Paired Human–AI Differences

We ask whether the paired differences can be explained by surface textual factors. Tab. 8 shows cross-system variation in length, argumentative-unit count, lexical diversity, BLEU similarity, and Self-BLEU; Tab. 9 controls for these factors. The adjusted differences remain positive for all dependent variables, with the strongest and most robust effect at the utterance level ( $\Delta H_{\mathrm{utt}}=+0.252$ , 95% CI $[+0.205,+0.300]$ ). This suggests local value diffusion is not reducible to surface form.

Generation-system effects are significant only for stability ( $p=.003$ ), indicating clearer system-level variation in cross-turn dynamics than in entropy. Motion-type effects are significant for all dependent variables, and Tab. 10 shows: fact motions have the largest debate-level entropy increase, value motions are more model-dependent, and policy motions are heterogeneous. Thus, paired differences persist after surface controls, but their magnitude and direction depend on motion type.

## 7 Human Evaluation

Setup.
We conduct a source-blind evaluation with 120 judges from university debate teams. The evaluation contains two complementary judgment components. Judges rate debates on overall quality, value clarity, value focus, perceived value stability, and perceived human-debate likeness using 7-point Likert scales. They also perform source-blind pairwise comparisons between debates on the same topic drawn from different sources and choose which debate better maintains a focused value core. Debate sources are hidden and the presentation order is randomized.

Results and analysis.
Figure 5 and Table 11 show a clear separation between value clarity and value focus. Human references receive the highest perceived human-debate likeness score ( $6.42\pm 0.38$ ), while the closest AI-generated debates are GPT-5.4 ( $5.09\pm 0.47$ ) and DeepSeek-V3.2 ( $4.88\pm 0.49$ ). By contrast, AI-generated debates receive high value-clarity ratings: GPT-5.4 obtains the highest clarity score ( $6.01\pm 0.46$ ), followed by DeepSeek-V3.2 ( $5.93\pm 0.49$ ), both above the human-reference mean ( $5.77\pm 0.61$ ). The contrast is most visible in value focus. Human references receive the highest focus score ( $5.91\pm 0.56$ ) and are selected in 74% of the pairwise focus comparisons involving a human-reference debate. These results support the paper’s central distinction: generated debates can present value appeals clearly while differing from human references in how those appeals are focused and organized.

## 8 Qualitative Case Study

Table 12 compares human-reference and R-Debater utterances on "Love is a necessity for humanity." Human turns concentrate on Universalism and Benevolence; R-Debater activates care, pleasure, conformity, achievement, security, autonomy, and stimulation within the same motion—illustrating local value diffusion without implying either pattern is superior. The case makes the entropy result interpretable: the difference lies in how selectively and consistently values are organized within a local argumentative frame.

## 9 Conclusion

We introduced a diagnostic framework for measuring value expression and value organization in AI-generated debate. Our results show that generated debates often invoke broad human-recognizable values, while differing from human references in local concentration and cross-turn dynamics. Debate evaluation should therefore consider not only which values appear, but how value appeals are structured across argumentative exchange.

## Limitations

Our framework studies textual value appeals through Schwartz’s Basic-10 value space. This provides a compact and theory-grounded basis for comparing human-reference and AI-generated debates, but it cannot cover every culturally specific, ideological, religious, rhetorical, or domain-specific value that may appear in debate. Other value frameworks may reveal complementary patterns.

Our main benchmark is based on Chinese competitive debate transcripts and a fixed-turn matched evaluation design. This improves comparability across human-reference and AI-generated debates, but it simplifies real debate interaction, where speakers may respond to interruptions, audience feedback, identity cues, and spontaneous strategic shifts. The cross-lingual analysis in Appendix C is exploratory, and broader multilingual evaluation remains future work.

The analysis also depends on automatic premise extraction, subsequent argumentative-unit construction, and Basic-10 value annotation. Although SAPE-Agent and VA-Agent are validated against human annotations, errors may remain for implicit, metaphorical, or culturally specific value appeals. In addition, value entropy, stability, drift, and persistence are descriptive measures of value organization, not direct quality scores. High entropy may indicate value diffusion, but it may also reflect inclusive or multi-perspective argumentation. For this reason, the framework is best understood as a lens for comparison and diagnosis. It identifies systematic organizational patterns, but it does not by itself determine whether a given value pattern is rhetorically desirable, ethically preferable, or strategically effective.

Finally, our results concern values expressed in generated debate text. They should not be interpreted as evidence about a model’s internal beliefs, moral commitments, or alignment state. Nor do they replace evaluations of factuality, persuasiveness, or debate success. The contribution is instead to show that value organization is a distinct and necessary dimension of debate evaluation.

## Ethical Considerations

This work studies values expressed in debate text. We do not infer private moral commitments of individual speakers, demographic groups, or model users, nor do we treat generated value appeals as evidence of a model’s internal beliefs or moral alignment state. Our analysis is conducted at the level of debate utterances and aggregate human-reference/AI-generation comparisons.

The human evaluation is source-blind and asks judges to assess debate texts. We collected limited demographic and debate-background information, including age, gender, education, and debate experience, solely for sample characterization and report these data only in aggregate. The main risks are annotator burden and possible exposure to controversial debate topics; these should be mitigated through clear task descriptions, informed consent, fair compensation, and the option to withdraw.

A value-based debate evaluation framework could be misused to optimize persuasive messaging or target audiences through value appeals. Our intended use is different: to provide diagnostic tools for evaluating, auditing, and understanding AI-generated debate. We therefore frame the metrics as descriptive measures of value expression and organization, not as tools for manipulating audiences, ranking speakers morally, or declaring one debate source inherently superior.

The ORCHID corpus is used in accordance with its publicly available release terms. Code and supplementary materials for our pipeline and paired benchmark are publicly available at https://github.com/Maoyuan-li/Value-Expression-and-Organization.

## Appendix B ORCHID Annotation Protocol

We manually annotate an in-domain ORCHID subset consisting of 50 debates and 400 utterances. For SAPE-Agent validation, human annotations identify premise spans within the sampled utterances. Each identified premise is paired with the stance-specific claim determined by the debate topic and stance to construct an argumentative unit $a=(c_{z},p)$ . The annotation was conducted by a pool of 50 annotators. For VA-Agent validation, each argumentative unit was independently annotated by three annotators using hard multi-label assignments over the Schwartz Basic-10 value set. For each value, we apply majority voting: if at least two of the three annotators assign the value to the argumentative unit, the value is retained as a gold label. This procedure allows each argumentative unit to express multiple values while avoiding reliance on a single annotator’s judgment. We use this subset as an in-domain generalization check for SAPE-Agent and VA-Agent, while the main validation tables are reported in Section 6. This annotation subset validates the analysis pipeline; the main paper’s claims concern aggregate value-organization patterns rather than correctness of individual value assignments.

For a source $s$ , we define its focus-preference rate as

## E.2 Evaluator Background Statistics

A total of 120 evaluators participated in the human evaluation. All evaluators were active members or former members of debate teams at internationally competitive universities. They were recruited because of their prior experience in competitive debating, argument assessment, and debate adjudication. To preserve anonymity, we report only aggregate background information and do not disclose names, institutions, contact information, or any other personally identifiable details.

The evaluators were primarily university students or recent graduates, with an age range of 18–29 years. The mean age was 22.4 years. The gender distribution was relatively balanced, with 58 female evaluators, 60 male evaluators, and 2 evaluators who preferred not to disclose gender. Most evaluators were undergraduate or master’s students, reflecting the composition of university debate teams.

All evaluators had formal debate experience. On average, they had 3.8 years of competitive debate experience, with most participants having competed in university-level tournaments. In addition, 87 evaluators reported prior experience as debate judges or adjudicators. Since the evaluated materials were debate texts, this background ensured that the participants were familiar with argumentative quality, rebuttal structure, value expression, and the organization of debate speeches.

Overall, the evaluator pool was selected to represent experienced debate readers rather than general crowdworkers. This design was intended to ensure that the human evaluation focused on debate-specific qualities, including overall quality, value clarity, value focus, value stability, and human-likeness.

## E.3 Single-Debate Rating Questionnaire Example

This appendix provides one concrete questionnaire item used in our human evaluation.
The item is source-blind: evaluators are not informed whether the debate was written
by human debaters or generated by a model. They are asked to evaluate the debate
only according to the displayed text.

## Background

You are invited to participate in a study on debate evaluation. In this task, you will read
one debate sample on the topic Is love a necessity for humanity? Please evaluate
the debate based only on the text shown.

Your responses are anonymous and will only be used for research purposes. Please do not
try to infer whether the debate was written by human debaters or generated by an AI system.

## Debate Topic

Topic: Is love a necessity for humanity?

Pro position: Love is a necessity for humanity.

Con position: Love is not a necessity for humanity.

## Debate Sample

Pro.

When we talk about kinship, we know it comes from blood relations and shared life
experience. When we talk about friendship, we know it comes from shared interests and
mutual support. These emotions have reasons, but love is unreasonable. I do not
understand why someone may not be objectively better, yet my heart is full of that
person’s shadow. I do not know why all my ideal standards disappear the moment I meet
that person. Even after hurt and conflict, when I receive an unexpected call late at
night, what I still say is: “I am here.” I cannot explain the reason, but I know it is
because you are you.

Therefore, love is somewhat absurd. Its absurdity lies in the fact that it escapes
instrumental logic. It is a form of unconditional recognition. We argue that love builds
the foundation of unconditional emotion and preserves for humanity a source of
self-confirmation beyond instrumental value. This is where human dignity lies.

First, love preserves human dignity by giving people the hope of complete recognition.
I may suffer misfortune. My wisdom, wealth, and beauty may all disappear. I may have no
instrumental value to anyone. I may be abandoned by others and fall into desperation.
But if humanity has love, I still retain hope: I may still be recognized by another
person simply because I am myself. This means that the world does not evaluate a person
only by their usefulness. Each person retains the possibility of being unconditionally
recognized.

Second, love crosses the boundaries that people construct between one another. The
unconditional nature of love makes it possible to transcend barriers and create human
connection. Without love, we are merely billions of separate individuals or isolated
groups. The unconditional nature of love allows humanity to cohere as a shared community.

Con.

A necessity is an economic concept. It refers to a demand that is inelastic. We argue
that calling love a necessity does not highlight its uniqueness or beauty. Instead, it
may pressure people’s pursuit of happiness and trap them in anxiety and uncertainty.
Love is not a necessity. In a pluralistic age, this view better allows people to live
more freely and comfortably.

First, defining love as a necessity creates value pressure. If something is treated as a
necessity, then when it conflicts with other values, it must be prioritized. But in
reality, many people cherish the freedom of being single more than the value of romantic
love. Some people find happiness in stable work, family health, self-realization, or
personal freedom. If we define love as a necessity, we risk using this value to suppress
people’s right to choose their own lives.

Second, treating love as a necessity may amplify the fear of lack. Food is a necessity,
so people fear hunger. If love is also treated as such a necessity, then “not being
loved” becomes a serious deficiency. This implication can push people into emotional
anxiety. Love-related issues already easily trigger irrational emotions. Adding the
label of necessity makes it harder for people to escape painful emotional attachments.

Love is not a superstition of fate or timing. It is a natural attraction. Once the fear
of lack is introduced, love changes its nature. Love is not exchangeable or measurable.
It can truly bloom only in freedom.

Pro.

We must first clarify that a necessity for humanity is not the same as a necessity for
every individual. We are not saying that every person must fall in love, just as
reproduction may be necessary for humanity even though many families choose not to have
children.

The opposition’s standard is simply that a person can survive without love. But this
standard lacks ambition and would lead humanity backward. By that logic, cars are not
necessary because we can walk; fire is not necessary because we can use candles; houses
are not necessary because we can return to caves. This reduces humanity to a primitive
condition.

Our standard is different. We argue that human beings should not be recognized merely
through their instrumental attributes. A person should be respected because they are
themselves. This is the irreducible bottom line of human dignity.

For example, we believe Black people should not be treated as tools of labor, and women
should not be treated as tools of reproduction. These movements for equality are based
on the idea that people should not be reduced to instruments. But even this is not
enough. If someone likes me only because I am beautiful or rich, I will not be truly
happy, because that person does not recognize me as myself. What I truly expect is to be
recognized because I am who I am. This is the purpose that love fulfills for humanity.

Con.

The opposition constantly shifts between love as a concept and love as a feeling. If you
talk only about concepts, then evil and terrorism are also concepts produced by humanity,
but they are obviously not necessities. If you talk about love as a feeling or enjoyment,
then not everyone experiences it. Some people may never have such a feeling, yet they
are still human.

More importantly, society assumes that survival is necessary, but not love. If someone
wants to die because of love, a counselor or firefighter will stop them. This shows that
we do not treat love as a necessity above life. If the proposition wants to overturn this
assumption, then it must accept the consequence that people should not be stopped from
dying for love. That is clearly unacceptable.

A necessity carries a cost: if one lacks it, one loses dignity. If I lack food, I may be
reduced to begging. If I lack freedom, I may become a slave. If I lack moral conscience,
I may be condemned as evil. But if someone lacks love, we should not say they have no
dignity. The most dangerous thing is precisely to believe that without a particular
person or a particular love, one cannot live or retain dignity. This is why treating love
as a necessity can be harmful.

Finally, if my daughter one day asks me what love is, I would tell her: never believe
that love is your necessity. If you have love, cherish it. If you do not have love, do
not feel inferior. Your value is written and defined by yourself, not by whether you are
loved.

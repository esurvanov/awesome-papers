Abstract

Training LLM agents for policy-constrained tool environments, such as an airline backend with booking, refund, and cancellation policies, requires tasks that exercise the environment’s rules individually and in combination. Existing synthesis pipelines let a model propose tasks and retain only those it immediately solves, biasing training data toward easier behaviors. We introduce Gradus (Graded Rungs of Ascending Difficulty from an Underlying System), which decouples what a task should teach from the concrete task and solution used to teach it. Without seed tasks, Gradus treats a fixed environment as a latent syllabus, extracting guarded behavioral operators that capture how actions depend on current conditions and how their effects reshape subsequent decisions. It composes these operators into increasingly demanding tasks while using corpus-level feedback to target missing outcome combinations. Crucially, Gradus jointly calibrates tasks and solutions against the environment: execution feedback identifies which side to refine while preserving the intended behavioral requirements. Verification thus becomes a recovery signal rather than a terminal filter, coupling coverage-guided exploration with the preservation of difficult examples. From off-the-shelf backbones, the resulting curriculum lifts a 30B model from 59% to 89% on $\tau^{2}$ -Bench and from 47% to 83% on CAR-Bench with an Opus 4.5 proposer, and to 76% and 62%, respectively, with an open-weight Qwen3-235B proposer. These results show the value of jointly controlling which behaviors are explored and which survive into verified training data.

## 1 Introduction

Training LLMs to act as agents requires data spanning diverse tool use, reasoning demands, and multi-turn interactions. Recent synthesis pipelines build generalist agents by generating tasks, workflows, and even entire environments at scale (Lyu et al., 2026; Wang et al., 2025; Song et al., 2026). These efforts broaden the range of situations agents encounter. A complementary problem arises when the environment is already given: how can we construct a curriculum that prepares an agent for the full range of behaviors required by that particular environment?

A fixed environment contains substantially more training structure than its list of APIs suggests. Depending on the current state and operating policies, the same action may be permitted, denied, or require additional steps. For example, in an airline booking environment, whether a cancellation is permitted depends on the cabin class, the time since booking, and whether travel insurance covers the stated reason, so the same request may end up with different agent behaviors in different states. Moreover, an earlier action may change the conditions governing later decisions. Constructing training data from this environment structure presents two coupled challenges: deciding which behaviors to teach and producing valid tasks and solutions that teach them.

A high-coverage curriculum must therefore identify these behavioral distinctions, compose them systematically, and track which remain underrepresented. Existing methods structure synthesis through task blueprints and workflows (Prabhakar et al., 2025), explicit constraints (Chen et al., 2026b), domain-entity graphs (Sun et al., 2026), or predefined behavioral and difficulty dimensions (Gu et al., 2026). These structures effectively guide individual proposals, but individually diverse or difficult tasks do not necessarily produce broad corpus-level coverage of policy outcomes and their interactions.

A second challenge is preserving the intended coverage during verification. Existing synthesis pipelines commonly use successful execution, constraint satisfaction, or exact state agreement as an acceptance gate, retaining verified trajectories while filtering unsuccessful candidates (Sun et al., 2026; Chen et al., 2026b; Lyu et al., 2026; Zeng et al., 2026). Although this improves data precision, it also makes verification a hidden curriculum selector. As tasks combine more rules and state changes, errors in both task construction and solution generation become more likely, causing difficult behaviors to be disproportionately dropped.

We introduce Gradus (Graded Rungs of Ascending Difficulty from an Underlying System), which separates what agent behaviors to teach and producing valid tasks and solutions that teach them. Starting from a fixed executable environment without seed tasks, Gradus organizes each action according to the conditions governing its use and the consequences of its possible outcomes. These reusable descriptions, which we call guarded operators, expose how one action can enable, invalidate, or otherwise alter the requirements of another. Gradus composes these relationships into increasingly demanding task blueprints, while corpus-level feedback directs generation toward underrepresented outcomes and combinations. Additional cross-space interaction dimensions vary how the same underlying requirements are surfaced by introducing ambiguity, indirect references, social pressure, or reasoning demands.

Crucially, as blueprints become more difficult, some fail to yield a valid task and solution on the first attempt, and neither the proposed task nor its solution is assumed correct. Using the executable environment as a common anchor, Gradus lets task design and solution construction calibrate each other: failures in solution construction feed back into task revision, flagging unsolvable tasks, hallucinated states or conditions, and ambiguous objectives, while the task and its oracle proposal anchor the solution search, steering it to fix departures from policy. Both sides are refined against environment execution, helping difficult compositions survive verification rather than being discarded.

From off-the-shelf backbones, the resulting curriculum lifts a 30B model from $59\%$ to $89\%$ on $\tau^{2}$ -Bench and from $47\%$ to $83\%$ on CAR-Bench with an Opus 4.5 proposer, and to $76\%$ and $62\%$ , respectively, with an open-weight Qwen3-235B proposer. These results demonstrate the value of jointly controlling which behaviors are explored and which survive into verified training data. Our contributions are:

• 

A compositional representation of environment behavior that connects action conditions, policy-dependent outcomes, and evolving state.

• 

Coverage-guided task construction with explicit controls over structural complexity and interaction demands.

• 

Environment-grounded joint task-solution refinement that recovers difficult examples while preserving their intended behavioral requirements, connecting proposed coverage to verified training data.

## 2.1 The Latent Syllabus

A fixed environment implicitly specifies what an agent must learn through its tools, states, policies, and executable transitions. In an airline system, for example, the same cancellation request may require a refund or a refusal depending on the booking state and policy; earlier booking changes may alter which options remain available. These conditional behaviors and their interactions form the environment’s latent syllabus. They are distributed across system artifacts rather than supplied as an explicit collection of training tasks.

Gradus turns this implicit syllabus into verified training data through

 $E\xrightarrow{\text{Organize}}\mathcal{G}\xrightarrow{\text{Coverage-guided search}}\mathcal{Z}\xrightleftharpoons[\text{Diagnose and re-design}]{\text{Construct and verify}}\mathcal{C}\xrightarrow{\text{Accept}}\mathcal{D}.$ 

(1)

where $G$ contains reusable descriptions of environment behaviors, $Z$ contains task blueprints, $C$ contains the realized curriculum, and $D$ is the resulting accepted training corpus. A guarded operator in $G$ describes an action together with the conditions governing its possible outcomes, such as when cancellation is allowed and what refund follows. Coverage-guided search composes these descriptions into blueprints that specify which behaviors a task should exercise, prioritizing underrepresented outcomes and interactions. A blueprint might combine a booking modification with a subsequent cancellation; additional interaction patterns can make the request ambiguous without changing its underlying requirements.

Each blueprint is then instantiated as a concrete task with a provisional reference solution and tested through agent interaction. Verification uses execution evidence and policy checks to assess the proposed reference and observed solution, guiding revision of either side when conflicts arise. Throughout refinement, the blueprint preserves what the task is intended to teach, connecting targeted behavioral coverage to verified examples.

## 2.2 Compiling Environment Artifacts to Guarded Operators

To make the environment’s behavioral requirements reusable during synthesis, we ask an LLM to organize the supplied policies, tool implementations, and state schemas into a persistent library. Each entry centers on one atomic action and records its purpose, parameters, relevant state fields, guarded-condition branches and conditional consequences. We call this a guarded operator:

 $g(a)=\{r_{a,i}\Rightarrow q_{a,i}\}_{i=1}^{m_{a}},$ 

(2)

where each guard $r_{a,i}$ combines applicable conditions using conjunction, disjunction, or negation, and $q_{a,i}$ describes the corresponding effects or required behavior, including state updates, follow-up actions, refusals, or failures. For example, a cancellation operator distinguishes eligible bookings that trigger refunds from ineligible requests requiring refusal.

These descriptions expose how an action’s effects can establish or invalidate another action’s conditions. They serve as an LLM-readable representation for composing behavioral requirements.

## 2.3 Coverage-Guided Scenario Evolution

Gradus uses the operator library to construct symbolic scenario blueprints. A blueprint specifies the user goal, relevant context, known and unknown information, and intended operator outcomes, while leaving concrete entities and database values unbound. Construction proceeds through the three stages below: at each stage, code governs the evolution schedule and samples the transformation instructions, and the LLM realizes each instruction into a contextually coherent blueprint.

## Atomic blueprints.

For each guarded operator, the LLM receives the full operator catalog and generates $k$ initial blueprints exercising its conditional behaviors. A shared coverage ledger tracks all the (policy, outcome) pairs of all operators represented in generated blueprints. Generation prompts include this ledger to prioritize underrepresented outcomes, both during atomic scenario generation and subsequent structural evolution.

## Structural evolution.

For each parent blueprint, code requests up to $K$ children by independently sampling a structural kernel for each attempt. It supplies the LLM with the parent, kernel instruction, operator catalog, and current coverage ledger. The kernel specifies how to extend the parent: causal connects actions through a state change, parallel adds an independent request, and repetition applies an action across a user-described set. The LLM chooses compatible operators and outcomes, retaining the parent’s requirements while adding new ones, or declines an incoherent extension. For example, causal evolution may add a booking modification whose effects change the conditions of a subsequent cancellation. Repeating this procedure builds a lineage tree whose branching factor and depth control the breadth and extent of composition.

## Interaction variation.

After structural evolution, code samples interaction kernels and asks the LLM to rewrite each blueprint accordingly. These kernels introduce indirect references, ambiguity, user pressure, or additional reasoning while preserving the intended actions and outcomes. Thus, structural kernels change what the task requires, whereas interaction kernels change how those requirements will be surfaced and interpreted. New interaction dimensions can be added through additional kernel instructions.

Across all stages, the programmatic controller allocates search across structural kernels, and interaction variations, while the LLM performs model-guided pruning: it realizes an assigned expansion, selects a compatible alternative, or declines when the scenario-kernel combination is unrealistic. This avoids enumerating the full Cartesian product while preventing generation from collapsing onto the model’s preferred patterns.

## 2.4 Preserving Coverage through Task and Solution Validity

A blueprint specifies what a task should teach, but its task, reference, and demonstration must still be constructed, with harder blueprints more likely to fail on the first attempt. Gradus therefore jointly refines both: task validity concerns whether the grounded task is coherent and feasible, while solution validity concerns whether a solution fulfills the task under the environment’s policies. The two processes take each other’s results as reciprocal feedback: Task design cross-checks simulated solution outcomes to diagnose task design defects and guide its next revision; solution search cross-checks the task and reference to produce reflection feedback for subsequent attempts. Environment execution anchors both directions. The process follows an initialization $\rightarrow$ simulation $\rightarrow$ verification loop, while preserving the blueprint’s behavioral requirements.

## Initialization: ground blueprints to environment states.

An LLM agent receives the blueprint, tools, policies, state schemas, and an environment-probing tool. It inspects the state and constructs a concrete task and a candidate reference action sequence. Code immediately replays the reference and returns execution errors for local revision. This first check establishes executability; whether the task is coherent and the reference fulfills it remains subject to cross-checking through simulation.

## Simulation: task-guided solution refinement.

A user simulator receives the task and interacts with a solving agent that sees only the conversation and tool responses, never the hidden task specification or reference, and their interaction produces a full demonstration candidate. Code launches $K$ parallel search branches with at most $m$ attempts each. A trajectory evaluator scores each attempt for action correctness, policy compliance, and communication quality, judging against the proposed task and reference, the policies, and the execution evidence, and returns feedback that guides the next attempts. The task and reference thus steer simulation reflection while remaining hidden from the solving agent. By the time an outcome reaches verification (next phase), parallel sampling and feedback-guided retries have already searched for the strongest demonstration the task admits.

## Verification: demonstration-guided task refinement.

This phase decides which (task, reference, demonstration) tuples to accept and, for the rest, arbitrates the refinement direction for the next loop: repair a flawed task, or keep searching for a better demonstration. To do so, code replays the reference and the demonstrated actions from the same initial state and compares their outcomes. Acceptance is decided by: when the two outcomes agree and the programmatic policy checks pass, the whole tuple is accepted as gold-standard supervision. When they do not, a diagnostic LLM reads the task, reference, demonstration, outcome differences, and check results and attributes the failure. Demonstration errors route the task back to further solution search, whereas evidence of a flawed task or reference (an incoherent or unsolvable task, hallucinated states, an incorrect reference or outcome) is summarized as feedback and returned to initialization. The language model only interprets the replay evidence and never certifies correctness itself, so a mutually agreeable but environment-inconsistent tuple cannot be accepted.

GRADUS thus treats task design and solution search as two provisional halves refined against each other, with execution in the environment as the shared arbiter that accepts a pair only when both hold. By recovering difficult blueprints rather than discarding them at their first failure, this loop carries the coverage produced by scenario evolution through to the training data, where the hardest lessons would otherwise be lost.

## 3 Experiments

## 3.1 Experimental Setup

Environments and evaluation.
We evaluate curriculum quality by downstream success on two customer-facing agent benchmarks: $\tau^{2}$ -Bench airline, retail, and telecom domains (Barres et al., 2025) and CAR-Bench (Kirmayr et al., 2026), an in-car voice assistant, with base, disambiguation, and hallucination task splits. Each task is scored by its benchmark’s executable evaluator as a pass or fail, and per-split success is reported as pass@1 averaged over five independent runs, $\overline{\mathrm{pass}}@1=\tfrac{1}{5}\sum_{i=1}^{5}\mathbb{I}[y_{i}=1]$ . Variance and consistency metrics are reported in Appendix A.9. Benchmark tasks and references are held out from training curriculum construction, training, and checkpoint selection.

Curriculum construction and training.
GRADUS uses either Claude Opus 4.5 (Anthropic, 2025) or Qwen3-235B-A22B-Thinking-2507 (Qwen Team, 2025b) for all model-mediated synthesis roles and trains Qwen3-8B (Qwen Team, 2025d), Qwen3-14B (Qwen Team, 2025a), and Qwen3-30B-A3B students (Qwen Team, 2025c). Shallow lessons (evolution depths 1-3) are routed primarily to SFT, whereas deeper lessons (depths 4-5) are routed primarily to RL. Full-parameter SFT is followed by GRPO-based RL (Shao et al., 2024), whose reward is the same executable acceptance signal used during construction, so RL optimizes verified targets rather than a learned or heuristic score.

Model references.
Table 1 reports proprietary frontier models (Opus 4.5 (Anthropic, 2025) and GPT-5.2 (OpenAI, 2025)), open-weight frontier-scale models: Kimi-K2-Thinking (Moonshot AI, 2025) and DeepSeek-V3.2 (DeepSeek-AI, 2025), and off-the-shelf Qwen3 backbones, including Qwen3-235B-A22B (Qwen Team, 2025b) as our open-weight proposal model. We also evaluate released 8B generalist-agent checkpoints to measure how agents broadly trained with generalist data synthesis recipes transfer to these policy-constrained environments (Song et al., 2026; Wang et al., 2025; Sun et al., 2026; Lyu et al., 2026).

Synthesis baselines.
We train the same Qwen3-8B student on the released Simia (Li et al., 2025), CoVe (Chen et al., 2026b), and WRIT (Gu et al., 2026) datasets using our full-parameter SFT recipe, matching the number of tasks and maximum tokens per example across the baselines and our methods; these comparisons are limited to airline and retail, where their data are available. We further compare three paradigms under the same Opus 4.5 teacher, Qwen3-8B student, and data budget: Model-prior synthesis provides the teacher with the environment policy, tools, and state schema but no explicit task structure or coverage guidance; the teacher freely proposes a blueprint, grounds it into a task and oracle, and retains it only when simulation produces the designed outcome. Graph-based synthesis replaces free generation with a set of ontology constructed from tool graphs, each generating multiple blueprints before applying the same grounding and simulation-based rejection procedure. Trajectory-first synthesis instead lets the LLM explore the live environment to obtain executable trajectories, uses a global tracker to discourage repeated action sequences, and derives a user scenario and oracle backward from each trajectory, followed by final replay. None of these baselines uses GRADUS’s adaptive coverage expansion or verification-guided task recovery.

## 3.3 Ablations on Curriculum Components

Table 2 isolates each curriculum component. Removing guarded operators lowers performance on both domains, as the proposer must then infer guards and outcomes from raw tool schemas, policies, and state definitions implicitly. The global ledger matters most for telecom, whose operators contain many policy-outcome pairs: corpus-wide coverage tracking is what steers the agent from skipping a required diagnostic step. Structural evolution matters as well, as it steers the proposer from overproducing familiar scenario structures. Interaction variation matters much more for CAR disambiguation, because varying how requests are surfaced demands different levels of interpretation, reasoning, and clarification. The validity ablations show a complementary effect. Removing verification causes a substantial drop, although the resulting curriculum remains useful because coverage-guided search can still produce difficult tasks whose solutions happen to succeed on the first attempt. Parallel search improves performance by combining outcome-based matching with multiple solution attempts. Adding policy-grounded reflection yields a particularly large gain on CAR disambiguation, where the proposer often fails on its first attempt but improves after receiving targeted feedback. Finally, task recovery uses simulation feedback to redesign failed tasks, preserving difficult yet valid examples and producing a more complete curriculum.

Panel-1,3: Policy-Outcome Coverage. Panel-2,4: Action Combinations. 

Because Gradus targets coverage gaps, it explores substantially more of the task space on both airline and retail (Figure 2). Panels (1,3) measure distinct (policy, outcome) combinations; coverage-guided search discovers $1.4\times$ to $2.9\times$ more combinations compared to baseline methods, exposing the student to a broader range of interacting policies and outcomes. Panels (2,4) measure action combinations among state-changing tools, where structural evolution expands coverage by up to ${\sim}5\times$ on airline and ${\sim}1.5\times$ on retail. In all four panels, baseline coverage plateaus as additional examples accumulate, indicating repeating familiar patterns, whereas Gradus continues discovering new ones. Proposer strength also matters: the two proposers coincide on retail (policy, outcome) coverage but differ by roughly $1.7\times$ on action combinations, indicating that composing unseen action sets depends on proposer capability more than enumerating policy branches does.

## 3.5 From Intermittent Demonstrations to Reliable Learning

As discussed in Section 2.4, solution generation is cast as a search problem; the two search strategies (parallel rollouts and reflection-guided retries) recover capability that a single reference-blind proposal model would miss at its first attempt (Figure 3). The two settings compare both proposal models: proprietary proposer (Opus 4.5 on CAR: Hallucination, top) and open-weight proposer (Qwen3-235B on airline, bottom). The proposer improves from 0.64 and 0.61 at pass@ $1$ to 0.72 and 0.84 at pass@ $4$ , and further to 0.88 and 0.88 with evaluator-guided retries. Training then converts this intermittent capability into reliable single-shot behavior: SFT lifts the student from 0.45 to 0.74 and from 0.63 to 0.69, surpassing both proposers’ pass@ $1$ . RL raises this further to 0.91 on CAR-Bench, exceeding the proposer’s observed search success rate of 0.88.

Figure 4 shows how iterative recovery preserves curriculum difficulty. In the left panel, mean zero-shot success of the off-the-shelf student model falls monotonically from $0.87$ to $0.17$ with evolution depth, demonstrating controlled difficulty growth. The middle panel traces the full initialization $\rightarrow$ simulation $\rightarrow$ verification loop, with the red arrows marking tasks to be re-initialized in the next round. This process is not a filtering funnel: the final curriculum accumulates the post-verification outputs from every round (all blue bars), rather than retaining only Round-3. The right panel shows that recovery disproportionately retains difficult examples: medium-hard and hard tasks comprise 41% of initially accepted tasks but 75% and 83% of those recovered in Rounds-2 and-3. ## 3.7 From Curriculum to Frontier Exams

Beyond synthesizing training data, we investigate whether Gradus could construct frontier exams for the proposer, since the environment is cast as one latent syllabus for both: coverage-guided search targets uncovered (policy, outcome) and action combinations, which initialization, simulation, and verification realize harder tasks. Along similar lines, TASTE (Keren et al., 2026) searches tool-name sequences and falls back to simpler variants when harder evolution fails; Gradus recovers the evolved task and its solution while preserving the difficulty requirements. With a frontier model as scout, this process therefore locates supported regions that the frontier model solves only intermittently.
Using Opus 4.5 as scout, we evolve airline, retail, and CAR-Bench base tasks to depth five and retain verified tasks whose gold outcome is matched in at most five of eight rollouts. Two annotators audited all 58 $\tau^{2}$ -Bench and 79 CAR-Bench candidates, judging $87.5\%$ , $100\%$ , and $90.5\%$ correct, with Gwet’s agreement coefficient AC1 (Gwet, 2008) of $0.79$ , $1.00$ , and $0.96$ (Appendix A.10).
On tasks both annotators accepted as correct, Opus-4.5 (proposal model) falls from $88\%$ , $89\%$ , and $85\%$ to $52\%$ , $60\%$ , and $43\%$ , and three other held-out models decline by $18\%$ to $44\%$ in every domain (Table 3), so the difficulty transfers to models held out from the exam construction process.

## 4 Conclusion

We introduced Gradus, a framework that treats a fixed executable environment as a latent syllabus and separates the behaviors to be taught from the tasks and solutions that teach them. Gradus organizes environment logic into guarded operators, uses a global coverage ledger to steer composition toward underrepresented policy outcomes and action combinations, and refines tasks and solutions through reciprocal feedback, so verification recovers difficult lessons rather than filtering them out. Under matched controls, the resulting curricula outperform alternative synthesis methods, transfer across student architectures, and support executable RL: the final 30B-A3B model reaches $89\%$ average success on $\tau^{2}$ -Bench and $83\%$ on CAR-Bench, surpassing all evaluated frontier model on the latter. The same machinery turns advanced syllabus regions into frontier exams that remain challenging for held-out frontier models. Thus, the agent-data synthesis could be framed as controlled curriculum search, requiring control over both which behaviors are explored and which survive verification. Extending this machinery beyond bounded environments with explicit policies and executable outcomes remains important future work.

## A.1 Related Work

Scaling data and environments for generalist agents.
A growing line of work scales agent training by synthesizing executable environments together with their tasks, rewards, or interaction trajectories (Fang et al., 2026; Cai et al., 2025; Tu et al., 2026; Song et al., 2026; Wang et al., 2026a). Complementary efforts construct broad tool-use corpora spanning many interfaces and domains, including ToolMind (Yang et al., 2025), GEM (Xu et al., 2026b), AgentSkiller (Sun et al., 2026), AgenticQwen (Lyu et al., 2026), Klear-AgentForge (Wang et al., 2025), and TOUCAN (Xu et al., 2025). These methods primarily seek breadth and transfer across many worlds. Gradus addresses the complementary problem of achieving systematic behavioral depth within a fixed target environment: it seeks systematic depth within that environment by expanding coverage over its guarded operations and their compositions, and by validating every lesson through deterministic replay in the system itself.

Diverse and verifiable task synthesis for interactive agents.
Existing methods obtain diversity through seed amplification or blueprint generation (Li et al., 2025; Prabhakar et al., 2025), explicit constraints or predefined dimensions (Chen et al., 2026b; Gu et al., 2026), domain-entity-graph traversal (Sun et al., 2026), iterative workflow expansion (Lyu et al., 2026), or execution-first exploration (Wang et al., 2026b; Chen et al., 2026a; Zeng et al., 2026), and assess solvability by exact state equivalence (Zeng et al., 2026), state-difference and action matching (Sun et al., 2026), generated programmatic checkers (Song et al., 2026), constraint checklists (Chen et al., 2026b), or LLM rubrics (Lyu et al., 2026); others transform tasks under instance-specific or oracle-preserving checks (Gao et al., 2026; Xu et al., 2026a). Gradus differs in what it synthesizes and how it is controlled. It makes the system’s guarded operations (policy-conditioned actions and their contrasting outcomes) the reusable unit of synthesis, and expands them within operations, across operations and into cross-space interaction challenges with ascending difficulty, a space broader than fixed dimensions, tool-graph paths, or behavior-tree branches. An adaptive coverage ledger steers generation toward under-covered targets globally, while the task verification is decided by executable environment replay with the model diagnosing rather than judging, and a selected lesson that first fails is recovered through task revision or expanded demonstration search instead of being discarded.

## A.2 Problem Formulation

## Agent-environment interaction.

We study an agent $\pi_{\theta}$ that communicates with a user and acts in a stateful tool environment. Given a user policy $\mu$ , the interaction with the environment from the agent’s perspective is a partially observable Markov decision process

 $\mathcal{M}_{\mu}(E)=\bigl(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{ag}},\mathcal{O}_{\mathrm{ag}},P_{\mu},Z_{\mu},\mathsf{r},\rho_{0},\gamma\bigr).$ 

(3)

The hidden state $x_{t}\in\mathcal{X}$ includes the backend state, task state, and information private to the user. An agent action $a_{t}\in\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{ag}}$ is either a message or a tool call, while its observations include user messages and tool responses. The agent chooses actions from the interaction history $h_{t}$ according to $\pi_{\theta}(a_{t}\mid h_{t})$ . Its standard objective is

 $J_{E}(\pi_{\theta};\mu)=\mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim(\pi_{\theta},\mu,E)}\left[\sum_{t=0}^{H}\gamma^{t}\mathsf{r}_{t}\right],$ 

(4)

where the reward measures successful task completion under the environment’s operating policies.

## Fixed executable environment.

The agent-environment interaction process should be distinguished from the fixed environment specification consumed by Gradus. We represent this specification as

 $E=\bigl(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{tool}},\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{op}},F\bigr),$ 

(5)

where $\mathcal{S}$ is the backend state space described by the state schema, $\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{tool}}$ is the set of parameterized tool actions, $\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{op}}$ is the written operating policy, and

 $F:\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{tool}}\rightarrow\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{O}_{\mathrm{tool}}$ 

is the executable tool semantics that returns a new state and tool response. For example, in the airline domain, $\mathcal{S}$ contains reservation and payment records, $\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{tool}}$ includes actions such as cancel_reservation, and $\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{op}}$ specifies when that action should be executed, refused, or preceded by additional steps.

The environment specification $E$ is fixed: Gradus neither generates nor modifies the definitions of states, tools, policies, or implementation. Combined with a task and user policy $\mu$ , however, $E$ induces the environment interaction process $\mathcal{M}_{\mu}(E)$ in which the agent acts.

## Budgeted Curriculum Construction

Equation 4 assumes a task distribution; the role of Gradus is to construct one with broad behavioral coverage. Let $\mathcal{U}_{E}$ contain the coverage units derived from $E$ , including atomic blueprints, structural evolutions, and interaction variations. Each blueprint $z$ covers a subset $\phi(z)\subseteq\mathcal{U}_{E}$ . For a curriculum $\mathcal{D}$ , define

 $n_{\mathcal{D}}(u)=\sum_{d\in\mathcal{D}}\mathbb{I}[u\in\phi(z_{d})]$ 

(6)

as the number of examples covering unit $u$ .

We formulate curriculum construction as

 $\displaystyle\max_{\mathcal{D}}$ 
 $\displaystyle F(\mathcal{D})=\sum_{u\in\mathcal{U}_{E}}\upsilon\!\left(n_{\mathcal{D}}(u)\right)$ 

(7)

 $\displaystyle\text{subject to}$ 
 $\displaystyle C(\mathcal{D})\leq B,$ 

 $\displaystyle V_{E}(d)=1,\qquad\forall d\in\mathcal{D},$ 

where $\upsilon$ is increasing with diminishing returns, $C(\mathcal{D})\leq B$ imposes the data or construction budget, and $V_{E}$ requires every retained task and solution to pass environment-grounded validation.

Gradus approximates this objective rather than solving it exactly. Its coverage ledger prioritizes underrepresented units, while joint task-solution refinement helps selected behaviors survive construction and enter the verified curriculum. Coverage serves as a controllable data-design objective; its effect on the trained policy is evaluated empirically.

## Supervised fine-tuning.

SFT establishes the behavioral prior: it teaches the student the tool-calling format, the
turn-taking discipline of the environment, and the policy-compliant action sequences that the
validity loop has already certified, giving RL a starting policy whose success rate is high
enough for group-relative comparisons to be informative. Given a verified curriculum
 $\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{SFT}}=\{(x_{i},y_{i})\}_{i=1}^{N}$ , where $x_{i}$ contains the task and
interaction context and $y_{i}$ is the verified assistant trajectory, we optimize the standard
autoregressive negative log-likelihood:

 $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{SFT}}(\theta)=-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{1}{\sum_{t}m_{i,t}}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_{i}|}m_{i,t}\log\pi_{\theta}\!\left(y_{i,t}\mid x_{i},y_{i,<t}\right),$ 

(8)

where $m_{i,t}\in\{0,1\}$ masks non-assistant tokens. Thus, loss is computed only over the
target assistant responses while the complete preceding interaction remains available as
context.

## Reinforcement learning with GRPO.

Starting from the SFT checkpoint,
we apply Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO). For each task $x$ , the old policy
 $\pi_{\mathrm{old}}$ samples a group of $G$ trajectories $\{y_{i}\}_{i=1}^{G}$ by interacting with
the live environment, which receive executable rewards $\{r_{i}\}_{i=1}^{G}$ from the same
environment-grounded checks used during verification. GRPO forms group-normalized advantages
 $\hat{A}_{i}=(r_{i}-\bar{r})/(\operatorname{std}(r)+\delta)$ , with $\delta$ a small constant for
numerical stability, and optimizes

 $\mathcal{J}_{\mathrm{GRPO}}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{x,\{y_{i}\}}\left[\frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^{G}\frac{1}{|y_{i}|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_{i}|}\min\!\left(\rho_{i,t}\hat{A}_{i},\,\operatorname{clip}(\rho_{i,t},1-\epsilon_{\mathrm{low}},1+\epsilon_{\mathrm{high}})\hat{A}_{i}\right)\right],$ 

(9)

where $\rho_{i,t}=\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})/\pi_{\mathrm{old}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})$ 
and the token index $t$ again ranges only over agent-generated tokens.

## A.3 Training Details

## SFT configuration.

We fine-tune all parameters for three epochs with learning rate $1.0\times 10^{-5}$ under a
cosine schedule with warmup ratio $0.1$ and maximum sequence length $32{,}768$ , instantiating
the mask $m_{i,t}$ so that loss falls on the final assistant turn of each multi-turn example.
The checkpoint with the lowest loss on a held-out $5\%$ validation split is selected and, for
the RL stage, initializes the policy. This recipe is held fixed throughout: the same
optimization settings, sequence length, epoch count, and checkpoint-selection rule are applied
to every student size in Table 8 and Table 9 and to every baseline curriculum in
Table 1, with no per-method or per-size tuning. Differences in downstream success
therefore reflect the training data under comparison rather than differences in how each
dataset was consumed.

## RL configuration.

RL uses GRPO advantage estimation with DAPO-style refinements (Yu et al., 2025): asymmetric clip thresholds
 $\epsilon_{\mathrm{low}}=0.2$ and $\epsilon_{\mathrm{high}}=0.25$ , token-level loss
aggregation, and KL regularization with coefficient $0.01$ under a low-variance estimator.
We train with learning rate $1\times 10^{-6}$ , batch size $8$ , and $G=16$ rollouts per task,
under a budget of 32k length per rollout. Rollouts are collected by
interacting with the live environment, so every reward reflects an executed outcome.

## A.4 Algorithm

Algorithm 1 follows the three stages in
Equation 1: constructing guarded operators, searching
for lesson blueprints, and realizing them as verified training examples. The
algorithm marks whether each operation is performed by an LLM or by code. Code
controls search and execution, while the LLM constructs the semantic content.

## A.6 Ablation Details

All ablations fix the proposer, student, data budget, and training procedure. $-$ Guarded operators replaces them with raw policies, tool schemas, and
state schemas, leaving the proposer to infer constraints and scenarios. $-$ Global ledger retains evolution but removes corpus-level (policy,
outcome) tracking and gap selection. $-$ Structural evolution instead generates scenarios in one shot, without structural kernels or iterative
expansion. $-$ Interaction variation sends structurally evolved scenarios directly to task realization. No validity trains on the first
rollout without verification. $+$ Solution: parallel samples $K$ rollouts and accepts one whose outcome matches the reference. $+$ Solution:
reflection additionally gives each rollout up to two evaluator-guided retries based on policy compliance, outcome agreement, and communication quality.
 $+$ Task recovery (full) : task- or reference-side errors trigger re-initialization, while solution-side errors trigger
further demonstration search, until verification succeeds or the recovery budget is exhausted.

## A.7 End-to-End Example: Evolving and Recovering a Policy-Interaction Lesson

This section traces one airline example through guarded-operator extraction, structural evolution, task initialization, reflection-guided simulation, and task-side recovery. The artifacts below are lightly abridged for readability; all policy conditions, task requirements, and state-changing actions are preserved.

## Latent lesson from guarded operators.

The extracted operators reveal a causal interaction that is not apparent from the tool names alone. An old economy reservation without insurance cannot be cancelled for a change of plans. However, changing its cabin to business makes the business-cabin cancellation guard true.

Given the guarded operators, we can directly identify policy interactions between flight modification and cancellation. For example, cancellation is blocked
when an economy reservation is uninsured, was booked more than 24 hours ago, and is being cancelled for a change of plans. Upgrading the reservation to
business class activates a separate cancellation trigger, making the same reservation eligible for cancellation:

 $\displaystyle\underbrace{\text{economy}\wedge\text{uninsured}\wedge\text{old booking}}_{\text{cancellation blocked}}\xrightarrow{\text{upgrade cabin}}\underbrace{\text{business}}_{\text{trigger enabled}}$ 

 $\displaystyle\underbrace{\text{business}}_{\text{trigger enabled}}\xrightarrow{\text{cancel}}\text{status}\leftarrow\text{cancelled}.$ 

## Coverage-guided structural evolution.

The following trace shows how each evolution step preserves the existing
requirements while adding one new structural demand.

Depth 0: Atomic cancellation lesson

The user asks to cancel an uninsured economy reservation booked more than
24 hours ago because their plans changed. None of the four cancellation
triggers holds, so the agent must refuse the cancellation.

 $\Downarrow$ 

Depth 1: Add a logical chain

Retained: the original cancellation request is initially blocked.
Added: The user asks any workarounds to get the flight canceled. The agent must discover an intermediate action that changes
the governing policy condition. It first suggests to upgrade the reservation to business
class, thereby activating the business-cabin cancellation trigger, and then
completes the original cancellation:

 $\texttt{upgrade\_to\_business}\;\longrightarrow\;\texttt{cancel\_reservation}.$ 

 $\Downarrow$ 

Depth 2: Add a parallel sub-task

Retained: the complete upgrade-cancel chain from Depth 1.
Added: In addition to cancel the flight, the user separately asks to update baggage on another
reservation. This request is independent of the cancellation, requiring the
agent to manage two distinct entities and action types without mixing their
state:

 $\bigl(\texttt{upgrade}(A)\rightarrow\texttt{cancel}(A)\bigr)\quad+\quad\texttt{update\_baggage}(B).$ 

 $\Downarrow$ 

Depth 3: Extend the causal chain

Retained: the upgrade–cancel workflow and the independent baggage
update.
Added: After cancelling the original trip and updating baggages on another trip, the user requests
replacement travel. The agent must search the environment and determine that
the requested booking cannot be completed because the target flight lacks
sufficient seats:

 $\texttt{update\_baggage}(B)\quad+\quad\texttt{upgrade}(A)\rightarrow\texttt{cancel}(A)\rightarrow\texttt{replacement denied}.$ 

 $\Downarrow$ 

Depth 4: Expand cancellation into an exception-set loop

Retained: the cabin-upgrade dependency, baggage update, and failed
replacement booking.
Added: the single cancellation is generalized to
“cancel all my reservations except $B$ .” The agent must enumerate the
user’s reservations, preserve the named exception, and apply the cancellation
policy separately to every remaining reservation. Different iterations produce
different outcomes: some reservations can be cancelled directly, one requires
the upgrade–cancel chain, and another cannot be cancelled because a segment
has already been flown.

 $\Downarrow$ 

Final step: Add interaction variation

Retained: all structural actions, dependencies, exceptions, and
outcomes from Depth 4.
Added: the same requirements are surfaced less directly. The user
buries an invalid compensation request among valid operations, accepts the
cabin upgrade only if its cost is below a threshold, describes the baggage
quantity relative to an unknown free allowance, and proceeds only when the
resulting fee is at most $100. The final task therefore requires policy
resistance, state-dependent parameter resolution, conditional action, and
boundary reasoning without changing its underlying structural lesson.

## Reflection-guided solution search.

The first rollout did not immediately solve the task. Rather than accepting it as a demonstration or discarding the lesson, the evaluator returned policy-grounded feedback for another simulation attempt.

Simulation and reflection trace

Reflection retry 1: incomplete policy verification.
The revised agent still denied cancellation of reservation 9FNHDR by

The agent incorporated the additional feedback and checked the flight status.

The two retries illustrate that reflection need not repair every error at once:
each evaluation narrows the remaining failure. Once the agent-side behavior is
corrected, the residual mismatch supplies evidence for routing the example back
to task initialization.

## Verification and task-side routing.

Execution verification compared the corrected rollout with the proposed reference state and found that the disagreement was caused by initialization, not by the agent.

Round-0 verification result

Route: initialization
Diagnosis 1. The task intended 9FNHDR to contain a flown segment, but flight HAT277 was initialized as cancelled, not landed or flying. The agent therefore correctly treated the airline-cancelled flight as a valid cancellation trigger.
Diagnosis 2. The reference assumed that replacement travel could not be booked. However, the request did not rule out basic-economy or connecting alternatives, and the executable environment contained an alternative the user could legitimately accept.
Recovery instruction. Re-initialize the task with a genuinely flown segment and make the replacement flight constraint explicit, or update the reference to include the available alternative.

Gradus returns it to initialization while keeping its structural lineage and interaction requirements fixed.

Among candidates judged valid, we form the set of exam candidates whose reference outcome is matched in at most five of the eight rollouts. Table 12 summarizes the workflow.

## Annotation protocol.

Two annotators independently assessed all 137 frozen candidates: 32 airline, 26 retail, and 79 CAR tasks. Before annotating each domain tasks, annotators read its policy and tool schema to understand the domain regulations and expected agent behaviors. For every task, they were given the task, initial states, and oracle action sequence (reference) and answered the following questions without conferring.

Q1: Correctness. Is the oracle outcome what a competent, policy-compliant assistant should produce?
A. Yes  B. No  C. Cannot tell
Q2: Realism. Would a real user plausibly make this request in this situation and in this way?
A. Natural  B. Plausible but unusual  C. Contrived
Q3: Oracle strictness. Could a policy-compliant assistant legitimately use different steps, parameters, or ordering while reaching the same outcome?
A. No  B. Partly  C. Yes, materially over-specified

## Annotation results.

Table 13 reports pooled label distributions, averaging the two annotators. Correctness is high across all domains: $87.5\%$ for airline, $100\%$ for retail, and $90.5\%$ for CAR. Both annotators marked 25/32, 26/26, and 70/79 tasks correct, respectively; only these jointly accepted tasks are used for the reported frontier-exam results. CAR requests are predominantly natural and have non-strict oracles. The evolved $\tau^{2}$ tasks are more often judged unusual or partly strict, reflecting greater subjectivity about scenario naturalness and interchangeable execution paths.

## Inter-annotator agreement.

Raw agreement and Gwet’s AC1 support the same conclusions
(Table 14). Correctness agreement is substantial
for airline (81.2%, AC1 $=0.789$ ) and near-perfect for retail and CAR
(96.2–100%, AC1 $=0.958$ - $1.000$ ). Agreement on oracle strictness is also
strong (AC1 $=0.728$ , $0.821$ , and $0.986$ ), indicating that annotators
generally agree on whether an oracle is materially over-specified. Realism is
more subjective for airline and retail (AC1 $=0.399$ and $0.596$ ), but
near-perfect for CAR (AC1 $=1.000$ ); the wider confidence intervals on
 $\tau^{2}$ reflect this uncertainty and the smaller audit sets. For realism and
strictness, AC1 is computed over binary acceptable-versus-problematic
judgments, while Table 13 retains the full label
distributions (Gwet, 2008). Overall, the audit provides strong
evidence for semantic correctness and non-strictness, with exception of disagreement concentrated in
subjective judgments of naturalness on Airline and Retail domain.

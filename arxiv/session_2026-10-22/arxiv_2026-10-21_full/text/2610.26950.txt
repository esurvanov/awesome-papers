Abstract

Multi-teacher on-policy distillation (MOPD) integrates domain specialists into a shared student, but determining how strongly each teacher should supervise remains challenging.
Existing discrepancy-based allocation can assign strong supervision even when the student already matches or exceeds the teacher’s task
performance, since behavioral mismatch does not necessarily imply a remaining capability deficit.
We propose Capability-Aware Multi-Teacher On-Policy Distillation (CA-MOPD), which combines specialist merging with capability-guided allocation to account for what the student has already learned.
CA-MOPD first merges reinforcement-learning specialists into a strong student initialization, then uses verifier-estimated teacher–student capability gaps to adjust domain-level distillation strength.
Supervision decreases as capability gaps close and increases when estimated student capability regresses, while dense token-level guidance remains provided by the teachers.
Across six benchmarks spanning mathematics, code generation, and instruction following, CA-MOPD achieves an average score of 32.22, outperforming naive MOPD, Open-MOPD, and the merged initialization by 0.86, 0.83, and 0.74 points, respectively.
Controlled comparisons further support the benefits of merged initialization and capability-gap guidance under matched training budgets.

## 1 Introduction

Reinforcement-learning (RL) post-training has become an effective way to develop language models with strong domain-specific capabilities, including mathematical reasoning, code generation, and instruction following (Shao et al., 2024; Pyatkin et al., 2026; Gao et al., 2026). These capabilities are often learned by independently trained specialists, making it costly to deploy and maintain one model per domain. Multi-teacher on-policy distillation (MOPD) offers a natural alternative by using domain-specific teachers to supervise trajectories generated by a shared student, thereby integrating multiple specialist capabilities into a single model (Agarwal et al., 2024; Ma et al., 2026).

Parameter merging offers another approach to integrating specialist capabilities into a single model by combining their parameters (Ilharco et al., 2022; Yadav et al., 2023). Parameter merging and MOPD are naturally complementary: merging can integrate specialist capabilities before distillation, potentially providing a stronger initialization for the student. However, the extent to which this initialization improves subsequent MOPD remains insufficiently understood.

A key challenge in MOPD is how to allocate optimization across teachers. Open-MOPD addresses this with token-share balancing, discrepancy-based allocation, and reward refresh (Gao et al., 2026), assigning larger weights to domains with greater teacher–student policy discrepancy. However, policy discrepancy measures behavioral mismatch rather than whether the teacher still has a task-level capability advantage. A student can remain distributionally different from a teacher while already matching or exceeding its task performance, especially after specialist merging.

Motivated by this distinction, we introduce Capability-Aware Multi-Teacher On-Policy Distillation (CA-MOPD), which first merges RL specialists with Task Arithmetic (Ilharco et al., 2022) to provide a stronger student initialization for subsequent on-policy distillation and then allocates domain-level distillation strength according to verifier-estimated teacher–student capability gaps. Supervision decreases as the estimated gap closes, while a regression-aware retention mechanism increases it when previously acquired capability deteriorates. We retain token-share balancing to correct response-length imbalance.

Figure 1: Overview of CA-MOPD. (1) Domain RL specialists sharing a MixSFT base are merged into a strong student initialization. (2) Student responses are verified to track capability relative to a fixed, calibrated teacher reference and the student’s historical best. (3) Remaining capability headroom and regression feedback control domain weights, which combine with token balancing and global scaling to modulate dense teacher supervision.

Across six benchmarks spanning mathematics, code generation, and instruction following, CA-MOPD achieves an average score of $32.22$ , compared with $31.36$ for naive MOPD, $31.39$ for Open-MOPD, and $31.48$ for the Task-Arithmetic initialization. The gains are concentrated in mathematics and instruction following, while code performance decreases relative to the merged initialization, reflecting a different cross-domain capability trade-off rather than uniform improvement.

Our main contributions are as follows:

• 

We show that initializing MOPD with merged RL specialists provides a stronger starting point than the shared SFT checkpoint and improves final aggregate performance under a fixed distillation budget.

• 

We distinguish teacher–student policy discrepancy from signed capability gap, showing that behavioral mismatch does not necessarily indicate remaining task capability to transfer.

• 

We propose Capability-Aware Multi-Teacher On-Policy Distillation (CA-MOPD), which combines specialist merging, verifier-based capability estimation, dynamic domain-level allocation, and regression-aware retention.

• 

We show across three capability domains that CA-MOPD improves aggregate performance over naive MOPD, Open-MOPD, and the merged initialization, and analyze the resulting allocation dynamics.

## 2 Background and Motivation

## 2.1 Multi-Teacher On-Policy Distillation

Let $\mathcal{D}$ denote the domain set, $\pi_{\phi_{d}}$ the frozen specialist for domain $d$ , and $\pi_{\theta}$ the shared student.
Given a prompt $x\sim\mathcal{P}_{d}$ , the student samples

 $y\sim\pi_{\theta_{\mathrm{old}}}(\cdot\mid x),$ 

(1)

and the domain teacher $\pi_{\phi_{d}}$ provides dense token-level supervision on this trajectory (Ma et al., 2026; Gao et al., 2026).

The OPD signal $A_{itv}$ corresponds to candidate token $v$ at response position $t$ of example $i$ .
The exact student Top- $K$ construction, candidate normalization, and clipped PPO objective are provided in Appendix A.2.

## 2.2 Discrepancy-Based Distillation Allocation

Domains can differ in response length and convergence rate even under balanced prompt sampling.
Open-MOPD (Gao et al., 2026) addresses these differences through token-share balancing and discrepancy-based allocation.

Let $\mathcal{S}_{it}$ denote the candidate-token support used to compute $A_{itv}$ , $M_{it}$ the valid-response mask, and
 $N_{d}=\sum_{i:d(i)=d}\sum_{t}M_{it}$ the number of valid response tokens from domain $d$ .
For the mean statistic used in our implementation, the domain discrepancy is

 $m_{d}=\frac{1}{N_{d}}\sum_{i:d(i)=d}\sum_{t}M_{it}\left|\sum_{v\in\mathcal{S}_{it}}A_{itv}\right|.$ 

(2)

Larger $m_{d}$ indicates a larger token-level mismatch and therefore receives stronger supervision under discrepancy-following allocation.

## 2.3 Policy Discrepancy Is Not Capability Deficit

Let $V_{d}(x,y)\in[0,1]$ be the domain verifier score for response $y$ to prompt $x$ .
We define policy capability as

 $J_{d}(\pi)=\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{P}_{d},\,y\sim\pi(\cdot\mid x)}\left[V_{d}(x,y)\right],$ 

(3)

and the signed teacher–student capability gap as

 $g_{d}(\theta)=J_{d}(\pi_{\phi_{d}})-J_{d}(\pi_{\theta}).$ 

(4)

Thus, $g_{d}>0$ indicates a teacher capability advantage, while $g_{d}\leq 0$ indicates that the student matches or exceeds the teacher under the same domain distribution and verifier.

Policy discrepancy $m_{d}$ measures behavioral mismatch without establishing a teacher capability advantage:

 $m_{d}>0\quad\mathchoice{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\kern 5.0pt\kern-5.27776pt$\displaystyle\not$\hss}{\Rightarrow}}}{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\kern 5.0pt\kern-5.27776pt$\textstyle\not$\hss}{\Rightarrow}}}{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\kern 3.98611pt\kern-4.45831pt$\scriptstyle\not$\hss}{\Rightarrow}}}{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\kern 3.40282pt\kern-3.95834pt$\scriptscriptstyle\not$\hss}{\Rightarrow}}}\quad g_{d}(\theta)>0.$ 

(5)

This distinction is especially relevant after specialist merging, which can transfer task capabilities while leaving policy discrepancy nonzero.

## 3 Capability-Aware Multi-Teacher On-Policy Distillation

Figure 1 summarizes the CA-MOPD workflow.
CA-MOPD implements the preceding principle in four steps.
It first merges independently trained RL specialists into a strong multi-domain initialization, calibrates each teacher’s task-level capability, tracks the student’s corresponding capability from verifier-scored on-policy rollouts, and converts the remaining capability gap into a domain-level distillation factor.
This factor is then combined with token-share balancing and applied to the common dense OPD objective.

## 3.1 Merged Initialization

We assume that all domain specialists are obtained by applying domain-specific RL to the same mixed-domain SFT checkpoint with parameters $\theta_{0}$ .
Let $\phi_{d}$ denote the parameters of specialist $d$ .
Following Task Arithmetic (Ilharco et al., 2022), we initialize the student as

 $\theta_{\mathrm{merge}}=\theta_{0}+\sum_{d\in\mathcal{D}}\alpha_{d}(\phi_{d}-\theta_{0}),\qquad\alpha_{d}=1.$ 

(6)

The merge transfers part of the specialist updates before on-policy distillation and therefore changes the residual capability profile that distillation must address.
CA-MOPD treats $\theta_{\mathrm{merge}}$ as the starting point and allocates subsequent supervision according to the capability that remains to be transferred rather than assuming that every teacher retains a positive task-level advantage.

## 3.2 Verifier-Calibrated Capability Tracking

Following Eq. (3), we estimate each teacher’s capability once on a held-out calibration set $\mathcal{C}_{d}$ :

 $T_{d}=\widehat{J}_{\mathcal{C}_{d}}(\pi_{\phi_{d}}).$ 

(7)

The merged student is evaluated with the same protocol to initialize

 $C_{d}^{(0)}=\widehat{J}_{\mathcal{C}_{d}}(\pi_{\theta_{\mathrm{merge}}}),\qquad C_{d}^{\star(0)}=C_{d}^{(0)},\qquad H_{d}^{(0)}=[T_{d}-C_{d}^{(0)}]_{+},$ 

(8)

where $[z]_{+}=\max(z,0)$ and $C_{d}^{\star}$ records the best student capability estimate observed so far.
The calibration split, sampling protocol, and score aggregation are detailed in Appendix C.1.

During training, CA-MOPD reuses verifier outcomes from the student’s existing on-policy rollouts rather than generating an additional rollout for capability estimation.
Let $Z_{d}^{(k)}$ denote the domain-level verifier observation at controller update $k$ .
We maintain an exponential moving average

 $C_{d}^{(k)}=\beta C_{d}^{(k-1)}+(1-\beta)Z_{d}^{(k)},$ 

(9)

and update the historical best as

 $C_{d}^{\star(k)}=\max\left\{C_{d}^{\star(k-1)},C_{d}^{(k)}\right\}.$ 

(10)

The online signed gap and positive headroom are then

 $g_{d}^{(k)}=T_{d}-C_{d}^{(k)},\qquad H_{d}^{(k)}=[g_{d}^{(k)}]_{+}.$ 

(11)

Thus, $T_{d}$ provides a fixed teacher reference, while $C_{d}^{(k)}$ tracks the student’s current capability under the same verifier family.

## 3.3 Capability-Aware Distillation Controller

The controller captures two signals: the amount of teacher advantage that remains and whether previously acquired student capability has regressed.

## Remaining capability headroom.

For domains with non-negligible initial headroom, we measure the fraction of the original deficit that remains:

 $r_{d}^{(k)}=\frac{H_{d}^{(k)}}{H_{d}^{(0)}}.$ 

(12)

The corresponding headroom factor is

 $f_{d,\mathrm{head}}^{(k)}=\operatorname{clip}\left(\left(r_{d}^{(k)}\right)^{p},f_{\min},f_{\max}\right).$ 

(13)

As the estimated teacher advantage closes, this factor decreases; a positive floor keeps teacher feedback available instead of permanently disabling the domain.
Numerical handling of near-zero initial headroom and all controller hyperparameters are given in Appendix A.1.

## Regression-aware retention.

Because continued optimization on other domains can reduce a previously acquired capability, we compare the current estimate with its historical best:

 $R_{d}^{(k)}=\left[C_{d}^{\star(k)}-C_{d}^{(k)}-\tau\right]_{+}.$ 

(14)

When $R_{d}^{(k)}>0$ , a bounded guard factor
 $f_{d,\mathrm{guard}}^{(k)}$ increases supervision according to the detected regression.
The final capability factor is

 $f_{d}^{(k)}=\begin{cases}\max\!\left\{f_{d,\mathrm{head}}^{(k)},f_{d,\mathrm{guard}}^{(k)}\right\},&R_{d}^{(k)}>0,\\
f_{d,\mathrm{head}}^{(k)},&R_{d}^{(k)}=0.\end{cases}$ 

(15)

The exact guard mapping, warm-up, and controller constants are provided in Appendix A.1.
The headroom term therefore allocates supervision toward capability that remains to be transferred, while the retention term responds when previously observed capability declines.

## 3.4 Capability-Weighted OPD

Capability allocation does not replace token-share balancing: the former estimates how much task-level transfer remains useful, whereas the latter corrects unequal token contributions caused by response-length differences.
Let $s_{d}^{(k)}$ be the observed response-token share of domain $d$ and $q_{d}$ its target share.
We retain the Open-MOPD balancing factor

 $b_{d}^{(k)}=\frac{q_{d}}{s_{d}^{(k)}}.$ 

(16)

The capability factor and token-balancing factor are combined into the actual per-token multiplier

 $w_{d}^{(k)}=F^{(k)}\frac{b_{d}^{(k)}f_{d}^{(k)}}{\sum_{j}s_{j}^{(k)}b_{j}^{(k)}f_{j}^{(k)}},\qquad F^{(k)}=\max_{j}f_{j}^{(k)}.$ 

(17)

For example $i$ from domain $d(i)$ , CA-MOPD scales the common dense OPD signal as

 $\widehat{A}_{itv}=\operatorname{sg}\left[w_{d(i)}^{(k)}A_{itv}\right].$ 

(18)

The resulting weighted signal is optimized with the token-normalized PPO objective (Schulman et al., 2017) used by the Open-MOPD implementation (Gao et al., 2026).
The full candidate-level OPD signal, clipping rule, and optimization objective are given in Appendix A.2.

Overall, CA-MOPD leaves the token-level teacher supervision mechanism unchanged and modifies only how strongly each domain contributes to optimization.
The teacher provides the local distillation direction, while verifier-estimated capability feedback determines the domain-level optimization pressure.

## 4 Experiments

## 4.1 Experimental Setup

We use SmolLM3-3B (Bakouch et al., 2025) with the MixSFT checkpoint and three RL specialists released by Open-MOPD (Gao et al., 2026). The specialists cover mathematics, code generation, and instruction following and share the same MixSFT initialization.
The main CA-MOPD model is initialized from the Task-Arithmetic merge of the three specialists and trained for 200 outer steps.
We evaluate mathematics on AIME24 and AIME25, code generation on LiveCodeBench v5 and v6 (Jain et al., 2025), and instruction following on IFEval (Zhou et al., 2023) and IFBench (Pyatkin et al., 2026).
All six benchmark scores are reported as percentages, and their equal-weight mean is used as the aggregate score.
Unless otherwise stated, comparisons use the same training budget and evaluation protocol.
Full training, decoding, and benchmark configurations are provided in Table 6 and Appendix C.2.

## 4.2 Overall Multi-Domain Performance

Table 1 shows that CA-MOPD achieves the highest six-benchmark average of $32.22$ , exceeding ParamMerge-TA, Naive MOPD, and Open-MOPD by $0.74$ , $0.86$ , and $0.83$ points, respectively.
It ranks first on both mathematics benchmarks and IFBench, with gains of $2.19$ points on AIME24 and $3.34$ points on IFBench over Open-MOPD.
These results demonstrate the effectiveness of CA-MOPD in integrating specialist capabilities into a single model, achieving stronger aggregate performance than parameter merging alone and existing MOPD baselines.

ParamMerge-TA already provides a strong multi-domain initialization, improving the aggregate score from $24.92$ to $31.48$ before any on-policy distillation. ## 4.4 Ablation of Capability-Gap Guidance

We first examine whether capability gap and policy discrepancy provide equivalent signals for allocating distillation effort. Figure 2 shows that they exhibit substantially different training dynamics. In particular, a nonpositive EMA capability gap coexists with positive policy discrepancy at 170, 80, and 129 of the 200 recorded steps for mathematics, code, and instruction following, respectively. This behavior is especially common because the ParamMerge-TA initialization already approaches or exceeds the calibrated teacher capability in several domains, while substantial token-level discrepancy remains. Thus, policy discrepancy can persist even when little or no positive teacher capability advantage remains.

## 4.5 Training Allocation Dynamics

Figure 3 shows how capability feedback modulates training allocation in the ParamMerge-initialized run.
We report the weighted token share
 $a_{d}^{(k)}=s_{d}^{(k)}w_{d}^{(k)}/\sum_{j}s_{j}^{(k)}w_{j}^{(k)}$ ,
which summarizes relative token weighting after response-length correction, but should not be interpreted as measured gradient contribution.
Table 4 relates these shares and regression-guard activity to domain-level performance changes over 200 training steps.

To isolate the guard’s effect on the controller output, we reconstruct the headroom-only factor on the same recorded capability trajectory, preserving clipping and warm-up.
The guard increases the final factor at 172, 92, and 107 of the 200 recorded steps for mathematics, code, and instruction following, respectively.
This effect is most pronounced in mathematics, where the initially nonpositive teacher advantage would otherwise keep the headroom-only factor near its floor.
Under the full controller, mathematics improves from $23.52$ to $25.03$ , while instruction following improves from $47.67$ to $49.45$ .
These trajectories are consistent with the guard retaining useful supervision even when the estimated teacher advantage is small.

Allocation magnitude, however, does not directly predict downstream gain.
Code receives the largest average weighted token share ( $40.13\%$ ) yet decreases by $1.08$ points, reflecting domain-specific factors such as teacher-signal quality, cross-domain interference, and optimization dynamics.
We therefore interpret allocation as a domain-conditioned response to the estimated capability state rather than as a universal predictor of downstream improvement.
Importantly, the observed trajectories confirm that CA-MOPD produces substantially different supervision patterns across domains in response to their evolving capability states, which is precisely the behavior targeted by the proposed adaptive allocation mechanism.

## 4.6 Computational Cost

CA-MOPD estimates online capability from verifier outcomes on existing student rollouts and uses these estimates to reweight domain-level supervision, without requiring additional rollout generation.
Table 5 reports the mean per-step runtime of the main training components over the 200 steps with available timing records.
Student rollout generation and actor optimization together account for $83.97\%$ of the measured step time, while model forward passes for computing token probabilities used in distillation account for $12.62\%$ .
Online capability feedback takes $33.30$ seconds per step, corresponding to $3.20\%$ of the measured step time.
These results indicate that online capability feedback constitutes a small fraction of the training runtime in this setting.

## 5 Related Work

## On-policy and multi-teacher distillation.

Knowledge distillation transfers teacher behavior to a student (Hinton et al., 2015; Kim and Rush, 2016), while GKD extends distillation to student-generated trajectories (Agarwal et al., 2024). MOPD further integrates multiple domain-specialized RL teachers into a shared student (Ma et al., 2026), with recent variants exploring richer teacher organizations (Wang et al., 2026; Yin et al., 2026). Our work focuses instead on how strongly each teacher should supervise once specialist capabilities have been partially integrated.

## Adaptive allocation in multi-teacher distillation.

Open-MOPD combines token-share balancing with discrepancy-based allocation and reward refresh (Gao et al., 2026), while D3-MOPD adapts training allocation using distillation trajectories and convergence dynamics (Sun et al., 2026). These approaches primarily infer optimization demand from distillation-side signals. SPOT uses verifier-scored student continuations to calibrate token-level distillation targets (Qu et al., 2026). CA-MOPD uses verifier outcomes to estimate signed domain-level capability gaps and adjust distillation strength while retaining the teacher-provided token-level signal.

## Model merging.

Weight-space merging integrates independently fine-tuned models without increasing inference-time model count, including Model Soups (Wortsman et al., 2022), Task Arithmetic (Ilharco et al., 2022), and TIES-Merging (Yadav et al., 2023). Rather than introducing a new merging operator, we use Task Arithmetic to obtain a strong initialization and study how subsequent on-policy distillation can refine the remaining capability gaps.

## 6 Limitations

CA-MOPD depends on verifier scores to estimate capability, so its allocation quality is limited by the calibration distribution and verification procedure, while domain-level averages may hide example-level differences. The current controller also relies on smoothed point estimates and historical-best tracking rather than calibrated uncertainty, making small capability gaps potentially sensitive to noise. In addition, CA-MOPD continuously reweights domains but does not skip teacher computation, so it provides neither hard non-forgetting guarantees nor automatic compute savings. Finally, our evaluation is limited to one 3B model family, three domains, and known domain routing; broader generalization to larger models, heterogeneous teachers, more domains, and learned routing remains open.

## 7 Conclusion

In this work, we presented CA-MOPD, which combines specialist merging with capability-aware multi-teacher on-policy distillation. Our experiments show that initializing CA-MOPD with merged RL specialists provides a stronger starting point and improves final aggregate performance over initialization from the shared SFT checkpoint under the same training budget. Building on this initialization, CA-MOPD uses verifier-estimated teacher–student capability gaps and student regression signals to adapt domain-level supervision, accounting for capabilities already integrated into the student. Across six benchmarks spanning mathematics, code generation, and instruction following, the resulting approach achieves higher aggregate performance than naive MOPD, Open-MOPD, and the Task-Arithmetic merged initialization, although gains are not uniform across domains. These findings support combining parameter merging with subsequent on-policy distillation and highlight the importance of considering both the student’s initial capabilities and the allocation of teacher supervision.

## Ethics Statement

This work studies the integration of publicly available language-model checkpoints on mathematics, code, and instruction-following tasks. It does not involve human-subject experiments or the collection of private personal data. The resulting methods may inherit harmful behaviors or biases from their base models, teachers, training data, and verifiers. We therefore do not claim that improved benchmark integration implies broader safety or reliability, and recommend task-specific safety evaluation before deployment.

## Appendix A Complete Method Details

This appendix provides the complete capability controller, optimization
objective, and training procedure omitted from the main text for clarity.

## A.1 Capability Controller

Recall that $T_{d}$ denotes the fixed calibrated capability of teacher $d$ ,
 $C_{d}^{(k)}$ is the exponentially smoothed student capability estimate, and

 $H_{d}^{(k)}=[T_{d}-C_{d}^{(k)}]_{+}$ 

denotes the remaining positive teacher advantage.

For domains with non-negligible initial headroom, the controller measures the
fraction of the initial capability deficit that remains:

 $r_{d}^{(k)}=\frac{H_{d}^{(k)}}{H_{d}^{(0)}},\qquad H_{d}^{(0)}>\epsilon.$ 

(19)

When the initial headroom is close to zero, directly dividing by
 $H_{d}^{(0)}$ would make the controller sensitive to estimation noise.
We therefore use

 $r_{d}^{(k)}=\begin{cases}H_{d}^{(k)}/H_{d}^{(0)},&H_{d}^{(0)}>\epsilon,\\[3.0pt]
0,&H_{d}^{(0)}\leq\epsilon\ \text{and}\ H_{d}^{(k)}\leq\epsilon,\\[3.0pt]
H_{d}^{(k)}/B_{d}^{(k)},&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}$ 

(20)

where

 $B_{d}^{(k)}=\max\left\{|T_{d}|,|C_{d}^{\star(k)}|,\epsilon\right\}.$ 

(21)

The corresponding capability-headroom factor is

 $f_{d,\mathrm{head}}^{(k)}=\operatorname{clip}\left(\left(r_{d}^{(k)}\right)^{p},f_{\min},f_{\max}\right).$ 

(22)

We use $p=1$ , $f_{\min}=0.05$ , and $f_{\max}=2$ .
The positive floor prevents a domain from being completely removed from
training when its estimated average capability deficit has closed.

## Regression-aware retention.

A small current teacher–student gap does not imply that the corresponding
capability will remain stable while other domains continue updating the shared
model. We therefore retain the historical-best student estimate
 $C_{d}^{\star(k)}$ and define

 $R_{d}^{(k)}=\left[C_{d}^{\star(k)}-C_{d}^{(k)}-\tau\right]_{+},$ 

(23)

where $\tau=0.02$ .

When regression is detected, the retention factor is

 $f_{d,\mathrm{guard}}^{(k)}=\min\left\{f_{\max},1+\lambda\frac{R_{d}^{(k)}}{B_{d}^{(k)}}\right\},$ 

(24)

with $\lambda=1$ .

The complete capability factor is

 $f_{d}^{(k)}=\begin{cases}\max\left\{f_{d,\mathrm{head}}^{(k)},f_{d,\mathrm{guard}}^{(k)}\right\},&R_{d}^{(k)}>0,\\[3.0pt]
f_{d,\mathrm{head}}^{(k)},&R_{d}^{(k)}=0.\end{cases}$ 

(25)

We override the controller with $f_{d}^{(k)}=1$ during the first three
controller updates to avoid reacting immediately to noisy capability
estimates. The EMA coefficient is $\beta=0.9$ , and the target token shares are
 $q_{d}=1/3$ for the three domains used in our experiments.

The historical-best mechanism should be interpreted as a bounded response to
estimated regression rather than a hard non-forgetting guarantee. The
controller continuously adjusts supervision and does not permanently disable
or reactivate domains.

## A.2 Capability-Weighted OPD Objective

All MOPD variants in our experiments use the same dense token-level teacher
signal and differ in how domain-level supervision is weighted.

For response position $t$ of example $i$ , the candidate support
 $\mathcal{S}_{it}$ consists of the old student’s Top- $K$ predictions, with
 $K=16$ . The dense distillation signal follows Open-MOPD (Gao et al., 2026):

 $A_{itv}=\operatorname{sg}\left[p_{itv}\left(\ell^{T}_{itv}-\ell^{\mathrm{old}}_{itv}\right)\right],\qquad v\in\mathcal{S}_{it}.$ 

(26)

CA-MOPD scales this signal using the effective domain multiplier
 $w_{d(i)}^{(k)}$ :

 $\widehat{A}_{itv}=\operatorname{sg}\left[w_{d(i)}^{(k)}A_{itv}\right].$ 

(27)

Let $s_{d}^{(k)}$ denote the observed valid-response-token share of domain $d$ 
in the current rollout batch. The token-balancing factor is

 $b_{d}^{(k)}=\frac{q_{d}}{s_{d}^{(k)}}.$ 

(28)

The final token multiplier is

 $w_{d}^{(k)}=F^{(k)}\frac{b_{d}^{(k)}f_{d}^{(k)}}{\sum_{j}s_{j}^{(k)}b_{j}^{(k)}f_{j}^{(k)}},\qquad F^{(k)}=\max_{j}f_{j}^{(k)}.$ 

(29)

This normalization satisfies

 $\sum_{d}s_{d}^{(k)}w_{d}^{(k)}=F^{(k)}.$ 

(30)

Thus, $f_{d}^{(k)}$ and $w_{d}^{(k)}$ represent different quantities.
The former captures capability-aware allocation, whereas the latter additionally
incorporates token-share balancing and global scaling. Likewise, an effective
weighted token share describes relative weighted-token contribution and should
not be interpreted as a direct measurement of gradient-vector magnitude or
parameter-space influence.

For optimization, let

 $\rho_{itv}(\theta)=\exp\left(\operatorname{clip}\left(\ell^{\theta}_{itv}-\ell^{\mathrm{old}}_{itv},-20,20\right)\right)$ 

(31)

denote the candidate-level likelihood ratio.

We use the PPO clipped surrogate (Schulman et al., 2017) with the asymmetric clipping bounds of our Open-MOPD-based implementation (Gao et al., 2026):

 $P_{itv}=\max\left\{-\widehat{A}_{itv}\rho_{itv},-\widehat{A}_{itv}\operatorname{clip}\left(\rho_{itv},0.8,1.28\right)\right\}.$ 

(32)

We apply dual clipping (Ye et al., 2020) with coefficient $c=3$ , giving the candidate loss

 $\ell_{itv}=\begin{cases}\min\left\{-3\widehat{A}_{itv},P_{itv}\right\},&\widehat{A}_{itv}<0,\\[3.0pt]
P_{itv},&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}$ 

(33)

For an optimizer batch $\mathcal{B}$ , the final token-normalized objective is

 $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{CA\text{-}MOPD}}(\theta)=\frac{\sum_{i\in\mathcal{B}}\sum_{t}M_{it}\sum_{v\in\mathcal{S}_{it}}\ell_{itv}}{\sum_{i\in\mathcal{B}}\sum_{t}M_{it}}.$ 

(34)

Entropy regularization and reference-policy KL regularization are disabled in
our main configuration. The dense OPD objective, candidate support, and
optimizer formulation are shared across the compared MOPD variants; CA-MOPD
changes the domain-level weighting mechanism rather than the underlying
token-level teacher signal.

## A.3 Training Algorithm

Algorithm A.3 summarizes the complete training procedure.

Algorithm 1: CA-MOPD training

Input:
shared checkpoint $\theta_{0}$ ;
specialists $\{\phi_{d}\}_{d\in\mathcal{D}}$ ;
calibration sets $\{\mathcal{C}_{d}\}$ ;
training prompt distributions $\{\mathcal{P}_{d}\}$ .

1. Merge specialists.

Construct

 $\theta_{\mathrm{merge}}=\theta_{0}+\sum_{d\in\mathcal{D}}(\phi_{d}-\theta_{0}),$ 

and initialize the student with
 $\theta\leftarrow\theta_{\mathrm{merge}}$ .

2. Calibrate capability.

For each domain $d$ :

 $T_{d}\leftarrow\widehat{J}_{\mathcal{C}_{d}}(\pi_{\phi_{d}}),\qquad C_{d}^{(0)}\leftarrow\widehat{J}_{\mathcal{C}_{d}}(\pi_{\theta}),$ 

 $C_{d}^{\star(0)}\leftarrow C_{d}^{(0)},\qquad H_{d}^{(0)}\leftarrow[T_{d}-C_{d}^{(0)}]_{+}.$ 

3. Repeat for each outer training step $k$ .

Sample prompts from all domains and generate student responses using
 $\pi_{\theta_{\mathrm{old}}}$ .

For each domain:

1. 

aggregate verifier scores into $Z_{d}^{(k)}$ ;

2. 

update

 $C_{d}^{(k)}=\beta C_{d}^{(k-1)}+(1-\beta)Z_{d}^{(k)};$ 

3. 

update the historical best

 $C_{d}^{\star(k)}=\max\left\{C_{d}^{\star(k-1)},C_{d}^{(k)}\right\};$ 

4. 

compute capability headroom and regression;

5. 

obtain capability factor $f_{d}^{(k)}$ ;

6. 

compute observed token share $s_{d}^{(k)}$ and effective multiplier
 $w_{d}^{(k)}$ .

Evaluate the corresponding teacher on the student’s Top- $16$ candidate
tokens, construct $\widehat{A}_{itv}$ , and optimize
Eq. (34).

Output:
the final shared student $\pi_{\theta}$ .

## B.2 Controller State and Checkpointing

The capability controller is stateful. For each domain, we store
 $C_{d}^{(k)}$ , $C_{d}^{\star(k)}$ , $H_{d}^{(0)}$ , and the controller-update
counter. Resuming training therefore requires restoring the controller state
together with the model, optimizer, data-sampler, and rollout states.
Restoring model parameters alone can lead to a different subsequent weighting
trajectory even when the model checkpoint is identical.

Teacher references $T_{d}$ and initial student estimates $C_{d}^{(0)}$ are tied
to the corresponding calibration split and generation configuration. For
reproducibility, we record the calibration split identifier together with the
cached capability statistics.

## Appendix C Capability Estimation and Evaluation Protocol

## C.1 Capability Calibration

For each domain $d$ , we construct a calibration set
 $\mathcal{C}_{d}$ containing 256 prompts. These prompts are excluded from
subsequent CA-MOPD training.

For each calibration prompt $x$ , both the frozen teacher and the initial
student independently generate four responses using the same decoding
protocol. Let $y_{x,1},\ldots,y_{x,4}$ denote these responses. We first
compute the prompt-level capability estimate

 $\widehat{J}_{x}(\pi)=\frac{1}{4}\sum_{r=1}^{4}V_{d}(x,y_{x,r}),$ 

(35)

and then average equally across prompts:

 $\widehat{J}_{\mathcal{C}_{d}}(\pi)=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{C}_{d}|}\sum_{x\in\mathcal{C}_{d}}\widehat{J}_{x}(\pi).$ 

(36)

This two-stage aggregation gives equal weight to every prompt regardless of
the number of sampled responses.

The teacher reference

 $T_{d}=\widehat{J}_{\mathcal{C}_{d}}(\pi_{\phi_{d}})$ 

(37)

is computed once and remains fixed throughout training. The initial student
capability

 $C_{d}^{(0)}=\widehat{J}_{\mathcal{C}_{d}}(\pi_{\theta_{\mathrm{merge}}})$ 

(38)

initializes both the EMA state and the historical-best state.

During subsequent training, no additional student rollout is generated solely
for capability estimation. Instead, the controller reuses verifier outcomes
from responses already produced by the on-policy rollout stage.

Calibration scores and online capability estimates should therefore be
interpreted as operational capability estimates under the specified prompt
distribution and verifier, rather than as claims about task performance
outside that evaluation setting.

Abstract

Hallucination stress tests can yield similar average scores across language models even when different prompts elicit hallucinations in each model. We develop a procedure that iteratively edits source-grounded seed prompts to expose hallucinations in a target model. An LLM editor revises each prompt in response to the target’s answer and a judge’s category-specific score and explanation. In the main setting, the target also receives feedback on its previous response. Experiments cover 500 seeds from seven datasets and ten topics, with intrinsic and extrinsic hallucinations evaluated across open-weight and closed-source models. Editing substantially increases judge-rated severity over the seeds. In the baseline configuration, retained responses fall in the judge’s strong-hallucination bin in 77.2% of runs targeting extrinsic hallucination and 74.2% targeting intrinsic hallucination. Later editing rounds account for more than half of the mean score increase. Among 219 prompts that pass model-assisted source-validity checks, 89 elicit responses containing unsupported claims. Across five models, 200 curated prompts yield similar average severity scores, although pairwise Spearman correlations between prompt-level scores are only $\rho=0.0387$ – $0.1897$ . Curated prompts can be reused across models, but their diagnostic value depends on source validity and the failures they expose in each model.

## 1 Introduction

Large language models (LLMs) can produce fluent answers that misrepresent the supplied evidence or introduce unsupported claims (Ji et al., 2023; Maynez et al., 2020; Farquhar et al., 2024). Hallucination benchmarks provide standardized tasks for assessing these failures (Niu et al., 2024; Li et al., 2023). For model diagnosis, a further question is whether prompts that expose hallucinations in one model reveal the same failures in another. Similar overall severity may conceal substantial differences in the prompts that elicit those failures.

Automated adversarial prompt search provides a starting point for investigating model vulnerabilities (Chao et al., 2025; Mehrotra et al., 2024; Liu et al., 2024). We study target-aware curation of hallucination stress tests from source-grounded seed prompts. The aim is to find prompts that expose failures in a specified model and determine what transfers when those prompts are presented to other models. Increased severity alone does not establish the diagnostic value of an edited prompt, since editing can also change the task or remove information needed to answer it. Our evaluation considers both the induced responses and the validity of the edited tasks.

The curation procedure comprises a prompt editor, a target model, and a hallucination judge. The editor revises a seed prompt, the target answers it, and the judge returns a category-specific severity score and explanation. Subsequent edits respond to the target’s answer and the judge’s feedback. From the second editing round onward, the target also receives feedback on its previous response and instructions to reduce hallucination. Each run retains the prompt associated with the highest-scoring response. We distinguish these feedback-conditioned search outcomes from transfer evaluations, in which saved prompts are presented to other targets without further editing or prior judge feedback.

We evaluate 500 seed prompts across ten topics, drawn from RAGTruth (Niu et al., 2024), BoolQ (Clark et al., 2019), ClimRetrieve (Schimanski et al., 2024), SportQA (Xia et al., 2024), LegalQA11

Experiments include open-weight and closed-source models and two operational hallucination categories. Intrinsic hallucinations concern task-internal interpretation or reasoning errors, while extrinsic hallucinations concern additions unsupported by the source.

Editing substantially increases judge-rated severity. In the baseline configuration, 77.2% of retained responses from runs targeting extrinsic hallucination and 74.2% from runs targeting intrinsic hallucination fall in the judge’s strong-hallucination bin. Later rounds account for 53.85% of the mean score increase, indicating that much of the observed gain occurs after the first rewrite. Human rescoring and source-validity audits help assess what these scores represent. Among 219 prompts passing model-assisted checks for answerability, source preservation, logical consistency, and absence of extra-source requests, 89 corresponding responses still contain unsupported claims. The occurrence of these failures within the screened subset shows that the search can expose unsupported responses even when the edited prompts satisfy stronger source-validity criteria.

Transfer exposes a distinction between overall difficulty and individual failure cases. Across five models, between 107 and 114 of the same 200 curated prompts receive scores of at least 80. Scores on individual prompts have pairwise Spearman correlations ranging from $\rho=0.0387$ to $0.1897$ . The prompt set remains difficult across models, but the prompts associated with the most severe responses differ. This finding motivates examining individual failures when curated prompts are reused for model diagnosis.

The paper contributes a target-aware curation procedure and an empirical account of its search behavior, source validity, and cross-model transfer. The resulting tests complement fixed benchmarks by examining susceptibility to adaptive prompt editing. Their scores describe observed failures under the search and evaluation protocols, rather than hallucination prevalence in ordinary use.

## 2 Related Work

Prior work has shown that neural generation systems can produce fluent but unfaithful or unsupported content, motivating extensive work on hallucination detection and evaluation (Ji et al., 2023; Maynez et al., 2020; Farquhar et al., 2024). Existing benchmarks vary in task setting and hallucination definition. RAGTruth studies hallucinations in retrieval-augmented generation (Niu et al., 2024). Other datasets are built from Wikipedia or focus on specific domains such as biomedicine (Dhuliawala et al., 2024; Li et al., 2024). Dialogue-oriented and factuality-oriented benchmarks further show that hallucination evaluation depends heavily on the task and the notion of support used by the benchmark (Li et al., 2023; Chen et al., 2024).
Constructing broad hallucination benchmarks is expensive. Many datasets require collecting model outputs and then identifying hallucinations through human annotation or manual verification. This is true for manually curated resources like RAGTruth (Niu et al., 2024) and ChineseFactEval (Wang et al., 2023), as well as semi-automatic pipelines like UHGEval (Liang et al., 2024) and HaluEval 2.0 (Li et al., 2023), which still require substantial human validation. These resources are valuable for standardized evaluation, but their construction costs make it difficult to quickly expand coverage over topics, models, and hallucination categories.
Prompt perturbation and adversarial prompt search offer another route to exposing model failures. Longpre et al. (2021) introduce entity-based knowledge conflicts to increase the likelihood of unfaithful generations. In model safety, automated jailbreak methods such as PAIR (Chao et al., 2025) and TAP (Mehrotra et al., 2024) iteratively refine adversarial prompts using target-model feedback, while AutoDAN (Liu et al., 2024) automatically searches for effective and semantically meaningful jailbreak prompts. These studies establish iterative and automated prompt optimization as an effective mechanism for discovering model vulnerabilities. Our work applies this general paradigm to a different objective: curating source-grounded hallucination stress tests. Rather than optimizing prompts to bypass safety policies, our editor uses target responses and hallucination-specific judge feedback to identify prompt transformations that expose intrinsic or extrinsic grounding failures while retaining the original source as the basis for evaluation.

## 3 Hallucination Measurement

We use two measurements. The first is an LLM-as-judge score for category-specific hallucination severity. The second is the semantic-uncertainty/verbal-uncertainty gap, abbreviated SU–VU (Ji et al., 2025). We treat both as operational signals, not as ground truth labels.

## 3.1 Operational Hallucination Categories

The terms intrinsic and extrinsic are used differently across parts of the hallucination literature. We define them operationally for source-grounded QA. An intrinsic hallucination is a task-internal interpretation or reasoning error between the prompt, the source material, and the final response. An extrinsic hallucination is an unsupported addition that goes beyond what can be directly inferred from the source. These definitions are used to condition both the editor and the judge. They are intended to support controlled curation, not to resolve terminology across prior work.

System: You are a helpful assistant.
User: Intrinsic hallucination occurs when a generated output involves incorrect reasoning between interpreting the prompt and producing the final response, such as misinterpreting the prompt or making wrong assumptions. Extrinsic hallucination occurs when the generated output includes additional information not directly inferred from the source material. Reply yes if you understand.
User: Here are 8 few-shot training examples. Please read them all, then reply with a brief confirmation that you understand.
User: Here is a prompt and its corresponding response. Here is the prompt: [prompt]. Here is the response: [response]. Based on your knowledge of [hallucination type], are there any instances of [hallucination type] in the response when looking at the prompt and source material? Do not say that there are instances of [hallucination type] if it is not clearly [hallucination type]. If you are uncertain, err on the side of it not hallucinating.
User: Based on your reasoning above, give me a score between 0 and 100 on how much the response is a [hallucination type] to the prompt, where 100 means that it is purely [hallucination type]. Please only respond with an integer between 0 and 100.
User: Are you sure about this score? Give me your reasoning. Reread the prompt and response again. Should you change your score?
User: Now give me the final score you decided on. Please just give me the integer between 0 and 100, nothing else.
User: Please give a reason for why you gave the final score.

## 3.2 LLM-as-Judge Scoring

The judge receives a prompt–response pair, a hallucination category, and few-shot examples. It assigns an integer score from 0 to 100 and provides a short explanation. A score of 0 indicates no hallucination in the specified category, while 100 indicates a response that is entirely hallucinated under that category. Figure 1 shows the template. Unless otherwise noted, we use LLaMA-3.1-8B-Instruct, denoted as LLaMA-8B, as the judge.

The judge is instructed to label only clear hallucinations and to assign a score of 0 when the target correctly states that the source lacks sufficient information. It also reconsiders its initial score before returning the final score and explanation, reducing one-pass scoring errors while providing feedback for subsequent editing.

Raw judge scores are not calibrated probabilities. We therefore also convert them into three severity bins corresponding to no, weak, and strong hallucination. The number of bins and thresholds are selected from a pooled analysis of judge scores on 2,200 model generations from the seven main experiments. We run K-means for $K=2,\ldots,6$ and select $K=3$ using an elbow diagnostic based on the distance-to-endpoint-line rule. We then repeat $K=3$ clustering separately for each judge model and set cutoffs at the midpoints between ordered cluster centers. Appendix A reports the resulting thresholds.

## 3.3 Human Validation of Judge Outputs

We conduct a small human validation study on Amazon Mechanical Turk (MTurk).33
 3

 https://www.mturk.com/ The goal is not to establish the judge as ground truth, but to assess whether its explanations and relative score orderings are generally reasonable. The questionnaires are provided in Appendix C.

For explanation validation, we sampled 20 intrinsic and 20 extrinsic cases. Human validators judged 90% of intrinsic and 95% of extrinsic explanations to be correct and comprehensive, with inter-annotator agreement (IAA) of 0.85 and 0.95, respectively. For score validation, annotators compared response pairs and identified which response was more detached from the prompt for extrinsic hallucination or more logically inconsistent for intrinsic hallucination. Majority votes agreed with the judge’s ordering for 85% of extrinsic pairs and 100% of intrinsic pairs, with IAA of 0.64 in both settings.

We further asked an NLP expert to independently rescore 50 retained prompt-response pairs, including 25 extrinsic and 25 intrinsic instances. Across all 50 pairs, the median absolute difference between the human and LLaMA-8B judge scores was 4 points, and 45/50 pairs differed by no more than 10 points. Scores were positively correlated ( $\rho=0.6835$ , $p=4.5093\times 10^{-8}$ ). For extrinsic hallucination, the median absolute difference was 3 points and $\rho=0.5976$ ( $p=0.0016$ ); for intrinsic hallucination, the corresponding values were 5 points and $\rho=0.7867$ ( $p=3.1133\times 10^{-6}$ ). Among the 41 responses scored at least 80 by LLaMA-8B, the human evaluator also scored 35 at least 80, while only three received human scores below 70.

We additionally perform a model-assisted claim-level audit of the 1,000 retained responses. Across 3,821 extracted factual claims, 1,352 are unsupported by the supplied source, and 577 responses contain at least one unsupported claim. Thus, high judge-rated severity frequently corresponds to identifiable source-unsupported content rather than only judge-specific preferences.

## 3.4 SU–VU as a Complementary Diagnostic

We follow Ji et al. (2025) and compute the SU–VU gap as an uncertainty-based diagnostic. Semantic uncertainty measures how much sampled responses to the same input vary semantically. Verbal uncertainty measures how much uncertainty the model expresses in the responses. Hallucination-related inconsistency can arise when semantic variability exceeds expressed uncertainty.

Across 2,200 query–response pairs from the seven main experiments, the judge score has a weak positive correlation with the signed SU–VU gap. The Pearson correlation is $r=0.0731$ ( $p=0.0153$ ) for extrinsic hallucination and $r=0.1400$ ( $p<0.0001$ ) for intrinsic hallucination. We therefore do not use SU–VU as an independent validator of the judge. We use it to characterize the uncertainty profile of induced hallucinations. A prompt can elicit a high judge score without increasing the SU–VU gap if the target repeatedly produces the same unsupported inference, or if it hallucinates while hedging. Appendix D includes the SU–VU plot and examples of such cases.

## 4 Target-Aware Stress-Test Curation

Figure 2: Target-aware hallucination stress-test curation. Underlined text indicates the controlled hallucination category. Text in red is provided to agents after the first editing round.

Given a seed prompt $x_{0}$ , target model $T$ , prompt editor $E$ , judge $J$ , and hallucination category $h$ , the method searches for an edited prompt that elicits a stronger hallucination from $T$ . At iteration $t$ , the target generates response $y_{t}$ to prompt $x_{t}$ . The judge evaluates $(x_{t},y_{t})$ for category $h$ and returns score $s_{t}\in[0,100]$ and explanation $r_{t}$ . The editor then proposes $x_{t+1}=E(x_{t},y_{t},s_{t},r_{t},h)$ .
The retained stress-test prompt is the edit associated with the highest-scoring response.

The editor receives the current prompt, previous target response, judge score, and explanation, together with the specified hallucination category. In the main experiments, it also receives two randomly sampled RAGTruth examples as guidance.

From the second editing round onward, the target receives the current prompt together with its previous judge score and explanation and is explicitly instructed to reduce hallucination. Thus, later edits must induce another failure even after the target has been informed of its previous error, making the main setting a conservative stress test. Appendix E reports a no-target-feedback variant, where the editor still receives judge feedback but the target does not; induction success rises to 96% for intrinsic and 98% for extrinsic hallucination.

Each run retains the highest-scoring prompt-response pair and stops after two consecutive score decreases. Open-weight configurations use at most five editing rounds, while closed-source configurations use at most ten. The saved artifact is the edited prompt rather than the judge-feedback context. In the transfer experiments, saved prompts are replayed on new targets without further editing.

Because the same judge can guide editing and evaluate the resulting response, judge coupling is a potential concern. We address this through independent human rescoring, alternative judges, claim-level verification, SU–VU diagnostics, and cross-target transfer, and therefore treat judge scores as operational measurements rather than ground truth.

We sample 500 source-grounded seed prompts from seven publicly available datasets spanning ten topics (Table 1). To assign topic labels consistently, we manually define the topic taxonomy and then use GPT-4o-mini to classify each instance into one category. We remove instances that naturally invite binary answers using regular-expression matching, since hallucinations in those cases can often be induced trivially by reversing the correct answer.

## 5.3 Evaluation Protocol

We evaluate hallucination-induction success, prompt proximity, and editing-strategy diversity.

For hallucination-induction success, we compare the target model’s responses before and after editing using the judge score, severity bin, and SU–VU gap. An attempt is successful if the retained response falls into a higher hallucination bin than the iteration-0 response. It is also successful if both responses are in the strong-hallucination bin and the retained response has both a higher judge score and a higher SU–VU gap.

For prompt proximity, we compute cosine similarity between SentenceBERT embeddings of the original and retained prompts. This measures topical proximity rather than semantic equivalence: high similarity indicates that the editor generally remains close to the seed but does not guarantee preservation of answerability.

For editing-strategy diversity, we identify six common, potentially overlapping strategies:

1. 

Removing instructional context. Removes grounding phrases such as “based on the source.”

2. 

Removing verification guardrails. Removes instructions to avoid unsupported claims or abstain when evidence is insufficient.

3. 

Asking for unsupported detail. Requests details or explanations beyond the source.

4. 

Introducing subjective framing. Recasts the task as subjective or opinion-seeking.

5. 

Context elongation. Increases prompt length by more than 20% with non-essential content.

6. 

Context shrinking. Removes source content, reducing prompt length by at least 20%.

Prompt editing uses max_new_tokens=256, temperature $0.7$ , and sampling. SU–VU evaluation uses ten sampled responses. The reference response uses temperature $0.1$ and top_p=1.0; sampled responses use temperature $1.0$ , top_p=0.9, sampling, and a 256-token limit.

## 6.1 Hallucination Severity After Editing

The main experiment uses Mistral-7B as both target and prompt editor, with LLaMA-8B as judge. From the 500 seed prompts in Table 1, we curate 500 extrinsic and 500 intrinsic stress-test prompts.

Editing substantially increases hallucination severity. In the baseline setting, 77.20% of extrinsic and 74.20% of intrinsic instances end in the strong-hallucination bin, with induction-success rates of 71.40% and 66.40%, respectively. Mean post-editing scores are 82.15 for extrinsic and 78.85 for intrinsic hallucination, compared with 25.11 and 29.06 on the original seeds. Figure 3 shows a clear shift toward high-severity responses.

The conclusion is robust to alternative success criteria. Across all 1,000 baseline instances, 81.60% increase in score, 77.60% increase by at least 10 points, and 70.80% increase by at least 20 points; 78.60% of retained responses score at least 70 and 75.60% score at least 80. Appendix B reports the full analysis.

The gains are not limited to the first rewrite. The first rewrite contributes 24.65 points on average, while later rewrites add another 28.77 points, accounting for 53.85% of the total increase. In 579 instances, a later rewrite outperforms both the seed response and the first rewrite; the median retained iteration is 2.

The SU–VU gap shows a more heterogeneous pattern. Similar judge-rated severity can arise from different uncertainty profiles. For example, with Mistral-12B as target, a Mistral-12B editor produces larger increases in signed SU–VU gap than a LLaMA-8B editor, whereas the latter more often elicits stable unsupported answers.

Judge variation changes score calibration but not the overall effect. With Mistral-7B as target and editor, LLaMA-3B yields mean post-editing scores of 62.24 and 56.10 for extrinsic and intrinsic hallucination, with success rates of 63.00% and 55.00%. Qwen2.5-7B yields corresponding scores of 73.25 and 56.70, with success rates of 46.00% and 39.00%. Appendix A reports the full results.

## 6.2 Variation Across Category and Topic

Extrinsic hallucination generally reaches higher severity than intrinsic hallucination. For example, mean post-editing scores are 71.76 versus 64.34 in Crime/Justice, 80.58 versus 69.30 in Local Business/Economy, and 83.30 versus 72.26 in Technology. This pattern is expected. Extrinsic hallucinations can often be induced by weakening grounding constraints or requesting unsupported elaboration. Intrinsic hallucinations require more subtle changes that distort source-grounded reasoning while leaving the task recognizable.

The method remains effective across all ten topics. In the baseline setting, topic-level induction-success rates range from 58.00% to 82.00% (Figure 4). Sports is the most difficult topic for extrinsic hallucination, with 58.00% success. Environment and Sports are the most difficult for intrinsic hallucination, each with 58.00% success. Local Business/Economy is easiest for extrinsic hallucination, reaching 82.00%. Cooking is easiest for intrinsic hallucination, reaching 80.00%. The topic variation shows that some domains are harder to stress test than others, but no topic fails outright.

## 6.3 Prompt Proximity and Source-Grounded Validity

Across topics and hallucination categories, mean cosine similarity between original and retained prompts ranges from 0.72 for Local Business/Economy extrinsic instances to 0.85 for Technology intrinsic instances. Mean similarity between consecutive rewrites ranges from 0.75 to 0.85, with mean prompt drift of 0.04 per iteration. These results indicate that edits generally remain topically close to their seeds, but similarity alone does not establish preservation of answerability.

We therefore directly evaluate source-grounded validity. In a random expert audit, 43/50 retained prompts remain answerable from the supplied source, and 35/50 are both answerable and do not request information beyond the source. In a separate human evaluation, 39/50 prompts are judged answerable without introducing outside information. Also, among 219 prompts passing stricter model-assisted checks for answerability, source preservation, logical consistency, and absence of extra-source requests, 89 corresponding responses still contain unsupported claims. Thus, hallucination remains inducible even when the edited task preserves strong source-grounded validity criteria.

We examine whether editing simply removes grounding requirements. Instructions directing the target to use the source or abstain when information is insufficient appear in 431/500 seed prompts and remain in 640/1,000 retained prompts. Together with the editor’s instruction to preserve source information and the target-side warning against hallucination after the first round, this indicates that the observed score increases are not solely explained by removing grounding constraints.

Table 2 gives representative edits. Common transformations weaken grounding, request unsupported elaboration, or create a mismatch between the requested inference and available evidence. These are best viewed as controlled stress-test transformations rather than ordinary paraphrases.

## 6.4 Editing Strategies and Randomness

The editor uses varied strategies rather than a single fixed template. Within each topic and hallucination category, mean pairwise edit distance for successful rewrites ranges from 0.90 to 0.97, and each group exhibits 2–7 strategy types. In the baseline setting, the most frequent strategies are context shrinking (3,595 occurrences) and removing verification guardrails (3,043), followed by context elongation (231). Appendix F provides the full cue-level analysis.

We test whether these dominant strategies also emerge on unseen data without target or judge feedback. Using 100 source-grounded SQuAD 2.0 examples excluded from the main experiments, the Mistral-7B editor produces one intrinsic- and one extrinsic-targeting rewrite per example without observing a target response, judge score, or explanation. Among the 200 rewrites, context shrinking occurs in 167 (83.5%) and verification-guardrail removal in 159 (79.5%), matching the two dominant strategies in the main experiments. Hallucination scores increase for 78 prompt pairs, and 77 edited responses score at least 80. The recurrence of these strategies before any feedback suggests that they reflect general editing tendencies rather than solely adaptation to the in-loop judge.

Results are also stable across random seeds. We rerun the full method three times on a fixed 200-instance subset under two representative model settings. The standard deviation of mean judge scores is 1.96/1.65 for extrinsic/intrinsic hallucination in the Mistral-7B setting and 2.82/0.44 in the GPT-4o-mini setting. McNemar tests find no significant rerun-pair differences in induction success. Appendix G reports the full analysis.

## 7 Transferability of Curated Prompts

Figure 5: Judge scores and signed SU–VU gaps for target-specific prompts and prompts transferred from the setting targeting Mistral-7B. Target-specific scores are shown for comparison.

Target-aware curation can be compute-intensive if run separately for every model. We therefore test whether prompts curated for one target can be reused for cross-model stress testing. We replay 200 post-editing prompts optimized for Mistral-7B, without further editing, on Mistral-Nemo-12B, Mistral-Small, LLaMA-8B, and GPT-4o-mini; all responses are scored by LLaMA-8B for consistency.

At the aggregate level, transfer remains effective. Mean hallucination scores on non-original targets range from 60.07 to 67.61 (Figure 5), and the number of prompts scoring at least 80 is comparable across models: 107 for Mistral-7B, 114 for Mistral-Nemo-12B, 111 for Mistral-Small, 114 for LLaMA-8B, and 111 for GPT-4o-mini. Relative to target-specific prompts, transferred prompts yield lower scores on Mistral-Nemo-12B and Mistral-Small, higher scores on LLaMA-8B, and mixed results on GPT-4o-mini.

At the instance level, however, the strongest failures differ substantially across models. Pairwise Spearman correlations for scores on the same prompts are only $\rho=0.0387$ – $0.1897$ , and Jaccard overlap between sets of prompts scoring at least 80 ranges from 39.74% to 47.06%. Only 20 prompts score at least 80 for all five models, while 33 reach that threshold for exactly one model. Severity-bin agreement between Mistral-7B and transferred targets is similarly modest, ranging from 41.00% to 52.00%. Thus, transferred prompts provide useful aggregate stress tests, but model-specific curation better identifies each model’s strongest failures.

The SU–VU gap shows a similar pattern. For Mistral-Nemo-12B, target-specific curation yields substantially higher gaps than transferred prompts, changing from $-0.09$ to $-0.69$ for extrinsic and from $-0.14$ to $-0.76$ for intrinsic hallucination, while differences are smaller for Mistral-Small, LLaMA-8B, and GPT-4o-mini. Overall, transfer captures general hallucination triggers, whereas target-aware editing remains preferable for fine-grained diagnosis.

## 8 Interpreting Curated Stress Tests

The curated prompts should be interpreted as diagnostic stress tests, not as samples from a natural user distribution. This distinction affects how the results should be used. A high post-editing hallucination score does not mean that the target model would hallucinate at the same rate under ordinary use. It means that, starting from a source-grounded seed prompt, the editor was able to find a nearby prompt variant that exposes a failure mode under the specified hallucination category. The relevant measurement is therefore vulnerability under controlled pressure, rather than prevalence.

This framing also clarifies the role of target-specific curation. When prompts are curated for a particular model, they can reveal model-specific weaknesses that may be absent from fixed benchmarks or from prompts optimized for other systems. The transfer results show that some edited prompts capture general hallucination triggers, since they remain useful for aggregate evaluation on other targets. At the same time, the low instance-level agreement across targets shows that the strongest failures are not interchangeable across models. In practice, transferred prompts can provide a cheap first-pass stress test, but target-specific curation is more appropriate when the goal is detailed diagnosis. Finally, the framework is best viewed as complementary to existing hallucination benchmarks. Fixed benchmarks support standardized comparisons across systems, while adaptive curation helps discover failure modes that fixed prompt sets may miss. The two uses answer different questions. A benchmark asks how a model behaves on a predefined evaluation distribution. Our method asks whether a model can be induced to hallucinate under controlled, source-grounded prompt transformations, and what kinds of transformations expose those failures.

## 9 Conclusion

We presented a target-aware procedure for curating hallucination stress tests. Iterative editing increases judge-rated severity, with later rounds accounting for over half of the baseline mean score increase. Some responses to prompts passing model-assisted source-validity checks still contain unsupported claims. Curated prompts transfer across models with similar aggregate severity but different prompt-level failures. Their diagnostic value depends on source validity and the failures observed in the target.
These findings show that adaptive stress testing can complement fixed benchmarks by uncovering model-specific hallucination vulnerabilities that static evaluations may miss.

## Limitations

Our experiments cover multiple target, editor, and judge models, but the space of model combinations is substantially larger. The results therefore support cross-setting applicability rather than exhaustive generalization across model families or deployment configurations.

We focus on single-turn, source-grounded QA and two operational hallucination categories. The conclusions do not directly extend to long-form generation, multi-turn dialogue, tool-augmented reasoning, or multimodal systems, which require setting-specific curation and evaluation.

The method relies on LLM-based judging, and the same judge can guide editing and evaluate retained responses. Independent human rescoring, alternative judges, and claim-level source checks substantially reduce this concern but do not establish the judge as ground truth; larger-scale independent evaluation remains useful.

Prompt editing can also alter the original task. Expert, human, and model-assisted audits show that many retained prompts remain valid source-grounded tasks and that unsupported responses persist under strict validity checks, but some edits reduce answerability or request outside information. Future work can impose stronger source-preservation constraints during search.

Finally, these prompts are adversarial stress tests rather than samples from a natural user distribution. Their success measures vulnerability under controlled pressure, not real-world hallucination prevalence.

## Ethics Statement

This work studies how hallucinations can be elicited from LLMs in controlled source-grounded settings. The goal is defensive evaluation. The method is intended to identify failure modes before they affect users. Because it can produce hallucination-inducing prompts, it could be misused if applied irresponsibly to attack deployed systems or generate misleading content. We therefore present it as a stress-testing and diagnostic tool for model development, evaluation, and safety analysis. Generated responses are used only for research evaluation, not for public-facing deployment.

Human annotation was conducted through Amazon Mechanical Turk. Annotators were informed about the task, and the questionnaires are included in the appendix for transparency. We did not collect sensitive personal information, and the annotation tasks were designed to avoid sensitive content. Annotators were compensated at an effective rate of approximately $12 per hour, above the U.S. federal minimum wage and the minimum wage in our state.

## Appendix B Robustness to Alternative Hallucination-Success Criteria

We evaluated five alternatives to the paper’s primary bin-based definition of hallucination-induction success: whether the retained hallucination-severity score (1) increased by more than 0 points, (2) increased by at least 10 points, (3) increased by at least 20 points, (4) reached at least 70, or (5) reached at least 80. Among the 1,000 baseline instances, 81.60% increased by more than 0 points, 77.60% increased by at least 10 points, and 70.80% increased by at least 20 points. The retained score reached at least 70 in 78.60% of instances and at least 80 in 75.60%.

Extrinsic hallucination had higher rates of success than intrinsic hallucination under all five definitions: 84.00% versus 79.20% for an increase of more than 0 points, 80.20% versus 75.00% for an increase of at least 10 points, 73.20% versus 68.40% for an increase of at least 20 points, 79.80% versus 77.40% for a retained score of at least 70, and 77.20% versus 74.00% for a retained score of at least 80. This shows that our conclusion that prompt editing increases hallucination severity holds across different relative score increases and under fixed final-score cutoffs.

## Appendix D LLM-as-Judge Score and SU–VU Gap Divergence

Figure D1 shows why the SU–VU gap should be interpreted as a complementary diagnostic rather than as a direct substitute for the judge score. The two measures are positively related, but the relationship is weak. This means that some edited prompts can produce clear judge-rated hallucinations without increasing semantic variability across sampled answers. The examples below illustrate this divergence.

Figure D1: Relationship between judge hallucination severity score and signed SU–VU gap over 2,200 instances. The relationship is positive but weak, so SU–VU is used as a complementary diagnostic rather than an independent ground-truth validator.

In an Environment extrinsic instance, the judge score rises from 0.00 to 100.00 because the response links a garnet-growth study to climate change and extreme weather, which the source does not support. The SU–VU gap decreases from $-0.80$ to $-1.00$ because sampled answers consistently make the same unsupported inference while using speculative language.

In a Crime/Justice extrinsic instance, the judge score rises from 0.00 to 100.00 because the response provides unsupported Ohio adoption steps. The SU–VU gap remains at $-1.00$ because sampled answers form a single semantic cluster and use legal-disclaimer hedging.

In a Cooking intrinsic instance, the judge score rises from 0.00 to 100.00 because the response supplies an unsupported temperature for cooking potatoes. The SU–VU gap decreases from $-0.61$ to $-1.00$ because sampled answers converge on the same hallucinated temperature range.

These examples explain the pattern in Figure D1. The SU–VU gap does not always increase when hallucination is successfully induced. Edited prompts can make the model produce the same unsupported inference across samples, or produce unsupported answers with hedging. In those cases, the retained response can be a clear hallucination under the judge while showing little increase in semantic variability relative to expressed uncertainty.

## Appendix E Feedback-Conditioned and No-Feedback Curation

In the main experiments, the target QA model receives the previous judge score and explanation after the first round. This creates a feedback-conditioned search setting in which the target is informed of its previous failure mode. To measure the effect of this design, we also disable target-side feedback. In that variant, the judge score and explanation are provided only to the prompt editor, and the QA model answers each edited prompt without seeing prior judge feedback.

On 100 queries in the no-feedback setting, the method succeeds on 96% of intrinsic and 98% of extrinsic hallucination attempts. These numbers are higher than in the main setting, suggesting that target-side feedback makes curation more conservative and more difficult. The no-feedback edits also tend to be simpler, because the editor does not need to overcome the target model’s prior awareness of its hallucination.

A representative no-feedback edited prompt is:

Summarize the following news within 180 words, while also interpreting the psychological effects these beauty trends may have on individuals. [News compressed for brevity]

For the same seed prompt, the feedback-conditioned setting produces a more distracting and specific edit:

Summarize the following news within 180 words, focusing on the influence of the Kardashian family on beauty trends and including information about how these trends compare to past practices in body modification: [News compressed for brevity]

## Appendix F Prompt-Editing Strategies

To analyze how the editor induces hallucinations, we compare each rewritten prompt with the prompt from the preceding iteration and retain only the newly introduced text. We then extract single-word and phrase-level cues for each editor model and strategy label.

The clearest contrast appears in asking for unsupported detail. For Mistral-7B, the most frequent word-level additions are additional (27 of 35 rewrites) and directly (16 of 35). The most frequent phrase-level cues are directly related (4 of 35) and personal experiences (2 of 35). For GPT-3.5-Turbo, the pattern is more concentrated. The word elaborate appears in 15 of 24 rewrites, and the phrase elaborate on appears in 14 of 24. Both editors instantiate the strategy by requesting information that extends beyond what the source supports. For example, a GPT-3.5-Turbo rewrite asks the target to elaborate on Mississippi’s secession from the Union and its position within the Confederate states.

The most frequent strategies overall are context shrinking and removing verification guardrails. A context-shrinking rewrite generalizes a grounded angle-cut question to a request for a step-by-step guide on how to calculate and cut roof angles. A verification-guardrail-removal rewrite reduces a grounded pepper question to the direct question, what type of pepper should I use in this recipe. In the expanded cue analysis, context-shrinking rewrites from Mistral-7B are led by the word cues additional (701 of 5,132 rewrites), use (564), and potential (551). The most frequent phrase cues are impact of (172), objective overview (101), and potential impact (86). Verification-guardrail-removal rewrites show a similar profile, led by use (568 of 4,341), additional (529), and potential (374), with impact of (101), does not specify (62), and west england (60) as frequent phrase cues.

Other strategies are rarer. Context elongation appears 438 times, or 2.53% of all rewrite steps, making it the most common lower-frequency strategy. It is used by every editor and is most frequent for Mistral-7B (3.15% of this editor’s rewrites) and Mistral-Small (2.62%). It is also relatively balanced across hallucination categories, appearing 199 times in extrinsic rewrites and 239 times in intrinsic rewrites. Removing instructional context appears 123 times (0.71%) and is more common in extrinsic than intrinsic rewrites (91 versus 32). Asking for unsupported detail appears 72 times (0.42%), is also more common in extrinsic rewrites (54 versus 18), and is used most often by GPT-3.5-Turbo (0.84% of its rewrites). Introducing subjective framing is the rarest strategy, appearing 47 times (0.27%), and appears more often in intrinsic than extrinsic rewrites (37 versus 10).

These patterns suggest that the editor mainly induces hallucinations through targeted modifications that weaken grounding, remove verification constraints, request broader or less-supported detail, or encourage speculative continuation. The modifications are usually topically related to the seed prompt, but they should be treated as stress-test transformations rather than guaranteed semantics-preserving rewrites.

## Appendix G Robustness to Randomness

Because hallucination depends on sampled model responses, we test whether repeated runs with different random seeds materially change the conclusions. Figure F1 summarizes the before/after judge scores and SU–VU gaps across the three reruns. We rerun the full method three times on a fixed 200-instance subset under two representative settings. The first uses Mistral-7B as both target and editor with LLaMA-8B as judge. The second uses GPT-4o-mini as target, GPT-3.5-Turbo as editor, and OpenAI o3 as judge.

In both settings, outcomes are stable across reruns. For the Mistral-7B target, the standard deviation of the mean judge score is 1.96 for extrinsic hallucination and 1.65 for intrinsic hallucination. The SU–VU gap standard deviations are 0.03 and 0.02. For the GPT-4o-mini target, the standard deviation of the mean judge score is 2.82 for extrinsic hallucination and 0.44 for intrinsic hallucination. The SU–VU gap standard deviations are 0.00 and 0.02. The plotted distributions in Figure F1 show the same pattern: editing consistently increases judge-rated hallucination severity, while seed-level variation remains small relative to the before/after shift.

We also apply significance tests. McNemar’s test on binary induction-success labels finds no significant rerun-pair differences, with $p=0.74$ – $1.00$ for the Mistral-7B setting and $p=1.00$ for the GPT-4o-mini setting. Wilcoxon signed-rank tests on paired instance-level judge scores are non-significant for all rerun-pair comparisons, with $p\geq 0.60$ for Mistral-7B and $p\geq 0.37$ for GPT-4o-mini. For the SU–VU gap, all but one comparison is non-significant. The only significant comparison is between Runs 2 and 3 in the Mistral-7B setting ( $p=0.04$ ). These results suggest that random seeds introduce limited variation and do not materially alter the conclusions.

We further examine editing-strategy stability across reruns. For each instance, we count the number of distinct strategy-label combinations assigned to retained rewrites across the three runs. The mean number is 1.74 and 1.56 for extrinsic and intrinsic hallucination in the Mistral-7B setting, and 1.59 and 1.54 in the GPT-4o-mini setting. The proportion of instances with exactly one strategy combination across all three runs is 43.00% and 55.00% in the Mistral-7B setting, and 50.00% and 52.00% in the GPT-4o-mini setting. Thus, while the editor exhibits some variability, the variation is limited.

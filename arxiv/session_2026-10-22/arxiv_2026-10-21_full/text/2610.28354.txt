Abstract

Spatial reasoning asks a model to work out where objects are in 3D space,
how they relate to one another, and how they move. Agentic VLMs have
recently brought large improvements on such tasks by letting the model
actively gather evidence with 3D perception tools instead of answering in a
single pass. Even so, on many questions the tools return the right
evidence and the model still does not arrive at the correct answer: the
gap between model and tools, where raw readings become usable
conclusions, is left unfilled. We present S-Agent 2.0, a spatial
reasoning agent that equips an off-the-shelf general coding
agent with a command-line perception toolkit, and we design the
interface around one principle: help the VLM close the evidence
loop—acquire scene evidence, remember it in a
persistent scene memory, and interpret each reading in plain
spatial language. This design keeps the full flexibility of a coding
agent, and every reading it acts on stays grounded in the tools’
geometry. On MMSI-Bench and ReVSI, the toolkit improves a strong
open-source model, Qwen3.8-27B, by $+13.9$ and $+20.5$ points over the
same agent without tools, and a strong closed-source model,
Gemini-3.8-Flash, by $+11.2$ and $+9.6$ points.
With the open backbone S-Agent 2.0 reaches $66.5$ on MMSI-Bench and $82.3$ 
on ReVSI, ahead of the specialized system SpatialClaw on the same backbone
( $62.6$ and $74.5$ ), and with Gemini-3.8-Flash it reaches $74.1$ and
 $90.5$ against its $67.0$ and $79.7$ . Both tool gains shrink as the
backbone grows more capable, but SpatialClaw’s shrinks far more on ReVSI,
from $+13.2$ to $+2.7$ , while ours stays at $+9.6$ .
S-Agent 2.0 also works across different general coding agent harnesses,
such as Codex and Claude Code.

## 1 Introduction

Spatial intelligence, the ability to understand where things are, how they
relate in three dimensions, and how the world would look as one moves
through it, is foundational to visual
perception [6, 40] and essential for any
agent that operates in the physical
world [12, 2, 30, 15, 3].
Agentic VLMs such as S-Agent [11] have recently brought large improvements here: instead of
answering in a single pass, the model actively gathers evidence with 3D
perception tools while it reasons [23, 7]. More recent work
such as SpatialClaw [10] responds with a specialized
harness: a custom state machine that drives the VLM through a hand-designed
loop (plan, act, reflect) over a persistent code kernel with a library of
3D perception and geometry utilities, leveraging the flexibility of code
to bridge the gap. Even so, on many
questions the tools return the right evidence and the model still does not
arrive at the correct answer (see Figure 4).

Watching such a system work reveals where it actually fails. A spatial answer
is grounded only when a complete evidence loop closes:
acquire the measurement, remember it while the rest of the
evidence is gathered, and interpret the reading into understanding.
Specialized systems invest almost entirely in
acquisition, in the form of ever richer perception and geometry libraries,
and leave the other two steps to the model. In our analysis of SpatialClaw
trajectories, acquisition is rarely what breaks. The failures come in two
forms. In the first, the model misreads a reading it holds: it flips the
sign of a rotation it measured correctly, or reads a correct number in the
wrong coordinate frame or the wrong sense. In the second, the evidence
never reaches the answer at all: the measurement sits in the workspace
while the model eyeballs the pictures, or a correct reading is discarded
for an unrelated stated reason. The second form exposes a break between
collecting evidence and drawing the conclusion. Once the readings are in,
the reasoning that produces the answer runs detached from them, and the
model falls back on its own perception of the scene. On the surface only
interpretation goes wrong; underneath, the evidence that was acquired is
never fully used. Closing the loop therefore places a requirement on each
step. Acquisition has to combine easily with the VLM’s own perception, so
that what the model recognizes and what the tools measure enter the same
scene. Acquired evidence needs a memory that holds it without effort, so
that it stays within reach until the answer is written. And interpretation
needs spatial language, so that a reading arrives in the terms the
question is asked in.

## 1.1 S-Agent 2.0

S-Agent 2.0 is our answer to those requirements: a spatial reasoning agent
assembled from a stock general-purpose coding agent [25, 1, 36] and a command-line
perception toolkit designed to meet them. General-purpose coding agents (the
harnesses behind modern software-engineering agents) already provide the
scaffolding a spatial reasoner needs (a shell, a file system, code
execution, long-horizon context management, a mature tool-calling loop),
and two of their habits bear directly on the requirements above. First,
looking at a frame or at a segmentation mask is an ordinary file view
in the agent’s stream, so the model’s own look at the scene and the
tools’ readouts interleave in one conversation, with no separate image
channel between them.[41] Second, a coding agent inspects and revises by
reflex: it views an output, corrects what is wrong, and runs again.
[41, 16, 29]
Applied to a scene, that reflex is what joins the two kinds of
perception, since the model’s own perception is checked against what the
tools registered before any reading is taken. What these agents lack is
perception itself; we keep the harness stock and add it as commands. The
design effort goes into the toolkit, which meets the three requirements
one by one.

Concretely, (i) acquisition combines two kinds of perception, the tools’
measurements and the model’s own: the model can register what it
perceives or imagines as instances in the scene and drop what the tools
got wrong, so both kinds land on one geometric footing;
(ii) everything measured persists in a scene memory that later
steps query by name instead of re-perceiving, so acquired evidence stays
within reach until the answer is written; and (iii) every measurement
comes back as an answer-ready reading stated in plain spatial language
with its interpretation attached.

Our contributions can be summarized as follows:

• 

We build a spatial reasoning agent from a stock general-purpose
coding agent and a command-line perception toolkit,
and show that the method works across different general
coding agent harnesses (§3.3).

• 

We design a command-line perception toolkit that closes the
evidence loop for the agent: each call acquires scene evidence, writes it
into a persistent scene memory, and returns its reading already
interpreted in spatial language, and the model’s own perception is
grounded in the same scene (§2).

• 

S-Agent 2.0 delivers strong performance. On MMSI-Bench and ReVSI,
the perception toolkit adds $+13.9$ and $+20.5$ points over the same
agent without tools, on the already strong Qwen3.8-27B backbone
(§3.2), and S-Agent 2.0 surpasses the state-of-the-art
agentic spatial reasoning method, whose tool gain on ReVSI shrinks far
more on the stronger frontier model Gemini-3.8-Flash while ours
remains substantial (§3.3).

## 1.2 Takeaways

1. 

The method works across coding-agent harnesses.
S-Agent 2.0 works across coding-agent harnesses, including OpenHands [36], Codex [25] and Claude Code [1]
(§3.3).

2. 

The coding agent’s inspect-and-revise habit joins the
model’s perception with the tools’. The model registers what it
perceives or imagines into the scene memory, drops what the tools got
wrong (§2.3), and cross-checks the memory
against the perception outputs (§3.4).

3. 

The remaining gap is model-to-tool grounding.
The model’s reasoning is mostly right; what remains is grounding the
question into a measurement, which fails in two classes
(§3.4).

## 2 Method

The diagnosis of §1 turns into three requirements that a
system must meet to close the evidence loop, beyond merely possessing tools.
Acquisition joined with the model’s own perception: what the model
recognizes and what the tools measure must enter one scene.
Persistent memory: evidence gathered at one step must remain
intact and addressable many steps later, without the model carrying it
through its own context. Interpretation on arrival: a measurement
should arrive in the spatial language the question is asked in.
Existing systems supply acquisition generously, offering a
Turing-complete kernel over perception and geometry libraries, but they
keep the model’s own perception in a separate channel and leave memory
and interpretation to the model. S-Agent 2.0 inverts that allocation: we
use a general coding agent as the spatial agent, and the design effort
moves into a tool interface that joins the two kinds of perception and
carries memory and interpretation itself.
Figure 2 sketches the overall framework.

## 2.1 A general coding agent as the spatial agent

S-Agent 2.0 has three parts: a general-purpose coding agent $\pi$ as
planner and executor; a command-line interface (CLI) perception toolkit
 $\mathcal{T}$ ; and a persistent per-episode scene memory $S$ 
that the toolkit reads and writes.
Formally, a spatial question consists of a question $q$ and frames
 $\mathcal{I}=\{I_{1},\dots,I_{n}\}$ . The agent receives a fixed instruction
 $u$ , the same for every episode, which holds a tutorial for the
toolkit’s commands and a recommended workflow: decide the evidence
 $\mathcal{E}$ the question requires, acquire $\mathcal{E}$ through the
toolkit, check the perception tool outputs, and answer from the
acquired readings. At reasoning step $t$ the agent’s
context holds the instruction $u$ , the question $q$ , and the history of
its own chain of thought $z_{<t}$ , actions $a_{<t}$ , and tool responses
 $o_{<t}$ ; from this context the policy $\pi$ produces the next thought
and action,

 $(z_{t},\,a_{t})\sim\pi\!\left(\cdot\mid u,\,q,\,z_{<t},\,a_{<t},\,o_{<t}\right),\qquad a_{t}\in\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{gen}}\cup\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{perc}},$ 

(1)

until it commits an answer. The action space includes two classes of
actions, both operated through the CLI. $\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{gen}}$ 
holds the general coding agent’s original actions, such as running
Linux commands, writing and executing code, and reading files and
images. $\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{perc}}$ holds the perception toolkit’s
actions, such as reconstruction, segmentation, and readouts of spatial
relations, which the following subsections detail. A perception action
returns a full output $m$ and a tool response $o^{\mathrm{perc}}$ . The response
 $o^{\mathrm{perc}}$ joins the context, while the full output $m$ ,
together with the original frames $\mathcal{I}$ , is kept on disk. The
agent reads these files through the file-reading actions of
 $\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{gen}}$ , deciding when and which to read, and what
it reads joins the context as $o^{\mathrm{gen}}$ . Across these steps
the model’s own work is the two maps $q\mapsto\mathcal{E}$ and
 $o^{\mathrm{perc}}\mapsto\mathcal{E}$ : deciding what evidence the
question requires, and reading each reply into a piece of that
evidence. Since this design
assumes only the CLI machinery every coding agent provides, our
framework works across general coding agent harnesses
(§3.3).

Figure 2: Overview of the framework. The agent keeps a coding agent’s
full generic freedom over the workspace (viewing images, reading and writing
files, writing and running code) and composes perception through the same
shell; the toolkit registers everything it perceives into the scene memory
and returns interpreted, answer-ready readings.

## 2.2 Scene memory kept by the environment

Closing the loop on a multi-view or video question takes many steps, and
the evidence gathered early must still be at hand when the answer is
committed, so some component has to hold it. The model’s context window can
serve as that holder, at the price of transcribing every intermediate
result into text and carrying it across a growing transcript.

We let the environment do the remembering instead: the toolkit maintains a
persistent scene memory

 $S\;=\;\big(\mathcal{I},\ P,\ \{(\nu_{i},\,g_{i})\}_{i}\big),\qquad g_{i}\;=\;\big(\mathbf{p}_{i},\,E_{i},\,T_{i}\big),$ 

(2)

holding the raw frames $\mathcal{I}$ , the fused metric point cloud $P$ in
a gravity-aligned world frame, and a registry of named instances. Each
instance pairs a name $\nu_{i}$ , usable in later calls, with its geometry
 $g_{i}$ : an $xyz$ position $\mathbf{p}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}$ , an extent $E_{i}$ 
(the subset of $P$ belonging to the instance), and a 6-DoF pose
 $T_{i}\in\mathrm{SE}(3)$ ; $E_{i}$ and $T_{i}$ may each be left empty. Both
the perception toolkit and the model can write to this memory;
§2.3 details both routes.

Because the scene memory records each instance’s position, extent, and
pose, measuring objects and their relations becomes a lookup, computed
in closed form from the stored geometry. Readout actions are a family
of functions $R_{\tau}(\nu_{1},\dots,\nu_{k};\,S_{t})$ with
 $\tau\in\mathcal{T}$ , over one or more
named instances, covering properties of a single instance, such as its
size and elevation, and relations between instances, such as pairwise
distance, relative direction, and motion between frames. A reading is derived fresh
from the stored geometry at each call, so the model can complete a
readout by remembering only the instance names.
Figure 3 lists what one episode’s memory holds.

## 2.3 Dividing perception by competence

The evidence loop involves two kinds of perceptual work with opposite
competence profiles. Deciding what to measure is semantic work
that VLMs do well, such as identifying which objects the question is
about and phrasing a segmentation prompt. Producing precise masks and
measurements is work that dedicated perception models do better. A
principled interface should cut exactly along this line. Formally, a
perception action pairs a toolkit command $\tau$ with the arguments $x$ 
the model supplies: the model contributes the map $q\mapsto x$ , the
grounding step that carries the evidence $\mathcal{E}$ it conceives into
terms the toolkit operates on, and the toolkit supplies
 $(x,S)\mapsto o^{\mathrm{perc}}$ . Our interface lets the model write
 $x$ directly from earlier replies, $o^{\mathrm{perc}}\mapsto x$ . The
call has semantics

 $(o^{\mathrm{perc}}_{t},\,S_{t+1})\;=\;\tau\!\left(x_{t};\,S_{t}\right),\qquad\tau\in\mathcal{T}=\mathcal{T}_{\mathrm{tool}}\cup\mathcal{T}_{\mathrm{model}},$ 

(3)

where $S_{t}$ is the scene memory of §2.2.

We build the division into the interface along these two write routes
into the scene memory. Tool perception
( $\mathcal{T}_{\mathrm{tool}}$ ) carries the measurement side. The
recon command fuses all frames into one metric, gravity-aligned
scene and registers every camera in the scene memory; the segment
command grounds objects to pixels, taking the model’s own words as
prompts, so deciding what to look for remains semantic work.
Model perception ( $\mathcal{T}_{\mathrm{model}}$ ) enters through
the add-instance command, which grounds the model’s high-level
understanding itself. The model names the new instance, such as an
object it recognizes or an observer the question imagines, and
initializes its position and pose from other named instances or from
pixel locations in the images. The toolkit then treats it like any other
instance, so a construct that exists only in the model’s reading of the
question is measured on the same geometric footing as the instances that
tool perception grounds. The same route also runs in reverse: with
drop the model removes an instance the tools registered wrongly,
such as a mask on a reflection, so its own look at the tool perception outputs corrects
the memory before any reading is taken from it.
Figure 3 shows model perception and tool perception apart in one episode.
Detailed descriptions of toolkit interfaces are given in Appendix B.

## 2.4 The language surface: numbers interpreted on arrival

With acquisition routine and memory kept by the environment, the
remaining break point is how a reading is consumed. Even when the
measurement itself is correct, the model may take it in the wrong
reference frame, invert which object it is measured from, or ignore it
entirely. A displacement vector alone does not say which object it points
from, whether its axes follow gravity or the camera, or which answer
option it favors. That knowledge lives outside the numbers, and the side
that computed the geometry holds it most reliably. In previous
tool-augmented systems [10], tool calls return bare
values or arrays, leaving the whole step from number to usable spatial
statement to the model. In the failures we observed, the model calls the
right tool and the tool returns the right geometry, yet the final answer
still comes out wrong.

We therefore have every reply interpret its own numbers, handing the
model the values and their plain-language reading together. Each command
distills its full output $m$ , against the scene memory $S$ , into a
compact structured report

 $o^{\mathrm{perc}}\;=\;c_{\tau}(m;\,S),$ 

(4)

with a fixed anatomy: a few typed numeric fields with units, together
with a conclusion, one sentence that restates the measurement in
plain spatial language. The conclusion names the entities involved, declares
the frame it speaks in (gravity-aligned, camera, or image plane), and
carries the reading all the way to the ordinary spatial vocabulary an
answer is written in. Figure 3 gives an example:
the responses describe relation in words first and keeps the numerical
fields that support it, so the model can act on a reading without
re-deriving it. And the complete
measurements and geometry stay in the full output $m$ on disk, where
the model can read them through the same CLI when it needs them.

Together these commitments close the evidence loop. Evidence travels in
rounds,

where $\pi$ is the VLM, $S$ is the scene memory $q$ is the question, $\mathcal{E}$ the evidence the model decides
the question requires, $x$ the arguments of a perception call, $m$ the
full output the call writes to disk, and $o^{\mathrm{perc}}$ the
interpreted reply; $\pi$ marks the model’s steps and $S$ the toolkit’s,
computed against the scene memory. The dashed returns close the loop:
the model reads each reply back into its evidence $\mathcal{E}$ and
into the arguments of the next call.

## 3 Experiments

## 3.1 Setup

Benchmarks. We evaluate S-Agent 2.0 on five benchmarks
covering multi-view and video spatial reasoning. For multi-view
reasoning, MMSI-Bench [44] provides
multiple images of the same scene and tests whether models integrate
evidence across views for positional relationships, geometric
attributes, motion, and multi-step reasoning;
MindCube [34] probes the spatial
mental model built from a few sparse views, asking for relations the
given views never show directly; SPAR-Bench [49]
(used for the harness-swap study) spans a hierarchy of
spatial capabilities from basic metric perception to multi-view
relational reasoning. For video reasoning,
ReVSI [51] evaluates 3D spatial
reasoning from dynamic observations, emphasizing relations that no
isolated frame reliably shows;
MMSI-Video-Bench [21] extends the MMSI setting
to long videos, with questions on spatial construction, motion
understanding, cross-video memory, and route planning. ReVSI and
SPAR-Bench scores follow the official protocols, macro-averaged over
question types; the others report overall accuracy.

Models. We use Qwen3.8-27B (FP8, served locally) as the primary backbone;
Gemini-3.8-Flash as the stronger backbone for the capability study. The
general coding agent harness is OpenHands [36] unless stated otherwise, and the
same workspaces and toolkit also run under Codex and Claude Code
(§3.3). The perception tools are served by
off-the-shelf models, all frozen: Depth Anything 3 [20] for the
metric reconstruction, $\pi^{3}_{\mathrm{mos}}$ [37, 54]
for per-pixel motion masks, GeoCalib [32] for the
gravity direction, and SAM 3 [5] for promptable
segmentation.

Baselines. We compare S-Agent 2.0 with three categories of
baselines: advanced proprietary VLMs, such as GPT-5.4, Grok 4, and the
Gemini series; open-weight general and spatially specialized models,
such as the Qwen series and the SenseNova-SI series; and agentic
systems, S-Agent [11] and SpatialClaw [10].

## 3.3 Ablations

Different general coding agent harness.

We rerun the benchmarks with the whole harness replaced by another
production coding agent, Codex or Claude Code, each with its own agent
loop, prompting and context management, driving the identical workspaces
and toolkit. Table 4 shows S-Agent 2.0 works under every
one.

Tools against no tools.

Table 6 varies the toolkit against the same agent on the
same items. The toolkit is worth a large gain on both benchmarks on
Qwen3.8-27B, and our score with tools keeps rising as the backbone grows
more capable. SpatialClaw scores below ours on every backbone and
benchmark, and its tool gain shrinks far more as the backbone grows.

Interface components.
Table 6 removes one commitment of §2 at
a time and leaves the rest of the system untouched. Without the scene
memory the agent carries every reading through its own context; without
the interpreted readout the replies come back as bare values;
without the model perception interface the model cannot add
instances to the scene or drop them from it.
Every removal costs
accuracy, and the ranking matches the diagnosis of §1: on
the surface only interpretation goes wrong, but underneath the acquired
evidence is never fully used. Removing the interpreted readout costs the
least ( $-1.3$ and $-1.2$ ); removing the scene memory costs more ( $-3.5$ 
and $-2.5$ ), since readings carried through the context are lost before
the answer is written; removing the model perception interface costs the
most ( $-8.8$ and $-5.9$ ). Closing the loop needs both the interpretation
design and the scene memory.

## 3.4 Qualitative analysis

Qualitative comparison with SpatialClaw.
On the same backbone and the same perception backends, the baseline
acquires the evidence and still does not close the loop
(Figure 4, left): the tools return the right masks and the
right positions, and the step from those numbers to the asked relation is
left to the model, which states a right-hand rule correctly and
implements it in reverse, or reaches the correct direction and lets a
second look at the pictures overturn it. Our runs
close the same loop because that step is not left to the model: the
question’s premise enters the scene as an oriented instance or a compass
anchor, and the relation comes back in the words the options use.
Seven further items, with the command-line record and the intermediate
images of both agents, are in Appendix E.

Cross-checking the two kinds of perception.
The memory closes the loop only if what it holds is right, and the agent
checks that itself. It compares what it sees in a perception output
against what the registry holds and edits the memory when the two disagree
(Figure 4, top right): it drops instances the output shows to
be wrong, and keeps a suspected duplicate once the output shows a genuine
second object. The habit is the coding
agent’s inspect-and-revise loop applied to the scene, and it is what joins
the model’s perception with the tools’ before any reading is taken.

What is left is grounding.
With acquisition, memory and interpretation carried by the interface, the
errors that remain sit where the question first enters the loop
(Takeaway 3, Figure 4, bottom right). The agent fails to
simulate an imagined viewpoint the question requires, registering an
observer with a borrowed heading, or it measures the right quantity in the
wrong sense, reading an object’s elevation above the floor where the
question asks for the object’s own height.

Figure 4: Qualitative analysis. Left: two items where SpatialClaw calls the
right tools and gets the right perception, and still answers wrong, with
S-Agent 2.0 correct on both (gemini-3.8-flash on both sides).
Top right: S-Agent 2.0 cross-checking its own perception against the
scene memory, dropping the wrong masks
before grounding the observer and reading the answer. Bottom
right: the two remaining grounding failure classes, a wrongly imagined viewpoint
and a quantity read in the wrong sense. Zoom in for better
readability.

## 4 Conclusion

We built a spatial reasoning agent from a stock general coding agent and a
command-line perception toolkit that closes the evidence loop for the
model, joining the tools’ measurements with the model’s own perception,
keeping every reading in a persistent scene memory, and returning each
reading interpreted in spatial language. Experiments show that with a 27B
open backbone and no training the agent is ahead of every agentic
baseline on all five benchmarks, and that with Gemini-3.8-Flash it has
the highest MMSI-Bench and ReVSI scores of any model we compare against,
including proprietary models such as GPT-6 and GPT-5.6. Its gain from the toolkit remains substantial
on the stronger backbone, where the specialized baseline’s shrinks far
more, and the same toolkit works under different coding agent
harnesses.

## Appendix A Related Work

## Spatial reasoning in vision-language models.

Spatial competence remains one of the weaker abilities of vision-language
models [6, 9, 30],
and most of the response has been to put the capability into the weights.
One line distills 3D annotations into spatial instruction data at scale, as
in SpatialVLM [6],
SpatialRGPT [9], Cambrian-S [43],
and SenseNova-SI [4]. A second line reshapes the backbone
itself with geometry-aware modelling or spatially targeted tuning, as in
Spatial-MLLM [38] and VST [42]. A third line shapes
the training signal through reinforcement learning, verifiable rewards, or
curricula, as in SpaceR [26], ViLaSR [39],
MindCube [34], and
SpatialLadder [19]. These models answer in a single
forward pass out of what training left in their parameters, taking no
measurement of the scene in front of them, so a new perception backend or a
shifted task distribution asks for another round of training. S-Agent 2.0
leaves the backbone untouched and moves the work to inference time, where
the agent acquires scene evidence on demand and every reading it acts on
stays grounded in the tools’ geometry.

## Tool-augmented visual agents and code as action.

Tool-use agents interleave reasoning with calls to external modules,
following the template that ReAct [46] set on the language side.
On the visual side, VisProg [17] and
ViperGPT [31] synthesize a whole program in one
pass and then execute it, while MM-ReAct [45],
HuggingGPT [28], and
OctoTools [22] dispatch requests turn by turn through a
structured tool menu, and PyVision [53] writes its
tooling as it goes. CodeAct [35] compared action
formats directly and found executable code to work better than JSON or free
text for general-purpose agents, which is the format a coding agent uses
natively. The tools all of these systems reach for target 2D recognition,
so their controllers are never asked to hold a metric 3D scene across views
and time, which is what a spatial question demands.

## Agentic spatial reasoning.

A recent line brings tool use to spatial questions.
VADAR [24] synthesizes a task-specific Pythonic API
and then a program over it, and pySpatial [23] composes
reconstruction, camera-pose recovery, and novel-view synthesis into a 3D
visual program. GCA [8] splits the model into a
part that formalizes the question as geometric constraints and a part that
executes tool calls within them, RieMind [27] queries
an explicit 3D scene graph through typed geometric operations, and
SpaceTools [7] fine-tunes a VLM to coordinate a
fixed tool set through supervised demonstrations followed by interactive
reinforcement learning. Think3D [52] selects camera
viewpoints, renders a reconstructed point cloud from them, and reasons over
the rendered images. Closest to us, S-Agent [11] schedules 3D
operations across the views and time steps of multi-view sets and long
videos, and SpatialClaw [10] drives the VLM through a
hand-designed plan, act, and reflect loop over a persistent code kernel
stocked with 3D perception and geometry utilities. What these systems share
is a controller written for the purpose and a design effort spent on
acquisition, on ever richer perception and geometry libraries, while
remembering and interpreting the evidence is left to the model and the
model’s own perception stays in a separate channel. S-Agent 2.0 writes no
controller and spends its design effort on closing the evidence loop at
the interface: the model’s own perception enters the same scene as the
tools’ measurements, every reading persists in a scene memory that later
calls query by name, and each reading comes back already interpreted in
spatial language.

## General-purpose coding agents.

Software-engineering agents such as SWE-agent [41] and
OpenHands [36], developed against benchmarks such as
SWE-bench [18], have matured into harnesses that
supply a shell, a file system, code execution, long-horizon context
management, and a tool-calling loop that the backbone models are trained
to follow. S-Agent 2.0 adopts such a harness unchanged and adds perception
as ordinary command-line tools, which is what lets the same method run
under more than one of them (§3.3). Two habits of
these agents carry over to spatial reasoning: viewing an image is an
ordinary step in the same stream as the tool replies, and inspecting an
output and revising it is a reflex; together they are what joins the
model’s own perception with the tools’ before a reading is taken
(§3.4).

## Long-video and multi-view perception.

Two families of methods supply the visual evidence these questions need.
Frame-compression methods sample, retrieve, or summarize a limited set of
frames for a long-context VLM, as in Video-LLaMA [48],
LongVA [50], and T⋆ [47], which keeps the context
tractable at the risk of dropping the frames a question turns on.
Reconstruction-first methods build an explicit 3D representation with
multi-view geometry or feed-forward models such as VGGT [33] and
Depth Anything 3 [20], which grounds the geometry well while paying
the reconstruction cost on every question. Both families hand their output
to the model as fixed context, leaving association across views and metric
comparison to implicit reasoning. S-Agent 2.0 uses reconstruction and
promptable segmentation backends of this kind as tool perception
(§2.3), with the agent deciding per question which
of them to call and what to ground; what they measure and what the model
registers itself land in one scene memory, and every reading comes back
interpreted.

## Spatial reasoning benchmarks.

Evaluation has moved from single-image probes such as BLINK [14]
and EmbSpatial [13] to settings where the evidence is spread
over many observations. MMSI-Bench [44] asks for evidence
integrated across images of one scene, MindCube [34]
probes the mental model built from a few sparse views, and
SPAR-Bench [49] spans a hierarchy from metric
perception to multi-view relational reasoning. For video,
VSI-Bench [40] opened the setting,
ReVSI [51] rebuilds its evaluation for a more accurate
assessment of 3D reasoning, and MMSI-Video-Bench [21] extends
the MMSI setting to long videos with questions on spatial construction,
motion, cross-video memory, and route planning. We evaluate on five of
these (§3.1).

## Appendix B Toolkit interface

The toolkit is one executable, python3 /app/perception.py
<command> [flags], run from the agent’s shell like any other program.
Every command reads and writes the scene memory $S$ of
§2.2; its reply is a JSON document on standard
output, and every image it renders (segmentation overlays, top-down maps)
is written to disk for the agent to open with its ordinary file viewer.
The instruction $u$ is a tutorial over these commands; this appendix lists
the interface itself. Table 7 gives every command the agent is
taught, grouped as in §2.3.

Instance names. Every argument that refers to something in the
scene is a name. recon registers camera:f<N> for the camera
of frame $N$ (position and orientation) and scene for the room
centre. segment registers each prompt’s label as an instance
(bed, desk); a second, separate object under the same label
becomes <label>_2, and f<N>:<label-or-id> names one frame’s
view of an object, by label or by the id stamped on that frame’s overlay.
add_instance names are chosen by the model. Two forms exist for
compass only: camplane:f<N>, the image plane of camera $N$ ,
and <name>|plane, the plane fitted through an instance’s points.

Positions and orientations for add_instance. A position
comes from one of three sources: --at-map f<N>:PX,PY, a pixel of
the top-down map rendered from frame $N$ (height filled in from the
reconstruction at that spot); --view N --pixel X,Y, a pixel of the
original frame lifted to its 3D point; or --at-camera N, the
position of camera $N$ . An orientation is a primary axis taken exactly and
a secondary direction adjusted to sit orthogonal to it; the axes are the
instance’s own (forward/back, right/left, top/bottom) and a direction
value is any existing instance, the literal gravity, or
bev:f<N>:START:END, an arrow between two points on the map from
frame $N$ . --facing V is the upright shorthand (gravity fills the
vertical, V gives the heading), --primary AXIS=V --secondary
AXIS=V the general form. A second add_instance call with the same
--name and only orientation flags orients the existing instance in
place.

Reply anatomy. Every reply has the same shape: a status flag,
typed numeric fields in metres on the reconstruction’s own scale (a
units field says whether the scale has been calibrated with
--known), and a conclusion sentence per target that restates
the measurement in spatial language.

## Appendix D SpatialClaw with our readouts

To separate what the readouts contribute from what the rest of the
interface contributes, we add our readout commands to SpatialClaw’s
kernel as plain functions and leave its harness, prompts and perception
backends untouched. The transplanted set is distance,
depth, side, elevation, motion and
compass; each returns the same interpreted reply as in
Table 7, packed into a Python dictionary with the same keys
(the numeric fields, the per-target conclusion and units),
so the model reads it in its kernel exactly as our agent reads the JSON on
standard output. list_instance is excluded because it reads
the scene memory that SpatialClaw does not keep, and bev because
SpatialClaw already renders a top-down map of its own.

## A Semi-Analytical Inference Framework for Structural Causal Models

Tatiana Boura\corresponding

Robert Peharz

Abstract

Structural Causal Models (SCMs) provide a principled framework for observational, interventional, and counterfactual reasoning, all of which rely on probabilistic inference. Existing frameworks either transform SCMs into alternative tractable representations or rely on approximate inference with expressive generative models. We instead perform inference using a message passing scheme directly on the SCM by representing structural mechanisms and probability densities in a common finite-dimensional function space. Forward propagation combines a numerical realization of the Law of the Unconscious Statistician, while propagated densities are recovered by spline projection from LOTUS moments. The same representation naturally yields backward message passing for conditioning, providing a unified framework for causal inference. Using tensor-product B-spline bases, the resulting algorithm expresses inference through numerical quadrature and linear algebra without explicitly constructing conditional distributions. Experiments on randomly generated continuous SCMs demonstrate accurate approximation of Monte Carlo reference solutions and systematic improvement with increasing basis resolution.

## 1 Introduction

The notion of causality has been central to philosophical discourse since antiquity. Structural Causal Models (SCMs) provide one of the most widely used mathematical frameworks for causal reasoning (Pearl 2009). Under the assumption that the modeled system is causally closed, uncertainty is confined to exogenous variables and endogenous variables are related deterministically (Peters et al. 2017).
SCMs support three levels of reasoning, known as Pearl’s causal hierarchy: association, intervention, and counterfactuals (Pearl and Mackenzie 2018). Although these three query types differ in their semantics, each ultimately requires evaluating probability distributions induced by the SCM. Efficient probabilistic inference therefore forms the computational core of causal reasoning. Observational queries require probabilistic inference in the observational distribution, i.e. the pushforward distribution of the exogenous distribution under the hierarchical causal mechanism,
interventional queries require inference after modifying the structural equations, and counterfactual reasoning combines conditioning with hypothetical interventions (Han et al. 2023; Pearl 2009).
Specifically, counterfactual inference can be formulated using a twin SCM or, equivalently, an abduct-intervene-infer procedure (Pearl 2009).

While much of the causal modeling literature focuses on causal discovery, identification, and effect estimation from data (Peters et al. 2017), comparatively less attention has been devoted to efficiently evaluating queries in a given SCM (Darwiche 2022; Wang and Kwiatkowska 2023; Zečević et al. 2021; Pawlowski et al. 2020). These directions are complementary: discovery and identification determine the causal model and identifiable effects, whereas causal inference assumes a given SCM and answers causal queries. This setting naturally arises when the model is provided by expert knowledge or a preceding learning stage.

Causal inference appears closely related to inference in Bayesian networks (BNs) (Koller and Friedman 2009), where algorithms such as variable elimination, belief propagation, and junction-tree message passing enable efficient exact and approximate inference (Pearl 1982; Zhang and Poole 1996; Wainwright and Jordan 2008). Since every acyclic SCM induces a BN, it is natural to ask whether these methods apply directly. However, BN inference requires explicit conditional distributions, whereas SCMs define them only implicitly through deterministic structural mechanisms and exogenous noise. Thus, inference depends both on the graph structure and the representation of the structural mechanisms.

Several approaches have addressed this challenge. One line of work compiles SCMs into alternative representations that support tractable inference, including tractable circuits and related representations (Darwiche 2022; Wang and Kwiatkowska 2023; Zečević et al. 2021). Another employs expressive structural mechanisms based on deep generative models (Pawlowski et al. 2020), where inference is typically performed approximately. Existing methods therefore either transform the SCM into a tractable representation or perform inference approximately using sampling or learned generative models.

In this work, we instead perform inference directly on the original SCM representation. We formulate message passing in a common finite-dimensional function space for structural mechanisms and probability densities. Forward propagation evaluates the Law of the Unconscious Statistician (LOTUS)
integrals numerically and recovers propagated densities through spline projection, yielding a semi-analytical inference procedure that avoids SCM compilation and sampling-based inference. The same representation naturally extends to backward message passing, providing a unified framework for observational, interventional, and counterfactual inference.

We instantiate the framework using tensor-product B-spline bases, whose approximation properties, numerical stability, and efficient quadrature algorithms (de Boor 2001; Schumaker 2007; Cottrell et al. 2009) allow inference to be expressed through linear algebra and numerical quadrature while representing highly nonlinear mechanisms.
Specifically, we model both densities and structural mechanisms using tensor-product B-splines.
Restricting structural mechanisms is not itself new (Hoyer et al. 2008; Bühlmann et al. 2014; Morville and Park 2025; Zhang and Hyvärinen 2009). Previous work, however, does so primarily to facilitate causal discovery or parameter estimation. In contrast, we employ a restricted mechanism family to perform efficient inference in fully specified continuous SCMs.
The main contributions of this work are as follows:

• 

We formulate probabilistic inference in continuous SCMs as message passing in a common finite-dimensional approximate functional space, avoiding the explicit construction of the distributions induced by nonlinear structural mechanisms.

• 

We derive forward and backward message-passing operators based on LOTUS and spline projection, reducing inference to linear algebra and numerical quadrature in this common function space.

• 

We instantiate the framework using tensor-product B-spline representations for both structural mechanisms and probability densities, resulting in a practical semi-analytical inference algorithm for continuous SCMs.

## 2 Preliminaries

We briefly review the concepts required throughout the paper and establish the notation.

## 2.1 Structural Causal Models

A Structural Causal Model (SCM) (Pearl 2009) is a tuple

 $\mathcal{M}=(\mathcal{U},\mathcal{V},\mathcal{F},P(\mathcal{U})),$ 

where

• 

 $\mathcal{U}$ is the set of exogenous variables;

• 

 $\mathcal{V}$ is the set of endogenous variables;

• 

 $\mathcal{F}=\{f_{X}\}_{X\in\mathcal{V}}$ is a collection of structural mechanisms,

 $X=f_{X}(\operatorname{Pa}(X),U_{X}),$ 

where each endogenous variable is a deterministic function of its endogenous parents, $\operatorname{Pa}(X)$ , and its associated exogenous variable $U_{X}$ . Throughout this work, we assume mutually independent exogenous variables and one exogenous variable per endogenous variable.

• 

 $P(\mathcal{U})$ is the joint probability distribution of the exogenous variables.

The structural mechanisms induce a directed acyclic graph (DAG) $\mathcal{G}$ whose vertices are the
endogenous variables and whose edges encode parent-child relationships. Under
the above assumptions, the SCM satisfies the causal Markov
property (Peters et al. 2017), implying the factorization
 $P(\mathcal{V})=\prod\nolimits_{X\in\mathcal{V}}P(X\mid\operatorname{Pa}(X)).$ 

SCMs support three principal classes of inference queries (Pearl and Mackenzie 2018).
Observational queries compute probabilities conditioned on observed
variables. Interventional queries evaluate the distribution resulting
from modifying one or more structural mechanisms via the $do(\cdot)$ 
operator. Counterfactual queries reason about hypothetical outcomes by
combining observed evidence with interventions on the same SCM instance. Although
these queries differ in their semantics, they all ultimately require
efficient probabilistic inference.
For an illustrative representation of these queries, we refer the reader to the Appendix.

## 2.2 Message Passing on Graphical Models

Exact probabilistic inference in graphical models is commonly performed by
message-passing algorithms, which exploit conditional independence to
decompose global inference into local computations
(Cowell et al. 1999; Koller and Friedman 2009). Rather than manipulating the full joint
distribution directly, neighboring regions exchange messages that summarize the
influence of the remaining variables.

For tree-structured graphical models, exact marginals are computed by the
sum-product algorithm, which propagates messages along the graph
edges (Pearl 1982). For non-tree models, exact inference first
constructs a junction tree whose nodes correspond to variable cliques. Messages
are then exchanged between neighboring cliques through their separator sets
using local multiplication, marginalization, and normalization (Lauritzen and Spiegelhalter 1988; Jensen et al. 1990; Shenoy and Shafer 1990).

Let
 $\mathcal{T}=(\mathcal{C},\mathcal{E})$ 
denote a junction tree, where each node
 $C\in\mathcal{C}$ is a clique and each edge
 $(C_{i},C_{j})\in\mathcal{E}$ connects neighboring cliques. The separator
associated with an edge is
 $S_{ij}=C_{i}\cap C_{j}.$ 
Each clique stores a joint state, while separator messages ensure consistency between neighboring cliques.
After the collect and distribute passes, calibrated clique states agree on their separator marginals.

Although every acyclic SCM induces a Bayesian network (Koller and Friedman 2009)
satisfying the causal Markov property (Section 2.1), its local
conditional distributions are not represented explicitly. Instead, they are
defined implicitly by deterministic structural mechanisms and exogenous
variables. Consequently, the local probability factors required by standard
message-passing algorithms are unavailable.
The proposed framework overcomes this limitation by propagating probability
measures directly through structural mechanisms rather than explicit
conditional densities. By combining numerical realizations of the LOTUS with
least-squares recovery in a common functional space, the resulting
algorithm retains the junction-tree message-passing paradigm while avoiding the
explicit construction of conditional probability distributions.

## 2.3 Spline Representation

Throughout, probability densities and structural mechanisms are
represented in a finite-dimensional B-spline space. Let

 $\{N_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}$ 

denote a B-spline basis of polynomial degree $3$ defined by the Cox-de Boor formula (de Boor 2001) over the
computational domain $\Omega$ . Any spline in this space can be written as

 $s(u)=\sum\nolimits_{i=1}^{n}w_{i}N_{i}(u),$ 

where $w_{i}\in\mathbb{R}$ are the spline coefficients.

The framework relies on two fundamental properties of B-splines. First,
 $N_{i}(x)\geq 0$ , so non-negative coefficients produce non-negative
functions. Second, the basis functions are piecewise polynomials, enabling
efficient evaluation of integrals by Gaussian quadrature.

Let $f\colon\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}$ be approximated in the B-spline space
spanned by $\{N_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}$ . The approximation is

 $\hat{f}(x)=\sum\nolimits_{i=1}^{n}w_{i}N_{i}(x)=\mathbf{N}(x)^{\top}\mathbf{w}.$ 

The spline coefficients are obtained by the $L^{2}(\Omega)$ projection (Cottrell et al. 2009), which yields the linear system

 $\mathbf{M}\mathbf{w}=\mathbf{b},$ 

where
 $M_{ij}=\int_{\Omega}N_{i}(\mathbf{x})N_{j}(\mathbf{x})\,d\mathbf{x}$ 
form the mass matrix and
 $b_{i}=\int_{\Omega}N_{i}(\mathbf{x})f(\mathbf{x})\,d\mathbf{x}$ 
the load vector.
Since the B-spline basis is linearly independent, the mass matrix is symmetric
positive definite and the projection is uniquely defined. Both the mass matrix
and load vector are evaluated efficiently using Gaussian quadrature. A
 $q$ -point Gaussian quadrature rule integrates polynomials of degree up to
 $2q-1$ exactly (Quarteroni et al. 2007).
More details in the Appendix.

Multivariate spline spaces are obtained by tensor products,

 $N_{\mathbf{i}}(\mathbf{x})=\prod\nolimits_{k=1}^{d}N^{(k)}_{i_{k}}(x_{k}),$ 

where $\mathbf{i}=(i_{1},\ldots,i_{d})$ is a multi-index, giving

 $s(\mathbf{x})=\sum\nolimits_{i_{1}=1}^{n_{1}}\cdots\sum\nolimits_{i_{d}=1}^{n_{d}}w_{i_{1},\ldots,i_{d}}\prod\nolimits_{k=1}^{d}N^{(k)}_{i_{k}}(x_{k}),$ 

where $w_{i_{1},\ldots,i_{d}}$ are the spline coefficients. The resulting basis inherits the properties of one-dimensional B-splines in each coordinate direction (Piegl and Tiller 1997; Hughes et al. 2005).
The tensor-product structure implies
factorization of the multidimensional mass matrix into products of one-dimensional matrices, allowing both the
projection and Gaussian quadrature to be performed efficiently by tensor
products of one-dimensional rules (Stoer and Bulirsch 2002).
These projection operators form the numerical foundation of this inference framework.

## 3 Method

This section presents the message-passing framework for inference
in fully specified SCMs. Whereas causal discovery and identification determine the causal model
and identifiable causal effects, we address the complementary problem of efficiently
answering causal queries. The algorithm operates directly on structural
mechanisms, avoiding the explicit construction of conditional densities.
Inference proceeds in two stages. The forward pass propagates probability
measures through the SCM to construct marginals and clique states for
observational and interventional inference. The backward pass then propagates
likelihood information through these states to incorporate evidence, enabling
conditional and counterfactual inference.

## 3.1 Densities and Mechanisms as Splines

The framework represents probability densities and structural mechanisms in a common finite-dimensional spline space,
allowing all inference operations to be expressed through spline coefficients, projection operators, and numerical quadrature.
Let $\operatorname{Pa}(X)$ 
denote the parent set of an endogenous variable $X$ and $|\operatorname{Pa}(X)|=M$ . For each parent variable $X_{m}\in\operatorname{Pa}(X)$ , let
 $\{N^{(m)}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n_{m}}$ 
be a one-dimensional B-spline basis defined over the computational domain
 $\Omega_{m}=[\alpha_{m},\beta_{m}]$ . The tensor-product basis associated with the parent set is

 $N_{\mathbf{j}}(\mathbf{x})=\prod\nolimits_{m=1}^{M}N^{(m)}_{j_{m}}(x_{m}),$ 

where
 $\mathbf{x}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{M})$ 
and
 $\mathbf{j}=(j_{1},\ldots,j_{M})$ .
The joint marginal density over the parent variables is represented by

 $\displaystyle p_{\operatorname{Pa}(X)}(\mathbf{x})$ 
 $\displaystyle=\sum\nolimits_{\mathbf{j}}w^{\operatorname{Pa}(X)}_{\mathbf{j}}N_{\mathbf{j}}(\mathbf{x})$ 

 $\displaystyle=\sum_{j_{1}=1}^{n_{1}}\cdots\sum_{j_{M}=1}^{n_{M}}w^{\operatorname{Pa}(X)}_{j_{1},\ldots,j_{M}}\prod_{m=1}^{M}N^{(m)}_{j_{m}}(x_{m}).$ 

The coefficients are collected in the tensor
 $\mathbf{w}_{\operatorname{Pa}(X)}$ , which characterizes the modeled
joint density (Schellhase and Kauermann 2012).

A tensor-product B-spline expansion is generally not a probability density.
Even so, non-negative coefficients guarantee a
non-negative spline (Section 2.3). Its total mass is

 $\int_{\Omega_{\operatorname{Pa}(X)}}\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!p_{\operatorname{Pa}(X)}(\mathbf{x})\,d\mathbf{x}=\sum\nolimits_{\mathbf{j}}w_{\mathbf{j}}^{\operatorname{Pa}(X)}c_{\mathbf{j}},\ \ c_{\mathbf{j}}=\int_{\Omega_{\operatorname{Pa}(X)}}\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!N_{\mathbf{j}}(\mathbf{x})\,d\mathbf{x},$ 

where $c_{\mathbf{j}}$ are the tensor-product mass coefficients. Hence, the
spline defines a valid probability density whenever

 $w_{\mathbf{j}}^{\operatorname{Pa}(X)}\geq 0,\qquad\sum\nolimits_{\mathbf{j}}w_{\mathbf{j}}^{\operatorname{Pa}(X)}c_{\mathbf{j}}=1.$ 

The structural mechanism associated with an endogenous variable $X$ is likewise represented using a tensor-product B-spline basis over its parent variables and the corresponding exogenous variable $U_{X}$ . Let
 $\{N^{(U_{X})}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n_{U}}$ 
be a one-dimensional B-spline basis defined over the computational domain
 $\Omega_{U_{X}}=[\alpha_{U},\beta_{U}]$ .
Then

 $\displaystyle f_{X}(\mathbf{x},u)={}$ 
 $\displaystyle\sum_{\mathbf{r}}\sum_{\ell=1}^{n_{U}}\theta^{X}_{\mathbf{r},\ell}\,N_{\mathbf{r}}(\mathbf{x})N^{(U_{X})}_{\ell}(u)$ 

 $\displaystyle={}$ 
 $\displaystyle\sum_{r_{1}=1}^{n_{1}}\cdots\sum_{r_{M}=1}^{n_{M}}\sum_{\ell=1}^{n_{U}}\theta^{X}_{r_{1},\ldots,r_{M},\ell}$ 

 $\displaystyle\times\left(\prod\nolimits_{m=1}^{M}N^{(m)}_{r_{m}}(x_{m})\right)N^{(U_{X})}_{\ell}(u).$ 

Throughout, we assume that the pushforward distributions
considered by the method are absolutely continuous with respect to Lebesgue
measure on their computational domains.

## 3.2 Forward Message Passing

The forward pass processes the SCM in topological order. For each endogenous
variable, it propagates the parent measure through the structural
mechanism to recover the child marginal. Whenever required to preserve downstream dependencies,
it additionally constructs joint spline states over selected variable scopes.
After forward propagation, scopes that are strict subsets of another
stored scope are discarded, and the remaining maximal scopes become
the cliques of the junction tree used during backward inference.

Rather than explicitly constructing the pushforward density induced by a
structural mechanism, the method characterizes the propagated measure
through its moments. By the LOTUS, these moments can be evaluated for any
measurable test function $\varphi$ (Pollard 2002). Thus, for an
endogenous variable $X$ with structural mechanism $f_{X}$ ,

 $\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\varphi(X)]=\mathbb{E}\!\left[\varphi\!\left(f_{X}(\operatorname{Pa}(X),U_{X})\right)\right].$ 

(1)

It remains to choose test functions that recover the spline coefficients.
Thus, the forward pass consists of three steps.
First, LOTUS is used to evaluate the moments of the propagated probability measure.
Second, these moments are projected onto the spline basis to recover a density.
Third, whenever dependencies must be preserved, the same procedure constructs
joint clique states used later during backward inference.

## Marginal propagation.

The natural choice is to select the B-spline basis functions themselves as test functions,
since the resulting moments are the entries of the load vector required by
the $L^{2}$ projection (Section 2.3). Consequently, the
forward pass computes the quantities needed to recover the spline
coefficients.
Given B-splines
 $\{N_{i}^{X}\}_{i=1}^{n_{X}}$ as test functions. Given the joint density $p_{\operatorname{Pa}(X)}$ and $f_{X}\colon\Omega_{\operatorname{Pa(X)}}\!\times\Omega_{U_{X}}\!\!\rightarrow\Omega_{X}$ , Eq. (1) reduces to

 $\displaystyle V_{i}^{X}\!=\!\!\int_{\Omega_{\operatorname{Pa}(X)}}\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!p_{\operatorname{Pa}(X)}(\mathbf{x})\int_{\Omega_{U_{X}}}\!\!\!\!\!\!\!p_{U_{X}}(u)\,N_{i}^{X}\!\left(f_{X}(\mathbf{x},u)\right)du\,d\mathbf{x},$ 

(2)

for $i=1,\ldots,n_{X}$ .
Let
 $p_{X}(x)=\sum_{j=1}^{n_{X}}w_{j}^{X}N_{j}^{X}(x)$ 
be the spline approximation of the propagated density. Since

 $\int_{\Omega_{X}}N_{i}^{X}(x)p_{X}(x)\,dx=\mathbb{E}\!\left[N_{i}^{X}(X)\right]=V_{i}^{X},$ 

the projection coefficients satisfy

 $\mathbf{M}_{X}\mathbf{w}_{X}=\mathbf{V}_{X},$ 

where
 $(\mathbf{M}_{X})_{ij}=\int_{\Omega_{X}}N_{i}^{X}(x)N_{j}^{X}(x)\,dx$ are the mass matrix entries
and
 $\mathbf{V}_{X}=\left(V_{1}^{X},\ldots,V_{n_{X}}^{X}\right)^{\top}$ is the load vector.

## Clique-state construction.

Marginal densities suffice only for single-variable queries. Message passing
requires joint clique states over the junction tree introduced in
Section 2.2. These joint
states preserve the dependencies required by downstream structural mechanisms
and later serve as the clique states updated during the backward pass.

For an endogenous variable $X$ , let
 $\mathbf{R}=\{R_{m}\}_{m=1}^{L}\subseteq\operatorname{Pa}(X)$ 
denote the parent variables retained in the joint state associated with
 $X$ , as determined by the required downstream dependencies. The resulting clique state has scope
 $C=\mathbf{R}\cup\{X\}.$ 
Let
 $\{N^{R_{m}}_{i_{m}}\}_{i_{m}=1}^{n_{R_{m}}}$ 
and
 $\{N^{X}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n_{X}}$ 
denote the corresponding one-dimensional B-spline bases.
The tensor-product basis on the clique $C$ is

 $N_{\boldsymbol{i}}^{C}(\mathbf{r},x)=\left(\prod\nolimits_{m=1}^{L}N_{i_{m}}^{R_{m}}(r_{m})\right)N_{i_{L+1}}^{X}(x),$ 

where
 $\boldsymbol{i}=(i_{1},\ldots,i_{L},i_{X}).$ 
Choosing the tensor-product basis functions as test
functions yields the load-tensor entries

 $\displaystyle V_{\boldsymbol{i}}^{C}$ 
 $\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\!\left[N_{\boldsymbol{i}}^{C}(\mathbf{R},X)\right]=\int_{\Omega_{\operatorname{Pa}(X)}}\!\!\!p_{\operatorname{Pa}(X)}(\mathbf{x})\int_{\Omega_{U_{X}}}\!\!\!\!p_{U_{X}}(u)$ 

 $\displaystyle\times\left(\prod\nolimits_{m=1}^{L}\!\!N_{i_{m}}^{R_{m}}(r_{m})\right)N_{i_{L+1}}^{X}\!\left(f_{X}(\mathbf{x},u)\right)\,du\,d\mathbf{x}.$ 

(3)

The expectation is evaluated over the full parent tuple, while the clique state
retains only the variables in $C$ . Variables in
 $\operatorname{Pa}(X)\setminus\mathbf{R}$ are marginalized automatically, since
their basis functions do not appear in the test function. Computationally,
this corresponds to quadrature summation over the omitted parent dimensions.
The clique-state coefficients satisfy
 $\mathbf{M}_{C}\mathbf{w}_{C}=\mathbf{V}_{C},$ 
where,
 $\mathbf{M}_{C}=\mathbf{M}_{R_{1}}\otimes\cdots\otimes\mathbf{M}_{R_{L}}\otimes\mathbf{M}_{X}.$ 
Its Kronecker structure enables matrix-free matrix-vector products, avoiding
explicit construction of the full mass matrix.

## Clique-tree construction.

Since non-maximal scopes can be recovered by marginalization, only maximal
scopes are retained. Let $\mathcal{S}$ denote the collection of scopes for
which the forward pass has constructed explicit joint spline states. Then

 $\mathcal{C}=\left\{C\in\mathcal{S}:\nexists C^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}\text{ such that }C\subsetneq C^{\prime}\right\}.$ 

The clique-intersection graph connects cliques with non-empty intersections,
weighted by $|S_{ij}|$ . A maximum-weight spanning tree (Kruskal 1956; Prim 1957) yields the junction
tree (Lauritzen and Spiegelhalter 1988; Jensen et al. 1990), which is accepted only if it
satisfies the running-intersection property; that is, for every variable
 $X$ , the cliques containing $X$ form a connected subtree.

## Quadrature and projection.

The moments in
Eqs. (2)
and (3)
are evaluated by tensor-product Gaussian quadrature.
The spline coefficients
are then recovered by a regularized non-negative least-squares approximation (NNLS) (Lawson and Hanson 1995)
of the corresponding $L^{2}$ -projection system.
Let
 $\{x_{q_{m}}^{(m)},\omega_{q_{m}}^{(m)}\}_{q_{m}=1}^{Q_{m}}$ ,
 $\{u_{s},\omega_{s}^{(U)}\}_{s=1}^{S}$ 
denote the quadtrature rule for the parent and
exogenous respectively. Defining
 $\mathbf{x}_{\mathbf{q}}=\bigl(x_{q_{1}}^{(1)},\ldots,x_{q_{M}}^{(M)}\bigr)$ 
and
 $\omega_{\mathbf{q}}=\prod\nolimits_{m=1}^{M}\omega_{q_{m}}^{(m)}$ ,
Eq. (3) becomes

 $\displaystyle V_{\boldsymbol{i}}^{C}$ 
 $\displaystyle\approx\sum\nolimits_{\mathbf{q}}\omega_{\mathbf{q}}p_{\operatorname{Pa}(X)}(\mathbf{x}_{\mathbf{q}})\sum\nolimits_{s=1}^{S}\omega_{s}^{(U)}p_{U_{X}}(u_{s})$ 

 $\displaystyle\times\left(\prod\nolimits_{m=1}^{L}N_{i_{m}}^{R_{m}}(r_{m,\mathbf{q}})\right)N_{i_{L+1}}^{X}\!\left(f_{X}(\mathbf{x}_{\mathbf{q}},u_{s})\right).$ 

To enforce non-negative coefficients, we solve the diagonally regularized NNLS

 $\mathbf{w}_{C}=\arg\min_{\mathbf{w}\geq 0}\left\|(\mathbf{M}_{C}+\lambda\mathbf{I})\mathbf{w}-\mathbf{V}_{C}\right\|_{2}^{2},$ 

where the diagonal shift $\lambda\mathbf{I}$ improves the numerical
conditioning of the projection operator.
The coefficients are then normalized,
 $\mathbf{w}_{C}\leftarrow\frac{\mathbf{w}_{C}}{\sum_{\mathbf{j}}w_{\mathbf{j}}c_{\mathbf{j}}},$ 
ensuring that the recovered spline represents a normalized probability density.

## Interventions.

Interventional inference requires no additional message-passing operator. Under
an intervention $do(X=x^{\star})$ , the structural mechanism of $X$ is
replaced by the constant assignment $X:=x^{\star}$ , yielding a modified SCM.
The forward pass is then reapplied, reusing the states of non-descendants and
recomputing only those affected by the intervention. Since a point intervention
induces a Dirac measure, the intervened variable is propagated
deterministically rather than represented as a spline density.

## 3.3 Backward Message Passing

The backward pass incorporates observations by propagating likelihoods through
the junction tree (Section 2.2). Starting from the prior clique states
computed during the forward pass, neighboring cliques exchange likelihood-ratio messages
that update these priors into posterior clique states. Whenever a message is propagated through a structural
mechanism, the corresponding conditional density is unavailable explicitly.
Instead, likelihoods are propagated through the structural mechanism via a local
backward operator.

## Local backward operator.

Let $X$ be an endogenous variable with structural mechanism $f_{X}$ and
 $m_{X}:\Omega_{X}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}$ 
denote the likelihood accumulated at $X$ .
Propagating this likelihood through the structural mechanism induces the
unnormalized likelihood

 $\widetilde{m}_{X}(\mathbf{x},u)=m_{X}\!\left(f_{X}(\mathbf{x},u)\right),$ 

defined over the mechanism inputs.
This representation can subsequently be marginalized onto any subset of the
mechanism inputs.
For ordinary backward message passing, the exogenous variable is marginalized,
yielding the propagated likelihood over the endogenous parents,

 $\mathcal{B}_{X}[m_{X}](\mathbf{x})=\int_{\Omega_{U_{X}}}\widetilde{m}_{X}(\mathbf{x},u)\,p_{U_{X}}(u)\,\mathrm{d}u,$ 

where
 $\mathbf{x}\in\Omega_{\operatorname{Pa}(X)}.$ 
Thus, evidence is propagated through the structural mechanism without
explicitly constructing the conditional density
 $p(X\mid\operatorname{Pa}(X)).$ 
Retaining the exogenous variable instead yields likelihoods over
 $U_{X}$ , which are used during the abduction step of counterfactual inference.

Using the Gaussian quadrature rule
 $\{u_{s},\omega_{s}^{(U)}\}_{s=1}^{S},$ 
parent likelihood is approximated at the quadrature nodes
 $\mathbf{x}_{\mathbf{q}}$ 
by

 $\mathcal{B}_{X}[m_{X}](\mathbf{x}_{\mathbf{q}})\approx\sum_{s=1}^{S}\omega_{s}^{(U)}p_{U_{X}}(u_{s})\,\widetilde{m}_{X}(\mathbf{x}_{\mathbf{q}},u_{s}).$ 

The propagated likelihood is combined with the stored prior parent state,
 $\widetilde{p}_{\operatorname{Pa}(X)}(\mathbf{x})=p_{\operatorname{Pa}(X)}(\mathbf{x})\mathcal{B}_{X}[m_{X}](\mathbf{x}).$ 
When $m_{X}$ represents the likelihood induced by the observations
 $\mathcal{O}$ , normalizing this quantity yields the posterior parent state
 $p_{\operatorname{Pa}(X)\mid\mathcal{O}}(\mathbf{x}),$ 
represented in the corresponding tensor-product B-spline space using the same
quadrature-based projection procedure as in the forward pass.

## Observation likelihoods.

Observed evidence is incorporated through local likelihoods. For an observation
 $Y=y^{\star}$ , we define
 $L_{Y}(y)=L(y^{\star}\mid y),$ 
where $L(y^{\star}\mid y)$ is a Gaussian kernel centered at $y^{\star}$ providing a smooth
approximation of the Dirac measure corresponding to exact conditioning.
If a clique contains multiple observations, their likelihoods are
multiplied. For
 $\mathcal{O}_{C}=\{\,Y_{r}=y_{r}^{\star}:Y_{r}\in C\,\},$ 
the local likelihood is
 $\Lambda_{C}(\mathbf{x}_{C})=\prod\nolimits_{Y_{r}\in\mathcal{O}_{C}}L_{Y_{r}}(x_{r}).$ 

## Junction-tree propagation.

The forward pass provides prior joint spline states $p_{C}$ for the cliques of
the constructed junction tree. Because these states are clique marginals,
separator messages are represented as likelihood ratios rather than posterior
densities. This avoids repeatedly incorporating the separator prior when neighboring clique beliefs are combined.
For a directed edge $C\rightarrow D$ , define

 $\Lambda_{C\setminus D}(\mathbf{x}_{C})=\Lambda_{C}(\mathbf{x}_{C})\prod\nolimits_{B\in\mathcal{N}(C)\setminus\{D\}}m_{B\rightarrow C}(\mathbf{x}_{S_{BC}}).$ 

The corresponding updated clique state is

 $p_{C}^{\,C\setminus D}(\mathbf{x}_{C})\propto p_{C}(\mathbf{x}_{C})\Lambda_{C\setminus D}(\mathbf{x}_{C}).$ 

After marginalizing the updated and prior clique states onto
 $S_{CD}$ , the outgoing message is

 $m_{C\rightarrow D}(\mathbf{x}_{S_{CD}})\propto\frac{p_{S_{CD}}^{\,C\setminus D}(\mathbf{x}_{S_{CD}})}{p_{S_{CD}}(\mathbf{x}_{S_{CD}})}.$ 

Thus, clique and separator densities are represented by
spline coefficients, while likelihood messages are evaluated as ratios of
these spline densities.
After the collect and distribute passes, the posterior associated with clique
 $C$ is

 $p_{C\mid\mathcal{O}}(\mathbf{x}_{C})=\frac{1}{Z_{C}}p_{C}(\mathbf{x}_{C})\Lambda_{C}(\mathbf{x}_{C})\!\!\prod_{D\in\mathcal{N}(C)}\!\!m_{D\rightarrow C}(\mathbf{x}_{S_{DC}}).$ 

To represent an updated density in the spline basis or perform normalization, the corresponding projection
is performed using tensor-product Gaussian quadrature.

## Counterfactuals. 

Counterfactual inference follows the abduct-intervene-infer procedure.
During the abduction step, the local backward operator is applied while
retaining the relevant exogenous variables in the propagated state, yielding
their posterior distributions conditioned on the observations (abduct). The intervention is then
applied by replacing the corresponding structural mechanisms
(intervene). Finally, the modified SCM is evaluated using the abducted
exogenous distribution (infer). This preserves the shared exogenous realization between
the factual and counterfactual worlds.

## 3.4 Complexity Analysis

Let $N$ denote the number of endogenous variables, $K$ the number of
B-spline basis functions and $Q$ the number of quadrature nodes per dimension,
and $S$ the number of quadrature nodes used for the exogenous variable. For a node with $M$ 
parents, the tensor-product representation contains $K^{M}$ coefficients and
uses a $Q^{M}$ -point quadrature grid. The implementation exploits
tensor-product structure throughout via tensor contractions and
Kronecker-structured operators, avoiding explicit tensor-product mass
matrices.

## Forward pass.

For a node with $M$ parents, the dominant cost is evaluating the structural
mechanism and parent density over the $SQ^{M}$ quadrature pairs and assembling
the $K$ child moments, requiring
 $\mathcal{O}(SQ^{M}K)$ 
operations. For an SCM with $N$ endogenous variables and maximum parent count
 $M_{\max}$ , the total forward-pass complexity is

 $\mathcal{O}\!\left(NSQ^{M_{\max}}K\right).$ 

## Backward pass.

Let $\mathcal{C}$ denote the clique set of the junction tree,
 $C_{\max}$ its maximum clique size,
and $\tau$ the treewidth of the triangulated SCM graph. The largest
junction-tree clique then has size $C_{\max}=\tau+1$ . A clique update evaluates local
likelihoods, separator messages, and, where required, the local backward
operator over a tensor-product quadrature grid. Excluding the projection solve,
the worst-case complexity is

 $\mathcal{O}\!\left(|\mathcal{C}|\,SQ^{C_{\max}}K\right).$ 

Collect-distribute sends two directed messages along each
junction-tree edge, making this bound tight up to a constant factor.

## Space complexity.

Let $\mathcal{J}$ denote the maintained joint states and $d_{J}$ its
dimension. Storing the spline coefficients requires
 $\mathcal{O}(\sum_{J\in\mathcal{J}}K^{d_{J}})$ memory, while the largest
tensor-product quadrature grid requires
 $\mathcal{O}(d_{\max}Q^{d_{\max}})$ , where $d_{\max}$ is the maximum state
dimension. Excluding optional operator caching, the total memory requirement is

 $\mathcal{O}\!\left(\sum\nolimits_{J\in\mathcal{J}}K^{d_{J}}+d_{\max}Q^{d_{\max}}\right).$

## 4 Experiments

For the experiments, we generate random directed acyclic SCMs with varying numbers of endogenous variables,
graph depths, and maximum parent orders.
For each configuration, we generate a random depth-constrained DAG. A
single-parent backbone first fixes the graph depth, after which the remaining
nodes are assigned random depths and connected to admissible parent sets from
preceding levels, subject to the maximum in-degree.
Exogenous noise distributions are sampled as random spline densities,
providing controlled variation in smoothness and multimodality. Endogenous densities and structural mechanisms
are represented using uniform cubic B-spline bases on $\Omega=[0,1]$ , with Gauss-Legendre quadrature for numerical integration.

The implementation exploits the resulting tensor-product structure through matrix-free tensor contractions and
Kronecker-structured operators. Quadrature-intensive computations are accelerated on
GPUs.11
 1

 Implemented in PyTorch 2.11.0 with CUDA 12.8 on an NVIDIA RTX PRO 4000 Blackwell GPU; 8 CPU cores per task. Parent-density, structural mechanism, basis, and projection evaluations are performed as batched tensor operations. Basis evaluations are cached and reused during inference.

Inference is compared against an ancestral Monte Carlo (MC) reference computed from $10^{6}$ 
independent samples.
Metrics are evaluated for every endogenous variable encountered during propagation and aggregated over the entire graph.
For each endogenous variable we report two accuracy measures.
For additional evaluation metrics, we refer the reader to the Appendix.
The first is the domain-normalized expectation error,

 $\epsilon_{\mathrm{dom}}=100\,\frac{\left|\mu_{\mathrm{ours}}-\mu_{\mathrm{MC}}\right|}{b-a},$ 

where $\Omega=[a,b]$ is the computational domain, measuring the expectation
error relative to the variable’s natural scale.
The second is the Hellinger distance,

 $H(p,q)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(\int_{\Omega}\left(\sqrt{p(x)}-\sqrt{q(x)}\right)^{2}\,dx\right)^{1/2},$ 

which compares the analytical density $p$ with the MC reference density $q$ .
It lies in $[0,1]$ , where $0$ indicates identical densities.

We compare only against MC simulation since no existing method, to the best of
our knowledge, performs general-purpose probabilistic inference in continuous
SCMs directly on arbitrary nonlinear structural mechanisms. Existing approaches
either target causal identification and estimation (Sharma and Kiciman 2020) or require
compiling the SCM into an alternative tractable representation (Darwiche 2022; Wang and Kwiatkowska 2023; Zečević et al. 2021).
MC is therefore the natural model-agnostic baseline, converging to the true solution with increasing samples.

## 4.1 Results

The main experiments consider tree-structured SCMs and DAGs where approximately 20% of the nodes have two parents,
reflecting the exponential tensor-product scaling of the
proposed representation.
We therefore focus on the regime where exact
tensor-product inference remains computationally practical, leaving sparse and
low-rank extensions to future work. Throughout, the number of quadrature points
scales with the basis resolution; eight points per basis function were found
best in preliminary experiments. For each graph size, we generate five
random SCMs.

## Forward pass.

Figure 1 reports the percentage of endogenous variables whose
domain-normalized expectation error exceeds $0.5\%$ . As expected, increasing
the number of basis functions consistently improves the approximation.
With $32$ basis functions, no high-error variables are observed, even for SCMs
with up to $10^{4}$ endogenous variables.
For two-parent SCMs, computational cost limited experiments to graphs of at most $500$ endogenous variables,
and to only $20$ variables when using $128$ basis functions.
For tree-structured SCMs, Figure 2 compares the runtime of the proposed
method with the high-accuracy MC reference. Our approach is approximately one
order of magnitude faster while maintaining comparable accuracy.
Finally, Figure 3 reports the mean Hellinger distance to the MC reference,
together with one standard deviation across experiments. The error decreases from approximately
 $0.2$ with $32$ basis functions toward zero as the basis is refined, demonstrating convergence of the inferred densities.

## Backward pass.

For backward evaluation, we consider smaller SCMs than in the forward
experiments, since conditioning evidence at leaf nodes becomes less informative
for distant ancestors. Validating backward inference requires a different MC
reference, as rejection sampling becomes impractical under conditioning.
Instead, we generate ancestral samples by forward simulation and approximate
conditioning using Gaussian kernel weights. Conditional expectations are
estimated via self-normalized importance sampling.

Figure 4 reports the average percentage of endogenous
variables with $\epsilon_{\mathrm{dom}}\geq 0.5\%$ for two-parent SCMs with
three observations, maximum clique size three, and maximum separator size two.
Increasing the number of B-spline basis functions consistently improves the
approximation, although backward inference requires a finer basis than the
forward pass to eliminate high-error variables. This is expected, since
conditional densities are more difficult to approximate and kernel weighting
reduces the effective sample size of the MC reference.
Figure 5 reports the corresponding Hellinger distances for both tree and two-parent SCMs.
Although backward inference exhibits larger errors and greater variability than
the forward pass, the distance decreases systematically with basis resolution.
As in the forward pass (Figure 3), the performance is largely
independent of the number of parents.

## 5 Conclusion

This work introduced a message-passing framework for fully specified SCMs that
performs observational, interventional, and counterfactual inference within a
unified B-spline representation. By combining forward propagation of
probability measures with backward propagation of likelihoods, the method avoids
explicit construction of pushforward and conditional densities.
Experiments demonstrate accurate probability densities and expectations
while scaling to large SCMs, with errors decreasing consistently as the spline resolution increases.
Future work will investigate low-rank tensor
approximations and adaptive basis refinement for higher-dimensional models with
larger parent sets.

## Appendix B B-spline Basis Functions

The proposed framework employs tensor-product B-spline basis functions to
represent probability densities over bounded computational domains.
Throughout this work, let

 $\Omega=[a,b]\subset\mathbb{R}$ 

denote the computational domain of a single variable. A univariate B-spline
basis is defined by a non-decreasing knot vector

 $\boldsymbol{\xi}=(\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{m}),\qquad\xi_{i}\leq\xi_{i+1},$ 

and polynomial degree $p$ . If the spline space contains $K$ basis
functions, then
 $m=K+p.$ 
Throughout this work, we employ open knot vectors, in which the first and last
knots are repeated $p+1$ times. Consequently, the spline basis is defined on

 $\Omega=[\xi_{p+1},\xi_{m-p}],$ 

which coincides with the interval bounded by the first and last distinct knots.
The B-spline basis

 $\{N_{i,p}\}_{i=1}^{K}$ 

is defined recursively by the Cox-de Boor formula. For
 $p=0$ ,

 $N_{i,0}(u)=\begin{cases}1,&\xi_{i}\leq u<\xi_{i+1},\\[2.84526pt]
0,&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}$ 

 $\displaystyle N_{i,p}(u)$ 
 $\displaystyle=\frac{u-\xi_{i}}{\xi_{i+p}-\xi_{i}}N_{i,p-1}(u)$ 

 $\displaystyle+\frac{\xi_{i+p+1}-u}{\xi_{i+p+1}-\xi_{i+1}}N_{i+1,p-1}(u),$ 

where fractions with zero denominator are interpreted as zero. Figure 7
illustrates an open cubic B-spline basis.
Any spline in the resulting approximation space can be expressed as

 $s(u)=\sum_{i=1}^{K}w_{i}N_{i,p}(u),$ 

where $w_{i}\in\mathbb{R}$ are the spline coefficients.

For multivariate functions, the computational domain is

 $\Omega=\Omega_{1}\times\cdots\times\Omega_{d}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}.$

## Appendix C Isogeometric Analysis

Isogeometric analysis (IGA) employs spline basis functions for numerical approximation. The proposed framework adopts several numerical techniques from IGA, including spline representations and efficient quadrature for evaluating integrals.
For this section, without loss of generality, we omit the degree notation in the B-spline basis.

We first consider the one-dimensional case. Let
 $f\colon\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}$ be approximated in a finite-dimensional spline space spanned by the B-spline basis
 $\{N_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}.$ 
The approximation is

 $\hat{f}(x)=\sum\nolimits_{i=1}^{n}w_{i}N_{i}(x)=\mathbf{N}(x)^{\top}\mathbf{w},$ 

where
 $\mathbf{N}(\mathbf{x})=[N_{1}(\mathbf{x})\ \cdots\ N_{n}(\mathbf{x})]^{\top}$ 
denotes the basis vector and
 $\mathbf{w}=(w_{1},\ldots,w_{n})^{\top}$ 
contains the spline coefficients.
The coefficients are determined through the $L^{2}(\Omega)$ projection by minimizing

 $J(\mathbf{w})=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}\left(f(\mathbf{x})-\mathbf{N}(\mathbf{x})^{\top}\mathbf{w}\right)^{2}\,d\mathbf{x}.$ 

The first-order optimality conditions yield the linear system

 $\mathbf{M}\mathbf{w}=\mathbf{b},$ 

where
 $M_{ij}=\int_{\Omega}N_{i}(\mathbf{x})N_{j}(\mathbf{x})\,d\mathbf{x}$ 
are the entries of the mass matrix and
 $b_{i}=\int_{\Omega}N_{i}(\mathbf{x})f(\mathbf{x})\,d\mathbf{x}$ 
the load vector ones.

The multivariate case is obtained by tensor products of the one-dimensional bases. Let $N_{\mathbf{i}}$ be a multivariate spline.
Then the entries of the mass matrix become

 $M_{\mathbf{i}\mathbf{j}}=\prod\nolimits_{k=1}^{d}\int_{\Omega}N^{(k)}_{i_{k}}(x_{k})N^{(k)}_{j_{k}}(x_{k})\,dx_{k},$ 

where the equality follows from the tensor-product structure of the basis. Consequently, multidimensional quadrature reduces to tensor products of one-dimensional Gaussian quadrature rules. These rules are exact whenever the integrand is a polynomial and the quadrature order is chosen to integrate its degree exactly.

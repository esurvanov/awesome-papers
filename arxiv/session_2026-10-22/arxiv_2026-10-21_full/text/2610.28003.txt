## Dancing Diffusion:

Learning Continuous Programs

for Image Generation and Editing

Abstract

Diffusion models generate samples through a multi-step denoising trajectory,
yet condition every step on the same instruction, so the trajectory cannot
be addressed at the level of individual operations. We introduce Dancing Diffusion,
which assigns each span of timesteps a dedicated continuous condition block
and thereby turns conditioning into a latent program. A compiler maps text
features into this program, and a fixed pretrained generator executes its
blocks over successive sampling intervals. The trajectory can then dance,
keeping its executed history while its continuation is redirected. A shared
prefix is executed once and reused, and a later operation is rewritten while
content fixed by earlier operations is preserved. The compiler is trained
through the images it produces, with visual feedback on both the requested
change and the retained content. On SANA, reusing an executed prefix
preserves markedly more of the scene than regenerating from noise at a
similar strength of edit, learned conditions preserve more background than
native prompt switching from the same saved state, and the learned program
improves generation quality over native SANA. Later branches weaken the
edit, which leaves semantic control over individual operations limited. By
converting a single-instruction trajectory into a program of addressable
operations, Dancing Diffusion lays a foundation for structured intermediate
computation in diffusion models.

## 1 Introduction

Diffusion models generate samples through a multi-step denoising trajectory,
yet condition every step on the same instruction. Consequently, the
trajectory is not addressable at the level of individual operations. In
practice, variants that share early operations must recompute them, an edit
to one attribute propagates to content the request never mentioned, and
intermediate operations can be neither inspected nor corrected.

We introduce Dancing Diffusion, which assigns each span of timesteps a dedicated
continuous condition block, turning conditioning into a latent program. A
compiler maps text features into this program, and a fixed pretrained
generator executes its blocks over successive sampling intervals
(Figure 1b). The trajectory thereby becomes
addressable at the level of individual operations, and it can dance, keeping
its executed history while its continuation is redirected. A shared prefix
can be executed once and reused, a later operation can be rewritten while
content fixed by earlier operations is preserved, and each block can be
intervened on in isolation to test what it controls
(Figure 1a).

(b) A compiler produces conditions for a shared generator, with

We formalize when resuming a saved state reproduces the executed history
exactly and when a change to future conditions leaves declared background
content bounded. The compiler is trained through the images it produces.
A visual observer assesses both the requested change and the retention of
unrelated content, gradients update only the compiler, and the observer is
absent at inference. Temporal conditioning and learned connectors have
substantial precedents, including stage-specific prompt representations and
timestep-aware language interfaces
(Zhang et al., 2023b; Hu et al., 2024; Huberman et al., 2026). Our contribution is the
combined compiler–executor formulation and its study under paired edit
feedback and controlled suffix interventions.

Our experiments test each of these properties directly. In a controlled
setting, learned conditioning changes when content appears, halving the
early subject error of a single-expert baseline while deferring background
detail. On SANA, resuming a saved state reproduces the source exactly, and
rewriting later operations leaves the executed history intact. At an early
branch, background LPIPS falls by up to 36% relative to full regeneration,
from 0.321 to 0.227 at 6/12/12, while the semantic change is nearly
unchanged (0.088 versus 0.092). The reduction holds in every schedule under
paired family-bootstrap intervals and is confirmed on all 2376 outputs by
independent detector regions that the training observer never sees. From
the same saved state, learned conditions preserve more background than
native prompt switching in all three schedules, with comparable
replacement counts in two of them. These edits do not cost image quality. The learned program lowers
FID below native SANA, from 7.349 to 7.145 at 6/12/12, and a shared prefix is computed
once for all its continuations. Semantic control over individual operations,
however, remains limited, since later branches weaken the edit and
interventions on blocks confirm routing rather than a learned semantic
factorization. By converting a single-instruction trajectory into a program
of addressable operations, Dancing Diffusion lays a foundation for structured
intermediate computation in diffusion models.

## 2 Related work

Temporal conditioning and generation trajectories.

Expert denoisers allocate capacity across noise regimes (Balaji et al., 2022),
while stage-specific embeddings, successive proxy prompts, and timestep-aware
connectors organize the information supplied to a generator
(Zhang et al., 2023b; Huberman et al., 2026; Hu et al., 2024).
ProSpect optimizes stage-specific embeddings for reference-image attributes;
SAP provides training-free stage-aware prompting; ELLA learns a language
connector to a fixed diffusion model. These works show that the timing of
conditioning and a learned connector can each shape generation. Prompt-conditioned interventions
also study when concepts influence generation (Görgün et al., 2026).
We instead learn a caption-dependent continuous program from paired editing
feedback and use its saved intermediate states as points at which generation
can be redirected, comparing learned and native responses at identical
states.

Learned control interfaces and visual feedback.

Prefix and prompt tuning adapt continuous inputs to language models;
concept embeddings and semantic connectors extend this approach to image
models (Li & Liang, 2021; Lester et al., 2021; Gal et al., 2023; Hu et al., 2024).
Spatial branches and gated grounding layers provide additional visual
control (Zhang et al., 2023a; Li et al., 2023). Policy-gradient and
differentiable-reward methods instead learn from generated outputs
(Black et al., 2024; Fan et al., 2023; Clark et al., 2024; Prabhudesai et al., 2024).
We combine interface adaptation with feedback through a fixed generator,
using edit-response and retention objectives. The observer supplies training
gradients and is absent at inference, unlike sampling-time semantic guidance
(Chefer et al., 2023). Fixed generator weights define the adaptation setting;
they do not prove that the complete behavior existed before interface training.

Image editing and semantic interventions.

Perturbation and inversion methods start from an input image, attention
control constrains modified-caption generation, and instruction-conditioned
models learn paired transformations
(Meng et al., 2022; Mokady et al., 2023; Hertz et al., 2023; Brooks et al., 2023).
FlowEdit constructs an inversion-free mapping between source- and
target-conditioned distributions (Kulikov et al., 2025).
Our interface resumes generated trajectories and replaces future conditions;
it does not accept arbitrary photographs through an inversion module.
We distinguish same-state suffix controls from image-input FlowEdit/SDEdit
adaptations, including their source access and compute. The SANA adaptations
do not reproduce published SD3/FLUX results. Interchange interventions motivate
testing whether low-level replacements realize high-level changes
(Geiger et al., 2022); exact prefix reuse alone verifies execution, not semantics.

Evaluating semantic change and preservation.

Generation benchmarks separate object presence, attribute binding, and
spatial relations (Ghosh et al., 2023; Huang et al., 2023). Editing
benchmarks additionally distinguish instruction adherence, content retention,
and perceptual quality through multimodal or regional assessments
(Ku et al., 2024; Yang et al., 2025; Ye et al., 2025; Jiang et al., 2026; Qian et al., 2025).
Tool-assisted evaluations also document omissions and hallucinated changes
(Wang et al., 2025; Yosef et al., 2025).
We report semantics and preservation separately, retain missing-output and
order-disagreement rates for the adapted VLM, and treat CLIP and grounding as
proxies, particularly where they overlap the training observer
(Radford et al., 2021; Liu et al., 2024).
CleanFID controls distribution-quality preprocessing (Parmar et al., 2022);
it cannot establish editing correctness.

## 3 Method

We edit a generation path by changing its future conditions while retaining
its executed history. This is the path’s “dancing”: temporal conditioning
becomes a program learned through execution and modified before resumption. The formulation
keeps three objects apart, namely a request, the conditions compiled from it,
and the sampler state reached under those conditions.

## 3.1 Compiling a continuous program

For a request $c$ , let $H(c)\in\mathbb{R}^{L\times d}$ and $A(c)$ denote the text
features and attention mask accepted by the generator. A compiler maps them
to $K$ condition blocks and validity masks:

 $(Z,M)=E_{\phi}(H(c),A(c)),\qquad Z\in\mathbb{R}^{K\times L\times d},\quad M\in\{0,1\}^{K\times L}.$ 

(1)

Each block retains the native token axis and enters the generator through
its conditioning interface. The program is $P(c)=(Z,M,\tau)$ , where
 $0=\tau_{0}<\cdots<\tau_{K}=1$ specifies successive execution intervals.
The representation permits arbitrary block counts and interval lengths;
the concrete architecture and schedule belong to the experimental instantiation.
Role-based supervision can initialize the blocks, but contextualized token
features need not contain exclusively the concepts assigned to their masks.
We therefore test block responses rather than assume semantic independence.

## 3.2 Executing and observing a path

A shared generator applies $N$ sampling updates from initial noise $x_{0}=\xi$ :

 $x_{n+1}=f_{\theta,n}(x_{n};Z_{k(n)},M_{k(n)}),\quad k(n)=k\ \text{when }n/N\in[\tau_{k-1},\tau_{k}).$ 

(2)

The final update belongs to the last interval. The compiler runs once per
request; execution determines which block is used at each step. We write
 $F_{a:N}(x_{a};P)$ for the remaining sampler updates and $D$ for image decoding.
A saved state $x_{a}$ contains sampling noise. To visualize a velocity-model
trajectory we instead decode its predicted-clean latent,
 $\widehat{x}_{0,n}=x_{n}-\sigma_{n}v_{\theta}(x_{n},\sigma_{n};Z_{k(n)},M_{k(n)})$ .
This readout describes the current prediction; it is not the state used to
resume the sampler.

## 3.3 Intervening on future conditions

Let a reference source program $\bar{P}(c)$ produce a source image $I_{s}$ and
saved state $\bar{x}_{b}$ after $b$ updates. For a target request $c^{\prime}$ , compile
 $P_{\phi}(c^{\prime})$ and replace conditions used from step $b$ onward:

 $I_{e}^{(b)}=D\!\left(F_{b:N}(\operatorname{sg}[\bar{x}_{b}];P_{\phi}(c^{\prime}))\right),\qquad I_{s}=D\!\left(F_{b:N}(\bar{x}_{b};\bar{P}(c))\right).$ 

(3)

Blocks that have already finished cannot affect the resumed computation.
An intervention can occur at a block boundary or a declared step within a
block; in the latter case it also replaces the remainder of the active block.
For $b=0$ , the target program executes from the original noise, giving full
regeneration. For $b>0$ , the source has already fixed a common history.

Under identical materialized inputs and deterministic execution, replaying
the source suffix reproduces the source endpoint exactly
(Appendix C). This protects the executed state, not future
pixels, since a new suffix can still move objects, change the background, or fail
to realize the target. Exact resumption and semantic preservation are therefore
separate tests. Native prompt switching can use the same saved state, making
it a direct control for the effect of learned suffix conditioning.
Appendix C.1 formalizes background–subject
independence as selective endpoint response, distinct from statistical
independence of encoded features.

## 3.4 Learning from an edit and its preservation cost

Training uses the intervention in Eq. (3). The source
image, source state and preservation region are fixed references. A visual
observer $Q$ assesses the edited image and the detached source. For example,
with old- and new-entity evidence $q_{\rm old},q_{\rm new}$ and margin $m$ ,
response terms can take the form

 $\displaystyle\ell_{\rm gain}$ 
 $\displaystyle=[m-(q_{\rm new}(I_{e})-\operatorname{sg}[q_{\rm new}(I_{s})])]_{+},$ 

(4)

 $\displaystyle\ell_{\rm remove}$ 
 $\displaystyle=[m-(\operatorname{sg}[q_{\rm old}(I_{s})]-q_{\rm old}(I_{e}))]_{+}.$ 

(5)

Terminal content terms assess the target endpoint, while preservation terms
penalize changes outside a declared source edit region. The objective is

 $\mathcal{L}_{\rm edit}=\sum_{r\in\mathcal{R}}\lambda_{r}\ell_{r}/s_{r},\qquad\lambda_{r}\geq 0,\quad s_{r}>0.$ 

(6)

The coefficients and scales are fixed before the formal optimization run.
“Edit-only” includes both requested change and content retention. It names
the adaptation objective, not an assumption about how the encoder was
initialized. Full-rollout paired training is the special case $b=0$ ; the
SANA experiments instead train on immutable source-prefix replays.

Figure 2: Training the conditions used after a saved prefix.
The reference path supplies a detached sampler state and source image.
The target compiler provides future conditions; the suffix output receives
observer feedback for change and retention. Only compiler parameters are
optimized. The source reference cannot move to make the edit loss easier.
At deployment the observer and loss are absent.

Where learning occurs.

With the schedule and Boolean masks held fixed, continuous values carry the
learning signal through execution:

 $\nabla_{\phi}\mathcal{L}=\sum_{k\,\mathrm{active\ after}\ b}\left(\frac{\partial Z_{k}}{\partial\phi}\right)^{\!\top}\nabla_{Z_{k}}\mathcal{L}.$ 

(7)

The generator, image decoder and observer can remain fixed while their
input derivatives train the compiler. The cached prefix is detached; discrete
masks and switching times receive no claimed gradient. Shared compiler
parameters mean that an update through a future block can also change the
compiler’s earlier outputs on a fresh rollout. This motivates separate
checks of same-prompt drift and generation quality.

## 3.5 Deployment and extensions

At deployment, compile the request and run the generator, optionally caching
a prefix for several target continuations. No observer is queried. The same
interface can also receive trajectory supervision,
 $\mathcal{L}_{\rm joint}=\mathcal{L}_{\rm edit}+\lambda_{\rm traj}\mathcal{L}_{\rm traj}/s_{\rm traj}$ ,
but the primary adaptation sets $\lambda_{\rm traj}=0$ .
The optional extension and the current initialization history are documented
in Appendix B; they are not assumed to improve
editing. The mechanism study separates consequences of the execution
interface from consequences of learning its conditions.

## 4 Experiments

We first test in a controlled setting whether learned conditioning can
change when content appears, then ask on SANA what changes when future
conditions are replaced. The two settings differ in data, supervision and
readouts, so their numbers are not pooled.

Question and controlled setting.

Can learned conditioning change when scene content becomes visible?
We study Patterned-Sphere3D, a controlled Shapes3D (Burgess & Kim, 2018) derivative with exact
foreground masks and independently varied object and wall patterns.
The dataset contains 120,000 images at $64\times 64$ resolution. We compare
an archived single-expert baseline, the original shared-denoiser two-block
model, a hard factor-mask variant, and the observer-guided two-block model
with texture supervision. All curves use the same single-expert reference;
these are historical model variants, not a one-variable ablation series.

Learning stage behavior through feedback.

The observer-guided model shares one denoiser across stages and keeps the
full factor input available to its learned conditioning slots, with hard
factor visibility masks disabled. A frozen factor observer supplies semantic
feedback, with its factor-loss gradients restricted to the conditioning
encoder. Thus the encoder can learn stage-dependent semantic behavior
without manually removing factors from its input. The stage schedule and
supervised responsibilities are specified by the training objective;
regional reconstruction and texture losses additionally encourage early
object reconstruction and later wall detail. This is learned semantic
allocation under explicit supervision, rather than an independently
verified claim that observer feedback is its sole cause.

Region-wise evaluation.

For 1,024 validation images and 41 noise levels, both methods receive the
same noised reference $(x_{t},t)$ and noise realization. We compare their
clipped predicted-clean images with the clean reference, using the exact
sphere mask for subject MSE and its complement for background MSE.
Errors are averaged over RGB channels and region pixels in the $[-1,1]$ 
image range. The horizontal axis is reverse-process position $s=199-t$ ;
these curves are matched-state reconstruction diagnostics, not errors
accumulated along a generated trajectory. Separate pure-noise DDPM-200 (Ho et al., 2020)
readouts (Figure 3b) show how the corresponding
models generate a scene over time.

Result and implication.

The two regions follow different error profiles
(Figure 3). At step 25, the observer-guided
model’s subject MSE is $0.435\times$ the single-expert baseline, whereas
its background MSE is $3.629\times$ the baseline. By step 85, these ratios
are $0.870$ and $0.791$ , respectively; at step 199 they are $0.912$ and
 $0.897$ . The early background penalty is consistent with delayed detail,
not an early quality improvement. The trajectory rows make the intended
effect visible, as the sphere is established before the wall pattern sharpens.
The displayed example has consistent final colors, but was selected for
that property; background-color failures elsewhere
remain, and low reconstruction error at late noise levels does not
rule them out. The controlled study therefore
supports changing the temporal allocation of visual content through
learned conditioning and explicit feedback. It motivates the SANA
prefix-reuse experiments without establishing universal generation quality
or attributing the effect to the observer alone.

## 4.2 Editing text-to-image generation with SANA

Setup and primary comparison.

We use SANA-600M (Xie et al., 2025) with a 273.1M-parameter compiler and three condition blocks.
The primary method is edit-only training, evaluated for the 3/9/18,
6/12/12, and 9/12/9 execution schedules. The final checkpoints are fixed
when training completed, before the present evaluation.
The 3/9/18 arm starts edit-only adaptation from initialization; the other
two arms inherit a 40-update joint warm start. This difference limits causal
comparisons across schedules. Their initialization histories are retained in
Appendix B.
The generator is fixed; the observer does not participate in generation.

The principal comparison is full regeneration: apply the target
conditions from the initial noise, using the same trained compiler,
sampler, schedule, and seed as the editing arm. This is branch point zero.
Our intervention instead resumes the stored source state and changes only
future conditions. Comparing these arms asks whether reusing an executed
prefix preserves scene structure while achieving the requested change.
It does not, by itself, isolate the benefit of compiler training.
Initialized programs test the effect of adaptation; native full-caption
suffixes test the conditions used at a matched state
(Appendix I). These controls answer different questions
from full regeneration. The source paths in the matched comparisons come
from the frozen initial compiler, not the target-trained compiler.

Evaluation and interpretation.

The training-disjoint evaluation uses 24 request families and three common
seeds, with branch points 0, 9, 15, and 21; all branch points are retained
rather than selected by their results. Identity resumption must reproduce
the uninterrupted source. Background LPIPS (Zhang et al., 2018) and pixel measurements compare
each edit with its actual generated source, outside a fixed source-derived
edit region. Every conditional metric retains its accepted-mask denominator.
Semantic CLIP measurements are observer-family proxies, while the local
VLM protocol supplies explicitly adapted automated judgments, not human
preferences. Better preservation is useful only together with adequate
instruction following.
Complete final measurements and their coverage are collected in
Appendix G. Additional image-input controls and
independent detector measurements are separated by source access and
validation status in Appendix E.

At branch 9, prefix reuse lowers background LPIPS relative to full
regeneration for all three schedules, while retaining a similar mean
semantic margin change. For example, at 6/12/12, LPIPS decreases from
0.321 to 0.227 and the CLIP margin change moves from 0.092 to 0.088.
Later branching strengthens preservation but eventually weakens the edit:
at branch 21, LPIPS is 0.033 and the margin change is 0.029.
These means support a controllable preservation–change
tradeoff, not superiority of the trained encoder over all conditioning
controls.
Paired family-bootstrap analysis confirms lower background LPIPS at branch 9
for all three schedules (Appendix H); the reductions
in valid-attempt means are 36.0%, 29.1%, and 23.6%, respectively.
The semantic proxy decreases for the first two schedules. Its interval
includes zero for 9/12/9, which does not establish equal editing success.
Independent category checks on 12 eligible families likewise show no uniform
ordering across schedules and detectors (Appendix E).

What changes beyond ordinary prompt switching?

At exactly the same saved source state, native full-caption conditions already
support substantial subject replacement. Both use the same path-editing
operation; this comparison isolates the effect of learned conditions on
the resulting preservation–change balance.
The completed native extension covers all three schedules (432 additional
outputs with exact source and prefix matches). At branch 9, source-box
background LPIPS is lower with learned conditions by 0.017, 0.022 and 0.030
for 3/9/18, 6/12/12 and 9/12/9 (5% padding; 65/72, 62/72 and 62/72

all paddings and branches are retained in Appendix J.2.
Late branching reverses this preservation advantage. At branch 9 on the fixed 36-attempt category cohort,
learned/native replacement counts are 14/14, 15/14 and 14/17 for Grounding

first two schedules have similar category counts, while 9/12/9 favors native

comparable replacement counts. The branch-point curves

What the mechanism tests establish.

Block interventions distinguish routing from specialization. Across all
measured branches, 1200 interventions on already inactive blocks leave the
latent endpoint exactly unchanged relative to the same-compiler continuation.
Active-block semantic responses change with execution timing, but similar
patterns are present at initialization. Schedule crossing also changes the
relation between execution time and the compiler’s learned clock. These tests
verify controllable routing without isolating a training-induced semantic
factorization. The complete schedule, source-path and block comparisons are
in Appendix L.

Caching a common prefix also reduces the cost of four continuations by
22.0–23.4% with identical endpoints, a benefit available to native prompt
switching as well (Appendix K).

## 5 Discussion and conclusion

Dancing Diffusion makes the generation path itself editable, with learned stage
conditions as a trainable interface for redirecting its future.
The controlled study demonstrates stage behavior
under explicit supervision. In SANA, early prefix reuse preserves more
background than full regeneration; later branching restricts the requested
change. Native suffix comparisons expose a preservation–replacement
tradeoff, and the learned program lowers FID below native SANA in two of
three schedules.
Caching saves computation with identical endpoints, as it would for
ordinary prompt switching.

Capability access through a trainable interface.

The behavior belongs to the compiler–generator system, and fixed generator
weights cannot distinguish learned control from improved access to existing
behavior. Appendix C.1 specifies tests for these alternatives
and discusses the hypothesis connecting interface training to LLM post-training
(Yue et al., 2025; Liu et al., 2025). Organizing sampling as a learned sequence
of decisions motivates the method; reasoning ability is not evaluated here.

Scope.

The compiler runs once per caption, and the SANA results come from a
training-disjoint evaluation set with one training run per schedule. CLIP and source masks
share their family with the training observer, so we complement them with
independent detectors and observer-independent regions. Extending the
program to be recompiled during generation, and to image inputs through
inversion, are natural next steps.

## Reproducibility statement

The appendix specifies sampling settings, model revisions, training weights,
checkpoint hashes, route definitions, and the family-level bootstrap unit.
Measured numerical figures are generated directly from archived machine-readable
reports. The preliminary long-run study separates selection diagnostics from
independent reporting; unmeasured results are not assumed. Schematic figures are labeled separately from measured results.
Model/data access and the complete experiment environment remain necessary
to reproduce generation; the manuscript does not report a fresh rerun of the
archived experiments.

Generative AI tools assisted with implementation, research discussion, formal arguments,
interpretation of experiment reports, manuscript drafting and revision,
literature lookup, and creation of vector diagrams and plotting code.
A Qwen model supplied role-span pseudo-labels for the compiler’s program data,
and a local Qwen vision–language model served as the automated pairwise judge
in the appendix evaluation.
All displayed numerical values come from experiment reports. The schematic diagrams are not generated-image evidence. Responsibility for the
paper’s claims, citations, and artifacts rests with the authors.

## Ethics statement

The method inherits the content and bias risks of its pretrained generator
and visual evaluators. It provides no guarantee of factuality or fair
performance across concepts. Our experiments concern generated trajectories;
we do not evaluate manipulation of photographs of identifiable people.

## Appendix B Current implementation and experimental provenance

Compiler and executor.

The SANA study uses three continuous blocks at native feature width 2304.
The 273.1M-parameter compiler is initialized from role-mask fitting at
update 800. A frozen copy of that initial compiler predicts the validity
masks and generates the source images and saved states, so the training
target does not move with later compiler updates. The SANA-600M 512-pixel
generator uses 30 steps and guidance 4. No trained decoder adapter is loaded
for the comparisons.

Training and warm start.

The replay cohort has 9226 source-image identities, shared across the three
schedules. The 3/9/18 run starts at update zero from the role-fitted compiler,
without a joint parent or optimizer state. The 6/12/12 and 9/12/9 runs resume
their joint checkpoints at update 40, including Adam state, and continue with
zero trajectory weight to update 1423, giving 1383 edit-only updates.
We refer to the terminal update-1423 checkpoint as the final checkpoint.
Cross-schedule differences therefore combine schedule and initialization
history. The main comparisons use the terminal checkpoint; update 600 is an
intermediate diagnostic. The encoder learning rate is $2\times 10^{-6}$ , with
global batch 16, microbatch 4, weight decay 0 and training seed 20260930.
Each configuration is a single training run.

Training branches after the first block, at steps 3, 6 and 9 for schedules
3/9/18, 6/12/12 and 9/12/9. The two remaining blocks receive the target
conditions, and all 27, 24 and 21 suffix updates are differentiated with
activation checkpointing. The source image, prefix, source-region mask and
frozen validity masks are detached. Evaluation at steps 9, 15 and 21 is a
separate common-step comparison, not three training branch points.

Objective.

Terminal and response terms use the frozen observer, and each raw term is
divided by its recorded frozen scale. The weights are target presence 2, old
absence 1, preservation 4, caption 0.4, background retention 1, response
presence 0.1, response absence 0.05, response heat 0.5 and response heat
absence 0.5. Relation and trajectory weights are zero. The source region uses
a 0.15 detection threshold and 0.02 padding, and accepted edit-area fractions
lie between 0.002 and 0.8. Exact configurations, recorded loss scales and
checkpoint identities are indexed in the reproducibility bundle.

Data and reporting scope.

Training and evaluation indices are distinct, and
identity checks compare source-image identifiers rather than file positions.
The 24-family evaluation set uses seeds 101, 202 and 303. Every attempt is
retained for semantic proxies, and missing source masks stay in the coverage
count. Automatically substituted target captions can retain inappropriate
actions or attributes, so category presence alone cannot certify full-caption
compliance.

Optional joint extension.

The joint objective adds a normalized velocity loss on a declared source
path with a detached target. The final edit-only results do not use it.
Historical joint runs and earlier pilots are archived separately and are not
pooled with the current comparisons.

## Appendix C Execution guarantees and what they do not imply

Proposition 1 (Protected-prefix equality).

With identical initial state, materialized prefix conditions, model parameters,
schedule and deterministic numerical execution, two runs agree at every step
of their common prefix. Saving and restoring the complete sampler state
reproduces the endpoint under the unchanged suffix.

Proof.

Each update in Eq. (2) has identical inputs in both runs, so
induction gives equality of the next state. The same induction starts at a
restored state for identity continuation. A stochastic sampler additionally
requires the same random state, and a multistep solver its history. Equality
across numerical runtimes is not asserted.
∎

The guarantee applies equally to native prompt switching. It is a
computational invariant and says nothing about preservation under a changed
suffix. Our endpoint-equality and inactive-block tests check it in the
deterministic implementation.

Differentiating a resumed program.

The reference state in Eq. (3) is detached, so the loss
differentiates the executed suffix and its continuous conditions but cannot
change the cached source path. A block whose interval ends before $b$ has no
direct effect on the loss, although shared encoder parameters can still
change its output after optimization. Frozen Boolean masks remove mask
selection from the learning path but not derivatives through valid
continuous features.

## C.1 Background–subject independence under path edits

Fix a source state $\bar{x}_{b}$ , schedule, masks and numerical randomness,
collectively $c$ . Let $z$ contain the continuous suffix conditions, and define
the endpoint and its declared background and subject readouts by

 $G_{b}(z;c)=D(F_{b:N}(\bar{x}_{b};z)),\qquad h_{B}(z;c)=\rho_{B}(G_{b}(z;c)),\quad h_{S}(z;c)=\rho_{S}(G_{b}(z;c)).$ 

(8)

The readouts specify the factors under test and need not exhaust image
semantics. Occlusion, shadows or relations can couple the two factors, so
independence is relative to the task, not a universal property of scenes.

Statistical and interventional independence.

For a hypothesized factorization $z=(z_{B},z_{S})$ , statistical independence
 $p(z_{B},z_{S}\mid c)=p(z_{B}\mid c)p(z_{S}\mid c)$ says nothing by itself about
which image features depend on each component. Independent scalars can still
give the entangled readout $h_{B}(z)=z_{B}+z_{S}$ , and correlated coordinates can
drive separate output factors. Our temporal blocks are not assumed to be
 $z_{B}$ and $z_{S}$ .

What matters for a subject edit is a small background response together with
a realized target. For source conditions $z$ and edited conditions $z^{\prime}$ , let

 $e_{B}=\|h_{B}(z^{\prime};c)-h_{B}(z;c)\|,\qquad e_{S}=d_{S}(h_{S}(z^{\prime};c),s^{\star}),$ 

(9)

where $s^{\star}$ is the declared subject target. Selective editing requires
 $e_{B}\leq\epsilon_{B}$ and $e_{S}\leq\epsilon_{S}$ for thresholds fixed in advance.
An unchanged image makes $e_{B}$ zero while failing the target, so background
retention alone is insufficient.

Proposition 2 (Bounded cross-factor response).

Suppose $h_{B}$ is continuously differentiable along the admissible segment
 $(z_{B},z_{S}+t\delta_{S})$ , $t\in[0,1]$ , and its cross-factor Jacobian has
operator norm at most $\kappa_{BS}$ on that segment. Then

 $\|h_{B}(z_{B},z_{S}+\delta_{S};c)-h_{B}(z_{B},z_{S};c)\|\leq\kappa_{BS}\|\delta_{S}\|.$ 

(10)

The analogous bound with $\kappa_{SB}$ holds for the subject response to a
background edit, and vanishing cross-factor Jacobians on a convex product
domain make each readout invariant to the other coordinate.

Proof.

The background difference equals
 $\int_{0}^{1}(\partial h_{B}/\partial z_{S})(z_{B},z_{S}+t\delta_{S};c)\delta_{S}\,dt$ ,
and the operator-norm bound gives Eq. (10).
Interchanging the factors gives the second bound.
∎

The statement bounds leakage, not attainment of $s^{\star}$ , and holds only at
a fixed source state. Background LPIPS and subject-replacement proxies
approximate the two sides of Eq. (9) at the
endpoint, rather than measuring a cross-factor Jacobian directly.

Interpreting capability access.

A fixed generator and a trained interface form a new composite system.
Separating improved access to a pretrained behavior from newly learned
control relies on a verified fixed executor together with native-prompt and
initialized-interface controls, both of which our experiments include. Finite
native-prompt search cannot show that an unobserved behavior is absent, and
the analogy to post-training in language models is a motivation rather than
a tested mechanism.

## Appendix D Trainability of the compiled interface

The results below concern optimizing the interface rather than executing it
(Appendix C). They show that the learning problem is well posed
and that its reported objective cannot be gamed by its own normalization.
They do not by themselves predict that optimization succeeds.

Setting.

Fix the generator $\theta$ , decoder $D$ , observer $Q$ , schedule
 $0=\tau_{0}<\cdots<\tau_{K}=1$ and Boolean masks $M$ , and optimize only the
compiler parameters $\phi$ . For a replay with source state $\bar{x}_{b}$ , source
image $I_{s}$ and keep-mask $m$ , the edited endpoint is
 $I_{e}=D(F_{b:N}(\operatorname{sg}[\bar{x}_{b}];Z_{\phi},M))$ as in Eq. (3),
and the objective is Eq. (6) with weights
 $\lambda_{r}>0$ and scales $s_{r}>0$ fixed before the run.

## D.1 The objective is well posed

Proposition 3 (Nonnegativity and a uniform lower bound).

Every deployed term is nonnegative, hence $\mathcal{L}_{\rm edit}\geq 0$ 
uniformly in $\phi$ . Each hinge term is exactly $0$ when its margin is met,
and each presence term is bounded above by $-\log\varepsilon$ .

Proof.

The deployed terms are $-\log p$ and $-\log(1-p)$ with $p$ clamped to
 $[\varepsilon,1-\varepsilon]$ , $\varepsilon=10^{-6}$ ; a masked mean squared
error; $\operatorname{softplus}(\cdot)$ ; $\operatorname{relu}(m-g)$ ; and
 $(1-s_{t})+\operatorname{relu}(0.05+s_{s}-s_{t})$ with $s_{\bullet}\in[-1,1]$ inner
products of unit-normalized CLIP features. Each is nonnegative, and a positive
combination of nonnegative terms is nonnegative, independently of $\phi$ .
The hinge vanishes iff $g\geq m$ , and the clamp bounds presence by
 $-\log\varepsilon\approx 13.82$ (the caption term by $4.05$ ).
∎

The proposition concerns the loss surface, not reachability. The terms
compete, so a common minimizer need not exist, and the softplus relation term
is never exactly zero (though of order $10^{-12}$ at the best attainable
margin). What it rules out is an apparent decrease caused by an unbounded
term.

Where the gradient can live.

Eq. (7) sums only over blocks active after $b$ .
The next lemma makes this exclusion exact.

Lemma 1 (Exact gradient support).

Let block $k$ have execution interval $[\tau_{k-1},\tau_{k})$ . If
 $\tau_{k}N\leq b$ , then $\mathcal{L}_{\rm edit}$ is constant in $Z_{k}$ , so
 $\nabla_{Z_{k}}\mathcal{L}_{\rm edit}=0$ identically.

Proof.

By Eq. (2) step $n$ reads only $Z_{k(n)}$ . If
 $\tau_{k}N\leq b$ then $k(n)\neq k$ for every $n\geq b$ , and the prefix is
detached, so nothing in the loss depends on $Z_{k}$ .
∎

For the $6/12/12$ schedule at $N=30$ the block boundaries are steps 6, 18 and
30, so at $b=6$ the first block receives exactly zero gradient. This is the
formal counterpart of the observation in Appendix N that
a background change requested after the boundary must be expressed by the
suffix. Because $\phi$ is shared, the lemma does not imply that block $k$ ’s
output is unchanged after an update, so same-prompt drift is measured
separately.

## D.2 Why the objective is frozen before training

The scales $s_{r}$ are measured once before training and then held fixed
(Appendix B).

Proposition 4 (Adaptive normalization admits no fixed objective).

If term $r$ is reported as $\ell_{r}^{(t)}/s_{r}^{(t)}$ with $s_{r}^{(t)}>0$ a
statistic of the optimization history, there exist sequences in which
 $\ell_{r}^{(t)}$ strictly increases while the reported value strictly decreases
to $0$ . A monotone decrease of the reported value then certifies nothing
about the modelled quantity.

Proof.

Take $\ell_{r}^{(t)}=t$ and $s_{r}^{(t)}=t^{2}$ . The raw sequence grows without
bound while the reported sequence $1/t$ decreases to $0$ . Since the objective
changes whenever $s_{r}^{(t)}$ does, the updates are not gradient steps on one
fixed function.
∎

Corollary 1 (Frozen scales preserve the ordering).

If $s_{r}$ is a positive constant, the reported term decreases if and only if
the raw term decreases, and $\mathcal{L}_{\rm edit}$ is one fixed function of
 $\phi$ .

Raw training terms are therefore checked from the first checkpoint against
preregistered directions, and a run whose raw preservation rises is stopped.
The same reasoning explains why preservation is scored against the paired
counterfactual from the source program rather than against a photograph.
Against a photograph the term has a floor that no edit can remove, measured at
 $0.1405$ before the encoder has moved and $0.150$ after $700$ updates, which
reflects the gap between a generator and a camera rather than edit damage.

## D.3 What the detached prefix does and does not protect

Proposition 5 (No reference-moving shortcut).

The source state $\bar{x}_{b}$ , source image $I_{s}$ , keep-mask $m$ and observer
text features enter $\mathcal{L}_{\rm edit}$ only through $\operatorname{sg}[\cdot]$ , so no
gradient step can reduce the loss by moving the target it is scored against.

Proof.

Immediate from $\partial\,\operatorname{sg}[u]/\partial\phi=0$ . Preservation against
 $\operatorname{sg}[I_{s}]$ , the response hinges against $\operatorname{sg}[q(I_{s})]$ and the caption term
against detached text features all have fixed second arguments.
∎

Three further shortcuts are excluded by construction. The keep-mask is not
re-derived from the edited image, which would let the optimizer shrink the
penalized region by hiding the subject. The response terms hinge on a gain
relative to the detached source, so inflating presence everywhere does not
help. When both ends of a relation resolve to the same detector anchor, the
pair is dropped and reported as relation_degenerate_fraction.

## D.4 Cost accounting

Lemma 2 (Suffix denoiser calls).

With $N$ steps, classifier-free guidance as one conditional and one
unconditional pass, and a branch at step $b$ , resuming a saved prefix costs
 $2(N-b)$ denoiser evaluations. The FlowEdit adaptation evaluates guided
velocities for a source and a target condition and costs $4(N-b)$ , twice as
many at every $b$ .

Proof.

Each remaining step performs one guided evaluation (two passes), or two for
FlowEdit (four passes).
∎

At $b\in\{0,6,9,15,21\}$ with $N=30$ this gives $60/48/42/30/18$ against
 $120/96/84/60/36$ . These are suffix costs only; source generation, text
encoding and VAE encode/decode are excluded. Native shared-state reuse has
the same $2(N-b)$ cost, and call counts do not translate directly into
wall-clock time.

## D.5 Scope

These results are structural. They concern the objective, its gradient
support, its normalization and its arithmetic, and assert no convergence,
rate or stationary point. The map $\phi\mapsto\mathcal{L}_{\rm edit}$ runs
through a deep sampler, a decoder and a detector-based observer with clamps,
hinges and a hard mask, and is neither convex nor globally smooth. A
standalone arithmetic script released with the code checks every numeric bound, the counterexample and the call counts without executing
the model.

Independent detector calibration.

Two frozen category detectors, Grounding DINO tiny (Liu et al., 2024) and an OWLv2 base
ensemble (Minderer et al., 2023), are distinct from the training YOLO observer. On COCO val2017,
image identities are hash-split into 1327 fit and 1352 audit queries over 23
categories, with up to 30 positives and 30 negatives per category and split.
The global threshold maximizes fit localization F1 subject to precision at
least 0.90. A positive requires a top detection with IoU at least 0.5 against
an annotated object, and a wrongly localized positive counts as both a false
positive and a false negative.

Behavioral separation.

We use separation in an operational sense, namely retaining one aspect of the
image while modifying another. At the same early saved state, learned
conditions change the background less than native conditioning
(Appendix J.2), with similar category counts
in the first two schedules. This is a localized improvement in the
retention–change balance, not a uniformly better frontier, and it does not
identify encoder pretraining as the cause, since temporal responses also
occur at initialization.

Matched source versus own source.

Three further diagonal conditions generate the source with the trained
compiler used for editing. Matched sources isolate the suffix intervention
at a fixed state, whereas own sources test the complete path but also change
the source image and prefix. Background retention is measured against each
condition’s actual source.

Block-specific interventions.

Source and target conditions share a common sequence length with
zero-valued masked padding, and a padding-only control (“nochange”) records
the resulting numerical drift. For the initialization and the three trained
compilers, at a common saved prefix, we replace the source conditions by
target conditions in $B_{0}$ , $B_{1}$ , $B_{2}$ , all blocks or none, at branches
0, 9, 15 and 21. Replacing a block whose execution has ended must have no
effect, which is a routing check rather than evidence of specialization.
Specialization would additionally require differentiated effects among active
blocks relative to initialization.

## Appendix M Stage readouts and single-block interventions

The model’s own generation path.

Here we generate from noise with the final 6/12/12 compiler itself, rather
than editing a frozen reference path. Eight fixed evaluation families at
close framing and seed 101 are compared with the role-fitted initialization.
Each model receives the source caption, subject-only and background-only
changes, and six single-block substitutions, for 144 trajectories and 576
archived images. The displayed case is the first family, not a selected
winner.

Figure 9: Generation along the learned path. Final compiler, identical
initial noise, with source, subject-changed and background-changed captions.
Columns show $\widehat{x}_{0}=x_{t}-\sigma_{t}v_{\theta}(x_{t},c_{k})$ from the last
executed denoiser evaluation in each block (steps 6, 18, 30), not decodes of
noisy states. B0 exposes coarse scene appearance, B1 makes the subject
recognizable and B2 completes the image. Changing the scene also changes
pose and appearance, so stage progression alone is not factor independence.

Which block controls the change?

Each substitution replaces exactly one source block and its mask with those
compiled from the target caption, holding all other conditions, masks and
noise fixed. In the displayed case, replacing B0 with the cat-conditioned
block leaves the dog largely unchanged, whereas replacing B2 changes its
category. A scene-conditioned B0 changes the dog and composition without
producing snow, and replacing B1 produces snow but also changes pose. Later
blocks can therefore still determine scene content, and B2 can change
semantics rather than only texture.

Interpretation and limits.

The eight-family gallery shows a useful temporal organization but not a
universal scene-only B0, since the zebra-on-beach caption already produces an
animal-shaped silhouette in its B0 readout, and single-block substitutions
can create hybrid anatomy such as elephant–giraffe mixtures.

Several features of the implementation let temporal organization and strict
independence differ. Role fitting masks a full-caption encoding whose
unmasked token features may retain subject context. The masks are cumulative
and the compiler shares a context trunk, so suffix training can change
early-block outputs. All blocks also act through the same spatial state and
denoiser. For fixed masks, with
 $x_{t+1}=x_{t}+\Delta\sigma_{t}v(x_{t},c_{k})$ , a condition perturbation propagates as

 $\delta x_{t+1}=(I+\Delta\sigma_{t}D_{x}v)\,\delta x_{t}+\Delta\sigma_{t}D_{c}v\,\delta c_{k}\quad\text{to first order}.$ 

(11)

Temporal routing alone does not force the background projection of this
response to vanish, consistent with the criterion in
Appendix C.1. Source-box metrics add their own
coupling, as they count legitimate changes outside the old silhouette as
background damage, and foreground rectangles contain background pixels.

## Appendix N An explicit-background input-isolation variant

Intervention and its scope.

The full-caption compiler masks contextual text features, so B0 can retain
subject information even when its direct token channel is masked. We
therefore test a training-free input intervention on the final 6/12/12
checkpoint. An explicit background sentence, taken from structured scenario
metadata and describing the scene and lighting, is encoded separately
through the text encoder, compiler and frozen mask predictor, and its B0
replaces the full-caption B0. B1 and B2 are unchanged and carry the subject,
framing and viewpoint instructions, so the early composition prior changes
as well. For a suffix resumed at step 6 or later the target B0 is no longer
executed, and a background change requested after that point must be
expressed by the suffix.

A precise invariance, rather than full-image independence.

Write $c_{0}=F_{0}(E(b))$ and $m_{0}=M_{0}(E(b))$ for the separately compiled
background $b$ , and let $s$ denote the subject description. For fixed model,
noise $z$ and deterministic sampler, the state at the B0 boundary satisfies

 $x_{6}(b,s,z)=\Phi_{0}(z;F_{0}(E(b)),M_{0}(E(b)))=x_{6}(b,s^{\prime},z).$ 

(12)

This invariance to subject input follows from the routing, not from
training. It does not make foreground and background pixels independent
after B1/B2, which still act on a shared spatial state.

Initial mechanism check.

All eight fixed evaluation families at seed 101 and close framing were
tested, giving 48 trajectories and 192 stage and endpoint images. Under
subject replacement, the isolated B0 condition, clean prediction and
post-block latent are exactly unchanged in 8/8 cases, against 0/8 for the
original compiler. Changing the background changes the isolated B0 state in
8/8 cases, which rules out a constant state. Under the full-caption compiler, by contrast, the
step-6 state already carries the replaced subject, with leak magnitudes from
0.081 to 0.510 in state and from 1.02 to 9.50 in the clean prediction. In the reviewed galleries the
target categories stay recognizable, but subject scale and composition
change.

Complete matched evaluation.

Because the check above fixes one seed and framing, we also ran the full
frozen protocol on the same checkpoint, with 48 sources (8 scene families,
2 framings, 3 seeds), both edit directions, five starts $\{0,6,9,15,21\}$ and
six methods. This gives 2,880 outputs plus 96 source and reconstruction
images. Every source is regenerated by the B0-isolated compiler and all
controls are rerun on it, with detector thresholds, source-box masks and the
CLIP evaluator reused without retuning.

Equation 12 holds bit-exactly, so the B0 boundary state is free of subject input. The rendered background can nevertheless change substantially. With the same seed, background sentence and B0 state, and only the subject text changed, the mean background-region LPIPS against the source is 0.1735 at 5% padding (48/48 defined). This residual comes from B1/B2 acting on a shared spatial state. Isolating the input makes the early program subject-independent but does not by itself preserve the background, which is what resuming a saved prefix does.

Recomputed source-size strata.

Source size is recomputed on the new sources, since the intervention can change subject scale. At zero padding the frozen Grounding DINO boxes place 40/48 sources in the small stratum, 8/48 in the medium stratum and none in the large stratum. No large-source contrast is therefore available, and earlier large-source findings are neither reproduced nor contradicted.

Interpretation.

At branch 9, learned versus native continuation yields 13 versus 8 strict
joint replacements, with similar background LPIPS (0.1195 versus 0.1210).
The paired replacement difference is 10.4 percentage points, with a
95% family-bootstrap interval of [2.1,22.9]; the background-LPIPS difference
is $-0.0015$ with interval $[-0.0044,0.0014]$ . For background edits at the
same branch, subject-region LPIPS is 0.2953 versus 0.3095 (difference
 $-0.0142$ , interval $[-0.0289,-0.0034]$ ), while the scene-direction proxy
is slightly higher. Intervals use 10,000 resamples of the eight families.
These are local improvements over native continuation. FlowEdit CFG 6
obtains 14 joint replacements with lower background LPIPS (0.1059) at
branch 9, so they do not establish superiority over the full FlowEdit
grid.

Source size and visual checks.

Source-box areas define small ( $<10\%$ , $n=11$ ), medium ( $10$ – $25\%$ ,
 $n=14$ ) and large ( $\geq 25\%$ , $n=23$ ) strata. At branch 9 the large-group
joint counts are 2, 2, 1, 3, 3 and 2 in table order, so a larger-subject
advantage is not supported. OWLv2 replacement saturates at branch 9, and
DINO has a calibration shortfall for the target class in 12/48 cases (8/23
large). Visual inspection finds stronger late subject changes, but also
hybrid anatomy, background edits that alter pose and late scene edits with
little visible effect.

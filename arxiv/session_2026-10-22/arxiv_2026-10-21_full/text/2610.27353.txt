Abstract

Beneath the apparent diversity of foundation models lies an increasingly homogeneous technical stack of common training pipelines, distillation methods, and data.
These shared algorithmic choices can expose an otherwise distinct model ecosystem to concentrated risks leading to a vulnerability monoculture. Yet current practice in assessing adversarial robustness is restricted to individual robustness scores and attack transfer rates, leaving open the unexplored fundamental question: how many attacks suffice for catastrophic widespread compromise? In this paper, we investigate the empirical nature of shared vulnerabilities within a model pool and introduce Systemic Adversarial Robustness (SAR), a novel measure of shared vulnerability of a model pool through the attack diversity needed to exploit it. Specifically, SAR interpolates between single-attack transferability and independent per-model robustness: small values reveal a vulnerability monoculture, whereas large values indicate that failures are dispersed across models. We theoretically formulate SAR as a partial set-cover problem with provable approximation guarantees—enabling the development of scalable algorithms to empirically measure the systemic adversarial robustness. We empirically test the shared vulnerability of vision and large language models and characterize their shared collective vulnerability curve. Specifically, we demonstrate that model pools distilled from DeepSeek-R1 and Claude Opus 4.6 exhibit lower estimated systemic robustness than their corresponding parent pools, revealing a potential systemic cost of frontier-model distillation. Our framework seeks to establish vulnerability monoculture as a measurable target for a more wholistic adversarial evaluation of model populations.

## 1 Introduction

Diversity is one of the oldest defenses against systemic failure. In nature, genetic diversity protects populations against extinction from deadly viruses, and a computer network running identical software can fall prey to a single exploit (Geer et al., 2003). As a result, the danger of fostering a monoculture lies not in any individual member being weak, but rather in all members in the population sharing the same weakness. This intuition applies in several facets of real-world life, but also in algorithmic decision-making as algorithmic monoculture (Kleinberg and Raghavan, 2021), which underscores the paradox: when many decision-makers converge on the same algorithm, the collective can be worse off even if the algorithm is better for each of them in isolation. The same effects of algorithmic monoculture are now, unfortunately, measurable in machine learning through the lens of outcome homogenization, wherein foundation models that are trained on common data and methodology make correlated errors in an ecosystem (Li et al., 2025; Bommasani et al., 2021; Toups et al., 2023).

As foundation models grow stronger in capabilities, the risk and potential damage of correlated errors are amplified (Goel et al., 2025; Kim et al., 2025). This growth in model capabilities is also coupled with the Cambrian explosion of new applications for foundation models and agentic systems that touch a diversity of economic sectors, including safety-critical domains such as healthcare, autonomous driving, and finance (Bommasani et al., 2021; Szpruch et al., 2025; Yurtsever et al., 2020). However, a diverse foundation model ecosystem need not have diverse vulnerabilities. Powering this vast foundation model zoo is a deceptively narrow technical stack of overlapping pre-training data, near-identical architectural classes, and shared post-training recipes, and, more recently, distillation methods from which student models inherit capabilities. This convergence to homogenization runs the risk of breeding a new vulnerability monoculture where the exploitable defects of larger foundation models are silently propagated to every student model distilled from it.

Indeed, the widespread adoption of distillation as a standard stage in post-training only serves to accelerate the convergence of shared vulnerabilities. For instance, open models are routinely fine-tuned on the outputs of a few frontier teachers (Xu et al., 2024). For example, DeepSeek-R1 (Guo et al., 2025) was distilled by its developers into Qwen (Bai et al., 2023) and Llama models (Grattafiori et al., 2024), and community checkpoints of open models from five developers have been post-trained on outputs attributed to Claude Opus 4.5. This surface area for shared vulnerabilities remains opaque, as distillation can transmit traits that are invisible in the training data itself (Cloud et al., 2025).

Despite the increasing risk of shared vulnerabilities, current adversarial robustness evaluations fundamentally cannot distinguish a vulnerability monoculture from a genuinely diverse pool. In particular, individual attack success rates (Carlini and Wagner, 2017)
assume adversaries that attack every model separately, and transferability assumes adversaries who attack with a single input. Importantly, real-world adversaries operate in between and may seek to maximize the attack surface exposed through shared vulnerabilities. At present, the quantity that governs this threat model lacks a prescriptive, systematic measure: how many distinct attacks does it take to compromise a pool of models?

Present work. In this work, we investigate an adversarial manifestation of the vulnerability monoculture in foundation models and seek to quantify the shared vulnerabilities.
To expose this, we introduce and define the notion of Systemic Adversarial Robustness (SAR) that aims to measure a new notion of model diversity, not through how different models are constructed but rather in how differently models can be broken. Specifically, SAR measures the minimum number of distinct attacks, within a budget, that together compromise at least a $1-\delta$ fraction of the model pool. Critically, sweeping $\delta$ traces the model pool’s full vulnerability profile, which complements any existing adversarial robustness measure. Interpreting this vulnerability profile also provides a signature wherein a low SAR corroborates high shared risk, as depicted in fig. 1 for distillation of foundation models.

We summarize our contributions as follows:

1. 

Systemic adversarial robustness. We introduce and define the concept of systemic adversarial robustness as a new form of risk assessment across an entire pool of models. Specifically, we aim to find the smallest number of perturbation vectors that manage to fool a given proportion of models in a fixed (distilled) model pool (§3.1).

2. 

Algorithms. We formulate SAR as a partial set-cover problem, and give a top-down greedy estimator in algorithm 1 that requires only a joint attack oracle (§3.3).
With an $\alpha$ -approximate oracle, the estimate lies within a factor $1+\alpha^{-1}\ln\lceil(1-\delta)M\rceil$ of the true value, a logarithmic dependence that cannot be improved in general. Importantly, the procedure attains a $(1-e^{-\alpha})$ fraction of the best coverage achievable with $k$ attacks.

3. 

Empirical analysis. We measure SAR on a DeepSeek-R1 distilled model pool and community checkpoints post-trained on outputs attributed to Claude Opus 4.5 (§4). Our investigation points to increasing evidence of an emerging vulnerability monoculture, as shown by measuring SAR.

## 2 Preliminaries

Notation and convention.
Let $\mathcal{D}:=\{(x_{i},y_{i})\}^{N}_{i=1}$ be a set of data points sampled from $\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y})$ where ${\mathcal{X}}$ is the input space
and $\mathcal{Y}$ forms a task-dependent output space (e.g., classification, regression, generation). When the task requires continuous inputs, we fix ${\mathcal{X}}:=\mathbb{R}^{d}$ , while in the case of discrete sequences such as in language modeling, we assume that for a sequence of length $L$ over a token vocabulary ${\mathcal{V}}$ , the input space is ${\mathcal{X}}:={\mathcal{V}}^{L}$ . We describe an arbitrary function, with learnable parameters $\theta\in\Theta$ , as $f^{\theta}:\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\mathcal{Y}$ . A collection of $M$ -many such functions is termed a model pool††

 For generative models (e.g., probabilistic large language models) we fix a decoding rule for sample generation, e.g., greedy decoding, such that each $f^{\theta}$ is a deterministic function. and is denoted as $\mathcal{F}:=\{f^{\theta}_{j}\}^{M}_{j=1}$ . To define a perturbation set, we write ${\mathcal{B}}_{\epsilon}(x)\coloneqq\left\{x^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{X}}:d(x,x^{\prime})\leq\varepsilon\right\}$ as the ball of “radius“ $\varepsilon$ around the original input $x$ . In the case of continuous spaces $d_{\infty}(x,x^{\prime})$ is the $\ell_{\infty}$ distance, while for token-level attacks we set $d_{\mathrm{edit}}(x,x^{\prime})$ as the Levenshtein distance, where $\varepsilon$ -budget constrains the number of single-token
insertions, deletions, and substitutions required to transform
 $x$ into $x^{\prime}$ .
Finally, an attack algorithm on the function $f_{j}$ is denoted $\mathcal{A}_{j}(\varepsilon):\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{X}$ which takes in an input $x$ and returns a perturbed input $x^{\star}\in{\mathcal{B}}_{\epsilon}$ 

## 2.1 Adversarial Robustness

We are interested in the problem of producing adversarial attacks in the permissive white-box setting, which traditionally considers the safety relationship between input-output pairs centered at an instance $(x,y)$ given oracle access to a target model $f$ or model pool ${\mathcal{F}}$ . In particular, if $S_{y}\subseteq\mathcal{Y}$ is the safe output region, the goal of an adversarial attack is to produce an adversarial example such that $f(\mathcal{A}(x))\notin S_{y}$ . This motivates
the natural measure of performance of an attack algorithm ${\mathcal{A}}$ :

 $\mathrm{ASR}\coloneqq\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathds{1}\left[f^{\theta}_{j}(\mathcal{A}_{j}(x_{i}))\notin S_{y_{i}}\right]\quad(x_{i},y_{i})\sim{\mathcal{D}}.$ 

(1)

The attack success rate (ASR) (Carlini and Wagner, 2017; Croce and Hein, 2020a) defined in eq. 1 underscores the ability of an attack algorithm to break a single model.
A different but related measure of the performance of an attack algorithm is to consider the transferability of an attack to a new function—i.e., $f^{\theta}_{k}(\mathcal{A}_{j}(x))\notin S_{y}$ for $k\neq j$ (Szegedy et al., 2014a; Papernot et al., 2016a; Liu et al., 2017a). Against a model pool (Liu et al., 2017a; Mao et al., 2022), we can equally measure the performance of an attack algorithm by computing its transfer attack success coverage:

 $\texttt{tASC}(j):=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{k=1}^{M}\mathrm{AS}(j,k),\quad\text{where}\,\mathrm{AS}(j,k)\coloneqq\mathds{1}\left[f^{\theta}_{k}(\mathcal{A}_{j}(x))\notin S_{y}\right],$ 

(2)

where $\mathrm{AS}(j,k)$ captures the transfer of an attack from models $j\to k$ . In addition to the usual transfer setting, it is possible to devise an attacker that crafts a transferable attack by leveraging a cluster of models $\mathcal{A}_{\mathcal{I}}$ where $\mathcal{I}\subseteq[M]:=\{1,\dots,M\}$ is the index set of models (Dong et al., 2018; Chen et al., 2024).
The resulting coverage is termed the joint attack success coverage, and is defined as $\texttt{jASC}(\mathcal{I}):=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{j=1}^{M}\mathds{1}\left[f^{\theta}_{j}\left(\mathcal{A}_{\mathcal{I}}(x)\right)\notin S_{y}\right]$ . Lastly, we note that $\texttt{tASC}(j)=\texttt{jASC}(\{j\})$ .

## 3 Method

We are interested in analyzing the collective adversarial robustness profile of a model pool ${\mathcal{F}}$ , in which subsets of the pool ${\mathcal{I}}\subset{\mathcal{F}}$ are potentially distilled from a common model within the pool. More precisely, we aim to characterize whether a shared teacher distillation setting that gives rise to the model pool can inadvertently expose a new form of vulnerability inherited by the students from a common teacher. In particular, we ask whether distillation creates this tension: can otherwise distinct students acquire vulnerabilities that a small collection of adversarial inputs can exploit?

Distinction for classical adversarial robustness.
At a cursory level, determining the shared adversarial robustness profile of ${\mathcal{F}}$ bears resemblance to conventional adversarial attacks, including both the transferable (tASC) and joint attack (jASC) settings. However, a key distinction is that individual attack success rates measure the individual vulnerability of a model, while tASC and jASC are fundamentally about the reachability of an optimized single adversarial input within ${\mathcal{F}}$ . Crucially, no setting assesses the shared vulnerability of the entire model pool. More concisely, widespread failure against ${\mathcal{F}}$ may require only a few broadly effective adversarial inputs, or many inputs targeting different models. These two extremes illuminate a new attack surface for shared vulnerabilities of model clusters within ${\mathcal{F}}$ that current training methodology, such as model distillation, blatantly expose.

We organize the remainder of the section as follows: in §3.1 we first rigorously define a new notion of adversarial robustness, termed Systemic Adversarial Robustness(SAR) to characterize the shared vulnerability of a model pool. We then build intuition in §3.2 by considering a warm-up exercise with attacking a model pool trained on MNIST. In §3.3 we present a top-down greedy algorithm to compute SAR against practical real-world models. Finally, in §3.4 we provide a theoretical perspective on SAR through the partial set cover problem, which helps codify approximation guarantees.

## 3.1 Systemic Adversarial Robustness

We now define Systemic Adversarial Robustness(SAR) to quantify the
shared and systemic robustness profile of a prescribed model pool ${\mathcal{F}}$ . Intuitively, given a fixed model pool and perturbation set SAR is the minimum number of distinct inputs needed to compromise a
target fraction of the pool, normalized by its size. Stated more formally, results in the following definition below.

{nfmdefinition}

[ $1-\delta$ Systemic Adversarial Robustness]

Let $\mathcal{F}=\{f_{j}\}_{j=1}^{M}$ be a pool of $M\geq 1$ 
deterministic models $f_{j}:\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{Y}$ .
Fix an instance $(x,y)\in\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}$ , an admissible
perturbation set $T_{x}\subseteq\mathcal{X}$ , a safe output region
 $S_{y}\subseteq\mathcal{Y}$ , and $\delta\in[0,1)$ .
For each admissible input $x^{\prime}\in T_{x}$ , let

 $C(x^{\prime}):=\{j\in[M]:f_{j}(x^{\prime})\notin S_{y}\}$ 

denote the indices of the models it compromises, where
 $[M]=\{1,\ldots,M\}$ .
The systemic adversarial robustness at coverage $1-\delta$ is the
minimum number of distinct admissible inputs needed to collectively
compromise at least a $1-\delta$ fraction of the pool, normalized
by its size:

 $\operatorname{SAR}_{1-\delta}(\mathcal{F};x,y):=\frac{1}{M}\min_{V\subseteq T_{x}}\left\{|V|\;:\;\frac{1}{M}\left|\bigcup_{x^{\prime}\in V}C(x^{\prime})\right|\geq(1-\delta)\right\}.$ 

(3)

In section 3.1 each model counts toward coverage if it is compromised by at least
one input in $V$ . If no collection satisfies the coverage constraint,
we define the systemic adversarial robustness to be $1$ .
At the level of shared vulnerabilities, small SAR values indicate a critical shared risk profile, in that only a few crafted adversarial inputs suffice for compromising a $1-\delta$ fraction of the model pool ${\mathcal{F}}$ . Specifically, a low SAR implies the existence of highly correlated vulnerabilities and thus provides a holistic robustness profile.
We also highlight that SAR does not provide commentary on individual robustness measurements, but rather SAR aids us in investigating whether
there are latent clusters—potentially due to model distillation—within ${\mathcal{F}}$ that are vulnerable and easier to exploit collectively.

## 3.2 Warmup with MNIST

We first illustrate the distinction between SAR and conventional adversarial robustness using a controlled experiment with MNIST classifiers. As the canonical attack algorithm ${\mathcal{A}}$ we use the PGD attack (Madry et al., 2019). We next systematically investigate two concrete threads as a warmup:

(Q1) 

Pool Diversity. How does SAR change as a function of diversity within the model pool ${\mathcal{F}}$ ?

(Q2) 

Distillation. How does distillation change the individual ASR and SAR?

(Q1) Pool Diversity.
We construct three pools of classifiers from low to medium to high diversity. The
low-diversity pool uses the same MLP architecture, training
data, and optimization procedure, varying $f_{i}\in{\mathcal{F}}$ only in initialization.
The medium-diversity pool additionally fixes the architecture but varies data
augmentation, training splits, optimization, and regularization. Lastly,
the high-diversity pool also varies architecture, combining
MLPs and variants of convolutional models. In all cases, the models are trained to convergence with mean test accuracies of $98.0\%$ , $98.3\%$ , and $99.2\%$ (full training
details are provided in §A.3.1).

The first controlled experiment is visualized in fig. 2 and illustrates perturbations crafted within ${\mathcal{B}}_{\varepsilon}$ , as we vary the radius $\varepsilon$ in an $\ell_{\infty}$ -sense. In particular, we allow each model’s safe region $S_{y}$ to be its own clean prediction, and count the model as compromised when its prediction falls outside $S_{y}$ , i.e.,
 $C(x^{\prime})=\{j:f_{j}(x^{\prime})\neq f_{j}(x)\}$ . We next compute the SAR across pools, perturbation budgets, and coverage thresholds, tracing a curve as a function of $1-\delta$ along the $x$ -axis. This SAR curve is each model pool’s vulnerability profile and exposes the main finding: the high-diversity pool at the stringent $\varepsilon=0.05$ attack budget exhibits substantially greater systemic adversarial robustness than the other pools. As the attack strength increases by increasing the perturbation budget, we observe that the high-diversity pool sustains its SAR advantage.

(Q2) Distillation.
We next consider a controlled distillation setup to assess the impact of training on annotations of a shared teacher. Specifically, we train a ResNet-18 teacher on MNIST ( $99.5\%$ test accuracy)
and use its predictions to label $240{,}000$ EMNIST-digits images.
For this experiment, we use $M=20$ of the high-diversity model pool as students and train each on these teacher-labeled examples.

In fig. 3 we visualize two plots of interest. First, we plot SAR and ASR as a function of distillation fine-tuning steps in comparison to the reference starting point of the model pools. This experiment aims to tease apart an initial counterintuitive phenomenon of SAR: its independence from individual model ASR. In particular, one can observe a substantial reduction in the required distinct attack count for a specified target coverage of $1-\delta=0.85$ , without a noticeable decrease in individual attack success. This not only reveals a change in collective vulnerability due to distillation, but points to the fact that ASR is an insufficient proxy for SAR.
Figure 3 illustrates the emergence of a vulnerability monoculture as distillation exposes students in the model ${\mathcal{F}}$ to higher shared risk. Additionally, we plot the distribution of the SAR at $1-\delta=0.85$ , which clearly displays a shift in model pool robustness over the course of distillation-based fine-tuning.

## 3.3 Estimating Systemic Adversarial Robustness

Estimating SAR as defined in section 3.1 requires finding a small collection of
admissible perturbations whose combined coverage reaches the prescribed
 $1-\delta$ threshold. At the scale of LLMs and other real-world security use cases, such an exhaustive search over collections is computationally intractable. Instead, we aim to provide a computationally tractable top-down greedy procedure that synthesizes a simple line of attack: find an input perturbation that compromises several models in ${\mathcal{I}}\subset{\mathcal{F}}$ , record its coverage, and repeat on the models left uncovered ${\mathcal{F}}\setminus{\mathcal{I}}$ . This top-down algorithm is outlined in algorithm 1 and defines a recursive procedure until $\tau\coloneqq\lceil(1-\delta)M\rceil$ is reached.

Discovering shared vulnerabilities.
Algorithm 1 maintains an active set of model indices
 $\mathcal{I}$ , initially $[M]$ , and a covered set ${\mathcal{R}}$ , initially
empty. At each iteration, a joint attack $\mathcal{A}_{\mathcal{I}}$ 
searches for an admissible input with broad coverage of the
active models. If it compromises at least one member of
 $\mathcal{I}$ , we retain the input, add the newly compromised
models to ${\mathcal{R}}$ , and remove them from further targeting.
On the other hand, if ${\mathcal{A}}_{{\mathcal{I}}}$ compromises no active model, we attempt
a single-model attack against one member of ${\mathcal{I}}$ . This procedure continues recursively until $|{\mathcal{R}}|\geq\tau$ or no active models remain. Specifically, if the target coverage is attained using
 $\hat{k}$ distinct inputs, the algorithm returns the SAR estimate
 $\widehat{\operatorname{SAR}}_{1-\delta}=\hat{k}/M$ which is an upper bound on the true systemic adversarial robustness of ${\mathcal{F}}$ .

It is instructive to note that the first attack launched in algorithm 1 targets the entire model pool $|{\mathcal{F}}|$ , and every subsequent accepted attack vector is optimized against all current models remaining in the pool ${\mathcal{F}}\setminus{\mathcal{R}}$ . Intuitively, clusters that share the same attack surface area emerge from the observed coverage. Furthermore, a broadly transferable attack may cover much of the pool, but the additional attacks needed to reach the target coverage
determine the estimated systemic adversarial robustness.

## 3.4 Analogy to Set Covering and Approximation Guarantees

Fix an instance $(x,y)$ and associate with every perturbed input $x^{\prime}\in T_{x}$ its cover $\mathcal{C}(x^{\prime}):=\{j\in[M]:f_{j}(x^{\prime})\notin S_{y}\}$ , i.e., the members of the pool that $x^{\prime}$ breaks. Definition 3.1 then reads

 $k^{\star}:=\min\Big\{|X|\;:\;X\subseteq T_{x},\ \big|\textstyle\bigcup_{x^{\prime}\in X}\mathcal{C}(x^{\prime})\big|\geq\tau\Big\}$ 

(4)

with systemic robustness $k^{\star}/M$ . Problem (4) is the partial set cover problem (Kearns and Vazirani, 1994; Slavík, 1997) over the universe $[M]$ with unit costs and the set system $\{\mathcal{C}(x^{\prime}):x^{\prime}\in T_{x}\}$ ; the choice $\delta=0$ recovers the classical set cover problem (Chvatal, 1979), so computing the value of Definition 3.1 exactly is NP-hard in general. Two features distinguish our setting from the textbook setting. First, the set system is only implicit: $T_{x}$ is continuous or exponentially large, and covers are exposed one at a time by the attack. Second, the attack returns some vector rather than the vector of maximal marginal coverage. Algorithm 1 is therefore the greedy algorithm for partial cover run with an approximate incremental oracle (Goundan and Schulz, 2007), whose quality we summarize in a single parameter.

{nfmdefinition}

[ $\alpha$ -approximate joint attack]
A joint attack $\mathcal{A}$ is $\alpha$ -approximate, $\alpha\in(0,1]$ , if for every non-empty $\mathcal{I}\subseteq[M]$ 

 $\big|\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{A}_{\mathcal{I}}(x))\cap\mathcal{I}\big|\;\geq\;\alpha\max_{x^{\prime}\in T_{x}}\big|\mathcal{C}(x^{\prime})\cap\mathcal{I}\big|.$ 

(5)

That is, the vector returned against the uncovered models breaks at least an $\alpha$ fraction of the number of models that the best vector in $T_{x}$ would break; $\alpha=1$ is an exact greedy oracle. Under Definition 3.4, and whenever (4) is feasible, the white-box fallback of Algorithm 1 is never executed: as long as fewer than $\tau$ models are covered, some uncovered model is attackable, so the joint attack covers at least one. The fallback only guards termination when the attack underperforms.

Theorem 1 (Approximation ratio).

Fix an instance $(x,y)$ and $\delta\in[0,1)$ for which (4) is feasible, and let $\hat{k}$ be the number of vectors returned by Algorithm 1 with an $\alpha$ -approximate joint attack. Then

 $k^{\star}\;\leq\;\hat{k}\;\leq\;\Big(1+\frac{\ln{\tau}}{\alpha}\Big)k^{\star},$ 

(6)

and the same ratio holds between the estimated and the true systemic robustness $\hat{k}/M$ and $k^{\star}/M$ . Being uniform over instances, it carries over to the average over $\mathcal{D}$ .

The value of theorem 1 is not a guarantee of performance, but an intuition about when our algorithm is known to perform well. Section 3.1 casts SAR as a partial set cover problem over the pool (Kearns and Vazirani, 1994; Slavík, 1997) whose sets, the covers $C(x^{\prime})$ of admissible inputs, are revealed only by running an attack. An estimate $\hat{k}$ from Algorithm 1 therefore carries two errors: the adversarial error of the attack, which may miss breaking inputs that exist, and the procedural error of the top-down procedure, which commits to covers greedily and may use more inputs than necessary. Our analysis in theorem 1 bounds only the procedural error, that is, the error the top-down procedure adds on top of the adversarial error.

Writing $\alpha_{t}$ for the fraction of the best achievable marginal coverage that the attack attains at step $t$ , and $\bar{\alpha}$ for its mean over all but the last step, every run that discards no model satisfies $k^{\star}\leq\hat{k}\leq 1+k^{\star}\ln\tau/\bar{\alpha}$ (Theorem 1). On the other hand, adversarial error itself cannot be bounded in general: for a single model, an efficient attack with any guaranteed $\alpha>0$ would decide neural-network verification, which is NP-hard (Katz et al., 2017). Because $\bar{\alpha}$ is not observable, the theorem is a decomposition rather than a certificate, and we use it as an empirical guide.

## 4 Experiments

In §3.2 we showed that models with similar individual robustness
can form pools with very different systemic robustness, and that fine-tuning on
a shared teacher can lower SAR while individual robustness holds. We now ask
whether the same happens to language models that share a post-training source.
To do so, we compare a pool of models before fine-tuning (parents) and after fine-tuning from a given teacher (descendants). We estimate
SAR with different pool-aware attacks, and control for models that already fail on
the unmodified request.

Model pools.
Our LLM experiments rely on three different model pools varying in model complexity and diversity. We evaluate the systemic adversarial robustness compared to the attack success rate over the course of distillation on a small model pool with LLMs ranging from 135M to 5B parameters. Additionally, we use a medium and large pool to further evaluate the impact of distillation on larger and more diverse base models. The medium pool consists of five Qwen models and one Llama model. The large pool is more diverse with eight models from five different model providers. All of our experiments are distilled on DeepSeek-R1 and/or Claude Opus 4.6 traces. For further details we defer to §A.3.2.

Behaviors and judge.
Harmful requests are drawn from the standard category of HarmBench (Mazeika et al., 2024). Decoding is deterministic, and a model counts
as compromised when the HarmBench Llama-2-13B
classifier (Mazeika et al., 2024) labels its response harmful.

Attacks.
We estimate SAR with the top-down procedure of section 3.3 and three
pool-aware joint attacks. Within the main text we consider attacks warm-started with samples from AmpleGCG (Kumar et al., 2024) and optimized using BEAST (Sadasivan et al., 2024) as joint attack. In the Appedix we show that the same trends are found using other optimization approaches including warm starting with AdvLLM (Sun et al., 2025), optimized with a joint GCG (Zou et al., 2023a), as well as using non-insertion based attacks such as AutoDAN (Liu et al., 2025).

Evaluation.
For joint BEAST, $\hat{k}$ is the number of attacks the top-down search selects, and behaviors whose coverage requirement is never met have $\mathrm{SAR}=1$ . A model that fails on the unmodified request is compromised without any attack. We report the relative change in mean $\hat{k}/M$ from the original pool.

In addition, we observe SAR to be a more meaningful indicator of pool robustness than ASR in LLMs, similar to motivating findings on MNIST. Figure 5 shows that SAR erodes faster and further than individual robustness ( $1-\mathrm{ASR}$ ), with $\mathrm{SAR}_{0.85}$ declining by $15.0\%$ compared to a $5.3\%$ decline in individual robustness. Moreover, SAR drops significantly after only $0.05$ epochs, while individual robustness only shows a significant drop after $0.15$ epochs. The final distilled pool is less systemically robust at every level of $\delta$ as can be seen on the right hand side of Figure 5.

Opus.
This pattern is amplified when further distilling the descendants of DeepSeek-R1 distillation on Opus 4.6 traces. In Figure 4 and Table 6 it is evident that the mean $\hat{k}$ continues to decline. This time the severity of the pattern inverts: the medium pool loses a further $\sim 44\%$ of its systemic robustness, while the large pool becomes only an additional $\sim 6\%$ less robust. Takeaways.
Across all three pools, fine-tuning on shared teacher traces lowers systemic robustness under AmpleGCG + BEAST (§A.3.2 for a more detailed analysis). SAR erodes faster and further over the course of distillation than individual robustness, making it a more sensitive indicator of pool-level risk. Neither teacher is consistently more harmful: DeepSeek-R1 distillation degrades the large pool most, whereas subsequent Opus 4.6 distillation degrades the medium pool most.

## 5 Related Works

Adversarial Attacks.
We can broadly divide adversarial attacks into white-box and black-box attacks. Prominent white-box attacks include the Fast Gradient Sign Method (FGSM) (Goodfellow et al., 2015), its iterative counterpart Projected Gradient Descent (PGD) (Madry et al., 2019), MI-FGSM (Dong et al., 2018), the Carlini-Wagner attack (Carlini and Wagner, 2017), elastic-net attacks (Chen et al., 2018), and fast adaptive boundary attacks (Croce and Hein, 2020a). Black-box attacks commonly rely on the transferability of white-box attacks, e.g., Square Attack (Andriushchenko et al., 2020), and HopSkipJumpAttack (Chen et al., 2020). AutoAttack (Croce and Hein, 2020b) applies white- and black-box attacks alike to automatically find suitable adversarial attacks.

Ensembles in Adversarial Contexts.
Ensembles for adversarial defense, and analogously for attacks, are extensively studied (Li et al., 2025; Wood et al., 2023). ADP (Pang et al., 2019) regularises training based on the Shannon entropy, TRS (Yang et al., 2021) enforces low loss-gradient similarity to reduce transferability among members, and Cui et al. (2022) trains ensemble members to minimise vulnerability overlap. GAL (Kariyappa and Qureshi, 2019) reduces the dimensionality of the subspace adversarial samples live in, while DVERGE (Yang et al., 2020) uses a novel diversity metric to reduce shared vulnerability. Li et al. (2025) provides a theoretical grounding and proposes iGAT.

Transferability of Adversarial Attacks..
Transferability was observed in seminal work by Szegedy et al. (2014b), subsequently leveraged in Papernot et al. (2016b), and expanded to substantially larger models by Liu et al. (2017b) and first theoretically analyzed in Demontis et al. (2019). Some works show that flat adversarial samples yield more transferable attacks (Fan et al., 2024; Zheng et al., 2026; Ge et al., 2023). For foundation models, Wei et al. (2022) introduces transferable attacks on vision transformers, while Zou et al. (2023b) and Liao and Sun (2024) show that attacks transfer across aligned LLMs, with Ma et al. (2024) further showing that alignment itself can increase transferability.

## 6 Conclusion

In this work, we introduce Systemic Adversarial Robustness (SAR), which measures how many distinct attacks are needed to compromise a target fraction of a model pool. We additionally provide a simple algorithm to compute the SAR and analyze it by analogy to the set-cover problem. Empirically, we demonstrate that models (from MLPs on MNIST to modern foundation models) often do share a small number of exploitable vulnerabilities. Further, we show that distillation from a shared model exacerbates the shared vulnerabilities and thus the SAR. Finally, while we provide a principled measure, characterizing shared vulnerabilities remains an important and understudied problem.

## A.2 Systemic Risk Evaluation Algorithms

Joint PGD for Image Classification Models.

For an image $x\in[0,1]^{d}$ , with pixel intensities scaled to $[0,1]$ , the
admissible perturbation set is the $\ell_{\infty}$ ball
 $\mathcal{B}_{\varepsilon}(x)$ within the valid pixel range,

 $\mathcal{B}_{\varepsilon}(x)=\bigl\{\,x^{\prime}\in[0,1]^{d}:\|x^{\prime}-x\|_{\infty}\leq\varepsilon\,\bigr\}.$ 

(7)

Objective.

Let $z_{j}(x^{\prime})\in\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{Y}|}$ denote the
logits of $f_{j}$ , so that $f_{j}(x^{\prime})=\arg\max_{c}z_{j}(x^{\prime})_{c}$ . Given an instance
 $x$ and a set of uncovered models $\mathcal{I}\subseteq[M]$ , joint
PGD seeks a single admissible input that compromises every uncovered model at
once, by maximizing their mean cross-entropy with respect to their own clean
predictions:

 $\max_{x^{\prime}\in T_{x}}\;\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{I}}(x^{\prime}),\qquad\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{I}}(x^{\prime})=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{I}|}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{I}}\mathrm{CE}\bigl(z_{j}(x^{\prime}),\,f_{j}(x)\bigr).$ 

(8)

This is an untargeted, white-box attack: every model in $\mathcal{I}$ 
contributes an equal share of the gradient, and models outside
 $\mathcal{I}$ , which are already covered, contribute nothing.

Optimization.

We solve (8) with $\ell_{\infty}$ projected gradient ascent.
Let $\Pi_{\mathcal{B}_{\varepsilon}(x)}$ be the projection onto $\mathcal{B}_{\varepsilon}(x)$ , which clips each coordinate
 $x^{\prime}_{i}$ to $[-\varepsilon,\varepsilon]$ around $x_{i}$ and then to $[0,1]$ . Both
constraints are coordinate-wise intervals that intersect, so these two clips
give the exact projection. Starting from a uniform random point
 $x^{\prime(0)}=\Pi_{\mathcal{B}_{\varepsilon}(x)}(x+u)$ with
 $u\sim\mathcal{U}([-\varepsilon,\varepsilon]^{d})$ , each step is

 $x^{\prime(t+1)}=\Pi_{\mathcal{B}_{\varepsilon}(x)}\Bigl(x^{\prime(t)}+\eta\,\operatorname{sign}\bigl(\nabla_{x^{\prime}}\,\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{I}}(x^{\prime(t)})\bigr)\Bigr),\qquad t=0,\dots,n_{\mathrm{it}}-1.$ 

(9)

We run $n_{\mathrm{rs}}$ independent random restarts and return the final
iterate of the restart with the highest objective
 $\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{I}}(x^{\prime(n_{\mathrm{it}})})$ (Algorithm 2).

Joint BEAST for Large Language Models.

For LLMs, an instance $x$ is a harmful request from AdvBench, paired with an
affirmative target response $y_{x}$ (e.g. “Sure, here is …”). The
attacker appends an adversarial suffix $s$ to the request, written
 $x\oplus s$ while adhering to an attack budget $L$ . A suffix $s$ is conforming with the budget constraint iff $|s|\leq L$ under a fixed canonical tokenizer.

Objective.

Each model $f_{j}$ is an autoregressive LLM with its own tokenizer. Let $p_{j}(\cdot\mid\cdot)$ be its next-token distribution. Following BEAST [Sadasivan et al., 2024], we optimise a surrogate: the per-token
negative log-likelihood of the target response,

 $\ell_{j}(x^{\prime})=-\frac{1}{|y_{x}|_{j}}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_{x}|_{j}}\log p_{j}\bigl(y_{x,t}\,\big|\,c_{j}(x^{\prime}),\,y_{x,<t}\bigr),$ 

(10)

where $y_{x}$ is tokenized with $f_{j}$ ’s own tokenizer and $|y_{x}|_{j}$ is its
length. Given an instance $x$ and a set of uncovered models
 $\mathcal{I}\subseteq[M]$ , joint BEAST 
 [
 ]
 seeks a suffix that makes every
uncovered model likely to begin its answer with the target, by minimizing
their mean target negative log likelihood (NLL).

Optimization.

We optimize for the NLL with a gradient-free beam search over suffix
tokens in which every active model proposes candidates
(Algorithm 3). The beam starts from $B$ seed suffixes of $L_{0}$ 
tokens, generated for the request $x$ by AmpleGCG [Liao and Sun, 2024]. The search then extends
every suffix by one token per step for $L-L_{0}$ steps. At each step, every
pair of beam suffix $s$ and active model $j\in\mathcal{I}$ proposes
 $n_{j,s}$ distinct tokens, sampled without replacement from

 $q_{j,s}=\operatorname{softmax}\bigl(z_{j}(x\oplus s)/T\bigr),$ 

(11)

where $z_{j}(x\oplus s)$ are the logits of $f_{j}$ at the last position of
 $c_{j}(x\oplus s)$ , that is, where its response would begin. Each proposal is decoded to text, re-encoded with the
canonical tokenizer, appended to $s$ , and truncated to $L_{0}+t$ canonical
tokens. The quotas $n_{j,s}$ are split as
evenly as possible over all $B\,|\mathcal{I}|$ source pairs, so that no
single model dominates the candidate set. Every candidate is then scored on
every active model, and the $B$ candidates with the lowest mean NLL
 $\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{I}}$ form the next beam. After the final step, the
attack returns all $B$ suffixes in the beam, not only the best one.

Joint GCG for Large Language Models.

For joint GCG, an instance $x$ is a harmful request from HarmBench. As for
joint BEAST, the attacker appends an adversarial suffix $s$ to the request,
written $x\oplus s$ . Unlike joint BEAST, the suffix length is not
constrained explicitly: it is set by the initial suffix, and each
optimization step substitutes a single token.

Objective.

Joint GCG optimises an untargeted surrogate. Instead of pulling each model
towards an affirmative target, it pushes each model away from a fixed refusal
 $r$ (“I cannot assist with that request.”). For model $f_{j}$ , the surrogate
is the per-token negative log-likelihood of the refusal.
Given an instance $x$ and a set of
uncovered models $\mathcal{I}\subseteq[M]$ , joint GCG seeks a suffix that
makes every uncovered model unlikely to refuse, by maximizing their mean
refusal NLL:

 $\max_{s}\;\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{I}}(x\oplus s),\qquad\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{I}}(x^{\prime})=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{I}|}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{I}}\ell_{j}(x^{\prime}).$ 

(12)

Optimization.

We optimize (12) with greedy coordinate gradient (GCG)
search [Zou et al., 2023a], adapted to a pool of models that do not share
a tokenizer (Algorithm 4). Suffixes are therefore exchanged between
models as text and never as token ids. At each step, one model
 $j\in\mathcal{I}$ proposes candidates, and the proposing model rotates over
 $\mathcal{I}$ so that every active model proposes for an (almost) equal
number of steps. The proposing model tokenizes the current suffix $s$ with
its own tokenizer as $(v_{1},\dots,v_{n})$ and computes the gradient of its
surrogate $\tilde{\ell}_{j}$ with respect to the one-hot encoding
 $e_{i}\in\{0,1\}^{|\mathcal{V}_{j}|}$ of each suffix token $v_{i}$ . Each of $n_{\mathrm{cand}}$ candidates is formed by
choosing a position $i$ uniformly at random and replacing $v_{i}$ with a token
drawn uniformly from the $n_{\mathrm{top}}$ tokens with the largest gradient
entries at that position. The candidate with the highest mean refusal NLL
 $\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{I}}$ replaces the current suffix if it improves
on the best objective found so far.

Joint AutoDAN for Large Language Models.

For joint AutoDAN, an instance $x$ is a harmful request from HarmBench.
Unlike joint BEAST and joint GCG, the attack does not append a suffix.
Instead, an attacker LLM $g$ rewrites the request into a complete
natural-language prompt $u$ that preserves its objective. A prompt is
admissible if it is at most 6,000 characters long, reads as ordinary
natural language (at least five words and at least 85% ASCII characters),
and does not duplicate an earlier prompt up to whitespace and case.

Objective.

The attack treats the target models as black boxes and uses only their text
responses. For each prompt $u$ , every model in the pool generates a greedy
response $f_{j}(u)$ , which the HarmBench classifier labels with a binary
outcome $b_{j}(u)\in\{0,1\}$ and a confidence
 $\sigma_{j}(u)\in[0,1]$ . The confidence is the classifier’s normalised probability of
answering "yes" or "no" at its first output token. Given an instance $x$ and a set of models
 $\mathcal{I}\subseteq[M]$ , joint AutoDAN seeks a prompt that compromises as
many models in $\mathcal{I}$ as possible. It ranks prompts lexicographically by

 $R_{\mathcal{I}}(u)=\Bigl(\,\sum_{j\in\mathcal{I}}b_{j}(u),\;\;\frac{1}{|\mathcal{I}|}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{I}}\sigma_{j}(u),\;\;q_{0.2}\bigl(\{\sigma_{j}(u)\}_{j\in\mathcal{I}}\bigr)\Bigr),$ 

(13)

that is, by the number of compromised models, then the mean confidence, then
the lower 20% quantile of the confidences. The mean and the quantile break
ties between prompts that compromise the same number of models, and favour
prompts that move the least-compromised models.

Optimization.

We optimize (13) with a strategy-guided search adapted
from AutoDAN-Turbo [Liu et al., 2025], in which the attacker revises
prompts using feedback from one anchor model at a in a round robin manner
(Algorithm 5). The attacker is guided by a frozen library of
abstract jailbreak strategies $\Sigma$ from the AutoDAN-Turbo warm-up library. The search first evaluates the unmodified request $x$ on every model.
The attacker then produces a cold-start rewrite of $x$ without strategy
guidance, which becomes the first candidate. The anchor then cycles once over
all $M$ models in the pool. For anchor $a$ , two parents are selected from all
candidates found so far: the systemic parent $u^{\mathrm{sys}}$ , which
maximizes $R_{\mathcal{I}}$ , and the specialist parent
 $u^{\mathrm{spec}}$ , which is best on the anchor alone, ranked by
 $(b_{a},\sigma_{a},R_{\mathcal{I}})$ . The strategies in $\Sigma$ are ranked by
lexical cosine similarity between their definitions and the request, the
systemic parent, and the anchor’s response to it. The attacker then generates
 $n_{\mathrm{p}}$ proposals. Proposals alternate between the systemic and the
specialist parent. Each of the first $n_{\mathrm{p}}-1$ proposals applies one
of the top-ranked strategies, and the last combines the top two. For every
proposal, the attacker receives the request, the parent prompt, the anchor’s
response to the systemic parent, whether that response was judged harmful,
and the strategy definitions. It returns a revised prompt, which is resampled
up to three times if it is not admissible. Every proposal is evaluated on
every model in the pool and added to the candidates.

## A.3.1 MNIST

For MNIST experiments, the base model pools are trained once, their configurations persisted and then reused across all experiments. All models are trained to convergence via an early stopping mechanism, triggered when a held out validation loss starts to plateau (with a patience of 10 train steps). After early stopping is triggered, we recover the best performing epoch checkpoint.

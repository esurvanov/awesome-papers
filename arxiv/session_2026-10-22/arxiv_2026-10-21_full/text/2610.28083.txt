Abstract

Electrocardiography (ECG) is a cornerstone of cardiac diagnosis, yet most machine learning methods for ECG analysis lack interpretability, limiting their clinical adoption. We present IECG, a weakly-supervised ECG foundation model that jointly performs diagnostic classification and abnormality localization without requiring manual abnormal region annotations, overcoming a long-standing bottleneck in interpretable ECG learning. Central to IECG is a weakly-supervised attention strategy that explicitly incorporates clinical priors by deriving abnormality heatmap pseudo-labels from established ECG clinical knowledge. These pseudo-labels guide the model’s CLS-token attention through a KL-divergence loss, encouraging it to focus on diagnostically relevant waveform regions. During pretraining, IECG is jointly optimized with clinically guided attention supervision, ECG-text contrastive alignment, and report captioning objectives, enabling it to learn rich multimodal representations while producing localized waveform evidence. By explicitly aggregating features from clinically guided attention regions, IECG grounds its diagnostic predictions in interpretable waveform evidence without sacrificing classification performance. Extensive experiments demonstrate that IECG achieves state-of-the-art abnormal region localization while maintaining strong classification performance, advancing trustworthy foundation models for ECG analysis.

## 1 Introduction

Deep learning architectures for electrocardiogram (ECG) analysis [37, 24, 23, 21] have achieved remarkable success in automated cardiac diagnosis. However, despite their strong predictive performance, these models remain largely uninterpretable: they predict what abnormality is present but fail to explain where the supporting evidence resides within the ECG waveform. This limitation substantially hinders their clinical adoption, as physicians rely not only on diagnostic predictions but also on verifying the underlying waveform morphology. An ECG analysis model should therefore identify both the predicted diagnosis and the localized waveform evidence supporting that prediction.

Despite its importance, abnormality localization remains an unsolved problem for ECG foundation models. The primary challenge is the lack of localized supervision. Large-scale ECG datasets such as MIMIC-IV-ECG [4] and PTB-XL [33] provide only recording-level diagnostic labels and contain no annotations identifying the waveform regions corresponding to each diagnosis. Consequently, existing models are optimized solely for classification and cannot learn to consistently associate predictions with clinically meaningful waveform regions. While various methods can highlight irregular beats [3, 7], they fail to provide specific diagnoses associated with those localized regions.

In this work, we address this challenge by exploiting a key observation: although localized annotations are unavailable, diagnostic criteria for many ECG abnormalities are well established in clinical practice. For example, a PR interval exceeding 200 ms is a diagnostic criterion for first-degree AV block, while ST-segment elevation greater than 0.1 mV indicates myocardial injury. We formalize these clinical heuristics into an automatic pseudo-label generation pipeline that produces abnormality heatmaps directly from ECG signals. By explicitly incorporating these clinical priors, we transform localization from a manual annotation problem into a weakly-supervised learning problem.

Building upon this insight, we propose IECG, an attention-enhanced interpretable ECG foundation model that jointly performs classification and abnormality localization. During training, clinically derived abnormality heatmaps supervise the model’s attention through a Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence objective, encouraging attention to align with diagnostically relevant waveform regions while learning disease classification. As a result, IECG produces both diagnostic predictions and localized visual evidence supporting those predictions. For example, when diagnosing atrial fibrillation, the model highlights the irregularly absent P-wave regions that cardiologists naturally inspect during clinical interpretation.

Extensive experiments demonstrate that IECG achieves state-of-the-art (SOTA) abnormal region localization, reaching 75.55% AUROC and outperforming existing attribution methods, including approaches specifically designed for this task, while archiving competitive classification performance. These findings suggest that clinically guided attention supervision can significantly enhance the interpretability of ECG foundation models without requiring manual attention annotations.

Our main contributions are summarized as follows:

• 

We propose IECG, an ECG foundation model that jointly performs classification and abnormality localization without requiring manual attention annotations.

• 

We introduce a weakly-supervised attention supervision framework that incorporates clinical priors to automatically generate abnormality heatmap pseudo-labels from established ECG diagnostic heuristics, aligning model attention with clinically meaningful waveform regions.

• 

We demonstrate SOTA abnormality localization and competitive classification performance, highlighting the effectiveness of clinically guided attention supervision for improving the interpretability of ECG foundation models.

## 2 Related Work

## 2.1 Foundation models for ECG

Foundation models have become a dominant approach for ECG analysis, typically pretrained on large-scale datasets and adapted to downstream tasks through transfer learning [21, 34, 30, 22]. However, existing approaches focus primarily on representation learning and predictive performance without explicitly addressing interpretability.

Recent work has explored both signal-only and multimodal pretraining strategies. METS [13], MERL [17], and TolerantECG [24] adopt multimodal alignment to learn joint representations between ECG signals and diagnostic reports. These methods improve semantic representation quality but do not explicitly localize diagnostic evidence within the waveform. In contrast, signal-only self-supervised methods such as ST-MEM [23], ECG-FM [21], and HeartLang [10] leverage masked reconstruction or contrastive learning to capture intrinsic spatio-temporal representations of ECG signals.

Existing ECG foundation models focus on representation, yet they do not explicitly solve where in the waveform the evidence for a diagnosis lies. In contrast, IECG is designed to enhance interpretability by construction: it introduces CGAL to generate attention pseudo-labels from clinical heuristics and uses these labels to directly supervise the model’s CLS-token attention via a KL-divergence loss, enabling anomaly localization without manual attention annotation while preserving strong downstream performance.

## 2.2 Interpretable AI and Localization

Explainable AI aims to enhance transparency in deep learning systems, which is particularly important in the medical domain, where clinicians must understand a model’s reasoning before trusting its predictions.

Saliency-based methods [29, 32] compute gradients of the model output with respect to the input to estimate feature importance. While these approaches can highlight influential regions, they often produce noisy and unstable explanations. In contrast, Grad-CAM [28, 25] generates coarse-resolution heatmaps by leveraging gradients and intermediate feature maps. However, in the context of ECG signals, such heatmaps primarily capture temporal importance and do not explicitly disentangle contributions from individual leads, which limits their clinical interpretability.

For Transformer architectures, attention calibration [20, 39] introduces auxiliary objectives to guide attention distributions toward more meaningful and interpretable patterns. Existing approaches typically derive such supervision signals from model-internal mechanisms, such as perturbation-based importance estimation or structural constraints, rather than relying on explicit ground-truth attention annotations.

However, these generic calibration strategies are not tailored to domain-specific interpretability requirements. In the ECG domain, clinically meaningful attention should align with abnormal waveform regions, yet such supervision signals are not readily available in existing datasets. Our work addresses this gap by automatically generating pseudo-labels from clinical heuristics, enabling domain-specific attention calibration without manual labeling.

## 3 Model Architecture

Figure 1: IECG overview. The ECG Encoder learns representations through ECG-Text Alignment and a self-supervised clinical abnormality localization mechanism with CGAL pipeline.

As illustrated in Figure 1, IECG consists of three main components: the ECG Encoder, ECG-Text Alignment, and CGAL. Given an ECG signal $\mathbf{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{L\times T}$ and its corresponding text report $\mathbf{R}$ , IECG learns multimodal representations while simultaneously learning to focus on clinically relevant regions.

The ECG Encoder (center) processes the raw 12-lead ECG through patching, Spatial-Temporal (ST) embedding (Section 3.1), and a Vision Transformer (ViT) to produce ECG representations. The CLS token aggregates global information, and its attention scores over the ECG patches form the basis for interpretability. ECG-Text Alignment (left) aligns ECG representations with text reports preprocessed by the Criteria Feature Retrieval (CFR) module, using a frozen Text Encoder and a Multimodal Text Decoder optimized through contrastive ( $\mathcal{L}_{contrast}$ ) and captioning ( $\mathcal{L}_{caption}$ ) losses (Section 3.2). CGAL (right) addresses the annotation bottleneck by automatically generating pseudo-labeled abnormal heatmaps. The process begins by segmenting ECG waveforms, after which the Abnormal Seeking module identifies irregular regions using clinical heuristics. Subsequently, the Criteria Filtering module refines these regions into pseudo labels based on clinical criteria retrieved by CFR. The attention loss ( $\mathcal{L}_{attn}$ ) then guides the CLS attention toward these clinically meaningful regions (Section 3.3).

## 3.1 Spatial-Temporal Embedding

Given a raw 12-lead ECG signal $\mathbf{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{L\times T}$ , where $L=12$ denotes the number of leads and $T=5000$ is the temporal length (10 seconds at 500Hz), we first apply a linear patch embedding layer. Each lead is divided into $P=T/s=100$ non-overlapping patches of size $s=50$ , yielding patch embeddings $\mathbf{Z}^{(l)}\in\mathbb{R}^{P\times D}$ for each lead $l\in\{0,\ldots,L-1\}$ , where $D$ is the hidden dimension. We then flatten across leads by concatenating all patches sequentially (I, II, III, aVR, aVL, aVF, V1, V2, V3, V4, V5, V6):

 $\mathbf{Z}=[\mathbf{Z}^{(0)};\mathbf{Z}^{(1)};\ldots;\mathbf{Z}^{(L-1)}]\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times D},$ 

(1)

where $N=L\times P=1200$ is the number of tokens.

In addition to the Transformer’s positional embedding $\mathbf{E}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times D}$ , we incorporate the Spatial-Temporal (ST) embedding proposed by Jin et al. [10] to enhance the model’s ability to capture lead-specific and temporal information, as illustrated in Figure 2. For spatial embedding, we define $\mathbf{E}^{s}\in\mathbb{R}^{L\times D}$ , assigning a unique embedding to each lead (e.g., $E^{s}_{\text{I}},E^{s}_{\text{II}},\ldots,E^{s}_{\text{V6}}$ ). For temporal embedding, we define $\mathbf{E}^{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{P\times D}$ , encoding the position within each lead (i.e., $E^{t}_{0},E^{t}_{1},\ldots,E^{t}_{P-1}$ ). The input to the Transformer is:

 $\mathbf{H}^{(0)}_{n}=\mathbf{Z}_{n}+\mathbf{E}_{n}+\mathbf{E}^{s}_{l(n)}+\mathbf{E}^{t}_{t(n)}$ 

(2)

where $l(n)=\lfloor n/P\rfloor$ and $t(n)=n\bmod P$ for $n=0,\ldots,N-1$ . Here $\mathbf{H}^{(0)}_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{D}$ is the $n$ -th token’s input representation (0-indexed), combining its patch embedding with positional embedding ( $E_{0},E_{1},\ldots,E_{N-1}$ ), spatial embedding, and temporal embedding.

Figure 2: Each ECG token is enriched with Spatial and Temporal embeddings in addition to Positional embeddings, providing explicit lead identity and time frame information.

## 3.2 ECG Signal and Text Report Alignment

Once the ECG tokens are enriched with spatial-temporal information, they are passed through the ECG Encoder, a standard Vision Transformer with $K$ layers. A learnable CLS token $\mathbf{h}_{cls}^{(0)}\in\mathbb{R}^{D}$ is prepended to the sequence:

 $\tilde{\mathbf{H}}^{(0)}=[\mathbf{h}_{cls}^{(0)};\mathbf{H}^{(0)}]\in\mathbb{R}^{(N+1)\times D}.$ 

(3)

The encoder applies $K$ transformer layers with multi-head self-attention (MHSA) and feed-forward networks (FFN):

 $\tilde{\mathbf{H}}^{(k)}=\text{FFN}(\text{MHSA}(\tilde{\mathbf{H}}^{(k-1)})),\quad k=1,\ldots,K.$ 

(4)

The final CLS token $\mathbf{h}_{cls}^{(K)}\in\mathbb{R}^{D}$ serves as the global ECG representation $\mathbf{E}$ for downstream tasks.

Following previous ECG pretraining approaches [35, 37], we employ two pretraining objectives: contrastive loss for representation learning and captioning loss for semantic alignment. Before encoding, text reports are processed by the Criteria Feature Retrieval (CFR) module [24], which retrieves relevant diagnostic criteria to enrich each diagnosis with their corresponding features. Following CoCa [36], the Multimodal Text Decoder employs causal self-attention and cross-attention over ECG encoder outputs for autoregressive report generation. The ECG Encoder and Multimodal Text Decoder are jointly optimized while the Text Encoder remains frozen.

Optimizing the contrastive loss enables alignment between ECG features $\mathbf{E}\in\mathbb{R}^{D}$ and corresponding text embeddings $\mathbf{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{D}$ . For a batch of $B$ samples, let $\{\mathbf{E}_{i},\mathbf{T}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{B}$ denote the paired ECG and text representations, and $\tau$ the temperature parameter:

 $\begin{split}\mathcal{L}_{contrast}&=-\frac{1}{2B}\Bigg(\sum_{i=1}^{B}\log\frac{e^{\mathbf{E}_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{T}_{i}/\tau}}{\sum_{j=1}^{B}e^{\mathbf{E}_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{T}_{j}/\tau}}\\
&+\sum_{i=1}^{B}\log\frac{e^{\mathbf{T}_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{E}_{i}/\tau}}{\sum_{j=1}^{B}e^{\mathbf{T}_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{E}_{j}/\tau}}\Bigg)\end{split}$ 

(5)

Optimizing the captioning loss encourages the ECG encoder to produce representations that enable accurate report generation. Given the ECG token sequence ${\mathbf{H}}^{(K)}\in\mathbb{R}^{(N)\times D}$ from the encoder (excluding CLS token) and target text tokens $\mathbf{t}=(t_{1},\ldots,t_{M})$ of length $M$ , the Multimodal Text Decoder with parameters $\theta$ autoregressively predicts each token, with the corresponding loss function:

 $\mathcal{L}_{caption}=-\mathbb{E}\Bigg[\sum_{m=1}^{M}\log P_{\theta}(t_{m}|t_{1:m-1},{\mathbf{H}}^{(K)})\Bigg]$ 

(6)

## 3.3 Clinically-Guided Attention Localization (CGAL)

CGAL is the key component that solves the annotation bottleneck. It generates attention pseudo-labels during training by encoding established clinical knowledge into a rule-based algorithm. The overall procedure is detailed in Section A of the Supplementary Material.

The first stage, Wave Segmenting, focuses on segmenting the ECG waveform into its constituent components. We begin by denoising the ECG signal through successive application of a high‑pass filter, notch filter, and low‑pass filter. A pretrained UNet3+ model [5, 12], trained on SemiSegECG [26] and RDB [19] datasets and frozen during our training, produces a segmentation map $\mathbf{S}\in\{0,1,2,3\}^{T}$ , where labels correspond to background (0), P wave (1), QRS complex (2), and T wave (3). The detailed performance of this segmentation module is reported in Section B of the Supplementary material.

The second stage, Abnormal Seeking, identifies candidate abnormal regions using clinical definitions from the Life in the Fastlane (LITFL) ECG library [16]. Firstly, when P wave is not visible (e.g., in atrial fibrillation), we insert a pseudo P wave between the preceding T wave and the current Q wave, and highlight this region to indicate the absence of a true P wave. Based on the segmented waveform boundaries, clinically relevant segments and intervals are computed, including the PR interval, defined as the interval from P wave onset to QRS onset, as well as the QT interval and the ST segment. For QT interval analysis, we apply Bazett’s correction to ensure clinical validity: $QTc=QT/\sqrt{RR}$ , where $QT$ is the measured interval and $RR$ is the preceding R-R interval. This correction accounts for the dependence of the QT interval on heart rate. Finally, regions exceeding clinically established amplitude and duration thresholds, as defined in Section C of the Supplementary material, are identified and flagged as potential abnormalities.

Figure 3: The Criteria Feature Retrieval (CFR) module retrieves diagnostic criteria by comparing the input diagnosis embedding with a pre-constructed vector database.

The third stage, Criteria Filtering, filters these candidates using the CFR module [24], illustrated in Figure 3. CFR maintains a vector database $\mathcal{D}=\{(\mathbf{d}_{j},c_{j})\}_{j=1}^{|\mathcal{D}|}$ of diagnosis-criteria pairs sourced from the LITFL ECG library [16], where $\mathbf{d}_{j}\in\mathbb{R}^{D}$ is the embedding of diagnosis $j$ and $c_{j}$ is its defining criteria (e.g., “prolonged PR interval $>$ 200ms” for first-degree AV block). Given an input diagnosis with embedding $\mathbf{q}\in\mathbb{R}^{D}$ , the CFR module retrieves the most similar criteria via cosine similarity.

We retain only those flagged regions that match the retrieved criteria. For example, regions corresponding to prolonged PR intervals are preserved for first degree atrioventricular block, while unrelated findings such as ST segment abnormalities are discarded. When no matching criteria can be retrieved, a fallback procedure is applied, which uses the input query itself to identify candidate abnormal regions, as detailed in Section D of the Supplementary material.

The selected regions are then converted into a binary token mask $\mathbf{m}\in\{0,1\}^{L\cdot T}$ , where a value of 1 indicates that the corresponding token lies within a flagged region in $\mathcal{R}$ , and a value of 0 denotes all other positions. When multiple diagnoses are associated with a signal, the final mask is obtained by applying a logical OR operation across the diagnosis-specific masks.

Attention Supervision. From the preceding stages, we obtain a binary mask $\mathbf{m}_{i}\in\{0,1\}^{L\cdot T}$ for sample $i$ , where $m_{i,k}=1$ indicates an abnormal region at timestamp $k$ . However, since the attention token got downsampled after proccessed by ECG encoder, we apply a MaxPool operation with kernel size and stride same as patch size $s$ . We further normalize this mask into a target probability distribution $\mathbf{p}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}$ :

 $\tilde{\mathbf{m}_{i}}=\text{MaxPool}(\mathbf{m}_{i},\text{kernel\_size}=s,\text{stride}=s)\in\mathbb{R}^{N}$ 

(7)

 $\mathbf{p}_{i,n}=\tilde{\mathbf{m}}_{i,n}/\sum_{j=0}^{N-1}\tilde{\mathbf{m}}_{i,j},\quad n=0,\ldots,N-1$ 

(8)

To extract the model’s global attention pattern, we compute the aggregated Attention Rollout across all transformer layers [1]. This technique accounts for the flow of information through residual connections and successive self-attention operations. For a given layer $k\in\{1,\ldots,K\}$ , let $\mathbf{W}^{(k)}\in\mathbb{R}^{(N+1)\times(N+1)}$ denote the attention matrix averaged across all $H$ heads:

 $\mathbf{W}^{(k)}=\frac{1}{H}\sum_{h=1}^{H}\text{softmax}\left(\frac{\mathbf{Q}^{(k,h)}{\mathbf{K}^{(k,h)}}^{\top}}{\sqrt{d_{h}}}\right)$ 

(9)

To incorporate residual connections, the attention weights are augmented with an identity matrix and re-normalize [1]:

 $\tilde{\mathbf{W}}^{(k)}=(\mathbf{W}^{(k)}+\mathbf{I})/2$ 

(10)

The rollout attention matrix $\mathbf{R}^{(k)}$ is computed recursively by multiplying the augmented attention matrices from the current layer with the rollout matrix from the previous layer:

 $\mathbf{R}^{(k)}=\tilde{\mathbf{W}}^{(k)}\mathbf{R}^{(k-1)}$ 

(11)

where $\mathbf{R}^{(0)}=\mathbf{I}$ . The final aggregated attention scores from the CLS token to the $N$ ECG tokens are extracted from the CLS-token row of the final rollout matrix $\mathbf{R}^{(K)}$ , denoted as $\mathbf{A}_{i}^{rollout}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}$ . The attention loss aligns this aggregated distribution with the pseudo-label via KL divergence:

 $\mathcal{L}_{attn}=\mathbb{E}\Bigg[\sum_{i=1}^{B}\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}{\mathbf{p}_{i,n}}\log({\mathbf{p}_{i,n}}/\mathbf{A}_{i,n}^{rollout})\Bigg]$ 

(12)

By utilizing attention rollout, the supervision signal explicitly guides the hierarchical information aggregation process of the entire Transformer, rather than constraining only the final block. This design ensures that intermediate layers progressively learn to filter and route relevant morphological features toward the CLS token, leading to more robust and coherent localization.

To further evaluate the effectiveness of this design, we additionally consider a variant in which attention supervision is applied only to the CLS-token attention scores from the final Transformer layer.

## 3.4 Training Objective

The overall training loss combines all objectives:

 $\mathcal{L}_{total}=\alpha\mathcal{L}_{contrast}+\beta\mathcal{L}_{caption}+\gamma\mathcal{L}_{attn}$ 

(13)

where $\alpha$ , $\beta$ and $\gamma$ control the relative contributions of contrastive learning, captioning, and attention supervision, respectively. For samples where a valid heatmap cannot be constructed by CGAL, attention supervision is omitted, and training proceeds using only $\mathcal{L}_{\text{contrast}}$ and $\mathcal{L}_{\text{caption}}$ .

Through this composite objective, contrastive loss aligns ECG features with textual representations, captioning loss enables accurate generation of clinical descriptions, and attention-guiding loss regularizes the model to focus on medically relevant regions.

## 3.5 Down-stream Evaluation

After pretraining, the ECG encoder can be deployed for a variety of downstream tasks. One direct application is zero‑shot abnormal region localization, achieved by collecting the output attention scores from input signal. CGAL is omitted during inference since the model has already learned to localize relevant regions. In addition, by attaching a linear classification layer on top of the encoder, the model can be further fine‑tuned for classification. Importantly, since only the classifier head is updated while the encoder remains frozen (linear probing), the ability to attend to clinically contributing regions is preserved.

## 4 Experiment

## 4.1 Datasets

We pretrain on MIMIC-IV-ECG [4], comprising over 800,000 12-lead ECG recordings (10-second duration) from approximately 160,000 patients. During preprocessing, we replace NaN/Inf values with zero. CGAL generates attention pseudo-labels without external annotations.

For abnormality localization, we benchmark using the dataset proposed by Jiang et al. [6], which we refer to as $\text{ECGAD}_{\text{data}}$ , consistent with the model’s name $\text{ECGAD}_{\text{model}}$ . The dataset consists of 400 PTB-XL test samples annotated by two cardiologists. Among these, only 190 samples are abnormal and include heatmap annotations. We use these annotated cases for benchmarking.

For classification evaluation, since $\text{ECGAD}_{\text{data}}$ is a subset of PTB-XL [33], we utilize the corresponding labels of these samples for this task. In addition, we evaluate on the full PTB-XL dataset, a clinical 12-lead ECG corpus comprising 21,799 recordings from 18,869 patients, using the predefined train, validation, and test splits. We benchmark on All-level, which includes all 71 diagnostic categories.

## 4.2 Configuration

We use a 1D 12-layer Vision Transformer with 12 attention heads, a hidden width of 768, and a patch size of 50 as the ECG encoder. The text encoder (MedCPT [11]) remains frozen. We flatten the 12-lead ECG signal to ensure the CLS token attends to all tokens. Training uses learning rate $1e-4$ , 20 epochs, batch size 32 on $4\times$ A100 GPUs. We set $\alpha=1$ , $\beta=1$ (following [35]), and $\gamma=0.5$ .

## 4.3 Metrics

Abnormality localization. To evaluate whether the attention scores of IECG accurately highlight relevant regions within each data sample, we frame this as a ranking task where the model should assign higher attention to abnormal tokens. We employ Precision at K (P@K) ( $\uparrow$ ) where K = 50% of the total token count (K=30,000 for $L\times T=60,000$ ), as the large sequence length makes fixed small K values less discriminative. We also report point‑level Area Under the Receiver Operating Characteristic Curve (AUROC) ( $\uparrow$ ), following the protocol in [6].

Classification.: We evaluate classification performance using FMax ( $\uparrow$ ), the maximum F1 score over all thresholds [31], summarizing precision–recall balance. We also report macro-averaged AUROC ( $\uparrow$ ), which measures overall discriminative capability across all classes regardless of class imbalance. For clarity, we denote localization and classification AUROC as AUROC ${}_{\text{loc}}$ and AUROC ${}_{\text{cls}}$ , respectively.

Faithfulness.: We evaluate the faithfulness of the generated attention maps using Insertion ( $\uparrow$ ) and Deletion ( $\downarrow$ ) tests [27]. Specifically, we measure AUROC after progressively inserting or deleting the top- $k$ attended regions, where $k\in\{10\%,20\%,\ldots,100\%\}$ .

## 4.4 Baselines

We compare IECG against a diverse set of baseline methods that cover both abnormality localization and classification.

To ensure a fair comparison, we retrain METS [13], MERL [17], and TolerantECG [24] using the same encoder architecture and training dataset as IECG. For ECG-FM [21] and HeartLang [10], we use the official pretrained checkpoint. In addition, since ST-MEM [23] was not originally trained on the MIMIC-IV-ECG dataset [4], we retrain it to match our configuration.

During evaluation, ST-MEM and ECG-FM do not include a CLS token. Following the design choices in their original implementations, where token representations are aggregated using average pooling before being passed to a classification head. We adopt the same aggregation strategy to derive the corresponding attention scores. In contrast, because METS, MERL, HeartLang, and TolerantECG share the same architecture as our model, their attention scores are extracted using the same procedure as ours.

For XAI4ECG [32] and ECG-XPLAIM [25], which generate attribution maps for interpretability, we retrain the models following their released implementations. However, ECG-XPLAIM is trained in a task-specific supervised setting and is designed for a limited set of classification tasks rather than the full multi-label All-level setting in PTB-XL. As a result, its training protocol is not directly compatible with our classification benchmark; hence classification and faithfulness results are not reported for this model.

In contrast, $\text{ECGAD}_{\text{model}}$ [6] is specifically designed for abnormality localization, and we use the publicly available checkpoint weights. Following [8], the latent representations produced by $\text{ECGAD}_{\text{model}}$ are passed through a ResNet classifier for downstream classification.

In all cases, we directly use the attribution or localization maps produced by each method as attention scores for evaluation.

## 5 Results and Analysis

## 5.1 Localization and Classification Results

Figure 4: Qualitative comparison of abnormality localization across different ECG conditions. For each example, the top panel shows the ECG waveform with ground-truth abnormalities highlighted in blue. The middle and bottom rows present the localization heatmaps produced by ECGAD ${}_{\text{model}}$ and IECG, respectively. Compared to ECGAD ${}_{\text{model}}$ , IECG produces more accurate and temporally consistent activations that better align with clinically relevant abnormalities across diverse conditions.

To evaluate the model’s ability to identify abnormalities, the pretrained IECG is directly applied to $\text{ECGAD}_{\text{data}}$ for inference, and the task is formulated as a binary pointwise classification problem [6]. The ground truth labels are derived from expert annotations, which ensures that the evaluation remains independent of the pseudo labels used during training. To align the predicted attention heatmaps with the ECG input and the corresponding labels, we upsample the heatmaps using interpolation prior to metric computation.

For classification, since the samples in this dataset are derived from PTB-XL, we retrieve the corresponding labels for each sample. We then perform classification by appending a linear classification head to each model and training only this head on the PTB-XL training split, while keeping the backbone parameters frozen. The trained classifiers are subsequently evaluated on the $\text{ECGAD}_{\text{data}}$ samples.

Table 1 summarizes the performance of all methods on both abnormality localization and classification tasks, as well as explanation faithfulness. Overall, both IECG variants achieve the best localization performance across all metrics while obtaining highly competitive classification and faithfulness results, demonstrating its ability to jointly optimize predictive accuracy and clinically meaningful interpretability.

In terms of abnormality localization, both variants of IECG consistently outperform all baseline methods by a clear margin across P@50% and AUROC ${}_{\text{loc}}$ . In particular, IECG-Rollout achieves the best AUROC ${}_{\text{loc}}$ of 75.55%, exceeding $\text{ECGAD}_{\text{model}}$ (71.62%), which is specifically designed for localization, by a notable margin. This result highlights the effectiveness of incorporating CGAL during training, enabling the model to focus on diagnostically relevant waveform regions without requiring manual annotations. The improvement in P@50% further indicates that IECG ranks abnormalities more accurately, which is important for clinical interpretability.

In contrast, foundation models such as METS, MERL, and ECG-FM exhibit localization performance that is closer to random, with AUROC ${}_{\text{loc}}$ ranging from 50% to 61%. This suggests that optimizing solely for classification does not guarantee meaningful attention behavior. Although explainability-focused methods such as XAI4ECG and ECG-XPLAIM provide moderate improvements over some base models, their performance remains substantially below that of IECG, indicating that post hoc explanation methods may lack precise alignment with clinically relevant regions.

As for classification, IECG-Last achieves the highest FMax of 42.41%, while TolerantECG attains the highest AUROC ${}_{\text{cls}}$ of 90.26%, with IECG-Last being highly competitive at 90.03%. This marginal gap suggests that introducing attention supervision does not significantly compromise classification performance. Instead, IECG preserves strong discriminative capability while substantially improving interpretability. In terms of explanation faithfulness, IECG-Rollout attains the highest Insertion score of 45.86%, confirming that the highlighted regions are indeed crucial for the model’s predictions, though some baselines report lower Deletion scores.

Overall, these results demonstrate that explicitly guiding attention using clinically informed pseudo labels produced by CGAL allows IECG to achieve a strong balance between localization, classification, and faithfulness. The proposed approach not only maintains state-of-the-art classification performance but also significantly improves the reliability and interpretability of the model’s predictions.

## 7 Conclusion

We propose IECG, an ECG foundation model that achieves strong diagnostic performance while enhancing clinically meaningful interpretability through abnormality localization, all without requiring manual attention annotations. IECG is trained using a weakly-supervised mechanism that derives abnormality heatmap pseudo-labels from clinical heuristics. This approach guides the model’s attention toward diagnostically relevant regions, effectively mitigating the localized annotation bottleneck in ECG data. By aggregating information from these clinically guided regions, IECG improves the grounding of its predictions in relevant waveform features. Experiments demonstrate that IECG delivers state-of-the-art performance on abnormality localization while maintaining robust classification capabilities.

## Limitations

First, CGAL’s pseudo-label generation relies on predefined clinical rules and the CFR database. It may miss rare conditions or abnormalities tied to patient-specific factors (e.g., age, gender), though incorporating free-form reports partially mitigates this.

Second, while IECG enhances interpretability, it is not inherently fully interpretable. Its single global attention map highlights the union of all abnormal regions, preventing it from attributing specific waveform segments to individual diagnoses in multi-diagnosis ECGs.

Future work will incorporate broader clinical guidelines to improve pseudo-label specificity and explore explicit mappings between localized regions and individual diagnoses to enable fine-grained, diagnosis-specific localization.

## Appendix A CGAL Pseudo-Label Generation algorithm

Algorithm 1 outlines the procedure for curating pseudo‑heatmap labels that allocate diagnostic regions. The design ensures that pseudo‑labels are both clinically grounded and diagnosis‑specific. Before identifying abnormal regions, Stage 1 employs a pretrained UNet3+ [5, 12] to segment the ECG signal into P wave, QRS complex, and T wave components. Stage 2 identifies candidate abnormal regions based on waveform segmentation and interval analysis, leveraging AddPseudoPwave (Algorithm 2) to heuristically insert missing P‑wave segments and ComputeIntervals (Algorithm 3) to derive clinically relevant intervals including PR interval, QT interval, and ST segmenet. Stage 3 refines these candidate regions by intersecting them with diagnostic criteria retrieved from CFR, while also applying fallback rules (discussed in Section D) to enhance robustness against database incompleteness. As a result, the generated binary attention masks provide reliable supervision signals that guide the encoder to focus on clinically meaningful regions, thereby improving anomaly localization and interpretability in downstream tasks.

## Appendix D CFR quality and fallback

The CFR component is designed to retrieve clinically relevant diagnostic criteria. To evaluate its performance, we collect all diagnostic classes from the MIMIC-IV-ECG dataset [4] and use them as query inputs to measure the acceptance rate. To mitigate these limitations, we adopt a fallback strategy: when diagnostic features cannot be retrieved, the diagnosis name itself is used to guide abnormal heatmap filtering. Specifically, if a query explicitly mentions a wave component (e.g., “PR interval,” “T‑waves”), we retain the corresponding highlights from Stage 2 of CGAL. For broader diagnostic terms such as “ischaemia,” “ischemic,” or “infarction,” we emphasize the ST segment and T wave, while terms like “atrial” direct attention to the P wave. In addition, ECG lead group phrases (e.g., anterior, septal, inferior) are leveraged to ensure that abnormal heatmap labels highlight the correct anatomical regions. This strategy helps reduces false‑negative annotations and produces more accurate pseudo‑labels for attention supervision.

## Appendix G Limitations of Ground-Truth Abnormal Annotations

Figure S5: Example illustrating annotation incompleteness in $\text{ECGAD}_{\text{data}}$ using Lead II. The ECG exhibits complete left bundle branch block (LBBB) and first-degree atrioventricular (AV) block, as indicated by prolonged QRS duration (e.g., 172–176 ms) and prolonged PR interval (e.g., 204–224 ms), respectively. The ground-truth (GT) heatmap highlights regions corresponding to widened QRS complexes associated with LBBB but fails to capture the prolonged PR intervals that define first-degree AV block. In contrast, IECG accurately highlights both QRS and PR regions, reflecting clinically relevant abnormalities across both morphological (ventricular depolarization) and temporal (atrioventricular conduction delay) features. This example demonstrates that missing annotations of temporal abnormalities can lead to underestimation of localization performance.

Although IECG achieves strong localization performance, the overall AUROC ${}_{\text{loc}}$ could be constrained by inaccuracies and incompleteness in the ground-truth annotations of $\text{ECGAD}_{\text{data}}$ . In particular, the provided abnormal region labels do not always capture all clinically relevant waveform features, especially for conditions characterized by temporal abnormalities rather than distinct morphological changes.

A representative example is shown in Figure S5, where the ECG exhibits complete left bundle branch block (LBBB) and first-degree atrioventricular (AV) block. The annotations correctly highlight the prolonged QRS complexes associated with LBBB, which is defined by a widened QRS duration ( $\geq 120$ ms) reflecting delayed ventricular depolarization [15]. However, they fail to capture the prolonged PR interval ( $>200$ ms) that characterizes first-degree AV block, which corresponds to delayed conduction between atrial and ventricular activation [14, 2].

Clinically, these abnormalities manifest in distinct regions of the waveform: LBBB primarily affects the QRS complex, while first-degree AV block is defined by prolongation of the PR interval preceding the QRS. As a result, the ground-truth heatmaps, which focus on QRS-based abnormalities, omit diagnostically important temporal features in the PR segment.

In contrast, IECG highlights both the widened QRS complexes and the prolonged PR intervals, producing attention maps that more closely reflect the full set of clinically relevant abnormalities. Such discrepancies indicate that the ground-truth annotations do not fully capture all relevant diagnostic evidence, causing the model to be penalized for correctly identifying waveform regions that are not labeled. This limitation partially explains the gap between the strong qualitative alignment of IECG and its quantitative evaluation scores.

## Appendix K Trade-off Between Interpretability and Downstream Performance

A central challenge in developing medical AI systems is balancing raw predictive performance with clinical interpretability. Across the experiments detailed in the main text and this supplementary material, we consistently observe a highly favorable trade-off when incorporating CGAL.

Impact on Classification: As demonstrated in the main text’s ablation studies, training IECG without attention supervision ( $\gamma=0$ ) maximizes classification metrics, achieving an AUROC ${}_{\text{cls}}$ of 92.42% on PTB-XL and 90.87% on ECGAD. However, this comes at the severe cost of interpretability, with localization performance dropping to 61.33%. By introducing CGAL with $\gamma=0.5$ , the model gains a massive +14.22% absolute improvement in localization (AUROC ${}_{\text{loc}}$ of 75.55%). This structural regularization imposes only a marginal penalty on diagnostic power: AUROC ${}_{\text{cls}}$ remains highly competitive at 92.21% on PTB-XL and 89.09% on ECGAD. Furthermore, the robust generalization of this balanced representation is confirmed on external benchmarks, where IECG maintains second-best overall performance on both the CPSC (96.32%) and Chapman (95.32%) datasets.

Impact on Report Generation: A similar regularizing effect is observed in complex generative tasks. As shown in the previous section on Report Generation, removing CGAL yields slightly higher exact text-matching scores (e.g., BLEU-4 of 12.57% vs. 12.14%). Nonetheless, the default IECG retains near-identical semantic coherence (BERT-score of 89.20% vs. 89.25%).

Architectural Contributions to the Trade-off: This favorable balance is fundamentally enabled by our specific architectural design choices, namely Attention Rollout and Spatial-Temporal (ST) embedding. As demonstrated in the main text’s experiments, applying attention supervision solely to the last attention layer yields a slightly higher classification AUROC ${}_{\text{cls}}$ on ECGAD (90.03%) compared to Attention Rollout (89.09%), but results in reduced localization accuracy (73.94% vs. 75.55%). Similarly, as detailed in Section E, removing the ST embedding marginally improves classification (90.31% on ECGAD) but degrades localization (75.01% vs. 75.55%). Both Attention Rollout and ST embedding introduce structural priors that regularize the network, intentionally trading a small fraction of raw predictive capacity to achieve more precise and clinically interpretable spatial-temporal grounding.

Conclusion: These results indicate that explicitly constraining the model’s attention to align with clinical heuristics inherently acts as an anatomical and temporal regularizer. While this regularization slightly bounds the unconstrained optimization of downstream objectives, the resulting trade-off is highly advantageous. IECG secures state-of-the-art or highly competitive predictive performance across diverse tasks while guaranteeing that its decisions are grounded in clinically valid waveform evidence, which is a critical prerequisite for clinical trust and real-world medical deployment.

## Appendix L Ethical considerations

This work uses publicly available datasets (MIMIC-IV-ECG, PTB-XL, ECGAD, CPSC and Chapman) that have undergone institutional de-identification procedures. No personally identifiable information was accessed. IECG is intended to support clinical decision-making and is not a substitute for professional medical judgment. All predictions and recommendations must be reviewed and validated by qualified healthcare professionals before any clinical action is taken.

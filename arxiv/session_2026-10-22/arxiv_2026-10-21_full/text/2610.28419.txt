## DISTRACT: Degradation-Indexed Study of Textual Reference Across Competing Targets

Dan Zlotnikov

Affiliation: Ben-Gurion University of the Negev, Israel

Ohad Ben-Shahar

Affiliation: danzlot@post.bgu.ac.il  ben-shahar@cs.bgu.ac.il

Abstract

Vision-language trackers (VLTs) accept descriptions intended to assist tracking, but accepting text does not establish that its content changes tracking decisions, particularly as visual evidence deteriorates. We introduce DISTRACT, a matched-intervention benchmark that holds video, target, and distractor fixed while crossing four text priors with four image degradations along a controlled severity axis. We evaluate five VLTs on four datasets using behavioral contrasts, layerwise representation probes, and activation patching.

Molmo2 and JointNLT exhibit directed text use. Some effects contract under degradation, whereas others show no significant endpoint change after multiplicity correction. UVLTrack, MMTrack, and DUTrack respond only weakly to changes in text semantics, with no directed behavioral effect surviving FDR correction in the tested settings. Model-internal analyses reveal that at decision-near states, correct-versus-conflicting text is decodable in Molmo2 and JointNLT but not in the other three systems. In the responsive models, decodability peaks at late states where replacement produces little behavioral change, whereas replacing selected earlier states redirects conflict-associated trajectories toward the target. Because matched-correct donors do not significantly outperform unrelated donors, these interventions identify earlier intervention-sensitive states without establishing target-specific semantic rescue. DISTRACT thus exposes when and where text affects tracking under degraded visual evidence.

## 1 Introduction

Visual object tracking must preserve an object’s identity as appearance,
position, and context change over time. Vision–language trackers (VLTs) add a
natural-language specification—for example, “the player in white moving
toward the basket”—to distinguish a target from a similar competitor
semantically [23, 37]. This capability is most
valuable outside clean laboratory video: robots, autonomous cameras, and
other perception–action systems encounter image degradations under which even
subtle perturbations can cause severe perception failures
[29, 26, 15].

Accuracy with a target-valid description, however, does not establish that a
tracker uses language’s semantic content to select the target over an
on-screen competitor. Prior robustness studies show that degraded visibility
harms trackers and VLMs [27, 13, 35, 31],
while VLT studies show that description content and delivery can alter
performance [18, 21, 1].
They generally vary visual quality and semantic validity separately, leaving
open whether language rescues weak vision, misdirects selection toward a
matching distractor, or is behaviorally redundant on the same clip.

We evaluate Molmo2 [3], JointNLT [41],
UVLTrack [24], MMTrack [40], and
DUTrack [22] on four diverse datasets, crossing four language priors with progressive visual
degradation (Fig. 1). UVLTrack, MMTrack, and DUTrack show only
small, inconsistent text-conditioned differences. Molmo2 and
JointNLT respond directionally on clean video, but their severity trajectories
are heterogeneous: some supported effects attenuate toward the null,
whereas for others—including the single harmful clean-input effect—we detect no
FDR-significant endpoint change.

Descriptions: target, “girl in red sitting on the right side of the table”; distractor, “The can next to the cake”.

However, behavior alone cannot reveal whether a description never reaches a tracking
pathway, remains represented but does not affect localization, or loses its
link to the spatial decision under degradation. We therefore complement the
benchmark with layerwise, source-video-grouped probes across all five systems
and targeted activation patching in the two responsive systems.
This distinguishes linearly recoverable text-condition information from stages
at which controlled state replacement changes the trajectory,
while donor-content controls test whether that redirection is target-specific.

Our contributions are:

• 

A matched four-condition, multi-severity VLT protocol spanning four perturbations, five systems, and four datasets, while preserving models’ supported point- or box-tracking interfaces.

• 

A five-model degradation study showing that text effects impact VLTs in meaningfully different ways, depending on the model, dataset, and perturbation.

• 

Layerwise probing and controlled state-replacement analyses that trace text-condition signals across all five systems and identify intervention-sensitive stages in Molmo2 and JointNLT.

## 2 Related Work

Evaluating language use in tracking.

Language-annotated benchmarks (OTB99-Lang [23], LaSOT [7], TNL2K [37], MGIT [14], WebUAV-3M [38]) and VLT methods (SNLT [8], ModaMixer [9], JointNLT [41], MMTrack [40], UVLTrack [24], DUTrack [22]) are evaluated by trajectory accuracy under valid descriptions – accuracy given truthful language, not whether counterfactually changing that language changes target selection. Nor do the language diagnostics we are aware of isolate visibility: DTLLM-VLT and DTVLT vary detail and update frequency while keeping text target-consistent [18, 19]; VLTVerse crosses multiple text variants with naturally occurring sequence-level factors, rather than applying controlled degradation [21]; VLT-MI changes text and the box together [20]; and CLDTracker’s substitutions are neither tied to an on-screen competitor nor tested across severity [1]. Our protocol addresses both gaps by independently varying text relation and severity on the same clip with a designated distractor.

Robustness under degraded visual evidence.

Common-corruption evaluation applies graded transformations to a fixed source [11], but tracking benchmarks instead use naturally difficult sequences (LaSOT [7], TNL2K [37], AVisT [27], adverse-weather person tracking [15]) that confound visibility with target, motion, and scene difficulty. DBVOT evaluates six progressive defocus levels [13], while adversarial-blur work synthesizes motion-blur attacks against vision-only trackers [10], and VLM corruption suites retain a valid prompt while scoring one-shot answers [35, 31] – neither directly measures changing text authority or temporal identity errors. Our graded blur, noise, pixelation, and compression experiments test whether language guides tracking as visual evidence weakens.

Cross-modal conflict and behavioral consequence.

CLASH and CrossCheck-Bench ask models to recognize controlled image–text contradictions, not to reveal their unprompted behavioral consequence in an ongoing spatial task [30, 34], and SIGNPOST-Bench shows that embedded scene text can bias still-image geolocation, but does not study a graded visual-degradation axis [17]. By holding scene content fixed, identifying the competing object, and measuring the direction and duration of tracking errors, our benchmark tests whether language remains actionable under imperfect sensing.

Mechanistic interpretability of grounding models.

Causal tracing and activation patching have probed how general vision–language models use visual information, including BLIP [28] and object hallucination in large VLMs [16], but not, to our knowledge, vision–language tracking, where a spatial decision is emitted every frame rather than a single caption. We apply the same causal-tracing toolkit to five VLT systems to test whether text condition is decodable at decision-near representations and whether controlled interventions at selected text-reachable states alter localization.

## 3 Benchmark and Protocol

## 3.1 Datasets and pair construction

We use source videos from MeViS [5], a referring video
object segmentation benchmark, and from the multi-object tracking benchmarks
DanceTrack [33], SportsMOT [4], and
PersonPath22 [32] (Table 1). MeViS
provides its native referring expressions and masks. For the three MOT
sources, human-written expressions and released point/mask trajectories come
from the public Molmo2-VideoTrackEval annotation release [3];
the original MOT datasets provide the source videos. We derive initialization
boxes from the released masks and enumerate ordered target–distractor pairs.
A pair is eligible only if the target $T$ and distractor $D$ are co-visible in at least five frames
and have mean co-visible mask IoU below 0.3. Every selected pair
receives the same text conditions and perturbation protocol.

The MeViS selector takes one eligible pair per source video, while for the
other three datasets, we construct outcome-enriched diagnostic stress
subsets. Molmo2’s clean conflicting-text run identifies cases for inclusion,
with seeded random filling of the remaining quota, yielding 16/60 DanceTrack,
2/30 SportsMOT, and 6/30 PersonPath22 persistent-takeover cases in the final
subsets.
These subsets let us track how conflict-relevant cases evolve under
degradation. Because pairs are
partly selected on an outcome related to what we measure, naive effect
estimates on this set risk running high; a supplementary
inverse-probability-weighted check estimates the corresponding effects over the full eligible candidate pool; effect directions are preserved, although some naïve stress-subset estimates—particularly $C_{T}$ —are larger. A second, more direct check in the supplementary reruns all four specialized trackers on the complete, outcome-independent eligible pool, reproducing the qualitative separation between JointNLT and the three small-effect specialized trackers.

## 3.2 Targets and distractors

Let a video contain an ordered pair of distinct annotated objects $(T,D)$ , where
 $T$ is the object initialized for tracking and $D$ is a co-visible distractor.
At evaluated frame $t$ , their annotated spatial supports are
 $\mathcal{M}_{T,t}$ and $\mathcal{M}_{D,t}$ . Each pair has a human-written
expression $e_{T}$ referring to $T$ and an expression $e_{D}$ referring to $D$ .

## 3.3 Text conditions

We vary the text input independently of the video through four conditions:

• 

Neutral ( $c_{\mathrm{neutral}}$ ): a generic tracking
instruction with no target-identifying description.

• 

Unrelated ( $c_{\mathrm{unrelated}}$ ): a real expression
 $e_{U}$ from another clip, that is not annotated as referring to either $T$ nor $D$ .

• 

Correct ( $c_{\mathrm{correct}}$ ): the target expression
 $e_{T}$ .

• 

Conflicting ( $c_{\mathrm{conflicting}}$ ): the distractor
expression $e_{D}$ .

The unrelated expression is sampled once from a different annotated clip with
the same category label as the pair, then fixed across every model, condition,
and severity.

## 3.4 Model initialization, inputs, and outputs

Every run is anchored to the same clean annotated target instance and uses
the same fixed post-initialization frames for every model, text condition,
and perturbation. Molmo2 is initialized with a
target point and predicts points; UVLTrack, MMTrack, JointNLT, and DUTrack are
initialized with a tight axis-aligned box and predict boxes, evaluating which
object is selected rather than boundary precision. Points/boxes are derived
from mask centroids/extents when masks are available, and otherwise from the
corresponding released box trajectory. We additionally report native-box and
absolute-performance metrics in the supplementary.

Molmo2 receives text through the scaffold “Track the object indicated by the
point,” with descriptive conditions appending “The target is: $e$ .”
UVLTrack, MMTrack, and JointNLT receive the raw expression $e$ directly, with
the generic token object as their neutral-condition input. DUTrack
shares this interface but normally regenerates a description from the current
frame, overwriting the assigned condition; we use static-external
mode and reset all adapter state between pair–condition–severity runs. A
supplementary dynamic-mode audit verifies this condition-preservation control.

## 3.5 Perturbations and fixed severity schedule

Each corruption is applied independently to every post-initialization frame;
the initialization frame remains clean and corruptions are never composed. We use Gaussian blur, additive Gaussian noise, pixelation, and JPEG
compression—common corruption families with fixed, ordered severity ladders
in robustness benchmarks [11]. Table 2
gives our disclosed, perturbation-specific parameter schedules; these were
chosen to produce visibly distinct changes at the native video resolution.
 $S0$ is an exact no-op. Severity is an ordinal dose axis within each
perturbation, not a claim of perceptual equivalence across perturbation types;
the supplementary reports descriptive PSNR/SSIM diagnostics. For noise, a
video–frame hash fixes one standard-normal field shared across conditions and
severities, so contrasts never compare different noise realizations.

## 3.6 Outcomes and metrics

Each post-initialization evaluated sample receives exactly one of the following labels:

• 

absent (no usable predicted point at that timestamp).

• 

target ( $\hat{p}_{t}\in\mathcal{M}_{T,t}$ ).

• 

distractor ( $\hat{p}_{t}\notin\mathcal{M}_{T,t}$ and $\hat{p}_{t}\in\mathcal{M}_{D,t}$ ).

• 

other.

Target takes precedence
when masks overlap, and absent samples remain in the denominator of all
frame-level rates.

For pair $p$ , condition $c$ , and severity $s$ , let
 $A_{T}^{p}(c,s)$ , $A_{D}^{p}(c,s)$ , $A_{O}^{p}(c,s)$ , and
 $A_{\emptyset}^{p}(c,s)$ be the fractions of post-initialization samples classified as
target, distractor, other, and absent. They sum to one. We aggregate frames
within a pair before aggregating pairs, preventing longer tracks from receiving
greater weight. In addition to condition-specific rates, the primary
contrastive semantic effects anchor every comparison at the correct
condition – the observed target accuracy when the model receives the ground-truth referring expression
– and measure the gap to each of the
other three conditions:

 $\displaystyle B_{T}(s)$ 
 $\displaystyle=A_{T}(\mathrm{correct},s)-A_{T}(\mathrm{unrelated},s),$ 

(1)

 $\displaystyle N_{T}(s)$ 
 $\displaystyle=A_{T}(\mathrm{correct},s)-A_{T}(\mathrm{neutral},s),$ 

(2)

 $\displaystyle C_{T}(s)$ 
 $\displaystyle=A_{T}(\mathrm{correct},s)-A_{T}(\mathrm{conflicting},s).$ 

(3)

 $B_{T}$ is the correct-versus-unrelated effect (a same-type but unrelated
description), $N_{T}$ is the correct-versus-neutral effect (no
target-identifying description), and $C_{T}$ is the correct-versus-conflicting
effect; each is signed and can be
negative. Two further effects, the directed distractor-capture effect $S_{D}$ 
and the conflict-specific target-loss effect $L_{T}$ , are defined in the
supplementary alongside the full endpoint test family.

Severity-integrated effect magnitude.

To summarize the size of a
condition effect across the severity ramp, we use integrated absolute
deviation (IAD). Severities are indexed $s\in\{0,\dots,5\}$ (
Table 2); for conditions $c_{1},c_{2}$ , let
 $\Delta_{c_{1},c_{2}}(s)=A_{T}(c_{1},s)-A_{T}(c_{2},s)$ and define

 $\mathrm{IAD}(c_{1},c_{2})=\sum_{s=0}^{4}\tfrac{1}{2}\big[|\Delta_{c_{1},c_{2}}(s)|+|\Delta_{c_{1},c_{2}}(s{+}1)|\big].$ 

(4)

Taking the absolute value before integrating preserves effects that
change direction over severity. IAD is nonnegative and symmetric in its two
conditions; with six severity levels, $\mathrm{IAD}/5$ is the mean absolute
condition gap per severity step. We report all $\binom{4}{2}=6$ unordered
condition contrasts as the upper triangle of a $4\times 4$ matrix per model
and dataset. Per-severity signed contrasts ( $B_{T}$ , $N_{T}$ , and $C_{T}$ ) remain
the primary directional measurements and are used for endpoint tests.

Behavioral inference.

We compute every contrast within pair and
use source video as the resampling unit, keeping all selected pairs from one
video together. We report 95% source-video-clustered bootstrap CIs and use
paired source-video sign-flip tests for clean effects and severity-endpoint
changes. Benjamini–Hochberg correction is applied within each stated
hypothesis family; IAD is descriptive rather than inferential. The
supplementary specifies the resampling algorithm, test statistics,
hypothesis families, and formulas in full.

## 3.7 Model-internal and mechanistic measurements

For internal analyses, we save compact pooled states at model-specific
functional stages—text encoding/projection, early and late multimodal
fusion, and the decision-near localization state—rather than treating raw
layer indices as comparable across architectures. Exact hooked tensors,
layer counts, and semantics per model are listed in the supplementary.

At a matched video frame, let $h_{l}^{c}$ be a pooled state at stage $l$ under
text condition $c$ . Let $m_{t}$ be the target–distractor output margin at
evaluated frame $t$ ,

 $m_{t}=\mathrm{IoU}(\hat{b}_{t},b_{T,t})-\mathrm{IoU}(\hat{b}_{t},b_{D,t}),$ 

(5)

with the analogous target/distractor label used for point output. We use
three procedures to characterize the text pathway before defining the
metrics computed from them.

Text-condition probe.

Following low-capacity probing practice
[12, 2], at every saved stage we
standardize the pooled state using statistics fit only on the training fold,
project it onto a training-fold SVD basis (at most 20 components), and fit a
ridge-regularized linear classifier for correct-versus-conflicting condition.
Cross-validation holds out complete source videos, so the probe cannot exploit
sentence- or token-identity shortcuts shared within a video; group-preserving
label permutations provide its null distribution. We report the probe’s
held-out balanced accuracy as Text BA at stage $l$ ( $0.5$ is chance).
Within conflicting runs, we repeat the same procedure for the observed
target-versus-distractor outcome. This establishes whether the prompt
distinction is linearly present in a state, not whether the tracker acts on it;
causal relevance is tested with the interventions below [6].

Activation patching.

We intervene on the forward pass directly, following intervention-based interpretability, causal-tracing, and activation-patching
work [36, 25, 39]. For a fixed video,
template, and evaluated-frame sequence, we run the network under a
target condition (typically conflicting) but replace its unpooled
native state at the identified text boundary with the corresponding state
from an independently run source condition
( $\mathrm{source}\in\{\mathrm{correct},\mathrm{neutral},\mathrm{unrelated},\mathrm{zero}\}$ )
on the same visual input,

 $h_{l}^{\mathrm{target}}\leftarrow h_{l}^{\mathrm{source}},$ 

(6)

before continuing the forward pass and subsequent sequential tracking
unmodified. Every patch site is validated with two controls: an exact
self-patch ( $\mathrm{source}=\mathrm{target}$ ), required to reproduce the
unpatched trajectory exactly, and an unpatched repeat, which confirms that
reset and replay are themselves deterministic.

For each activation-patching intervention, we report the paired change in trajectory-mean $m_{t}$ and the count of
target–distractor switches, with source-video bootstrap CIs and paired
sign-flip tests; these controls establish patch integrity and deterministic replay.
They support interpreting differences as consequences of the intervention,
but do not by themselves establish target-specific semantic mediation;
that stronger interpretation requires donor-content controls.

Full-depth layer sweeps.

Rather than committing in advance to one
decision-near stage, we apply the text-condition probe at every saved
functional stage for every model, (for Molmo2, at all 37 pre-coordinate
decoder depths). This traces the depth at which the
correct/conflicting distinction becomes linearly decodable, from text
encoding through fusion to the localization output, reducing the risk that a null result at one selected stage is incorrectly generalized to the model’s entire saved pathway.

## 4.1 UVLTrack, MMTrack, and DUTrack show limited behavioral sensitivity to text semantics

Fig. 3 plots each system’s severity-integrated
absolute target-accuracy effect as a $4\times 4$ grid over the four text
conditions, one grid per model on a shared color scale, where larger,
warmer cells mark condition pairs whose accuracy diverges more across the
full clean-to-severe range as text effects differ. Two grids show consistently larger effects;
the remaining three stay pale in every cell. This qualitative
split holds numerically: the three less-responsive systems’ largest target-accuracy IAD
cells are $0.05$ (DUTrack), $0.09$ (MMTrack), and $0.10$ (UVLTrack),
versus JointNLT’s mean $0.24$ (maximum $0.35$ ) and Molmo2’s mean $0.27$ 
(maximum $0.47$ ), both peaking at the correct–conflicting contrast.
These observed separations remain small after taking absolute values before integration,
so their small IAD values are not caused by cancellation between opposite-signed severities.
The corresponding distractor-capture IAD is also small
( $0.02$ / $0.05$ / $0.08$ for DUTrack/MMTrack/UVLTrack vs. $0.29$ / $0.37$ for
JointNLT/Molmo2), again largest at the correct–conflicting contrast,
consistent with conflict-directed capture rather than a uniform
output-quality change.

Thus, we detect no FDR-supported directed semantic-text effect in these three systems, without implying absent internal language processing yet. Their 62–68% correct-text $S_{0}$ target accuracy argues against a simple floor/ceiling explanation and leaves room for change in either direction.

## 4.2 Directed language effects degrade unevenly, including harmful effects

The deployment-relevant question for the two responsive systems is not
merely whether accuracy falls with degradation, but whether a
CI-supported clean-input text effect reliably weakens as visual evidence degrades. We
test every effect with a CI-supported $S_{0}$ signal (95% CI excludes zero) for
FDR-significant contraction toward zero across all four main perturbations:
14 target-accuracy cases and 4 distractor-capture cases, spanning both
responsive systems and all four datasets (Table 4).

Of the 14 target-accuracy effects with a CI-supported clean-input value, four show FDR-significant contraction under at least one perturbation, including one that contracts under all four; the remaining ten show no FDR-significant endpoint change under any perturbation. None of Molmo2’s five cases yields a significant
contraction after correction. These nonsignificant change tests should not
be interpreted as evidence that the effects remain unchanged.

One of the ten cases is JointNLT’s SportsMOT $B_{T}$ , the study’s only
significantly harmful clean-input effect ( $-11.2\%$ , 95% CI excluding zero). Under the primary point-in-support metric, its estimated
S5 changes substantially reduce or reverse the clean effect, but these
changes do not survive FDR correction.

The distractor-capture effect $S_{D}$ exhibits a similar inferential
pattern. Of four model–dataset combinations with a CI-supported
clean-input misdirection effect, JointNLT/MeViS shows significant
contraction under three of four perturbations. For Molmo2/PersonPath22,
the largest clean capture effect in the study ( $+11.3$ pp), no endpoint
change survives correction; nevertheless, the point estimates contract
almost completely under blur, noise, and pixelation and remain more
clearly positive under compression.

Thus, contraction is established in some settings, while the remaining changes are statistically inconclusive rather than evidence of persistence. Their behavior varies across models, datasets, effects, metrics, and perturbations.

## 4.3 Model internals

The behavioral results identify FDR-supported directed text effects for JointNLT and Molmo2, in contrast to the demonstrably smaller effects observed for MMTrack, DUTrack, and UVLTrack. They do not, however, localize where the supported effects arise. We probe this with the
internal-mechanism captures of
Sec. 3.7, restricted to MeViS: its pairs are the only
ones with one independent source video each (Table 1); its mask-native annotations pool a hooked
state over exactly the target/distractor’s tokens, not a
background-containing box; and its native task is itself referring
expression segmentation.

Of the 120 one-pair-per-video MeViS entries, 115 have at least two co-visible
target/distractor frames. JointNLT’s
causal study further uses an outcome-enriched
90-video subset (23 persistent captures, 67 retention controls) so the
rarer capture outcome is well represented.

This onset is consistent with its token order: image tokens precede the appended description, so condition information can emerge only after the description is processed. Thus, both systems carry decodable text to decision-near states through distinct pathways; intervention effects are tested next.

## 4.4 Limitations

DISTRACT uses controlled comparative evidence across five systems, four
datasets, four perturbations, and fixed $S_{0}$ – $S_{5}$ schedules; native
interfaces make cross-system differences whole-system rather than
architectural, and three datasets are stratified toward conflict-relevant
pairs, so raw error frequencies are not prevalence estimates. Controlled
perturbations and four priors isolate image quality and directed
target–distractor semantics, respectively, but not natural adverse conditions
or relational, temporal, multi-turn, and pragmatic language. They also do not
reproduce the coupled weather, scene, and sensor shifts of deployment.
Model-internal clean sweeps use 115 MeViS videos, and degraded internals are observational and
JointNLT-only; state replacement localizes sensitivity, not target-specific
semantic mediation. Broader models, datasets, and held-out causal tests are
therefore needed to further validate these claims.

## 5 Discussion and Future Work

DISTRACT treats text as an experimental variable, testing whether counterfactual descriptions redirect tracking decisions and how these effects change under degraded vision. Three of five systems show limited sensitivity to text semantics and no directed behavioral effect surviving FDR correction, demonstrating that correct-text accuracy alone does not establish discriminative language use. Molmo2 and JointNLT show supported directed effects, but their severity trajectories vary across settings: some contract significantly, while others remain inconclusive after correction.

Internal analyses localize this distinction. Text condition is decodable in four of five systems, showing that decodability alone is insufficient evidence of behavioral use. In the responsive systems, replacement at selected earlier sites produces larger changes than at peak-decoding states, but correct donors do not outperform unrelated donors, identifying sensitive stages without establishing target-specific semantic mediation. Neither architecture nor clean-input responsiveness consistently anticipates degradation behavior across the tested panel; robustness and correct language use should therefore be evaluated separately.

Future work.

Our internal analyses are restricted to MeViS and, under degradation, to JointNLT (Sec. 4.4). Extending them across datasets, models, and newer VLT architectures could reveal whether architectural properties explain the responsive/non-responsive split. Additional datasets and relational, temporal, and multi-turn text conditions are natural extensions of DISTRACT.

 $\displaystyle x_{\min}$ 
 $\displaystyle=\min_{(x,y)\in\mathcal{M}}x,$ 
 $\displaystyle y_{\min}$ 
 $\displaystyle=\min_{(x,y)\in\mathcal{M}}y,$ 

 $\displaystyle x_{\max}$ 
 $\displaystyle=\max_{(x,y)\in\mathcal{M}}x,$ 
 $\displaystyle y_{\max}$ 
 $\displaystyle=\max_{(x,y)\in\mathcal{M}}y,$ 

 $b=\big(x_{\min},\;y_{\min},\;x_{\max}-x_{\min},\;y_{\max}-y_{\min}\big).$ 

Tracker boxes are reduced to their centers
 $\hat{p}=(x+w/2,y+h/2)$ for common scoring; Molmo2 supplies its point directly.

## A.7 Outcome definitions and behavioral inference

For every post-initialization frame, a usable prediction is target,
distractor, or other according to the common point-in-support rule above;
missing, nonfinite, or invalid predictions are absent. Target takes precedence
when supports overlap, and absent frames remain in every denominator. For each
pair, condition, and displayed severity, frame fractions are aggregated within
pair before pairs are aggregated. In addition to $B_{T},N_{T},C_{T}$ in the main
paper, the endpoint family contains

 $\displaystyle L_{T}(s)$ 
 $\displaystyle=A_{T}(\mathrm{unrelated},s)-A_{T}(\mathrm{conflicting},s),$ 

(1)

 $\displaystyle S_{D}(s)$ 
 $\displaystyle=A_{D}(\mathrm{conflicting},s)-A_{D}(\mathrm{unrelated},s).$ 

(2)

Persistent takeover and recovery require three consecutive distractor and
subsequent target labels, respectively. For a fixed model, dataset,
perturbation, and displayed severity $s\in\{0,\ldots,5\}$ , let $d_{p,s}$ 
denote a paired effect for pair $p$ ; for example,
 $d_{p,s}=A_{T}^{p}(\mathrm{correct},s)-A_{T}^{p}(\mathrm{unrelated},s)$ for
 $B_{T}$ . The reported point estimate is the pair-weighted mean

 $\hat{\theta}_{s}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{p=1}^{N}d_{p,s}.$ 

(3)

Pairs from the same source video are not treated as independent. Let
 $g(p)\in\mathcal{V}$ assign pair $p$ to its source video, and let
 $S_{v}=\sum_{p:g(p)=v}d_{p,s}$ and $n_{v}=\sum_{p}\mathbf{1}[g(p)=v]$ .

For each of $R=10{,}000$ bootstrap replicates, we draw $|\mathcal{V}|$ 
videos with replacement, $Z_{1}^{(r)},\ldots,Z_{|\mathcal{V}|}^{(r)}$ , and
retain every selected pair. The replicate estimate is

 $\hat{\theta}_{s}^{(r)}=\frac{\sum_{j=1}^{|\mathcal{V}|}S_{Z_{j}^{(r)}}}{\sum_{j=1}^{|\mathcal{V}|}n_{Z_{j}^{(r)}}}.$ 

(4)

The 95% CI is the empirical $[2.5,97.5]$ percentile interval.

For a two-sided paired sign-flip test, we independently draw
 $\epsilon_{v}^{(r)}\in\{-1,+1\}$ for every source video and form

 $\hat{\theta}_{s,0}^{(r)}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{v\in\mathcal{V}}\epsilon_{v}^{(r)}S_{v},\qquad p=\frac{1}{R}\sum_{r=1}^{R}\mathbf{1}\!\left[|\hat{\theta}_{s,0}^{(r)}|\geq|\hat{\theta}_{s}|\right].$ 

(5)

For a severity-endpoint test, we apply the same procedure to
 $d_{p,S_{5}}-d_{p,S_{0}}$ .

For every model–dataset experiment, the endpoint family contains the five
effects $B_{T},N_{T},C_{T},L_{T},S_{D}$ , each tested for nonzero effect at $S_{5}$ and
for the $S_{0}\rightarrow S_{5}$ change within each of the four perturbations
( $40$ tests), plus one nonzero-effect-at- $S_{0}$ test per effect ( $5$ tests):
the $S_{0}$ record is the shared clean baseline and is identical across every
perturbation’s ladder, so it is tested once per effect rather than once per
perturbation, for $45$ tests total. We apply Benjamini–Hochberg to all
 $m$ p-values in this family. For ordered p-values $p_{(1)}\leq\cdots\leq p_{(m)}$ , the reported adjusted value is

 $q_{(i)}=\min\!\left\{1,\min_{j\geq i}\frac{m}{j}p_{(j)}\right\}.$ 

(6)

IAD is excluded from this testing family because it is descriptive.

## A.8 Perturbation realization and displayed severity

Each corruption is applied independently to every post-initialization frame
(the initialization frame is always clean); corruptions are never composed.
For a frame $I\in[0,255]^{H\times W\times 3}$ , the four perturbations are
implemented as follows.

Blur. Per-channel Gaussian smoothing with $\sigma_{s}=k_{s}\cdot\min(H,W)$ ,
kernel truncated at $\pm 3\sigma_{s}$ : $I^{\prime}_{\cdot,\cdot,c}=I_{\cdot,\cdot,c}\ast\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_{s}^{2})$ for each channel $c$ . $k_{0}=0$ is an exact identity.

Noise. The frame is normalized to $[0,1]$ ; a single standard-normal
field $\eta\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)^{H\times W\times 3}$ , seeded deterministically
from a hash of (video, frame index) only – never condition, pair, or
severity – is drawn once per frame and reused unchanged across all four
text conditions and every severity for that frame:
 $I^{\prime}=\mathrm{round}\!\left(255\cdot\mathrm{clip}\!\left(\frac{I}{255}+\sigma_{s}\,\eta,\,0,\,1\right)\right)$ . $\sigma_{0}=0$ is an exact identity; because only
the scale $\sigma_{s}$ changes for a fixed draw of $\eta$ , severities differ in
dose, not in noise realization, so contrasts across conditions at one
severity are never confounded by different noise instances.

Pixelation. Block-mosaic resampling: downscale to
 $(\mathrm{round}(r_{s}W),\mathrm{round}(r_{s}H))$ and upscale back to $(W,H)$ ,
both steps using PIL’s BOX (area-average) filter – the ImageNet-C
pixelate corruption. $r_{0}=1$ is an exact identity.

Compression. The frame is re-encoded to JPEG at quality $q_{s}$ 
(libjpeg’s 1–100 scale) and immediately decoded back to RGB – the
ImageNet-C jpeg_compression corruption. $q_{0}=100$ is treated as an
exact identity (the untouched frame is passed through, not a quality-100
re-encode).

## C.3 Outcome-independent sample confirmation

The main-paper MOT subsets intentionally retain rare Molmo2
conflicting-text takeover cases before seeded random filling. This confirmation
instead retains the complete annotation-eligible DanceTrack, SportsMOT, and
PersonPath22 pool: 404 pairs from 81 source videos (214/17, 110/31, and
80/33 pairs/videos, respectively). Pair inclusion requires at least five
co-visible annotated frames, mean co-visible mask IoU below 0.3, and the
required target, distractor, and deterministic category-matched unrelated
expressions; it uses no model output, accuracy, or takeover label.

JointNLT, UVLTrack, MMTrack, and DUTrack (Molmo2 is out of scope) were run under all four text conditions
at every displayed $S_{0}$ – $S_{5}$ severity level of blur, noise, pixelation, and
compression.

Figure 70 compactly summarizes each
system’s target-accuracy IAD matrices. Within a model, we equally average the
three source-dataset matrices and, within a dataset, the four
perturbation-specific IAD matrices over the paper’s six-position severity
ladder. Larger cells therefore indicate a larger average absolute condition
gap, not a directional or inferential test.

## Appendix D Reproducibility

We release the complete experimental pipeline as a self-contained,
anonymized repository in the supplementary material, structured to
reproduce every table, figure, and statistical test in this paper
through a single configuration file and entry point rather than the
roughly twenty scripts and five separate Python environments the
pipeline spans internally. The repository provides the inference,
validation, consolidation, statistics, and model-internals code exactly
as used to generate the reported results; it introduces no
reimplementation of the underlying methodology.

The archive does not redistribute raw third-party video or image frames.
It provides code, frozen manifests and provenance metadata, annotations only
where their upstream terms permit redistribution, and scripts that obtain and
prepare source media from the original providers (Table 1,
Table 2). The packaged operational smoke fixture
is an author-created synthetic two-object sequence. It exercises the shared
specialized-tracker input, four-condition text, perturbation, output-schema,
and validation paths, but is not a scientific replication or an estimate of
tracking or text-prior performance. Reproducing the full five-model study
requires obtaining the source media, annotations, and checkpoints under their
respective upstream terms. A CPU-only test suite covering adapter contracts,
outcome-label invariants, and the statistical correction routines requires
neither a GPU nor a model checkpoint.

Scaling from the synthetic operational fixture to the full paper-scale grid
(every selected pair, all six severities) requires obtaining the complete
datasets and model checkpoints per their documented licenses
(Table 1, Table 2)
and otherwise leaves the pipeline unchanged. The repository is
anonymized for review; the complete, de-anonymized version
will be made publicly available upon acceptance.

## Appendix E Ethics and Societal Impact

DISTRACT evaluates existing vision-language trackers; it does not train a
tracker, collect new personal data, or release raw third-party media. Its
purpose is to reveal failures under ordinary image degradation, not to certify
a model as safe for deployment. Tracking can be used in privacy-sensitive or
surveillance contexts, although the code adds no surveillance-specific
capability. Users must obtain data lawfully, respect upstream terms and privacy
obligations, and apply suitable oversight and safeguards before operational use.

## E8-QAT: Efficient 2-Bit Quantization-Aware Training with Implicit Lattice Codebooks

Xinyu Lei1  Hong Huang2  Jiebin Zhang1  Hongyu Li2  Jinlong Song2

Decheng Wu3  Guanghua Yu3  Jianchen Zhu3  Sujian Li1

1Peking University  2City University of Hong Kong  3Tencent

xinyulei25@stu.pku.edu.cn  lisujian@pku.edu.cn  lucayu@tencent.com

Abstract

Low-bit quantization reduces the memory footprint of large language models, but preserving accuracy with efficient training remains challenging.
Scalar quantization encodes weights independently, while explicit-codebook vector quantization introduces costly search during training. We propose E8-QAT, a
2-bit quantization-aware training framework that uses the $E_{8}$ lattice as an implicit
eight-dimensional codebook. It jointly quantizes weight groups through arithmetic
projection without storing or searching an explicit codebook during the training forward
pass. A 2-bit core encoding and sparse extension preserve the selected lattice
points exactly, adding less than 0.1% to core index storage.
On Qwen3.6-35B-A3B, E8-QAT matches the performance of the full-precision model in terms of the average score across the four tasks.
E8-QAT improves over the state-of-the-art baseline by 2.03% on IFEval and by 1.51% on LiveCodeBench, under the same data, schedule, and bit width.
The 2-bit representation reduces peak memory by 80% and supports a $4\times$ larger batch.
At each representation’s largest feasible batch, E8-QAT reaches $1.97\times$ the decoding throughput of BF16.

## 1 Introduction

Large language models (LLMs) have made substantial progress across language tasks (Wu et al., 2023), but growing demands for privacy-preserving, offline, and cost-efficient deployment have made running them on resource-constrained platforms a pressing need (Yao et al., 2024; Liagkou et al., 2024). Weight quantization addresses this by reducing model size (Lin et al., 2024). Post-training quantization (PTQ) incurs low computational cost but can suffer accuracy degradation at ultra-low bit widths (Frantar et al., 2023). Quantization-aware training (QAT) recovers accuracy by allowing the model to adapt to quantization errors through weight updates, but training requires substantial data, compute, and memory (Jacob et al., 2018; Chen et al., 2025). This motivates quantization schemes that achieve strong accuracy at ultra-low bit widths without prohibitive training overhead.

Existing QAT approaches primarily rely on scalar quantization, which assigns quantization levels to each weight coordinate independently (Liu et al., 2025). While scalar quantization supports efficient per-coordinate arithmetic, it limits the flexibility of codeword arrangements across dimensions. Vector quantization (VQ) addresses this by jointly encoding groups of weights through a shared codebook (Gray, 1984), allowing richer codeword arrangements that better capture cross-dimensional weight structure and reduce quantization distortion at a given bit budget (Egiazarian et al., 2024; Tseng et al., 2024). Recent work has therefore explored integrating vector quantization into quantization-aware training (VQ-QAT) (Malinovskii et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2026a).

However, conventional VQ-QAT requires an explicit codebook that must be stored and searched at every training step, posing two challenges at scale: (1) nearest-codeword search during the training forward pass introduces substantial overhead, and (2) codebook size grows exponentially with vector dimension at a fixed bit width, making high-dimensional VQ impractical for both training and inference.

To address these challenges, we propose E8-QAT, a QAT framework that uses the $E_{8}$ lattice (Conway and Sloane, 1982) as an implicit eight-dimensional codebook, as illustrated in Figure 1. During the training forward pass, each eight-dimensional weight group is projected onto the $E_{8}$ lattice by rounding, parity correction, and a constant number of candidate comparisons,
reducing the per-group computational cost from $O(v\,2^{kv})$ for exhaustive codebook search to $O(v)$ , independent of the nominal codebook size, where $k$ and $v$ denote the bit width and vector dimension, respectively.
Exploiting the observed concentration of quantized weight groups within the finite core, E8-QAT combines a 2-bit core encoding with a sparse extension that, at very low additional memory cost, exactly preserves the lattice points selected during training with an identity straight-through estimator (STE) (Bengio et al., 2013).

Our main contributions are as follows:

• 

Search-free joint quantization for QAT.
We jointly quantize each group of eight weights by closed-form projection
onto the infinite $E_{8}$ lattice in the QAT forward pass. The projection
requires $O(v)$ operations per group, independent of the nominal codebook
size, without codebook storage, search, or learning.

• 

Exact export beyond the finite core.
Lattice projections during QAT can fall outside the finite core, and
clipping these points at export would alter the trained quantized weights.
We combine a dense core index with sparse extension records to preserve
every trained lattice point exactly.

At the end of training, the sparse records add less than $0.1\%$ to the 2-bit index payload.

• 

Matched-budget 2-bit evaluation on agentic and long-form tasks.
We evaluate the performance and efficiency of E8-QAT on Qwen3.6-35B-A3B (Qwen Team, 2026) and Llama-3.1-8B (Grattafiori et al., 2024).

On Qwen3.6-35B-A3B, E8-QAT improves over LC-QAT by 2.03% on IFEval and by 1.51% on LiveCodeBench, under the same training corpus, schedule, and bit budget.
Its four-task average is 73.93, equal to the reported BF16 average.
Relative to BF16, the exported model reduces peak memory usage by 80%,
enabling a $4\times$ larger batch size. Decoding throughput reaches
 $1.97\times$ that of BF16 when each representation is evaluated at its
largest feasible batch size.

## 2 Related Work

## 2.1 Low-bit weight quantization

Representative PTQ methods include GPTQ (Frantar et al., 2023) and AWQ (Lin et al., 2024), which use calibration data to guide scalar quantization of individual weight coordinates.
Other PTQ methods use VQ to jointly encode groups of weights.
QuIP# (Tseng et al., 2024) uses predefined codebooks, while AQLM (Egiazarian et al., 2024) represents each weight group as a sum of codewords from learned codebooks.
QAT methods such as EfficientQAT (Chen et al., 2025) and ParetoQ (Liu et al., 2025) incorporate scalar quantization into training, allowing model weights to adapt to quantization errors.

Figure 1: Training-time quantization.
(a) Explicit-codebook vector quantization searches a stored codebook of $K=2^{kv}$ entries.
(b) LC-QAT rounds each coordinate and applies a fixed scale and shift, $\mathbf{q}=s\mathbf{z}+\mathbf{b}$ .
(c) E8-QAT projects eight normalized coordinates onto $E_{8}$ by arithmetic, without a stored codebook.

## 2.2 Vector quantization-aware training

Recent work has also explored VQ-QAT, with PV-Tuning and LC-QAT adopting different strategies for updating quantized weights.

PV-Tuning optimizes both continuous parameters and discrete codeword assignments.
For learned VQ codebooks, it combines gradient-based updates of codeword values with discrete assignment updates for selected weight groups.
These assignment updates require nearest-codeword search, or beam search when multiple additive codebooks are used, introducing additional computational cost during training (Malinovskii et al., 2024).

LC-QAT avoids explicit codebook lookup in the training forward pass through an affine mapping of discrete vectors (Wang et al., 2026a).
However, it rounds and clips each coordinate independently before applying a fixed scale and shift (AI9Stars, 2026).
The resulting quantizer retains the per-coordinate structure of scalar quantization, limiting the flexibility of codeword arrangements across dimensions.

Consequently, these VQ-QAT designs face a trade-off between training efficiency and quantization accuracy.
They either rely on costly codeword search to determine the discrete assignments, or give up the joint encoding that makes VQ accurate.
E8-QAT addresses this challenge by using the $E_{8}$ lattice as an implicit eight-dimensional codebook, enabling joint weight quantization through arithmetic projection (Conway and Sloane, 1982) without storing or searching an explicit codebook during the training forward pass.

## 3 Preliminaries

At a fixed bit budget, quantization error depends on both the
distribution of the weights and the geometry of the codebook.
We first explain how Hadamard preprocessing redistributes weight
magnitudes, then introduce vector quantization as a way to
represent the transformed weights jointly. These considerations
motivate the use of the $E_{8}$ lattice, whose geometric properties
and efficient nearest-point projection provide the mathematical
basis for E8-QAT.

## 3.1 Hadamard Preprocessing

Randomized Hadamard preprocessing mixes weight coordinates before
quantization (Tseng et al., 2024). Within a transform block, it maps
a weight vector $\mathbf{w}$ to

 $\widetilde{\mathbf{w}}=\mathbf{H}\mathbf{D}\mathbf{w},\qquad\mathbf{H}^{\top}\mathbf{H}=\mathbf{I},$ 

(1)

where $\mathbf{D}$ applies independent random sign flips and
 $\mathbf{H}$ is a normalized Hadamard matrix. Each output
coordinate is a normalized sum of signed weights from the
same block, while the transform preserves the sum of
squared weights.

The central limit theorem (Durrett, 2019) shows that when many independent signed weights contribute to each output coordinate and no single weight dominates, the resulting marginal distribution approximates a Gaussian distribution. To verify this, we sampled 14 weight tensors from a pre-quantized BF16 model and observed that the transformed marginal kurtosis is about $3.02$ , close to the Gaussian value $3$ .

Under the zero-mean isotropic Gaussian approximation, the probability density depends only on the distance from the origin and decreases with that distance. We observed that the radial probability distribution of the rotated weights is geometrically similar to a finite $E_{8}$ codebook (Tseng et al., 2024), which can improve lattice utilization and representation accuracy in vector quantization.

## 3.2 Vector Quantization

After Hadamard preprocessing and scaling, we partition each row
of a weight matrix into non-overlapping groups of $d$ consecutive
weights along the input dimension. Let
 $\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}$ denote one such group.
Given a finite codebook $\mathcal{C}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}$ 
with $K$ codewords, vector quantization assigns the entire group
to its nearest codeword:

 $Q_{\mathcal{C}}(\mathbf{x})=\operatorname*{arg\,min}_{\mathbf{c}\in\mathcal{C}}\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{c}\|_{2}^{2}.$ 

(2)

With $K=2^{kd}$ codewords, each group requires a $kd$ -bit index.

Unlike scalar quantization, VQ is not restricted to a rectangular
grid and can better match the distribution of weight groups.
This flexibility can reduce quantization error at the same
index budget (Gray and Neuhoff, 1998).

However, exhaustive nearest-codeword search costs $O(dK)$ 
operations per group, with $K=2^{kd}$ growing exponentially
in the group dimension at a fixed per-weight bit budget.
This motivates structured point sets whose nearest points
can be computed directly rather than found by exhaustive search.

## 3.3 $E_{8}$ Lattice Quantization

The $E_{8}$ lattice is a classical eight-dimensional construction
from sphere-packing and lattice-quantization theory
(Conway and Sloane, 1999), not a codebook learned from model weights.
Its points have either all integer or all half-integer
coordinates, with an even coordinate sum:

 $E_{8}=\left\{\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{Z}^{8}\cup\left(\mathbb{Z}^{8}+\tfrac{1}{2}\mathbf{1}\right):\ \mathbf{1}^{\top}\mathbf{z}\in 2\mathbb{Z}\right\},$ 

(3)

where $\mathbf{1}$ is the all-ones vector. Its geometry and
algebraic structure offer three useful properties.

 $E_{8}$ combines favorable covering geometry with an algebraic
structure that enables efficient exact quantization. At equal
cell volume, its smaller covering radius than $\mathbb{Z}^{8}$ 
provides a tighter worst-case error bound throughout the
infinite lattice (Conway and Sloane, 1999), while its structure
permits exact nearest-point projection without exhaustive
codebook search (Conway and Sloane, 1982). This combination makes
 $E_{8}$ attractive for QAT, where weights are repeatedly projected
as they evolve during training. We exploit these properties
by using an approximately ball-shaped core
 $\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{core}}\subset E_{8}$ of $2^{16}$ low-norm
points for compact encoding, while retaining the full lattice
as the training-time projection space. This design pairs
 $2$ -bit-per-weight indices for core points with the flexibility
to project beyond the core boundary; the following section
describes how these core-external projections are encoded
and their additional cost is accounted for.

E8-QAT separates training-time quantization from post-training
encoding, combining efficient joint quantization with compact

As illustrated in Figure 2, the framework

an identity straight-through estimator, and preserves the

## 4.1 Lattice QAT from PTQ Initialization

Let $\mathbf{L}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}$ denote the trainable
latent weights of a layer with $n$ input and $m$ output
channels.
Assuming $n$ is divisible by $d=8$ , we partition each row
into contiguous groups
 $\bm{\ell}_{g}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}$ .
The quantization mapping is

 $\mathbf{x}_{g}=w_{s}\bm{\ell}_{g},\qquad\mathbf{q}_{g}=Q_{E_{8}}(\mathbf{x}_{g}),\qquad\overline{\mathbf{w}}_{g}=c_{s}\mathbf{q}_{g}+c_{z}\mathbf{1}_{d},$ 

(4)

where group $g$ contains $d=8$ latent weights $\bm{\ell}_{g}$ .
The fixed per-tensor scale $w_{s}$ maps them to lattice coordinates $\mathbf{x}_{g}$ ,
and $Q_{E_{8}}$ returns the nearest lattice point $\mathbf{q}_{g}$ .
The fixed decode scale $c_{s}$ and zero-point $c_{z}$ map that point back to weights;
 $\mathbf{1}_{d}$ adds $c_{z}$ to every coordinate.

Weight reconstruction.

The decoded groups in Equation 4 are assembled
into $\overline{\mathbf{W}}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}$ . The effective weight
matrix is reconstructed as

 $\widehat{\mathbf{W}}=\operatorname{diag}(\mathbf{s}_{V})\,H_{\mathrm{out}}\,\overline{\mathbf{W}}\,H_{\mathrm{in}}\,\operatorname{diag}(\mathbf{s}_{U}),$ 

(5)

where $\mathbf{s}_{U}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}$ and
 $\mathbf{s}_{V}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}$ are trainable input- and output-channel
scaling vectors, respectively. The matrices $H_{\mathrm{in}}$ and
 $H_{\mathrm{out}}$ are fixed orthogonal block-Hadamard transforms
with block size $128$ . For an input $\mathbf{a}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}$ ,
the layer computes $\mathbf{y}=\widehat{\mathbf{W}}\mathbf{a}$ .

Finite-core construction.

The finite core $\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{core}}$ consists of the first
 $2^{16}$ points of $E_{8}$ ordered by increasing squared norm, with ties
resolved lexicographically. It includes all $56{,}881$ points with
squared norm at most $10$ and the first $8{,}655$ points in lexicographic
order from the shell at squared norm $12$ . Initialization, export,
and decoding use the same point set and index ordering.
Since the outermost shell is only partially retained, a norm threshold
does not suffice for escape detection; a projected point is classified
as an escape if and only if it lies outside $\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{core}}$ .

Numerical precision.

Latent weights are stored in BF16, while projection candidates and squared distances are evaluated in FP32.
Gradients through the hard projection use an identity straight-through estimator.
Hadamard transforms, affine decoding, and channel scaling lie outside the projector and use ordinary backpropagation.

PTQ initialization.

We initialize from an $E_{8}$ -based PTQ checkpoint obtained
using Hadamard preprocessing and LDLQ (Tseng et al., 2024; Chee et al., 2023).
PTQ uses a fixed core
 $\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{core}}=\{\mathbf{c}_{i}\}_{i=0}^{2^{16}-1}\subset E_{8}$ ,
shared with the export representation.
Given the PTQ index $i_{g}^{(0)}$ , we initialize

 $\bm{\ell}_{g}^{(0)}=\frac{\mathbf{c}_{i_{g}^{(0)}}}{w_{s}}.$ 

(6)

Thus, $\mathbf{x}_{g}^{(0)}=\mathbf{c}_{i_{g}^{(0)}}$ in exact
arithmetic.
This places each group at a quantization-cell center, providing
a local stability margin against small perturbations (Conway and Sloane, 1999).
The finite-precision condition is given in
Appendix A; subsequent optimization is not
constrained to remain within the core.

Lattice training.

Each forward pass applies the arithmetic projector in
Equation 4.
Rounding, parity correction, and comparison of two candidates
require $O(d)$ operations per group (Conway and Sloane, 1982), without explicit codebook
search or restriction to the finite core.
Since hard projection is piecewise constant, we use an identity
STE (Bengio et al., 2013; van den Oord et al., 2017).
For the autoregressive language-modeling loss $\mathcal{L}$ ,
the forward computation and surrogate latent gradient are

 $\displaystyle\mathbf{q}_{g}$ 
 $\displaystyle=\mathbf{x}_{g}+\operatorname{sg}\!\left(Q_{E_{8}}(\mathbf{x}_{g})-\mathbf{x}_{g}\right),\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial\bm{\ell}_{g}}$ 
 $\displaystyle\approx w_{s}c_{s}\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial\overline{\mathbf{w}}_{g}},$ 

(7)

where $\operatorname{sg}$ is the identity in the forward pass
and has zero derivative in the backward pass.
This substitutes $I_{d}$ for the projection Jacobian while
retaining the hard forward assignment.

The assignment therefore remains jointly constrained by
 $E_{8}$ , while latent updates can move groups across Voronoi
boundaries.
We optimize the latent weights, per-channel scales, and other
trainable parameters without learning the lattice or its core
codewords.
The STE supplies a surrogate gradient rather than the true
derivative of hard projection.

## 4.2 Exact Encoding with Sparse Extensions

Training on the infinite lattice permits projections outside
the finite core.
Let
 $\mathbf{q}_{g}^{\star}=Q_{E_{8}}(\mathbf{x}_{g}^{(T)})$ 
denote the final projection after $T$ training steps.
For $G$ quantized groups, define

 $\mathcal{E}=\left\{g:\mathbf{q}_{g}^{\star}\notin\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{core}}\right\},\qquad\rho=\frac{|\mathcal{E}|}{G}.$ 

(8)

An escape is determined by core membership, not solely by
a norm threshold.
Reprojecting escaped points onto the core would alter the
quantized weights selected during training.

Instead, we store a 16-bit core index $i_{g}$ for every group.
For an in-core group, it represents $\mathbf{q}_{g}^{\star}$ 
directly; for an escaped group, it identifies a legal fallback.
Each escape additionally stores its position and doubled
coordinates
 $\mathbf{v}_{g}=2\mathbf{q}_{g}^{\star}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}$ .
Decoding gives

 $\widehat{\mathbf{q}}_{g}=\begin{cases}\mathbf{c}_{i_{g}},&g\notin\mathcal{E},\\[2.0pt]
\mathbf{v}_{g}/2,&g\in\mathcal{E}.\end{cases}$ 

(9)

The sparse record overrides the fallback rather than adding
absolute coordinates to it.
When positions and doubled coordinates fit the representation,
 $\widehat{\mathbf{q}}_{g}=\mathbf{q}_{g}^{\star}$ for every group.
Export therefore introduces no additional lattice-coordinate
quantization error.

With $B$ additional bytes per escape, the payload cost is

 $b_{\mathrm{payload}}=\frac{16G+8B|\mathcal{E}|}{dG}=2+\frac{8B}{d}\rho\quad\text{bits per quantized weight}.$ 

(10)

Our coordinate format uses $B=20$ , giving
 $b_{\mathrm{payload}}=2+20\rho$ for $d=8$ .
This accounts for core indices and escape records, excluding
shared codebooks, scales, and format metadata.
Appendix B details the representation,
range checks, and optional root packing.
Section 5.2 measures the encoding
overhead empirically.

After export, latent weights are discarded, leaving core
indices, sparse records, and reconstruction parameters.
Inference may use a shared decoding table, whereas
training-time projection remains lookup-free.

We summarize the experimental configuration below and provide further details in Appendix C.1.

Baselines.

We compare E8-QAT against two categories of 2-bit methods:
(1) post-training quantization, including round-to-nearest (RTN) and GPTQ;UniSVQ (Wang et al., 2026b; AI9Stars, 2026), the scalar PTQ used to initialize LC-QAT;
AQLM with a single learned $2^{16}$ -entry codebook shared across all feed-forward
matrices of a layer, which matches our bit width; and E8-PTQ, the first stage of our method; and
(2) quantization-aware training, including PV-Tuning, initialized from the same
AQLM model, and LC-QAT.

Models, data, and evaluation.

We use Qwen3.6-35B-A3B and Llama-3.1-8B as our base models.
Both stages draw on the UltraData collection (OpenBMB, 2026): stage-1 calibration uses 1,680 sequences of 16,384 tokens constructed from 2,000 agentic conversations in UltraData-SFT-Agent, shared by all PTQ methods, and stage-2 training uses 2,000 long sequences from the same collection, totaling 50.8M tokens per pass, with a 375-step schedule for Llama-3.1-8B.
We evaluate model performance with lm-evaluation-harness (Gao et al., 2024) on four zero-shot benchmarks, ARC-Challenge (ARC-C) (Clark et al., 2018), HellaSwag (HelS) (Zellers et al., 2019), PIQA (Bisk et al., 2020), and WinoGrande (WinG) (Sakaguchi et al., 2020), together with LiveCodeBench (LCB) (Jain et al., 2025) pass@1, BFCL (Patil et al., 2025) v3 overall accuracy, IFEval prompt-level strict accuracy (Zhou et al., 2023), and GPQA (Rein et al., 2024) accuracy.
All models are evaluated with the same harness and settings; details are given in Appendix C.1.

Figure 4: Layer-wise stage-1 output distortion at matched bit width (lower is better).

Implementation details.

All Qwen3.6-35B-A3B training runs use eight GPUs.
E8-QAT and LC-QAT use 750 steps, a warmup–stable–decay schedule annealed to zero,
and a peak learning rate of $10^{-4}$ .
The Llama-3.1-8B second stage uses 375 steps, with the peak learning rate scaled by the inverse weight scale.
PV-Tuning uses its released recipe. In paired 375-step runs, the E8-QAT stage completes 4.5% faster than LC-QAT.
Inference is measured with fused decode kernels and 16-bit indices, launching a separate process for each configuration.

## 5.2 Main Results

Comparison with 2-bit methods.

To evaluate whether the lattice codebook yields the strongest 2-bit model at a matched bit budget,
we compare E8-QAT against the PTQ and QAT baselines on Qwen3.6-35B-A3B.
As shown in Table 1, E8-QAT achieves the best quantized score on ARC-Challenge, HellaSwag, and WinoGrande, and on LiveCodeBench and IFEval.
Compared with LC-QAT, which shares the same data, schedule, and bit width, E8-QAT improves accuracy by 1.51 percentage points on LiveCodeBench and by 2.03 percentage points on IFEval.

On the six-task average, E8-QAT scores 76.95, against 75.97 for LC-QAT.
On Llama-3.1-8B, its WikiText perplexity is 13.77, against 16.73 for LC-QAT.Appendix C.4 gives ARC-Easy, BoolQ, and the perplexity for every Llama row.
These results demonstrate that replacing the scalar grid with the $E_{8}$ lattice improves both base-model quality and agentic capability at 2 bits.

Generalization to dense models.

To examine whether this advantage transfers across architectures, we further evaluate E8-QAT on
the dense Llama-3.1-8B.
As shown in Table 2, E8-QAT again achieves the best quantized score on all four
tasks, outperforming LC-QAT by 2.99% on average.
These results confirm that the lattice advantage transfers across architectures once the learning
rate is matched in lattice space.

Inference efficiency.

We compare the serving capacity of E8-QAT and BF16 to validate that 2-bit storage allows a single GPU to serve larger batches
As shown in Figure 2, E8-QAT reduces peak memory by 80% relative to BF16,
supports a $4\times$ larger batch, and achieves 97% higher throughput at each model’s largest feasible batch.
These results indicate that the lattice model nearly doubles serving throughput on the same hardware.

Most accurate initialization.

At equal bits, the lattice should reconstruct weights more accurately than a scalar grid or
a learned codebook.
We measure the layer-wise output distortion
, which weighted by
routed-token frequency, with the same Hessians for all methods.
As shown in Figure 4, E8-PTQ has the lowest distortion in 39 of the
40 layers.
Averaged over layers, it is 20.4% below UniSVQ, which runs the same LDLQ pipeline with a
scalar grid, and 19.4% below AQLM.
These results confirm that the lattice provides the most accurate starting point at matched bits.

Figure 5: Performance of PTQ and QAT model with the 750-step second stage.
Dashed lines mark BF16.

Gains from second-stage QAT.

Reconstruction-based PTQ is expected to preserve short-form ability but not long-form generation,
which QAT should recover.
To verify this, Figure 5 compares each pipeline before and after the 750-step second stage.
QAT improves LiveCodeBench by 24.36% and GPQA by 17.67% for E8-QAT.
E8-QAT is ahead of the LC-QAT pipeline both before and after this stage. These results demonstrate that second-stage QAT is necessary for long-form generation at 2 bits and
that the lattice advantage persists through training.

Exact export at two bits.

Allowing QAT to move groups outside the finite core is expected to incur little additional storage.
To quantify this overhead, Figure 6 tracks the escape rate during training, its
sensitivity to the peak learning rate, and the resulting payload.
Escape growth slows as the learning rate anneals, with the final 50 steps of the 750-step run raising
the escape rate by only 0.25%; halving the peak learning rate reduces the final escape rate by
 $40.7\times$ ; and at the 750-step endpoint, the sparse records add only 0.086% to the 2-bit index payload.
These results confirm that a finite core with a sparse extension keeps exact export within 0.1% of
two bits, preserving the trained weights exactly with only a small number of exceptions.

## 6 Conclusion

In this work, we introduce E8-QAT, a QAT framework designed to address the trade-off between training efficiency and quantization accuracy at ultra-low bit widths.
By using the $E_{8}$ lattice as an implicit eight-dimensional codebook, E8-QAT enables joint weight quantization through arithmetic projection without storing or searching an explicit codebook during the training forward pass.
An identity STE supports weight updates during training.
To preserve the lattice points selected during training, we combine a 2-bit core encoding with a sparse extension, enabling exact encoding at very low additional memory cost in our experiments.

Experimental results on Qwen3.6-35B-A3B and Llama-3.1-8B show that E8-QAT achieves higher average accuracy than LC-QATon the four zero-shot benchmarks. On Qwen3.6-35B-A3B, E8-QAT also reduces peak memory by 80% relative to BF16 at batch size 1 and achieves $1.97\times$ the BF16 serving throughput on the same hardware, with throughput measured at each model’s largest feasible batch.

## Appendix A Initialization and Implementation Details

Geometric margin over the integer lattice.

At equal cell volume, $E_{8}$ has covering radius smaller than $\mathbb{Z}^{8}$ by a factor of $\sqrt{2}$ , and its normalized second moment is $0.0716821$ against $1/12$ for $\mathbb{Z}^{8}$ (Conway and Sloane, 1999).
This is a granular gain of $0.654$  dB, or a $14.0\%$ reduction in mean squared error at matched rate under a high-resolution model.

Local stability of initialization.

In the standard normalization of $E_{8}$ , the minimum distance between distinct $E_{8}$ points is $\sqrt{2}$ .
Let

 $\epsilon_{g}^{(0)}=\left\|\mathbf{x}_{g}^{(0)}-\mathbf{c}_{i_{g}^{(0)}}\right\|_{2}$ 

denote the finite-precision initialization error of group $g$ .
The initial assignment is preserved at step $t$ whenever

 $\epsilon_{g}^{(0)}+\left\|\mathbf{x}_{g}^{(t)}-\mathbf{x}_{g}^{(0)}\right\|_{2}<\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\mathbf{q}_{g}^{(t)}=\mathbf{c}_{i_{g}^{(0)}}.$ 

(11)

To see this, write $\mathbf{c}=\mathbf{c}_{i_{g}^{(0)}}$ .
The triangle inequality gives $\delta=\|\mathbf{x}_{g}^{(t)}-\mathbf{c}\|_{2}<1/\sqrt{2}$ .
Every other lattice point $\mathbf{c}^{\prime}$ then satisfies

 $\|\mathbf{x}_{g}^{(t)}-\mathbf{c}^{\prime}\|_{2}\geq\sqrt{2}-\delta>\delta,$ 

so $\mathbf{c}$ remains the unique nearest point.

## Appendix B Sparse Encoding and Decoding Verification

Coordinate records.

Each eight-weight group retains a 16-bit core index.
An escaped group additionally stores its 32-bit tensor-local position and doubled lattice coordinates,

 $\mathbf{v}_{g}=2\mathbf{q}_{g}^{\star}\in\mathbb{Z}^{8}.$ 

(12)

Every $E_{8}$ point has all-integer or all-half-integer coordinates, so $\mathbf{v}_{g}$ is integral.
Eight signed 16-bit coordinates and one 32-bit position occupy 20 bytes per record.
During decoding, the record supplies the absolute lattice coordinates and overrides the fallback codeword, so the exported point is the lattice point selected in training.

Export checks that every position fits the selected index representation and that the stored coordinates satisfy

 $-2^{15}\leq(\mathbf{v}_{g})_{j}\leq 2^{15}-1,\qquad j=1,\ldots,8.$ 

Conditional root packing.

A smaller record is available when an escaped point differs from a selected core fallback by one $E_{8}$ root.
Define

 $\mathcal{R}=\left\{\mathbf{r}\in E_{8}:\|\mathbf{r}\|_{2}^{2}=2\right\},\qquad|\mathcal{R}|=240.$ 

(13)

When the residual belongs to $\mathcal{R}$ , decoding becomes

 $\mathbf{q}_{g}^{\star}=\mathbf{c}_{i_{g}}+\mathbf{r}_{j_{g}},\qquad\mathbf{r}_{j_{g}}\in\mathcal{R}.$ 

(14)

An 8-bit root identifier and a 24-bit position within a chunk form a 4-byte record, excluding chunk metadata.
All $26{,}435$ escaped groups in the 375-step artifact satisfy this condition.
Payloads reported in the paper use the 20-byte coordinate record, including the 750-step endpoint.

Fused decoding.

For the corresponding group of input-side transformed activations $\mathbf{h}_{g}$ , linearity gives

 $\left\langle\mathbf{h}_{g},\mathbf{q}_{g}^{\star}\right\rangle=\left\langle\mathbf{h}_{g},\mathbf{c}_{i_{g}}\right\rangle+\left\langle\mathbf{h}_{g},\mathbf{r}_{j_{g}}\right\rangle.$ 

(15)

The fused matrix-vector path adds the sparse correction to the core-codeword contribution before the output-side Hadamard transform.
The decoding scale $c_{s}$ applies to both lattice terms, and the offset $c_{z}$ is applied once.
The corrected weight matrix is never materialized.

Core decoding agrees on all $65{,}536$ codewords, and every escaped group in the 375-step artifact lies one root from its fallback.
The second count is consistent with $\rho=6.540\times 10^{-6}$ over the $4.04\times 10^{9}$ quantized groups of the model.

## Appendix D Limitations

Evaluation scope.

Our experiments cover Qwen3.6-35B-A3B and Llama-3.1-8B, with long-form generation and agentic evaluations focused on the MoE model.
Extending the evaluation to additional architectures, model scales, and application domains would further characterize the applicability of the method.

Weight-only quantization.

This work quantizes weights.
Activations and the KV cache remain in higher precision.
Combining the lattice weights with activation and KV-cache quantization is a direct way to reduce memory further in long-context inference.

Deployment configurations.

The inference results use the fused kernels and batch sizes reported in the main text.
The memory reduction is what admits the larger batch.
Further kernel work and other hardware platforms are natural next measurements.

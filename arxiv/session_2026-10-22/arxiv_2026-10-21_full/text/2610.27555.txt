Abstract

Safety pretraining has emerged as a promising paradigm for embedding safety deeply into a model during pretraining, yielding robustness to subsequent fine-tuning that conventional safety post-training often lacks. We refer to this safety acquired during model pretraining as native safety. Yet native safety is expensive to acquire, confined to the model in which it was pretrained, and often accompanied by lower general utility. In this work, we rethink the practical value of safety-pretrained models and introduce, for the first time, the native safety reuse problem: Can native safety be transferred from a specialized, potentially smaller, safety-pretrained “teacher” to standard-pretrained “students,” enabling them to inherit its robustness while preserving their general utility? We show that native safety can “travel” through on-policy distillation. A standard-pretrained student generates responses to safety-pretraining prompts, while a small safety-pretrained teacher supervises the student’s token-level predictions along its own generated trajectories; cross-tokenizer alignment further enables transfer across model families. The resulting students exhibit substantially greater safety robustness than their safety-post-trained counterparts while retaining higher general utility than the teacher: subsequent fine-tuning raises the average attack success rate (ASR) of the 1.7B student by only 5.00%, compared with 11.58% for safety post-training, and its average utility of 48.20% far exceeds the aligned teacher’s 27.42%.
Unlike conventional large-to-small knowledge distillation, our framework enables selective, small-to-large safety transfer: it reuses the robust safety expertise of a smaller pretrained teacher without inheriting its utility limitations.
It further enables native safety to “travel” to broader applications, including robust machine unlearning and vision-language model safety alignment.
These findings establish native safety as a reusable model property rather than one permanently tied to the originally pretrained model.

A Preprint

## 1 Introduction

Large language models (LLMs) are increasingly deployed in open-ended and high-stakes settings, making their safety a priority. The prevailing approach is safety alignment through post-training, which uses supervised fine-tuning, preference optimization, reinforcement learning, or related techniques to shape the behavior of a pretrained model (Ouyang et al., 2022; Rafailov et al., 2023; Bianchi et al., 2024; Zou et al., 2024; Liu et al., 2025; Jia et al., 2025; Ji et al., 2023b; Shen et al., 2023).
Although safety post-training can substantially reduce harmful compliance at the resulting checkpoint, this protection is often fragile. Aligned models remain vulnerable to jailbreaking attacks (Zou et al., 2023; Wei et al., 2023; Chao et al., 2025; Qi et al., 2025).
More alarmingly, their safety can deteriorate during subsequent fine-tuning even when the downstream data are entirely benign and unrelated to harmful behavior (Qi et al., 2024; He et al., 2024; Huang et al., 2024a; Wang et al., 2025).
Thus, ordinary model adaptation can inadvertently undo safety previously acquired through post-training.

From the perspective of downstream adaptation, the difficulty of preserving post-training safety has been viewed as a fine-tuning-induced misalignment problem (Siddiqui et al., 2026a). However, it also exposes a fundamental limitation of safety post-training itself: the learned safety may remain shallow (Qi et al., 2025; Qi et al., 2024; Tice et al., 2026). Post-training updates an already pretrained model using a comparatively small alignment dataset and limited optimization budget, often inducing only modest changes to its weights (Siddiqui et al., 2026b; Liu et al., 2019). Consequently, the model may learn safe behavior at the surface level without deeply integrating safety into its underlying representations (Qi et al., 2025). Subsequent safety-irrelevant updates can then overwrite this behavior when improving performance on the intended downstream task, such as mathematical reasoning.

To overcome the limitations of safety post-training, safety pretraining has recently emerged as a promising paradigm for embedding safety more deeply into a model (Korbak et al., 2023; Maini et al., 2026; Sam et al., 2026; O’Brien et al., 2026; Roland et al., 2026; Stranisci and Hardmeier, 2026; Tice et al., 2026).
This paradigm integrates safety-relevant data and learning objectives directly into the pretraining process. Recent studies show that safety-pretrained models preserve their safety substantially better under subsequent fine-tuning than models aligned solely through post-training. We refer to this deeply embedded and adaptation-resilient property as native safety: safety acquired during pretraining, characterized not only by safe behavior at the current checkpoint, but also by robustness to subsequent fine-tuning.

Despite its promise, safety pretraining faces some practical limitations. First, pretraining a model from scratch is substantially computationally expensive, making it impractical to repeat for every architecture, scale, or application. Second, native safety is confined to the particular model in which it was pretrained and cannot directly benefit the broader ecosystem of open-weight models.
Third, stronger native safety may come at the cost of lower general utility (Maini et al., 2026), and thus, directly deploying a safety-pretrained model can sacrifice capabilities already available in standard models. These limitations motivate the central question of this work:

(Q) Can native safety “travel”—that is, be transferred from a safety-pretrained model and reused by larger, more capable standard models?

We refer to (Q) as the native safety reuse problem. Solving it can improve the value and scalability of safety pretraining: rather than treating native safety as a model-specific property that must be relearned through costly pretraining, we could acquire it once and reuse it across architectures and modalities. Our objective differs fundamentally from conventional knowledge distillation (Hinton et al., 2015; Gou et al., 2021; Kim and Rush, 2016; Hsieh et al., 2023; Gu et al., 2023; Xu et al., 2024; Song and Zheng, 2026), which typically transfers the general capabilities of a large teacher to a smaller student for efficient deployment.
Instead, a particularly valuable setting in our work is small-to-large transfer, where a smaller safety-pretrained teacher transfers its native safety expertise to a larger, more capable student without compromising the student’s general utility.

Our central finding is that native safety travels most effectively on-policy: the student generates responses to safety-pretraining prompts, and the teacher supervises its token-level predictions along these trajectories, directly correcting the states and failure modes encountered by the student (see Fig. 1 for an overview). To enable reuse across model families, we further address tokenizer mismatch through cross-tokenizer on-policy distillation (pati~no2025_unlocking_on_policy_distillation_for_any_model_family; Niu et al., 2026; Sun et al., 2026), which aligns token sequences and vocabulary distributions before transferring teacher supervision. In our study, this allows the small SafeLM-1.7B teacher (Maini et al., 2026) to improve larger students from another model family, including Llama-3.2-3B and Llama-3-8B (Grattafiori et al., 2024).
Beyond LLM safety alignment, we demonstrate native safety reuse in machine unlearning, where it makes unlearning more robust, and in multimodal safety alignment, where a language-based safety-pretrained teacher enhances the safety robustness of a vision-language model (VLM) without modifying its visual components. Our main contributions are summarized as follows:

 $\bullet$ We introduce the native safety reuse problem, reframing safety pretraining as a reusable resource for improving other models rather than a costly, model-bound outcome.

 $\bullet$ We show that native safety travels effectively through on-policy distillation, requiring both a safety-pretrained teacher and on-policy supervision. Cross-tokenizer alignment further enables small-to-large transfer across model scales and architectures, allowing SafeLM-1.7B to improve larger Llama students without inheriting the teacher’s utility limitations.

 $\bullet$ Across multiple safety and general-utility benchmarks, distilled students exhibit greater robustness to subsequent fine-tuning than their safety-post-trained counterparts while retaining higher utility than the safety-pretrained teacher.
See Fig. 1(b) for some experimental highlights.

 $\bullet$ We extend native safety reuse to robust machine unlearning and VLM safety alignment, demonstrating transfer beyond the teacher’s original architecture and task.

## 2 Related Work

Safety pretraining and robustness to subsequent fine-tuning.
Safety pretraining embeds safety into a model by rewriting or filtering its pretraining corpus (Maini et al., 2026; O’Brien et al., 2026; Stranisci and Hardmeier, 2026), controlling the timing and structure of safety data (Sam et al., 2026; Tice et al., 2026; Roland et al., 2026), or conditioning training on preference scores (Korbak et al., 2023). Maini et al. (2026) show that the resulting safety-pretrained model, SafeLM, preserves safety under subsequent fine-tuning, whereas safety introduced through post-training can erode even on benign data (Qi et al., 2024; He et al., 2024; Huang et al., 2024a). A complementary line of work protects post-trained models during fine-tuning (Huang et al., 2024b; Ham et al., 2025) or makes their weights resistant to tampering (Siddiqui et al., 2026b; Wang et al., 2025). These approaches protect only the model being pretrained or defended. In contrast, we ask whether robustness acquired during safety pretraining can be transferred or reused by a standard-pretrained student, and improve the safety robustness of the resulting student under subsequent fine-tuning without additional defenses.

On-policy and cross-tokenizer distillation.
On-policy distillation trains a student on its own rollouts under teacher supervision (Lin et al., 2020; Agarwal et al., 2024; Gu et al., 2023; Song and Zheng, 2026). Its effectiveness can depend more on teacher–student proximity than on teacher strength (Li et al., 2026b; Zhu et al., 2026), and can improve by matching the teacher’s top- $k$ distribution rather than only sampled tokens (Fu et al., 2026b). Cross-tokenizer distillation methods align predictive distributions across vocabularies through optimal transport (Boizard et al., 2024), sequence and vocabulary alignment (pati~no2025_unlocking_on_policy_distillation_for_any_model_family), or finer-grained aligned units (Niu et al., 2026; Sun et al., 2026). Prior work primarily studies capability transfer, including weak-to-strong settings in which smaller models supervise larger ones (Burns et al., 2023; Yu et al., 2026). In contrast, we distill safety from a small safety-pretrained teacher to students of equal or larger scale, and study whether the transferred safety survives subsequent fine-tuning.

Safety distillation.
Safety post-training can distill refusal behavior from aligned LLMs (Bianchi et al., 2024; Jiang et al., 2024), although off-policy distillation from an aligned teacher may yield a less safe student (Zhang et al., 2025; Jahan and Sun, 2025). On-policy distillation has also recently been applied to safety alignment: supervising the student’s own rollouts lowers the safety tax (Fu et al., 2026a), and restricting the supervision to safety-critical tokens further limits the utility loss (Li et al., 2026a). In these methods the teacher is the student itself under a safety prompt, a constitution, or a steering vector (Fu et al., 2026a; Wen et al., 2026; Li et al., 2026a), so the distilled safety is bounded by that of the prompted student (Zhu et al., 2026). Other approaches employ a smaller corrector to align a larger model (Ji et al., 2024) or use distillation to make unlearning resistant to relearning (Lee et al., 2026). Yet, little work has studied distillation from safety-pretrained models.

## 3 Problem and Motivation: Native Safety through Pretraining

Safety pretraining: A costly yet more robust alternative to safety post-training.
As a concrete setup, we consider the safety-pretraining framework of Maini et al. (2026) and adopt their released safety-pretrained model SafeLM-1.7B in our subsequent study (whose standard-pretrained counterpart is SmolLM2-1.7B).
Their pipeline constructs a rich safety-pretraining corpus from web documents in the SmolLM2 pretraining corpus (Allal et al., 2025), which are then scored according to their safety risk. Moderately unsafe documents are rephrased as safe, context-rich text (SafeWeb), whereas highly unsafe documents are transformed into refusal dialogues (RefuseWeb) and rewritten as educational articles (MoralEducation).
By contrast, the standard safety post-training pipeline considered by Maini et al. (2026) relies on a comparatively limited collection of instruction-following and alignment datasets, including UltraChat-200k (Ding et al., 2023), WildGuardMix (Han et al., 2024), and WildJailbreak (Jiang et al., 2024).

Based on the above, we can apply the standard safety post-training to two pretrained base models: the standard-pretrained SmolLM2-1.7B and its safety-pretrained counterpart, SafeLM-1.7B. As demonstrated by Maini et al. (2026) and shown in Fig. 2, initializing from the safety-pretrained model (SafeLM-1.7B) produces a substantially more robust safety-aligned model under subsequent fine-tuning on GSM8K (Cobbe et al., 2021).
We report the average ASR across four safety benchmarks, HarmBench (Mazeika et al., 2024), TDC (Maloyan et al., 2024), JailbreakBench (Chao et al., 2024), and AdvBench (Zou et al., 2023), before (BF) and after (AF) fine-tuning

Although both aligned models (initialized from the safety-pretrained SafeLM-1.7B and the standard-pretrained SmolLM2-1.7B, respectively) achieve satisfactory safety before fine-tuning, the SmolLM2-1.7B-initialized model exhibits a sharp increase in harmful compliance after fine-tuning, whereas the SafeLM-1.7B-initialized model largely preserves its safety.
This native-safety advantage is perhaps expected: compared with safety post-training, safety pretraining leverages a substantially larger and richer corpus of safety-relevant data and incorporates safety learning throughout training from scratch. On the downside, the model initialized from the safety-pretrained SafeLM-1.7B exhibits substantially lower general utility than its standard-pretrained SmolLM2-1.7B counterpart, even after the same post-training procedure. This highlights a nontrivial trade-off: safety pretraining can yield robust native safety, but potentially at the cost of weaker general-purpose capabilities.

Problem of our interest: Native safety reuse through knowledge distillation.
As motivated above, safety pretraining provides a clear native-safety advantage, but this advantage comes with costs. For example, instilling native safety into each model through pretraining is prohibitively expensive. In addition, safety pretraining produces a model with robust safety expertise at the expense of its general utility, as shown in Fig. 2.
These limitations motivate us to ask whether the benefits of safety pretraining can be reused beyond the model in which they were originally acquired.

Thus, we propose the native safety reuse problem: Can the robust safety acquired through an expensive safety-pretraining procedure (even by a relatively small model such as SafeLM-1.7B) be transferred to standard-pretrained student models, including larger and more capable ones, while preserving their general utility?
We cast this problem as teacher–student distillation, where the safety-pretrained model serves as the teacher and a standard-pretrained model as the student. That is,

 $\displaystyle\begin{array}[]{cc}\displaystyle\minimize_{{\bm{\theta}}}&\ell_{\text{distill}}(\bm{\theta};{\bm{\theta}}_{\mathrm{t}},\mathcal{D}),\quad\text{ ${\bm{\theta}}_{\mathrm{t}}$: small-scale safety-pretrained teacher}\end{array}$ 

where $\ell_{\text{distill}}$ is a distillation loss to be designed later, and $\mathcal{D}$ is the associated distillation dataset.
In the next section, we elaborate on how to solve the native safety reuse problem in (3) and identify the distillation principles needed to transfer robust native safety to the student model.

## 4 Native Safety “Travels” On-Policy

Distillation principle for safety reuse: Why on-policy rather than off-policy?
To instantiate the distillation objective in (3) for native safety reuse, we first determine whether distillation should be off-policy or on-policy. Here, off-policy distillation trains the student on responses generated by the teacher (Hinton et al., 2015; Kim and Rush, 2016; Gou et al., 2021; Hsieh et al., 2023; Xu et al., 2024),
whereas on-policy distillation trains on responses sampled from the student’s evolving policy, with the teacher’s next-token distribution supervising the student at each position (Li et al., 2026b; Agarwal et al., 2024; Gu et al., 2023; Lin et al., 2020; Fu et al., 2026b; Zhu et al., 2026; Song and Zheng, 2026; Yu et al., 2026).
We prioritize on-policy for two reasons. First, querying the teacher along student-generated prefixes enables its native safety to correct the student’s own continuations, including trajectories that the teacher itself would be unlikely to generate (Agarwal et al., 2024; Fu et al., 2026a). Second, token-level supervision provides the student with the teacher’s full predictive distribution at each position, yielding a richer safety signal than a single sampled teacher response.

Concretely, let $\pi_{{\bm{\theta}}}$ denote the student’s generative policy parameterized by ${\bm{\theta}}$ , and let $\pi_{{\bm{\theta}}_{\mathrm{t}}}$ denote the frozen teacher policy.
For on-policy distillation, the student samples a response
 $\hat{y}=(\hat{y}_{1},\ldots,\hat{y}_{L})\sim\pi_{{\bm{\theta}}}(\cdot|x)$ given a prompt $x$ , where $L$ is the generation length.
Both the student and teacher then evaluate the probability given each student-generated prefix $\hat{y}_{<l}$ at token position $l$ :
 $p_{l}(v)=\pi_{{\bm{\theta}}}(v|x,\hat{y}_{<l})$ and $q_{l}(v)=\pi_{{\bm{\theta}}_{\mathrm{t}}}(v|x,\hat{y}_{<l})$ 
for every token $v$ in their shared vocabulary $\mathcal{V}$ . The on-policy loss minimizes the reverse Kullback–Leibler (KL) divergence between the student and teacher distributions along the student-generated trajectory:

 $\displaystyle\displaystyle\ell_{\mathrm{on}}({\bm{\theta}};{\bm{\theta}}_{\mathrm{t}},\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{safe}})=\mathbb{E}_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\sim\ \mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{safe}}\\
\hat{y}\sim\pi_{{\bm{\theta}}}(\cdot|x)\end{subarray}}\left[\sum_{l}D_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\left(p_{l}\,\|\,q_{l}\right)\right],$ 

(2)

where
 $D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p\,\|\,q)=\sum_{v\in\mathcal{V}}p(v)\log\!\left(p(v)/q(v)\right)$ 
for distributions $p$ and $q$ defined over the same token set, and $\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{safe}}$ denotes the safety dataset used to distill knowledge from the teacher ${\bm{\theta}}_{\mathrm{t}}$ . It comprises SafeWeb, RefuseWeb, and MoralEducation, the safety-pretraining corpora introduced in Maini et al. (2026) to train the safety teacher.
Furthermore, we adopt the top- $k$ on-policy strategy (Li et al., 2026b): at each position $l$ , we restrict the divergence to the student’s $k$ most probable token candidates within the vocabulary $\mathcal{V}$ and renormalize both $p_{l}$ and $q_{l}$ over this set.

In contrast, off-policy distillation evaluates $p_{l}$ and $q_{l}$ along teacher-generated rather than student-generated trajectories. That is, replacing
 $\hat{y}\sim\pi_{{\bm{\theta}}}(\cdot\mid x)$ 
with
 $\hat{y}\sim\pi_{{\bm{\theta}}_{\mathrm{t}}}(\cdot\mid x)$ 
yields the off-policy objective, which otherwise follows the same form as (2) but uses the forward KL divergence
 $D_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\left(q_{l}\,\|\,p_{l}\right)$ .
Thus, on-policy lets the safety-pretrained teacher supervise prefixes actually encountered by the evolving student,
whereas off-policy restricts supervision to trajectories generated by the teacher.

As shown, on-policy distillation from the safety-pretrained teacher outperforms its off-policy counterpart, achieving a $\Delta\mathrm{ASR}$ closest to that of SafePre while largely preserving student utility.
Indeed, off-policy distillation can yield even weaker safety robustness than the safety-post-training baseline.

Fig. 3(Right) explains this difference by examining how teacher supervision reshapes the student’s token predictions on harmful prompts. Along student-generated prefixes, on-policy distillation shifts substantially more probability mass from harmful continuations toward safe-oriented tokens than off-policy distillation. Specifically, for the harmful tokens shown on the x-axis of Fig. 3(Right), on-policy distillation suppresses their probabilities far more strongly than off-policy distillation, nearly matching SafePre, whereas safety post-training has little effect.
This is because
off-policy supervises only teacher-preferred trajectories, potentially missing unsafe states encountered by the student. But on-policy instead queries the teacher on student-generated trajectories, letting its native safety directly correct the student’s failure modes.

Extending native safety reuse across model architectures.
We next improve the scalability of native safety reuse by extending it to larger students from model families different from that of the safety-pretrained teacher. The central challenge is tokenizer mismatch: the teacher and student may tokenize the same response into different sequences, and their predictive distributions are defined over different vocabularies (Boizard et al., 2024; Niu et al., 2026; Sun et al., 2026). Let $\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{s}}$ and $\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{t}}$ denote the student and teacher vocabularies, respectively. In the cross-tokenizer setting, $\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{s}}\neq\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{t}}$ , whereas (2) assumes a shared vocabulary, $\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{s}}=\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{t}}=\mathcal{V}$ .

To address this cross-tokenizer challenge, we adopt General On-policy Logit Distillation (GOLD) (pati~no2025_unlocking_on_policy_distillation_for_any_model_family). GOLD first partitions the student and teacher token sequences into $J$ pairs of aligned groups, with each pair covering the same span of response text. It then merges the token distributions within each group using the chain rule of probability. Specifically, $\tilde{p}_{j}(v)$ , for $v\in\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{s}}$ , represents the student’s probability of emitting token $v$ followed by the remaining generated tokens in group $j$ , while $\tilde{r}_{j}(u)$ , for $u\in\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{t}}$ , denotes the corresponding teacher probability under its own tokenization.
Here, $j$ indexes aligned group pairs spanning the same text, which replace individual token positions as the units of comparison. Within each group, token probabilities are combined using the chain rule.
Tokens sharing identical strings across vocabularies are aligned one-to-one as matched; all others are unmatched. Superscripts $\mathrm{m}$ and $\mathrm{u}$ restrict $\tilde{p}_{j}$ and $\tilde{r}_{j}$ to the corresponding token sets. The resulting cross-tokenizer objective compares matched-token distributions using the Jensen–Shannon (JS) divergence, $D_{\mathrm{JS}}(\tilde{p}_{j}^{\mathrm{m}}\|\tilde{r}_{j}^{\mathrm{m}})$ , and unmatched-token distributions using the Universal Logit Distillation (ULD) distance (Boizard et al., 2024), $D_{\mathrm{ULD}}(\tilde{p}_{j}^{\mathrm{u}}\|\tilde{r}_{j}^{\mathrm{u}})$ . The latter computes the sum of absolute differences between the two probability vectors after sorting them in descending order and padding the shorter vector with zeros. The GOLD objective is then given by

 $\displaystyle\displaystyle\ell_{\mathrm{GOLD}}({\bm{\theta}};{\bm{\theta}}_{\mathrm{t}},\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{safe}})=\mathbb{E}_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\sim\ \mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{safe}}\\
\hat{y}\sim\pi_{{\bm{\theta}}}(\cdot|x)\end{subarray}}\left[\sum_{j}\bigl(w_{1}\,D_{\mathrm{JS}}(\tilde{p}_{j}^{\mathrm{m}}\|\tilde{r}_{j}^{\mathrm{m}})+w_{2}\,D_{\mathrm{ULD}}(\tilde{p}_{j}^{\mathrm{u}}\|\tilde{r}_{j}^{\mathrm{u}})\bigr)\right],$ 

(3)

where $w_{1}>0$ and $w_{2}=1-w_{1}$ control the contributions of the matched- and unmatched-token terms, respectively.
In principle, cross-tokenizer distillation can instead use vanilla ULD alone, i.e., setting $w_{1}=0$ and $w_{2}=1$ in (3). Compared to GOLD, vanilla ULD pairs student and teacher tokens directly by position, without explicit sequence or vocabulary alignment.
Appendix A further explains why ULD alone cannot effectively transfer native safety.

Preserving student utility during distillation. The safety-pretrained teacher is a base model trained on plain text rather than an instruction-tuned model. The target student, however, must retain the instruction-following ability and general capabilities associated with its standard post-training pipeline. We therefore augment the safety-distillation objective, implemented through on-policy distillation or GOLD, with a utility-preservation loss on the student.

Leveraging the post-training pipeline associated with the safety-pretrained teacher in Maini et al. (2026), we introduce the utility-preservation component as the cross-entropy loss on responses from the instruction-tuning dataset $\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{instr}}$ , including UltraChat-200k (Ding et al., 2023), WildGuardMix (Han et al., 2024), and WildJailbreak (Jiang et al., 2024),
using the student model ${\bm{\theta}}$ , denoted by $\ell_{\mathrm{util}}({\bm{\theta}};\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{instr}})$ .
The overall safety-distillation objective in (3) is therefore given by

 $\displaystyle\displaystyle\ell_{\mathrm{distill}}({\bm{\theta}};{\bm{\theta}}_{\mathrm{t}},\mathcal{D})=\alpha\,\ell_{\mathrm{on}\text{ or }\mathrm{GOLD}}({\bm{\theta}};{\bm{\theta}}_{\mathrm{t}},\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{safe}})+(1-\alpha)\,\ell_{\mathrm{util}}({\bm{\theta}};\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{instr}}),$ 

(4)

where ${\bm{\theta}}$ is initialized from the target standard-pretrained student. The safety-distillation term is instantiated as $\ell_{\mathrm{on}}$ in (2) when the teacher and student share a tokenizer, and as $\ell_{\mathrm{GOLD}}$ in (3) when their tokenizers differ. Here,
 $\alpha\in[0,1]$ balances native safety transfer against utility preservation.
We select $\alpha=0.7$ by the safety–utility trade-off observed in our experiments (Fig. A2 in Appendix B).

## 5 Experiments

## 5.1 Experimental Setup

Data-model setup.
As in Sec. 3, we use the safety-pretrained SafeLM-1.7B (Maini et al., 2026) as the teacher and consider three standard-pretrained students: SmolLM2-1.7B (Allal et al., 2025) (i.e., the teacher’s standard-pretrained counterpart), and the larger Llama-3.2-3B and Llama-3-8B (Grattafiori et al., 2024) from a different model family, with a different tokenizer from the teacher (i.e., the cross-tokenizer setting).
Safety distillation draws prompts from $\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{safe}}$ , comprising SafeWeb, RefuseWeb, and MoralEducation (Maini et al., 2026). Utility preservation during distillation in (4) uses $\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{instr}}$ , comprising UltraChat-200k (Ding et al., 2023), WildGuardMix (Han et al., 2024), and WildJailbreak (Jiang et al., 2024).
The safety-post-training baseline (SafePost in Tab. 1) is trained on the same data but initialized from a standard-pretrained base model.

Distillation training and baselines.

## 6 Conclusion, Limitations, and Future Work

We ask whether safety acquired during pretraining can be reused by standard-pretrained students. On-policy distillation from a safety-pretrained teacher yields students that retain better robustness after subsequent fine-tuning than their safety-post-trained counterparts, while attaining higher general utility than the teacher. Cross-tokenizer alignment extends the approach to larger students from another model family, and application studies suggest benefits for VLM safety and pretraining-induced native unlearnability.

The limited availability of safety-pretrained models restricts our study to a single teacher. Although native safety transfers across model scales, architectures, and modalities, the distilled students do not fully recover the teacher’s robustness: a gap remains under cross-family transfer, and the advantage over safety post-training vanishes under heavily harmful fine-tuning. In addition, on-policy distillation requires access to safety-pretraining prompts, while our VLM and unlearning results serve as initial cross-domain application studies.

Future work could investigate more diverse and larger safety-pretrained teachers, improve transfer fidelity across tokenizers and under strongly harmful adaptation, and evaluate robustness to broader model modifications, including continual instruction tuning, preference optimization, model merging, pruning, and quantization. Other promising directions include logit-free native safety reuse, principled teacher and data selection, a theoretical characterization of transferability, and broader validation in multimodal safety and machine unlearning.

## Ethics Statement

Safety behavior can change after a model is fine-tuned. This work studies whether safety acquired during pretraining can be reused by other models while retaining robustness to such adaptation. The language-model evaluation uses harmful requests from HarmBench, TDC, JailbreakBench, and AdvBench; the harmful fine-tuning evaluation uses unsafe BeaverTails examples; the vision-language application uses harmful VLGuard requests; and the unlearning application uses WMDP-Bio, which contains hazardous biological knowledge. Findings about when safety degrades or forgotten knowledge recovers are dual-use: they can inform defenses, but could also help identify vulnerable models or adaptation settings. Our results describe behavior under the tested protocols, not a guarantee of deployment safety or complete removal of hazardous knowledge.

## Appendix A Cross-Tokenizer Sequence Alignment

Fig. A1 compares how ULD and GOLD align the token sequences of the same refusal. The teacher tokenizes “ammonium” as one token, while the student uses “ammon” and “ium”; subsequent ULD pairs therefore cover different text, and the longer student sequence is truncated to the teacher’s length. At each paired position, ULD independently sorts the teacher’s probability vector over $\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{t}}$ and the student’s over $\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{s}}$ in descending order, pads the shorter vector with zeros, and computes an $L_{1}$ distance between probabilities of the same rank (Boizard et al., 2024). ULD uses probability ranks without matching token identities.
GOLD aligns groups covering the same span of response text. Within each group, it applies $D_{\mathrm{JS}}$ to matched-token distributions and $D_{\mathrm{ULD}}$ to unmatched-token distributions, as defined in Eq. (3).

Figure A1: Sequence alignment across tokenizers on the refusal “I cannot help you build a bomb with ammonium nitrate.”, tokenized by the teacher’s tokenizer (SafeLM-1.7B) and the student’s (Llama-3.2-3B); the shared prefix is omitted. ULD pairs tokens by position, so the pairs are wrong from the first word that the two tokenizers split differently, and the student’s trailing tokens are dropped. GOLD first merges tokens into groups covering the same text and then compares the groups, summing the log-probabilities of the student’s pieces within each group. The quantitative comparison is in Tab. 3.

## Appendix C Additional Training Details

## C.1 Distillation Training Details

Eq. (4) jointly optimizes the safety-distillation loss $\ell_{\mathrm{on}}$ or $\ell_{\mathrm{GOLD}}$ on prompts $x\sim\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{safe}}$ and the utility-preservation loss $\ell_{\mathrm{util}}$ on $\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{instr}}$ . The student samples on-policy rollouts $\hat{y}\sim\pi_{{\bm{\theta}}}(\cdot\mid x)$ , and the frozen teacher $\pi_{{\bm{\theta}}_{\mathrm{t}}}$ provides token-level supervision along these rollouts.

We use a learning rate of $10^{-5}$ . Student optimization uses AdamW with learning-rate warmup followed by cosine decay. An iteration denotes one optimizer step after gradient accumulation. Each prompt is capped at 256 tokens. For the same prompt $x$ , the student independently samples two responses from its current policy $\pi_{{\bm{\theta}}}(\cdot\mid x)$ . Each response is an on-policy rollout with at most 256 new tokens. For the same-size student, the on-policy divergence is restricted to the student’s top- $k$ candidate tokens at each generated token position, with $k=16$ . For cross-tokenizer students, we use the GOLD loss defined in Eq. (3). The teacher remains frozen throughout training.

## C.2 Subsequent Fine-Tuning Training Details

Fine-tuning in the robustness evaluations. For the results in Tabs. 2 and 3, each model is fine-tuned on the GSM8K training set for one epoch. The objective follows Qi et al. (2024):

 $\arg\min_{\Delta\theta}\sum_{i=1}^{m}-\log p(a_{i}\mid[s_{i},u_{i}];\theta+\Delta\theta).$ 

(A1)

Here, $u_{i}$ is the GSM8K question, $a_{i}$ is its target answer, $s_{i}$ denotes the system prompt, and $\theta$ is the model before fine-tuning. Prompt tokens are masked, and the implemented loss averages cross-entropy over supervised answer tokens.
For the robustness evaluation, GSM8K fine-tuning uses AdamW with cosine decay, a learning rate of $10^{-5}$ , and a batch size of 4.

Harmful fine-tuning. For the study in Fig. 4, unsafe question–answer pairs from the BeaverTails training set replace a specified fraction of the GSM8K fine-tuning examples. The total number of training examples remains fixed, and fine-tuning on GSM8K serves as the baseline. The answer tokens are supervised for examples from both sources.

## C.3 Safety Evaluation Protocol

For the robustness evaluations in Tabs. 2 and 3, responses to HarmBench, TDC, JailbreakBench, and AdvBench are generated with greedy decoding and a limit of 256 new tokens. The HarmBench classifier labels each response as harmful or safe, and ASR is the percentage labeled harmful within each benchmark.

For the harmful fine-tuning experiment in Fig. 4, responses to held-out BeaverTails prompts are judged by the BeaverTails moderation model.

## Appendix E Cross-Modal Distillation Protocol

In the vision-language application, the Qwen2.5-VL-3B-Instruct student samples text-only on-policy rollouts from prompts $x\sim\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{safe}}$ . The text-only SafeLM-1.7B teacher scores these rollouts without seeing an image. The vision-language utility loss uses cross-entropy on the VLGuard training set and general visual instruction examples. Training keeps the vision encoder frozen and updates the remaining student parameters.

Safety is evaluated on the VLGuard test set before and after subsequent fine-tuning, following the Safety Mirage (Chen et al., 2026) evaluation pipeline. Subsequent fine-tuning on ScienceQA uses training examples with images, questions, and lettered answer options. Each target response contains the answer followed by its explanation. Utility is evaluated on ScienceQA and TextVQA. Both the distilled student and the SafePost baseline are trained on the VLGuard training set.

## Appendix F Native Unlearnability Evaluation Protocol

The unlearning application uses the Deep Ignorance teacher and Zephyr-7B-beta student described in Sec. 5. The prompts $x\sim\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{safe}}$ in Eq. (4) are replaced with prompts from the WMDP-Bio forget corpus. We use GOLD for distillation.

The post-hoc RMU baseline is the RMU-unlearned Zephyr-7B-beta checkpoint released by Li et al. (2024). Its objective combines a forget loss, which redirects activations on hazardous text, with a retain loss, which preserves the frozen model’s activations on benign text:

 $\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{L}_{\text{forget}}+\beta\cdot\mathcal{L}_{\text{retain}}.$ 

(A2)

The $\beta$ in Eq. (A2) weights RMU’s retain loss.

Forgetting is evaluated using WMDP-Bio cloze accuracy (O’Brien et al., 2026). The question is shown without its multiple-choice options. Each of the four candidate answer strings receives a conditional log-likelihood score given the question. The score is normalized by the number of characters in the candidate answer, and the highest-scoring candidate is selected.

To evaluate the robustness of forgetting, the RMU-unlearned and distilled Zephyr-7B-beta models are fine-tuned on the GSM8K training set for four epochs with a learning rate of $2\times 10^{-6}$ . Fig. 5 shows checkpoints after 0, 0.5, 1, 2, and 4 epochs.

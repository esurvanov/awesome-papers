## Large Flow Language Models

Seunghoon Hong

Abstract

Continuous flow language models offer a path to parallel generation with tools for control and acceleration unavailable to discrete diffusion, yet effective techniques for building them at the billion-parameter scale remain less established.
We present Lafa (Large flow language model), a fast, high-performing flow language model scaled to 8B parameters in a data-efficient manner.
Lafa is a blockwise flow model with causal attention on the prefix and bidirectional, asynchronous flow within each block.
This design enables efficient adaptation of pretrained autoregressive (AR) models and fast inference via prefix caching and blockwise parallel decoding.
We train Lafa at 0.5B, 7B, and 8B scales with fewer than 30B tokens, more efficient than previous diffusion models, and evaluate it on instruction following, math, and coding, where the learned flow matches or surpasses the quality of AR and can be distilled into flow maps for one- and few-step generation.
Notably, flow training preserves or even improves pretrained AR capabilities via a multi-token prediction effect, which we leverage in a self-speculative decoding scheme that verifies flow-based drafts autoregressively within a single model.
This yields 2.54 $\times$ throughput of an AR model at comparable quality, outperforming multi-token prediction and discrete diffusion methods.
Our findings show that continuous flows can serve as a scalable foundation for language modeling.

## Introduction

Figure 1: Large flow language models.
Lafa 7B attains 2.02–2.54 $\times$ the throughput of an AR model at a matched or better quality, outperforming existing MTP and diffusion-based approaches.

Today’s large language models are predominantly trained as autoregressive (AR) models that predict the next token from the preceding text [1, 2, 3].
The parallelism of the transformer architecture and teacher forcing [4, 5] allows these models to be trained efficiently at the scale of billions of parameters and trillions of data tokens.
However, their generation is inherently sequential, producing one token per forward pass, which makes inference latency a bottleneck.
To accelerate inference, prior work has explored approaches to parallel generation, namely multi-token prediction (MTP) [6, 7, 8], discrete diffusion models [9, 10, 11], and continuous flow language models [12, 13, 14].

Distinguished from the other approaches, flow language models perform parallel generation by integrating a continuous flow from noise to token representations [12].
By operating in a continuous space, they can leverage powerful techniques developed for controllable and accelerated generation of images and video, many of which are unavailable to discrete methods [13, 14].
Yet, existing flow language models have been demonstrated at small scales and on simple tasks [13, 15, 14, 16, 17], and a prior attempt of scaling has identified challenges in training inefficiency [18].
Together, effective techniques for building performant flow language models at the billion-parameter scale remain less established.

In this paper, we present Lafa (Large flow language model), a fast and high-performing flow language model scaled to 8B parameters in a data-efficient manner.
Motivated by the left-to-right directionality of text [19], we design Lafa as a blockwise flow model, with causal attention on the prefix, and bidirectional but left-to-right-biased asynchronous flow within each block.
This brings its architecture and inductive bias close to AR models, enabling efficient training by adapting pretrained models.
Further, this allows fast inference via prefix caching and parallel flow decoding, which can be further distilled into flow maps for one- and few-step generation.
We train Lafa with blockwise denoising for the flow, along with AR prediction on the prefix, and find that flow training preserves or even enhances pretrained AR capabilities through a multi-token prediction effect.
We leverage this in a self-speculative decoding scheme that verifies flow-based drafts autoregressively.
With these techniques, we train Lafa across 0.5B, 7B, and 8B scales with fewer than 30B tokens, cheaper than comparable adaptation of discrete diffusion models that use up to 500B tokens [20, 21].
Across benchmarks spanning instruction following, reasoning, and coding, Lafa attains a quality–speed frontier that surpasses similarly sized AR, MTP, and discrete diffusion language models, as shown in Figure 1.

Figure 2: Overview.
(Top) Lafa learns a block denoiser that also performs AR prediction on the prefix.
(Bottom) Lafa supports flow decoding, which can be further distilled into a flow map for one-step generation.

An overview of the approach is in Figure 2.
Our main contributions are as follows:

• 

We introduce Lafa, a large flow language model that learns blockwise asynchronous flow, enabling efficient training by adapting pretrained AR models and fast inference through prefix caching and parallel flows.

• 

We identify key design choices for scalability, including the architecture and asynchronous flow formulation, training and flow map distillation objectives, and inference schemes including self-speculative decoding.

• 

We scale Lafa to 7–8B parameters with $<$ 30B tokens and show that its learned flow matches or surpasses the quality of AR and is distillable into flow maps, and its learning improves pretrained AR capabilities.

• 

By jointly leveraging the flow and AR capabilities for self-speculative decoding, Lafa attains $2.54\times$ the throughput of a strong AR baseline at matched quality, outperforming existing parallel generation methods.

Our findings show that continuous flows offer a practical, scalable foundation for parallel language modeling.

## Large flow language models

In this section, we introduce Lafa, a class of scalable and high-performing large flow language models.
We first formulate blockwise asynchronous flows, then describe the architecture, training, and inference procedures.
See Appendix A for theoretical results and Appendix B for algorithmic details.

## Block asynchronous flows

## Blockwise formulation.

We develop our method upon standard block-autoregressive factorization [22], as in block discrete diffusion [23], retaining causal generation and prefix caching across blocks.
For vocabulary $V$ , prefix ${\bf c}\in V^{N}$ , and continuation ${\bf y}\in V^{M}$ , we factorize into blocks of size $B$ (assuming $B\mid M$ ):

 $p({\bf y}\mid{\bf c})=\prod_{r=0}^{M/B-1}p\bigl({\bf y}^{rB+1:(r+1)B}\mid{\bf c},{\bf y}^{\leq rB}\bigr).$ 

(1)

## Embedded flows.

We embed each block ${\bf y}\in V^{B}$ using vocabulary embeddings ${\bf E}\in\mathbb{R}^{|V|\times d}$ as ${\bf x}_{1}\coloneqq({\bf E}_{{\bf y}^{1}},\ldots,{\bf E}_{{\bf y}^{B}})^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{B\times d}$ .
This reduces the per-token dimension from $|V|$ of one-hot embeddings [13] to $d$ , making training more scalable.
Using flow matching [24, 25], we construct a conditional probability path $p_{t}(\cdot\mid{\bf c})$ , $t\in[0,1]$ , from Gaussian noise ${\bf x}_{0}\sim\mathsf{N}(0,\sigma^{2}I)$ with $\sigma>0$ to the distribution of embedded data ${\bf x}_{1}$ .

## Asynchronous flows.

Standard flow matching uses the interpolant $I_{t}\coloneqq(1-t){\bf x}_{0}+t{\bf x}_{1}$ , where $I_{t}\sim p_{t}$ , with a shared time $t$ for all tokens.
To introduce a left-to-right bias within each block to match the inherent directionality of text [19], we use tokenwise time [26, 27, 28, 29, 30], allowing earlier positions to progress faster.
A tokenwise time is a function $\tau:[0,1]\to[0,1]^{B}$ , differentiable and increasing in each coordinate, mapping a base time $t$ to a tokenwise time $\tau^{i}(t)$ for each $i=1,...,B$ , satisfying $\tau(0)={\bf 0}$ and $\tau(1)={\bf 1}$ .
Given $\tau$ , we can construct a probability path $p_{t}(\cdot\mid{\bf c})$ in base time $t$ through an interpolant $I_{t}$ defined positionwise [27]:

 $I_{t}^{i}\coloneqq(1-\tau^{i}(t))\,{\bf x}_{0}^{i}+\tau^{i}(t)\,{\bf x}_{1}^{i},\qquad i\in[B],\qquad I_{t}\sim p_{t}(\cdot\mid{\bf c}),\qquad t\in[0,1].$ 

(2)

For each given ${\bf c}$ , the probability path $p_{t}(\cdot\mid{\bf c})$ admits a velocity field $b_{t}(\cdot;{\bf c}):\mathbb{R}^{B\times d}\to\mathbb{R}^{B\times d}$ that transports a block of noise to clean data embeddings through the following ODE [27], which can be used for generation:

 $\dot{\bf x}_{t}=b_{t}({\bf x}_{t};{\bf c}),\qquad{\bf x}_{0}\sim p_{0},\qquad{\bf x}_{t}\sim p_{t}(\cdot\mid{\bf c}),\qquad t\in[0,1].$ 

(3)

If $\tau$ progresses faster for the earlier tokens (i.e., $\tau^{i}(t)>\tau^{j}(t)$ for $i<j$ ), both interpolation and generation will reveal earlier tokens faster.
In practice, we learn $\tau$ from data instead of fixing it, with details in Section 2.2.

## Block denoiser.

Instead of learning the asynchronous velocity $b_{t}(\cdot;{\bf c})$ directly, following related work [12, 13], we learn a block denoiser $D_{t}(\cdot;{\bf c}):\mathbb{R}^{B\times d}\to(\Delta^{|V|-1})^{B}$ that predicts clean tokens via tokenwise classification:

 $D_{t}({\bf x};{\bf c})^{i}\coloneqq p({\bf y}^{i}=\cdot\mid{\bf c},I_{t}={\bf x}),\qquad i\in[B],$ 

(4)

where $\Delta^{|V|-1}$ is the space of probability vectors of length $|V|$ .
Notably, learning the denoiser recovers the velocity through the following identity that involves embedding interpolation, which we prove in Section A.1:

 $b_{t}({\bf x};{\bf c})^{i}=\frac{\dot{\tau}^{i}(t)}{1-\tau^{i}(t)}(D_{t}({\bf x};{\bf c})^{i}{\bf E}-{\bf x}^{i}).$ 

(5)

The formula scales the direction from noisy input ${\bf x}$ to its denoising $D_{t}({\bf x};{\bf c}){\bf E}$ by the tokenwise rate of progress $\dot{\tau}(t)$ , thereby implementing the asynchronous progress prescribed by tokenwise time $\tau$ .

## Learning the flow from an autoregressive model

As our formulation keeps a causal structure, we learn the blockwise flow by adapting a pretrained AR model $f$ into a block denoiser.
The challenge is parallel denoising of continuous, noisy embeddings while retaining AR prediction and prefix caching.
Our adaptation combines tokenwise time conditioning, block-bidirectional attention, and constrained updates to pretrained embeddings with joint AR and denoising training.

## Tokenwise time conditioning.

We feed $f$ the embedded prefix ${\bf c}$ followed by the noisy block ${\bf x}$ , and condition each position on its corruption level through adaptive layer normalization (adaLN) [31].
Prefix positions always receive time $1$ , indicating the absence of noise, while each block position $i\in[B]$ receives its tokenwise time $\tau^{i}(t)$ .
This lets a single forward pass handle both the clean prefix and asynchronous denoising within the block.
We initialize adaLN to the identity, so that adding time conditioning initially leaves the pretrained computation unchanged.
We denote the resulting model by $f_{t}({\bf x};{\bf c})$ .

Figure 3: Attention mask for block-bidirectional attention.

## Block-bidirectional attention.

We make attention bidirectional within the noisy block, allowing partially denoised later tokens to inform earlier ones (Figure 3).
Block positions also attend to the entire clean prefix, whose attention remains causal and excludes the noisy block [32, 33].
Together with conditioning prefix positions on time $1$ , this makes their keys and values independent of the block state $({\bf x},t)$ , allowing reuse across denoising steps and subsequent blocks.
The prefix outputs retain their AR role: $f_{t}({\bf x};{\bf c})^{i}$ predicts ${\bf c}^{i+1}$ for $i<N$ , and the first block token ${\bf y}^{1}$ for $i=N$ .
We write these block-independent predictions as $f({\bf c})^{i}$ and use them to verify the denoiser’s drafts in self-speculative decoding (Section 2.3).
This requires retaining or improving pretrained AR capabilities, which we achieve through training.
We find that bidirectional attention within blocks outperforms causal (Table 3).

## Shared and learned embeddings.

We initialize the vocabulary embeddings ${\bf E}$ from the pretrained model, share them between the prefix and the embedded flow, and train them jointly with the denoiser.
Unconstrained updates, however, can inflate embedding norms relative to the fixed noise scale $\sigma$ , making denoising easier by reducing the effective corruption [12, 13].
We therefore fix the L2 norm of each embedding ${\bf E}_{v}$ to its pretrained value and learn only its direction, allowing adaptation to the flow while preserving the embedding scale.
This outperforms both frozen and unconstrained embeddings (Table 3).

## Training loss.

We train the block denoiser $\hat{D}_{t}({\bf x};{\bf c})^{i}\coloneqq f_{t}({\bf x};{\bf c})^{N+i}$ jointly with AR on the prefix.
For each training pair $({\bf c},{\bf y})$ , we draw $t\sim\mathsf{U}[0,1]$ and noise ${\bf x}_{0}\sim p_{0}$ , and build $I_{t}$ via Equation 2 with ${\bf x}_{1}^{i}={\bf E}_{{\bf y}^{i}}$ .
We minimize

 $\mathcal{L}(\hat{D})\coloneqq\mathbb{E}_{{\bf c},{\bf y},t,{\bf x}_{0}}\Bigg[\underbrace{\sum_{i=1}^{N}-\log f({\bf c})^{i}_{{\bf c}^{i+1}}}_{\text{AR cross entropy}}+\underbrace{\sum_{i=1}^{B}-\log\hat{D}_{t}(I_{t};{\bf c})^{i}_{{\bf y}^{i}}}_{\text{denoising cross entropy}}+\underbrace{\sum_{i=1}^{B}\bigl|\hat{D}_{t}(I_{t};{\bf c})^{i}{\bf E}-{\bf E}_{{\bf y}^{i}}\bigr|^{2}}_{\text{embedding squared error}}\Bigg],$ 

(6)

where ${\bf c}^{N+1}\coloneqq{\bf y}^{1}$ , and the AR and denoising predictions share the transformer parameters.
The denoising cross entropy supervises token probabilities Equation 4, while the squared error supervises the mean embedding that determines the flow velocity Equation 5.
Combining these denoising losses improves flow accuracy, and we unexpectedly find that AR supervision improves it further (Table 3).
Notably, joint training also improves AR accuracy over matched AR SFT (Section 3.2), consistent with the benefits of multi-token prediction [6, 8, 34].

Figure 4: Parallelized training over a sequence composed of two blocks of size three.
The uncorrupted sequence uses causal attention and the AR loss, while each noisy block attends to the preceding clean tokens and bidirectionally within itself, and uses the denoising losses.

## Parallel training.

To avoid recomputing overlapping prefixes, we train all blocks of a sequence in parallel.
Following prior work on block diffusion [23, 32, 33, 35], we pair each clean training sequence with a copy partitioned into noisy blocks of size $B$ (Figure 4).
Clean positions attend causally, while each noisy block attends only to the preceding clean tokens and bidirectionally within itself.
A single forward pass then produces AR predictions for the clean sequence and denoising predictions for every block (details in Section B.1).

Figure 5: Tokenwise time for each block position learned by Lafa 7B.

## Learning tokenwise time.

We learn the tokenwise time $\tau:[0,1]\to[0,1]^{B}$ from the denoising loss during training.
Inspired by the loss-aware time warping of CDCD [12], we construct each $\tau^{i}$ as the inverse CDF of the normalized denoising loss across noise levels, estimated online for position $i$ (details in Section B.1).
This slows progress where the loss is high, so uniform sampling and discretization of base time $t$ allocate more training samples and integration steps to difficult noise levels.
The learned schedules generally satisfy $\tau^{i}(t)>\tau^{j}(t)$ for $i<j$ (Figure 5), with earlier positions denoised ahead of later ones, even though this left-to-right bias is not manually imposed.

## Self-conditioning.

Following prior work [14, 15, 36], we use self-conditioning [37] to improve sample quality.
With a fixed probability during training, we obtain a denoising prediction without gradients, feed it back as an additional input, and compute the loss on a second forward pass (details in Section B.2).
During flow decoding, each Euler step reuses the previous step’s prediction for conditioning, requiring no added forward.

## Flow map distillation.

Flow decoding requires multiple denoiser evaluations per block.
To reduce this cost, we distill the learned flow into a flow map $X_{s,t}$ that transports a state directly from time $s$ to $t$ [38].
For this, we make a novel extension of simplex-valued two-time denoisers [13] to asynchronous flows over vocabulary embeddings.
Specifically, $\delta_{s,t}({\bf x};{\bf c})\in(\Delta^{|V|-1})^{B}$ predicts token probabilities, which we map through ${\bf E}$ before interpolating with the noisy state ${\bf x}$ :

 $X_{s,t}({\bf x};{\bf c})^{i}\coloneqq\frac{1-\tau^{i}(t)}{1-\tau^{i}(s)}{\bf x}^{i}+\frac{\tau^{i}(t)-\tau^{i}(s)}{1-\tau^{i}(s)}\,\delta_{s,t}({\bf x};{\bf c})^{i}{\bf E},\qquad i\in[B],\quad 0\leq s<t\leq 1.$ 

(7)

This retains categorical predictions for distillation while transporting states in embedding space.
We initialize the student $\hat{\delta}_{s,t}$ from the frozen teacher $\hat{D}_{s}$ , adding an identity-initialized adaLN for the destination time $t$ .
Following semigroup-based distillation [13, 36], we match the student’s diagonal predictions at $s=t$ to the teacher $\hat{D}_{s}$ and its off-diagonal predictions at $s\neq t$ to a stop-gradient target formed by composing two shorter student transitions.
We use KL losses for both terms and additionally distill the teacher’s prefix AR predictions.
The student preserves prefix caching and supports generation in as few as one step through $\hat{X}_{0,1}$ .
The derivation and full objective are in Section A.2.

## Inference schemes

We generate each block by integrating the learned flow or evaluating its distilled flow map.
We further accelerate generation through self-speculative decoding, using the retained AR predictor $f$ to verify flow-based drafts.
All schemes reuse the prefix key-value (KV) cache and extend it with newly committed tokens.

## Flow and flow map decoding.

Starting from $\hat{\bf x}_{0}\sim p_{0}$ , we take $K$ steps on the grid of base times $t_{k}=k/K$ , which induces an asynchronous temporal grid $\tau(t_{k})\in[0,1]^{B}$ .
With ${\bf p}_{k}\coloneqq\hat{D}_{t_{k}}(\hat{\bf x}_{t_{k}};{\bf c})$ , an Euler step gives

 $\hat{\bf x}_{t_{k+1}}^{i}=\hat{\bf x}_{t_{k}}^{i}+\frac{\tau^{i}(t_{k+1})-\tau^{i}(t_{k})}{1-\tau^{i}(t_{k})}({\bf p}_{k}^{i}{\bf E}-\hat{\bf x}_{t_{k}}^{i}),\qquad\hat{\bf y}^{i}=\mathsf{argmax}({\bf p}_{K-1}^{i}),\qquad i\in[B],$ 

(8)

where the final prediction determines the generated tokens $\hat{\bf y}$ .
Each step reuses the previous prediction for self-conditioning.
We further strengthen prefix conditioning with prefix guidance [39], analogous to classifier-free guidance [40] (details in Section B.2).
This adds one prefix-free evaluation per step, which is efficient as it processes only the $B$ block tokens.
The distilled flow map model replaces each Euler step with a learned jump, which amounts to using $\hat{\delta}_{t_{k},t_{k+1}}$ in place of $\hat{D}_{t_{k}}$ in Equation 8:

 $\hat{\bf x}_{t_{k+1}}=\hat{X}_{t_{k},t_{k+1}}(\hat{\bf x}_{t_{k}};{\bf c}),\qquad\hat{\bf y}^{i}=\mathsf{argmax}\bigl(\hat{\delta}_{t_{K-1},1}(\hat{\bf x}_{t_{K-1}};{\bf c})^{i}\bigr),\qquad i\in[B].$ 

(9)

Setting $K=1$ gives one-step block generation through $\hat{X}_{0,1}$ .

## Self-speculative decoding.

We draft a block with one Euler step in tokenwise time Equation 8, without prefix guidance.
Since $\tau^{i}(0)=0$ and $\tau^{i}(1)=1$ , this reduces to a single denoiser evaluation from $\hat{\bf x}_{0}\sim p_{0}$ :

 ${\bf p}^{i}\coloneqq\hat{D}_{0}(\hat{\bf x}_{0};{\bf c})^{i},\qquad\hat{\bf x}_{1}^{i}={\bf p}^{i}{\bf E},\qquad\hat{\bf y}^{i}=\mathsf{argmax}({\bf p}^{i}),\qquad i\in[B].$ 

(10)

One-step drafts can be inconsistent across positions, so we verify them with the retained AR predictor $f$ [41].
We commit the longest draft prefix agreeing with greedy AR predictions, and if the first token disagrees, we instead commit $\mathsf{argmax}(f({\bf c})^{N})$ .
The resulting output therefore matches greedy AR decoding with $f$ .
To improve efficiency, following fused drafting and verification schemes [41], we verify the previous draft while generating candidate next drafts with one-step flow decoding in a single forward pass.
Here, each candidate conditions on a possible accepted prefix, and after verification, we keep the candidate corresponding to the verified acceptance length (details in Section B.3).
As an alternative to self-speculation, a draft may be accepted by confidence thresholding [33], which we detail in Section C.3.

## Experiments

In our experiments, we evaluate whether Lafa learns flows that reach AR quality through data-efficient adaptation, and whether flow map distillation improves few-step generation (Section 3.1).
We then examine how flow training affects AR accuracy (Section 3.2) and use the retained AR predictor for self-speculative decoding, measuring both parallelism and wall-clock throughput (Section 3.3).
Finally, we ablate the main training and inference choices (Section 3.4).
Evaluation details are in Appendix C.

## Models and data.

We build Lafa at 0.5B and 7B by adapting the corresponding Qwen2.5 Instruct checkpoints [42] as described in Section 2.
At 8B, we use Qwen3 8B Base [43], as further training of its heavily post-trained Instruct counterpart degraded performance in our experiments.
We train all models on Dolci-Instruct-SFT [44] for 5–15 epochs (9–27B tokens), using at least $18\times$ fewer training tokens than the reported budgets of Dream, LLaDA, and Efficient-DLM at comparable scales [20, 45, 21].
We use 0.5B for ablations, 7B for the main results, and 8B for scale-matched comparisons with additional baselines.

## Baselines.

We compare against multi-token prediction, block-parallel decoding, and discrete diffusion methods that represent existing approaches for parallel language modeling (Section 4).
At 0.5B, we evaluate MTP [6], DeepSeek-MTP [8], MARS [33], SBD [46], BD3-LM [23], and PTP [47].
At 7–8B, we additionally compare against Fast-dLLM v2 [48], Dream [20], LLaDA [45], and Efficient-DLM [21].
Baselines marked † are trained by us with the same AR initialization, data, and training budget as Lafa, providing controlled comparisons.
The remaining baselines use released checkpoints with different training data and budgets.
We also fine-tune each AR initialization on the same data to obtain AR SFT, our reference for both quality and speed.
Unless stated otherwise, we use each method’s native decoding protocol.

## Benchmarks.

We evaluate on six benchmarks spanning instruction following, reasoning, and code generation: IFEval (0-shot) [49], BBH (3-shot) [50], MMLU-Pro (0-shot) [51], GPQA (0-shot) [52], GSM8K (0-shot) [53], and HumanEval (0-shot) [54].
These downstream tasks assess generation quality without relying on generative perplexity, whose limitations are documented by Franca and Tong [55].
All methods are evaluated with lm-evaluation-harness [56] using each benchmark’s default prompts.
We report prompt-level strict accuracy for IFEval, pass@1 for HumanEval, and accuracy for the remaining tasks.
Generation is capped at 1024 tokens per sample, except 512 for GSM8K and 640 for HumanEval.
We report six-benchmark macro-averages for single-token accuracy and self-speculative accuracy and TPF (Tables 1, 7 and 2).
For flow and flow map decoding, throughput, and ablations, we average over IFEval, GSM8K, and HumanEval, covering instruction following, math, and coding, considering their more costly evaluations.

## Autoregressive decoding

We next examine how joint flow training affects the retained AR capability (Section 2.2), evaluating Lafa and baselines under single-token decoding.
As shown in Table 1, Lafa improves average accuracy over matched AR SFT across all three model scales (per-benchmark results in Section C.1).
It also outperforms the matched multi-token and block-parallel baselines on average at 0.5B and 7B.
At 8B, Lafa achieves similar accuracy to Efficient-DLM evaluated with single-token unmasking, while using at least $18\times$ fewer training tokens.
These results show that learning the flow can also improve AR prediction, consistent with the benefits of multi-token training [6, 8, 34].
We therefore use this retained AR predictor to verify flow-based drafts for self-speculative decoding (Section 3.3).

We measure both parallelism, in tokens committed per forward pass (TPF), and end-to-end wall-clock throughput.
Each block size uses its corresponding trained checkpoint, and self-speculation preserves that checkpoint’s greedy AR output.

## Parallelism.

Figures 7 and 2 report accuracy and TPF averaged over all six benchmarks (protocols detailed in Section C.1).
At 0.5B, Lafa with block size eight achieves about twice the TPF of DeepSeek-MTP, MARS, and PTP while attaining higher average accuracy.
At 7B, increasing the block size from four to sixteen nearly doubles TPF while keeping accuracy close to AR SFT.
With block size four, Lafa exceeds DeepSeek-MTP in both accuracy and TPF; with block size eight, it matches the accuracy of MARS with block size four at about $1.5\times$ its TPF.
At 8B, Lafa achieves similar accuracy and TPF to Efficient-DLM despite using at least $18\times$ fewer training tokens.
Across scales, Lafa achieves approximately 2.5–5 TPF while remaining close to or above AR SFT accuracy.

## Wall-clock throughput.

Since forward-pass costs differ across methods, we also measure end-to-end throughput at 7–8B scales on a single H100 GPU with batch size one, using each method’s native decoder.
Figure 8 reports accuracy and throughput in tokens per second (TPS), averaged over IFEval, GSM8K, and HumanEval (settings and per-benchmark results in Section C.2).
With block size sixteen, Lafa 7B achieves $2.54\times$ the throughput of 7B AR SFT at comparable average accuracy.
With block size four, it is about twice as fast while also more accurate on average.
DeepSeek-MTP and MARS match or exceed AR SFT accuracy but achieve at most $1.4\times$ its throughput.
Diffusion baselines fall below 7B AR SFT accuracy, faster settings showing larger losses.
Thus, while flow decoding requires multiple integration steps to reach AR quality (Section 3.1), verifying one-step flow drafts enables substantially faster generation at comparable quality.

Accepting drafts by confidence thresholding instead of AR verification trades accuracy for speed.
We therefore use self-speculation as our default fast decoding mode and report confidence decoding results in Section C.3.

## Related work

In this section, we review existing representative approaches for parallel language modeling.

## Block-autoregressive language models.

Block-autoregressive models reduce sequential generation by producing blocks of tokens conditioned on preceding blocks.
Approaches to parallel block generation include multi-token prediction (MTP) heads or modules [6, 8], and predictors that take masked or noisy blocks as input [23, 46, 33].
Many parallel decoders predict tokens independently within an update, which can degrade quality when many tokens are committed at once [13].
Some methods mitigate this through AR verification or confidence-based acceptance of a draft prefix [41, 8, 33].
Some recent methods learn deterministic noise-to-token maps that can capture joint dependencies without independent output sampling [47, 57].

## Discrete diffusion language models.

Discrete diffusion models generate text by reversing a discrete corruption process, such as masking or random token replacement, through iterative denoising [58, 59, 60, 61].
As with other parallel decoders, independent sampling within each update can limit quality in few-step generation [62, 63].
These models have been scaled to billions of parameters [20, 21, 45, 11], with generation performed over entire sequences or individual blocks.
Block diffusion [23] combines iterative denoising within each block with autoregressive generation and prefix caching across blocks.

## Continuous diffusion and flow language models.

Continuous diffusion and flow models generate text through noisy continuous representations, including token embeddings, simplex representations, and binary encodings [64, 12, 65, 28, 66, 37, 67, 18].
Continuous flows transport joint sequence representations and admit flow maps that can be learned for few-step generation [38, 13].
Recent work has demonstrated competitive generation quality and improved few-step decoding [13, 15, 14, 16, 17, 68, 36], while a few recent efforts have explored billion-parameter scales [69, 70, 71, 72].
Related approaches also study autoregression and diffusion over autoencoded continuous representations [73, 74, 75, 76].
Realizing the benefits of continuous flows at large and practical scale calls for data-efficient training and fast inference.
Lafa addresses both by aligning blockwise asynchronous flows with the causal structure of pretrained AR models.
This enables efficient adaptation and prefix caching, while retained AR prediction supports self-speculative generation.

## Conclusion

We introduced Lafa, a blockwise flow language model scaled to 8B parameters by adapting pretrained AR models with fewer than 30B training tokens.
Lafa combines causal generation across blocks with learned asynchronous flows within each block, preserving prefix caching while enabling parallel generation.
With sufficient integration steps, flow decoding approaches or surpasses AR SFT accuracy, and flow map distillation improves few-step generation.
Joint training also improves AR accuracy over matched AR SFT.
This retained AR predictor verifies one-step flow drafts within the same model, enabling self-speculative decoding.
With this approach, Lafa 7B achieves $2.54\times$ the throughput of the 7B AR SFT baseline at comparable average accuracy.
These results demonstrate a practical path to scaling continuous flows for language modeling through data-efficient AR adaptation.
Improving flow map distillation, particularly its incorporation of self-conditioning and prefix guidance, remains a promising direction for further accelerating flow generation.

## Appendix A Theoretical results

## Asynchronous flow velocity and denoiser

In this subsection, we derive the relationship between the velocity field and the denoiser of the asynchronous flow Equation 5 used in Section 2.1.

Proposition A.1.

For the interpolant Equation 2, the velocity $b_{t}({\bf x};{\bf c})=\mathbb{E}[\dot{I}_{t}\mid{\bf c},I_{t}={\bf x}]$ satisfies, at each position $i$ ,

 $b_{t}({\bf x};{\bf c})^{i}=\frac{\dot{\tau}^{i}(t)}{1-\tau^{i}(t)}\big(D_{t}({\bf x};{\bf c})^{i}{\bf E}-{\bf x}^{i}\big),\qquad t\in[0,1).$ 

Proof.

The time derivative of the interpolant is

 $\dot{I}_{t}^{i}=\dot{\tau}^{i}(t)({\bf x}_{1}^{i}-{\bf x}_{0}^{i}),$ 

(11)

and its conditional expectation gives the velocity [25, 27].
Solving Equation 2 for the noise gives

 ${\bf x}_{0}^{i}=\frac{I_{t}^{i}-\tau^{i}(t){\bf x}_{1}^{i}}{1-\tau^{i}(t)},\qquad\text{so}\qquad{\bf x}_{1}^{i}-{\bf x}_{0}^{i}=\frac{{\bf x}_{1}^{i}-I_{t}^{i}}{1-\tau^{i}(t)}.$ 

Substituting into $b_{t}({\bf x};{\bf c})=\mathbb{E}[\dot{I}_{t}\mid{\bf c},I_{t}={\bf x}]$ with $\dot{I}_{t}^{i}=\dot{\tau}^{i}(t)({\bf x}_{1}^{i}-{\bf x}_{0}^{i})$ ,

 $b_{t}({\bf x};{\bf c})^{i}=\frac{\dot{\tau}^{i}(t)}{1-\tau^{i}(t)}\,\mathbb{E}\big[{\bf x}_{1}^{i}-I_{t}^{i}\mid{\bf c},I_{t}={\bf x}\big]=\frac{\dot{\tau}^{i}(t)}{1-\tau^{i}(t)}\Big(\mathbb{E}\big[{\bf x}_{1}^{i}\mid{\bf c},I_{t}={\bf x}\big]-{\bf x}^{i}\Big).$ 

Since ${\bf x}_{1}^{i}={\bf E}_{{\bf y}^{i}}$ , the conditional mean is the posterior mean embedding under Equation 4,

 $\mathbb{E}[{\bf x}_{1}^{i}\mid{\bf c},I_{t}={\bf x}]=\sum_{v\in V}p({\bf y}^{i}=v\mid{\bf c},I_{t}={\bf x})\,{\bf E}_{v}=D_{t}({\bf x};{\bf c})^{i}{\bf E}.$ 

(12)

∎

## Asynchronous embedded flow maps

In this subsection, we derive the embedded flow map used in Section 2.2, first characterizing the two-time denoiser Equation 7 and then giving the full distillation objective Equation 20.

## Setup.

Throughout, the prefix ${\bf c}$ is fixed, and the vocabulary embeddings ${\bf E}\in\mathbb{R}^{|V|\times d}$ and tokenwise schedules are held fixed.
A block has positions $i=1,...,B$ with clean embeddings ${\bf x}_{1}^{i}={\bf E}_{{\bf y}^{i}}$ .
For each position $i$ , let $\tau^{i}$ be continuously differentiable with $\dot{\tau}^{i}(t)>0$ for $t<1$ , $\tau^{i}(0)=0$ , and $\tau^{i}(1)=1$ , and write $\tau_{t}^{i}\coloneqq\tau^{i}(t)$ .
Since all tokenwise times are driven by the single global time $t$ , they trace a one-dimensional monotone curve in $[0,1]^{B}$ , and the flow map is indexed by two global times [30, 29]. Let $\hat{D}_{t}$ be the frozen teacher denoiser (Section 2.2). As in Equation 5, it induces the velocity and flow map

 $\displaystyle b_{t}({\bf x};{\bf c})^{i}$ 
 $\displaystyle=\frac{\dot{\tau}^{i}(t)}{1-\tau_{t}^{i}}\big(\hat{D}_{t}({\bf x};{\bf c})^{i}{\bf E}-{\bf x}^{i}\big),$ 

(13)

 $\displaystyle\partial_{t}X_{s,t}({\bf x};{\bf c})$ 
 $\displaystyle=b_{t}(X_{s,t}({\bf x};{\bf c});{\bf c}),\qquad X_{s,s}({\bf x};{\bf c})={\bf x}.$ 

We work on $0\leq s\leq t<1$ and assume unique ODE solutions and that all denoisers are continuous and differentiable in time.
Expressions at the endpoint $t=1$ , used in flow map inference, are understood as limits when they exist.

## Two-time denoiser.

For $0\leq s<t<1$ , we define the average velocity $v_{s,t}$ between $s$ and $t$ as [38]

 $v_{s,t}({\bf x};{\bf c})\coloneqq\frac{X_{s,t}({\bf x};{\bf c})-{\bf x}}{t-s}.$ 

(14)

To express transport between two times through a denoising prediction, we define a two-time denoiser [13, 77] $\delta_{s,t}({\bf x};{\bf c})\in(\Delta^{|V|-1})^{B}$ through its mean embedding,

 $\delta_{s,t}({\bf x};{\bf c})^{i}{\bf E}={\bf x}^{i}+\frac{(1-\tau_{s}^{i})(t-s)}{\tau_{t}^{i}-\tau_{s}^{i}}v_{s,t}({\bf x};{\bf c})^{i}.$ 

(15)

Substituting Equation 14 and rearranging gives Equation 7.
Since $|V|\gg d$ , distinct probability vectors can share the same mean embedding.
Thus Equation 15 fixes only the mean embedding $\delta_{s,t}{\bf E}$ and not $\delta$ itself.
The following proposition constructs a specific $\delta$ and identifies the conditions under which a learned denoiser recovers the flow.

Proposition A.2.

There exists a two-time denoiser $\delta$ satisfying Equation 15 with the following two conditions.

1. 

(Diagonal condition) $\delta_{s,t}\to\hat{D}_{s}$ as $t\to s$ , so that $\delta_{s,s}\coloneqq\hat{D}_{s}$ .

2. 

(Semigroup condition) For $s<u<t$ ,

 $\displaystyle\delta_{s,t}({\bf x};{\bf c})^{i}=\gamma^{i}\delta_{s,u}({\bf x};{\bf c})^{i}+(1-\gamma^{i})\delta_{u,t}(X_{s,u}({\bf x};{\bf c});{\bf c})^{i},\qquad\gamma^{i}=\frac{(1-\tau_{t}^{i})(\tau_{u}^{i}-\tau_{s}^{i})}{(1-\tau_{u}^{i})(\tau_{t}^{i}-\tau_{s}^{i})}.$ 

(16)

Conversely, for any two-time denoiser $\delta_{s,t}$ satisfying the diagonal and semigroup conditions, its associated flow map defined by Equation 7 equals the true flow map $X_{s,t}$ .

Proof.

By the velocity in the setup,

 $\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}r}\frac{X_{s,r}^{i}}{1-\tau_{r}^{i}}=\frac{(1-\tau_{r}^{i})\,\partial_{r}X_{s,r}^{i}+\dot{\tau}^{i}(r)\,X_{s,r}^{i}}{(1-\tau_{r}^{i})^{2}}=w^{i}(r)\,\hat{D}_{r}(X_{s,r})^{i}{\bf E},\qquad w^{i}(r)\coloneqq\frac{\dot{\tau}^{i}(r)}{(1-\tau_{r}^{i})^{2}},$ 

(17)

where we suppress ${\bf x}$ and ${\bf c}$ .
Define

 $\delta_{s,t}^{i}\coloneqq\frac{1}{Z_{s,t}^{i}}\int_{s}^{t}w^{i}(r)\,\hat{D}_{r}(X_{s,r})^{i}\,{\rm d}r,\qquad Z_{s,t}^{i}\coloneqq\int_{s}^{t}w^{i}(r)\,{\rm d}r=\frac{\tau_{t}^{i}-\tau_{s}^{i}}{(1-\tau_{t}^{i})(1-\tau_{s}^{i})}.$ 

(18)

This is a weighted average of probability vectors, hence a probability vector.

Integrating Equation 17 over $[s,t]$ and multiplying by $1-\tau_{t}^{i}$ gives
 $X_{s,t}^{i}=\frac{1-\tau_{t}^{i}}{1-\tau_{s}^{i}}{\bf x}^{i}+\frac{\tau_{t}^{i}-\tau_{s}^{i}}{1-\tau_{s}^{i}}\delta_{s,t}^{i}{\bf E}$ ,
which is Equation 15.

Diagonal condition.
As $t\to s$ , the normalized weights $w^{i}/Z_{s,t}^{i}$ concentrate at $r=s$ , so $\delta_{s,t}\to\hat{D}_{s}$ by continuity of the teacher along the trajectory.

Semigroup condition.
Split the integral at $u$ .
The part over $[s,u]$ is $Z_{s,u}^{i}\delta_{s,u}^{i}$ .
For $r\geq u$ , $X_{s,r}=X_{u,r}(X_{s,u})$ , so the part over $[u,t]$ is $Z_{u,t}^{i}\delta_{u,t}(X_{s,u})^{i}$ .
Dividing by $Z_{s,t}^{i}=Z_{s,u}^{i}+Z_{u,t}^{i}$ gives Equation 16 with $\gamma^{i}=Z_{s,u}^{i}/Z_{s,t}^{i}$ .

Converse.
Let $\hat{X}$ be defined from $\delta$ by Equation 7.
Then $\hat{X}$ is the flow map of $b_{t}$ if $\hat{X}_{s,s}={\rm Id}$ and it satisfies the tangent condition $\partial_{t}\hat{X}_{s,t}|_{t=s}=b_{s}$ and the semigroup condition $\hat{X}_{s,t}=\hat{X}_{u,t}\circ\hat{X}_{s,u}$ [38].
We verify each.
 $\hat{X}_{s,s}={\rm Id}$ holds by construction.
The tangent condition follows from the diagonal condition by differentiating Equation 7 at $t=s$ .
Substituting Equation 7 twice, $\hat{X}_{u,t}\circ\hat{X}_{s,u}$ has the form of Equation 7 with denoiser $\gamma^{i}\delta_{s,u}^{i}+(1-\gamma^{i})\delta_{u,t}(\hat{X}_{s,u})^{i}$ , so the semigroup condition on $\delta$ gives the semigroup condition on $\hat{X}$ .
Hence $\hat{X}=X$ .
∎

## Distillation objective.

By Proposition A.2, learning the flow map reduces to satisfying the diagonal and semigroup conditions.
We impose both with KL losses on a student $\hat{\delta}_{s,t}$ initialized from the frozen teacher $\hat{D}_{s}$ (Section 2.2).

For $0\leq s<u<t<1$ , the off-diagonal target composes two shorter student transitions [13],

 $\bar{\delta}_{s,t}({\bf x}_{s};{\bf c})^{i}\coloneqq\mathsf{sg}\Big[\gamma^{i}\,\hat{\delta}_{s,u}({\bf x}_{s};{\bf c})^{i}+(1-\gamma^{i})\,\hat{\delta}_{u,t}({\bf x}_{u};{\bf c})^{i}\Big],\qquad{\bf x}_{u}\coloneqq\hat{X}_{s,u}({\bf x}_{s};{\bf c}),$ 

(19)

with $\gamma^{i}$ as in Equation 16.
Since $0\leq\gamma^{i}\leq 1$ , the target is a categorical distribution.
The intermediate state ${\bf x}_{u}$ is computed by the student, and $\mathsf{sg}$ stops gradients through the entire target.
Since prefix positions are conditioned on time $1$ , the student retains the prefix cache and an autoregressive predictor $f^{\hat{\delta}}$ .
We train the student with semigroup-based flow map distillation [13] plus autoregressive distillation:

 $\mathcal{L}_{\mathsf{distill}}(\hat{\delta})\coloneqq\underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{{\bf c}}\,\mathsf{KL}(f({\bf c})\|f^{\hat{\delta}}({\bf c}))}_{\text{AR distillation loss}}+\underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{t,{\bf c},I_{t}}\,\mathsf{KL}(\hat{D}_{t}(I_{t};{\bf c})\|\hat{\delta}_{t,t}(I_{t};{\bf c}))}_{\text{diagonal distillation loss}}+\underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{s,u,t,{\bf c},I_{s}}\,\mathsf{KL}(\bar{\delta}_{s,t}(I_{s};{\bf c})\|\hat{\delta}_{s,t}(I_{s};{\bf c}))}_{\text{off-diagonal distillation loss}},$ 

(20)

where each $\mathsf{KL}$ sums tokenwise over prefix or block positions.
The first two loss terms match the student’s AR predictions and instantaneous denoising with the teacher, while the off-diagonal term enforces the semigroup property $\hat{X}_{s,t}\approx\hat{X}_{u,t}\circ\hat{X}_{s,u}$ , bootstrapping long jumps from short ones up to one-step generation $\hat{X}_{0,1}$ .

The following proposition shows that the two flow map terms of this objective are well posed, in that their population minimizers recover the teacher flow map.

Proposition A.3.

Assume full support of the sampling distributions over $(s,u,t)$ and the states.
Over all two-time denoisers $\delta$ , the population objective

 $\mathcal{L}(\delta)=\mathbb{E}_{t,I_{t}}\sum_{i=1}^{B}\mathsf{KL}\big(\hat{D}_{t}(I_{t};{\bf c})^{i}\,\|\,\delta_{t,t}(I_{t};{\bf c})^{i}\big)+\mathbb{E}_{s,u,t,I_{s}}\sum_{i=1}^{B}\mathsf{KL}\big(\bar{\delta}_{s,t}(I_{s};{\bf c})^{i}\,\|\,\delta_{s,t}(I_{s};{\bf c})^{i}\big)$ 

(21)

attains its minimum zero, and every population minimizer recovers the teacher flow map $X$ through Equation 7.

Proof.

The denoiser constructed in Proposition A.2 satisfies both conditions, so both KL terms are zero.
Conversely, a zero loss implies the diagonal and semigroup conditions almost surely, and by full support and continuity, everywhere.
By the converse of Proposition A.2, $\hat{X}=X$ .
∎

Note that the two terms of Equation 21 are the diagonal and off-diagonal terms of Equation 20. The autoregressive term depends only on the prefix readout and is zero at $f=f^{\hat{\delta}}$ , so it does not change the population minimizers of the two flow map terms.

To train on many blocks at once, following prior work on block diffusion [23, 32, 33, 35], we pair each clean training sequence with a copy which is split into noisy blocks of size $B$ .
The attention mask applies prefix-causal and block-bidirectional structure to every block at once, such that clean positions attend causally, and each noisy block attends to all clean tokens preceding it and bidirectionally within itself, but not to later clean tokens or other noisy blocks.
Under this setting, each step of training progresses as in Algorithm 1.

During training, inspired by the loss-aware time warping of Dieleman et al. [12], applied separately to each block position, we learn each tokenwise time as the inverse cumulative distribution of its denoising loss.
Let $\ell_{i}(\tau)>0$ be the expected denoising cross-entropy loss of position $i$ at tokenwise time $\tau$ , and define

 $\tau^{i}(t)\coloneqq F_{i}^{-1}(t),\qquad F_{i}(\tau)\coloneqq\frac{\int_{0}^{\tau}\ell_{i}(r)\,{\rm d}r}{\int_{0}^{1}\ell_{i}(r)\,{\rm d}r}.$ 

(22)

To estimate $F$ during training, we keep an exponential moving average ${\bf L}\in\mathbb{R}^{B\times M}$ of the per-position denoising cross entropy in $M$ uniform time bins, and set each $F_{i}$ to the piecewise-linear CDF of the normalized row ${\bf L}_{i,:}$ .
We initialize ${\bf L}$ uniformly, and every $S\in\mathbb{N}$ optimization steps, run four gradient-free forward passes with independently sampled uniform times for each noisy block (see Algorithm 2 for details).
For every position-time bin $(i,m)$ encountered during these forward passes, we update

 ${\bf L}_{i,m}\leftarrow\beta{\bf L}_{i,m}+(1-\beta)\bar{\bf L}_{i,m},$ 

(23)

where $\bar{\bf L}_{i,m}$ is the mean cross entropy recorded at position $i$ in time bin $m$ , and bins that are not visited retain their previous value.
This independent uniform estimation prevents the learned sampling distribution from starving low-probability regions.

## Flow decoding

To improve flow decoding, we apply self-conditioning during training with a fixed probability by obtaining an initial prediction without gradient and using its expected token embeddings to condition a second forward pass.
Following Chen et al. [15], we incorporate this prediction through additional projection layers:

 $\bar{\bf x}_{1}=\mathsf{sg}\big(D_{t}(I_{t};{\bf c}){\bf E}\big),\qquad\tilde{I}_{t}=I_{t}+I_{t}{\bf W}_{1}^{\top}+\bar{\bf x}_{1}{\bf W}_{2}^{\top},$ 

(24)

where ${\bf W}_{1},{\bf W}_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$ are trainable and initialized to zero.
For training under self-conditioning, we replace the noisy block input with $\tilde{I}_{t}$ and compute the training loss on the second pass.
Otherwise, we train with a single pass on $I_{t}$ .
In practice, we use a probability $p_{\mathsf{sc}}=0.5$ of using self-conditioning for each training batch.

During inference, we use the denoiser prediction from the previous integration step for self-conditioning.
In single-step update of Euler integration of Equation 8, we replace $D_{t_{k}}(\hat{\bf x}_{t_{k}};{\bf c})$ with ${\bf q}_{k}$ , defined by

 $\tilde{\bf x}_{t_{k}}=\hat{\bf x}_{t_{k}}+\hat{\bf x}_{t_{k}}{\bf W}_{1}^{\top}+({\bf q}_{k-1}{\bf E}){\bf W}_{2}^{\top},\qquad{\bf q}_{k}=D_{t_{k}}(\tilde{\bf x}_{t_{k}};{\bf c}),\qquad{\bf q}_{0}=D_{0}(\hat{\bf x}_{0};{\bf c}).$ 

(25)

Detailed procedure for flow decoding can be found in Algorithm 3, where we write, for compactness:

 $\mathsf{SC}({\bf x},{\bf q})\coloneqq{\bf x}+{\bf x}{\bf W}_{1}^{\top}+({\bf q}{\bf E}){\bf W}_{2}^{\top},\qquad\mathsf{SC}({\bf x},\varnothing)\coloneqq{\bf x}.$ 

(26)

In addition to self-conditioning, during flow decoding, we employ prefix guidance to strengthen the conditioning on the prefix, analogously to history guidance in video diffusion models [39].
We define the guided denoiser as

 $D_{t}^{g}({\bf x};{\bf c})\coloneqq D_{t}({\bf x};\varnothing)+g(D_{t}({\bf x};{\bf c})-D_{t}({\bf x};\varnothing)),\qquad g\geq 1,$ 

(27)

and replace $D_{t_{k}}(\hat{\bf x}_{t_{k}};{\bf c}){\bf E}$ in (self-conditioned) Euler integration Equation 8 with $D_{t_{k}}^{g}(\hat{\bf x}_{t_{k}};{\bf c}){\bf E}$ .
Guidance scale $g=1$ recovers the usual prediction, whereas $g>1$ changes the velocity and hence the sampled distribution.

Self-speculative decoding drafts a block using the denoiser $D$ and commits its longest accepted prefix using AR verification with $f$ .
We find it possible to build a fused operation that uses a single forward pass to simultaneously perform verification of a previously drafted block and drafting of the next block.

In each step of the fused decoding, we are given a prefix ${\bf c}\in V^{N}$ , the AR prediction on the prefix ${\bf q}\coloneqq f({\bf c})\in(\Delta^{|V|-1})^{N}$ , and a previously drafted block ${\bf p}\in(\Delta^{|V|-1})^{B}$ with $\hat{\bf y}=\mathsf{argmax}({\bf p}^{i})_{i=1}^{B}\in V^{B}$ .
We would like to simultaneously accept a prefix $\hat{\bf y}^{1:a}$ using AR verification with $f$ , and also draft the next block via $D_{0}({\bf z};{\bf c}\oplus\hat{\bf y}^{1:a})$ where ${\bf z}$ denotes noise and $\oplus$ denotes concatenation.

Since we have the AR prediction ${\bf q}$ on the prefix, the verification for the first token $\hat{\bf y}^{1}$ can be done for free by comparing against ${\bf q}^{N}$ .
If $\hat{\bf y}^{1}\neq\mathsf{argmax}({\bf q}^{N})$ , then we simply replace $\hat{\bf y}^{1}$ by $\mathsf{argmax}({\bf q}^{N})$ and accept $a=1$ .
Drafting then evaluates $D_{0}({\bf z};{\bf c}\oplus\hat{\bf y}^{1})$ , which simultaneously produces the AR prediction $f({\bf c}\oplus\hat{\bf y}^{1})$ on the extended prefix.

When $\hat{\bf y}^{1}=\mathsf{argmax}({\bf q}^{N})$ , we need block verification.
To perform this verification and next-block drafting in a single forward pass, we construct the input to the transformer underlying $D$ and $f$ as a concatenation of a clean prefix ${\bf c}\oplus\hat{\bf y}$ and $B$ blocks of $B$ noise tokens, ${\bf z}^{(1)},...,{\bf z}^{(B)}\in\mathbb{R}^{B\times d}$ , each drawn from $\mathsf{N}(0,\sigma^{2}I)$ .
We then construct the attention mask such that the prefix ${\bf c}\oplus\hat{\bf y}$ attends causally to itself, while each block ${\bf z}^{(j)}$ attends bidirectionally to itself and causally to a prefix of the prefix, ${\bf c}\oplus\hat{\bf y}^{1:j}$ (Figure 9).
Accordingly, we build tokenwise time such that ${\bf c}\oplus\hat{\bf y}$ is conditioned on one and all ${\bf z}^{(j)}$ on zero.
Since this produces the AR prediction $f({\bf c}\oplus\hat{\bf y})$ on the prefix, we can determine acceptance $\hat{\bf y}^{1:a}$ as follows (Section 2.3):

 $a=\max\{j\in[B]:\hat{\bf y}^{i+1}=\mathsf{argmax}(f({\bf c}\oplus\hat{\bf y})^{N+i})\text{ for every }1\leq i<j\}.$ 

(28)

Then, instead of an additional forward pass, we simply take output tokens corresponding to ${\bf z}^{(a)}$ since their production is computationally identical to $D_{0}({\bf z}^{(a)};{\bf c}\oplus\hat{\bf y}^{1:a})$ .
The next step of fused decoding then operates on extended prefix ${\bf c}\oplus\hat{\bf y}^{1:a}$ , drafted block $D_{0}({\bf z}^{(a)};{\bf c}\oplus\hat{\bf y}^{1:a})$ , and prefix AR prediction $f({\bf c}\oplus\hat{\bf y}^{1:a})=f({\bf c}\oplus\hat{\bf y})^{1:N+a}$ .
In practice, we further reuse the key-value cache of the prefix to improve efficiency (Algorithm 4).

## TPF evaluation and per-benchmark scores

## Tokens per forward (TPF).

Across evaluation examples $j$ , we compute TPF as

 $\text{TPF}=\frac{\sum_{j}T_{j}}{\sum_{j}F_{j}},$ 

(29)

where $T_{j}$ is the number of committed tokens and $F_{j}$ is the number of forward passes.
AR has TPF one by definition.
For Lafa and Efficient-DLM, $F_{j}$ counts the number of fused verification-and-redraft forward passes in addition to one forward pass for initial drafting, excluding prompt prefill and cache refreshes for Efficient-DLM.
For Fast-dLLM v2, $F_{j}$ includes top-level denoising, prefill, and cache update forward passes.

Figure 8 shows end-to-end generation throughput measured on a single H100 GPU with batch size one with bfloat16 inference and a 32-token warm-up.
We report tokens per second (TPS), which is the total generated tokens divided by total generation time.
Table 8 reports the per-task values underlying Figure 8, and method-specific settings are given below.

• 

AR SFT. Qwen2.5 7B AR SFT uses standard HuggingFace greedy generation and emits one new token per decoding forward.

• 

Lafa (Ours). For each block size $B\in\{4,8,16\}$ , we use the corresponding trained checkpoint with self-speculative decoding.
A one-step denoiser draft of the next block is verified against the model’s own greedy AR predictions.
The fused pass packs the clean draft with $B$ noisy successor blocks, one for each possible accepted-prefix length.
Clean draft positions attend causally to produce the verification logits, while each successor block attends to the prefix and its candidate accepted draft prefix and bidirectionally within the block.
After selecting the longest verified prefix, we retain its KV states and use the corresponding successor block as the next draft, avoiding a separate redrafting forward.

• 

DeepSeek-MTP.
We train 7B scale DeepSeek-MTP with one DeepSeek-style auxiliary multi-token-prediction stage ( $D=1$ [8]).
Greedy verified self-speculation compares the multi-token proposal against the base model’s greedy AR prediction.

• 

MARS.
We use the released MARS Qwen2.5 7B checkpoints [33] for $B\in\{4,8,16\}$ .
We report results using the paper’s probability-threshold decoder at threshold $0.95$ , with its sliding KV cache.
Each round commits the leftmost proposal, then commits the longest consecutive continuation whose top-token probabilities remain at least the confidence threshold.

• 

Fast-dLLM v2.
We use the released Fast-dLLM v2 7B checkpoint [48] with its native confidence decoder at threshold $0.9$ .
Block caching is disabled, and the global and sub-block sizes are both set to $B\in\{4,8,16\}$ .

• 

Efficient-DLM.
We evaluate Efficient-DLM 8B [21] with its native confidence-threshold decoder at threshold $0.7$ for $B\in\{4,8,16,32\}$ , together with the $B=32$ speed–quality sweep at confidence thresholds in $\{0.5,0.6\}$ .
We use the model’s native prompt format over Qwen chat templates.

• 

Dream.
We use Dream-v0 7B Instruct [20] with its native entropy-based unmasking, targeting a mean of $k\in\{1,2,4\}$ filled canvas positions per denoising forward.
For throughput, we follow Efficient-DLM [21] and compute TPS as the total number of generated canvas tokens divided by total generation time.

## Confidence decoding

Figure 10: Confidence decoding, averaged over GSM8K, HumanEval, and IFEval.
In the right panel, Lafa uses 8B for $B=4$ and 7B for $B\in\{8,16\}$ .
Efficient-DLM ( $B=32$ ) and LLaDA are 8B, and other baselines are 7B.

As an alternative to self-speculative decoding, we follow MARS [33] and accept the longest left-to-right prefix of a one-step flow draft whose token confidence exceeds a threshold $\lambda$ (Section B.3), and apply the same rule to block-autoregressive baselines.
We implement it with the fused decoding of Algorithm 4, where $\hat{\bf y}^{1:a}$ is instead the longest prefix of $\hat{\bf y}$ with confidences $({\bf p}^{i})_{\hat{\bf y}^{i}}\geq\lambda$ (where we always set $a\geq 1$ ).
Since $a$ is known before the forward pass, only the block ${\bf z}^{(a)}$ is needed instead of all $B$ blocks.
To apply this strategy, Dream and LLaDA use their native global entropy or confidence ordering over masked positions, whereas Fast-dLLM v2 applies the threshold within the active sub-block.
In Figure 10, we show an accuracy–TPF tradeoff measured by sweeping over confidence thresholds.
At 0.5B, Lafa with block size four attains the highest peak accuracy among all methods, including AR SFT, and its accuracy remains at or above that of pure AR at high thresholds.
At 7B, Lafa exceeds AR SFT at high thresholds, while MARS, which is trained for confidence decoding, degrades more gracefully as the threshold is lowered.
At 8B, Lafa remains above AR SFT across the tested range and on par with Efficient-DLM.

Since AR verification avoids the degradation at lower thresholds without tuning a threshold, we use self-speculative decoding as our default in the main text.

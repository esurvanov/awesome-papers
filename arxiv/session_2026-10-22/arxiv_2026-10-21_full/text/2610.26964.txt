Abstract

Large language models (LLMs) perform poorly on classical planning, but it is unclear whether this reflects the intrinsic structural complexity of a planning instance or the difficulty of interpreting its symbolic representation. Existing evaluations conflate the two. We study seven STRIPS domains modeled on IPC domains, labeled with breadth-first-search (BFS) optimal plan lengths, and ask two questions. First: can structural difficulty be estimated without solving an instance? A classical, zero-training delete-relaxation heuristic ( $h_{\text{add}}$ ) estimates optimal plan length on held-out domains far better than every alternative we test, including ARC, a purpose-built episodic retrieval architecture: mean Spearman $|\rho|=0.931$ for $h_{\text{add}}$ , versus $0.707$ for ridge regression on structural features, $0.657$ for object count, and $0.40$ for ARC despite five-seed training with early stopping. ARC does not beat a linear model on its own input features. We take this as the paper’s primary finding: for estimating structural planning difficulty, a decades-old AI planning heuristic outperforms a dedicated learned retrieval model, and future work in this space should be judged against it. Second: does an LLM’s actual planning success track structural difficulty? We introduce a controlled intervention that progressively renames predicates while preserving the transition system and optimal plans, so structural difficulty is unchanged by construction, and measure planning success with a state-simulating validator: valid-plan success falls from $67.5\%$ to $40.0\%$ as the fraction of renamed symbols grows from $0\%$ to $100\%$ , with refusal rate flat at $0\%$ throughout. We further show that an LLM given ARC’s own conditioning information estimates difficulty in-context (mean $|\rho|=0.826$ ) far better than ARC does after training, and that hybrid routing between an LLM and a symbolic planner depends more on calibration with target-model outcomes than on which structural score is used. Together, these results indicate that structural planning difficulty is already well estimated by existing, non-learned methods, and that the open problem for hybrid planning systems is modeling an LLM’s semantic, vocabulary-dependent behavior rather than building better structural-difficulty predictors. The complete code is available at https://github.com/mdebnath22/plan.

## 1 Introduction

Planning - synthesizing goal-directed action sequences over a state space - is a canonical test of systematic reasoning. Large language models (LLMs) remain unreliable at it. Valmeekam et al. (2023a) report that frontier models solve fewer than 3% of standard PDDL benchmarks; Valmeekam et al. (2023b) show the failures persist under chain-of-thought prompting Wei et al. (2022) and self-critique Stechly et al. (2023). Unlike symbolic planners, LLMs perform no explicit combinatorial search, which makes them brittle on problems that require systematically exploring a large state space Kambhampati et al. (2024); Silver et al. (2024).

Why a given instance is hard is less understood. Two distinct factors can make a planning instance difficult: structural complexity, the combinatorial difficulty intrinsic to the state space and goal structure, and semantic interpretability, how easily an LLM can ground the symbolic vocabulary describing that same problem. Existing benchmarks conflate them, so a failure cannot be attributed to either cause specifically.

This matters for hybrid planning systems, which route easy instances to an LLM and hard instances to a symbolic planner (Kambhampati et al., 2024), use an LLM for heuristic guidance (Tang et al., 2025), or for candidate generation with symbolic verification (Valmeekam et al., 2023b). Every such system needs an upstream difficulty estimate. Existing approaches use problem size, domain identity, or domain-specific classifiers (Chiari et al., 2025), none of which transfers to a domain with unfamiliar predicates or object vocabulary.

We address this in two parts. First, we ask whether structural difficulty can be estimated without solving an instance, and whether a learned, cross-domain model is the right tool. We build ARC (Asymmetric Retrieval for Cross-domain difficulty estimation), which uses a foundation-model embedding to retrieve structurally similar instances across domains, with structural features providing within-domain discrimination; within a single domain, such embeddings are expected to concentrate in a narrow region of the embedding space, limiting their ability to discriminate difficulty (Section 3). We compare ARC against object count, goal-fact count, ridge regression on structural features, LogME (You et al., 2021), H-score (Bao et al., 2019), a non-episodic MLP, and $h_{\text{add}}$ , a delete-relaxation heuristic computed from an instance’s grounded action set via a relaxed planning graph, with no learning and no target-domain data. The result is decisive: $h_{\text{add}}$ reaches mean $|\rho|=0.931$ across three held-out domains; ARC reaches $0.400$ , below every baseline except LogME and H-score, and below a linear model fit on exactly its own structural-feature input. We verify $h_{\text{add}}$ directly: an independent reconstruction of each domain’s grounded action set reproduces every saved optimal plan with zero failures across $1{,}006$ solved instances in seven domains.

Second, we ask whether an LLM’s actual planning success tracks this structural difficulty. We introduce an intervention that renames a domain’s predicates while leaving its transition system, every optimal plan, and every structural feature unchanged. Any change in LLM success under this intervention is attributable to semantic interpretability, not structure. Valid-plan success on Blocksworld falls from $67.5\%$ with no renaming to $40.0\%$ with every predicate renamed, while refusal rate stays at $0\%$ throughout: the model always attempts a plan, and the effect is carried entirely by a rising rate of malformed and inapplicable actions.

## Contributions.

• 

We show that structural difficulty is well estimated by a classical, zero-training delete-relaxation heuristic ( $h_{\text{add}}$ , mean $|\rho|=0.931$ ), which exceeds every alternative we test, including ARC, a retrieval architecture we purpose-built for this problem and evaluated under conditions fixed before training (five seeds, early stopping, a pre-declared criterion for added value). ARC reaches only $0.400$ – $0.403$ (ensembled: $0.595$ ), below a linear model on its own structural-feature inputs ( $0.707$ ). We report this as a substantive negative result for learned cross-domain retrieval, not a limitation of one implementation: Appendix C shows the shortfall holds across attention directions, representation combinations, and residualization hyperparameters.

• 

We introduce a semantic intervention based on progressive predicate renaming that holds structural difficulty fixed while varying symbol interpretability. LLM valid-plan success falls from $67.5\%$ to $40.0\%$ as renaming goes from none to complete, with refusal rate flat at $0\%$ , demonstrating that semantic interpretability affects LLM planning success independently of structural difficulty.

• 

We show that structure-only routing cannot substitute for target-model calibration, and that an LLM given ARC’s own conditioning information outperforms ARC substantially at difficulty estimation (mean $|\rho|=0.826$ vs. $0.400$ ).

## 2 Related Work

## LLMs for PDDL planning.

LLMs struggle with classical planning, including simple Blocksworld instances (Valmeekam et al., 2023b; Valmeekam et al., 2022; McDermott et al., 1998), across IPC-style domains (Valmeekam et al., 2023a; Long and Fox, 2003; Ferber et al., 2019; Silver and Chitnis, 2020). Self-critique does not reliably fix this (Stechly et al., 2023; Wei et al., 2022; Guan et al., 2023). Instruction-tuned PDDL generation improves performance (Verma et al., 2025), but models still generalize poorly to instances larger or structurally different from training (Silver et al., 2024; Tang et al., 2025; Tantakoun et al., 2025; Wei et al., 2025). We take these limitations as given and ask instead when an LLM should be used at all.

## Hybrid planning systems.

The LLM-Modulo framework (Kambhampati et al., 2024) wraps LLM calls with formal verifiers (Gundawar et al., 2024). Related work uses LLMs for heuristic guidance (Chiari et al., 2025; Corrêa et al., 2026) or candidate generation with symbolic verification (Li et al., 2024; Yu et al., 2025). Every such architecture needs an upstream router; existing routing signals do not transfer across domains with unfamiliar vocabulary.

## Feature transferability metrics.

LogME (You et al., 2021) and H-score (Bao et al., 2019) estimate how well a frozen feature space supports a downstream task without fine-tuning (Bao et al., 2018; Nguyen et al., 2020; Sae-Bae and Laohakiat, 2025), typically by fitting a linear-Gaussian model and reporting its marginal likelihood or an information-theoretic divergence. We use both as zero-shot baselines, fit on source-domain instances and scored on held-out domains.

## Meta-learning.

MAML (Finn et al., 2017) performs an explicit inner-loop update per episode; Prototypical Networks (Snell et al., 2017) score a query by its relation to a labeled support set with no inner-loop adaptation. ARC follows the latter: one set of parameters, updated once per episode, with no meta-/task-parameter distinction. “Episodic” here refers only to the sampling procedure (Blanchard et al., 2011; Blanchard et al., 2021): each step draws one source domain and conditions on a labeled support set from it, drawing on the geometric structure of PDDL embeddings (Reimers and Gurevych, 2019; van den Oord et al., 2018).

## 3 Background and Problem Formulation

## PDDL and planning instances.

A classical planning instance is a pair $\pi=\langle D,P\rangle$ , where $D$ specifies a domain’s predicates and lifted operators in PDDL, and $P=\langle O,s_{0},g\rangle$ gives a set of objects $O$ , an initial state $s_{0}$ , and a goal condition $g$ . A plan is a sequence of ground actions $\mathbf{a}=(a_{1},\dots,a_{L})$ whose execution from $s_{0}$ satisfies $g$ . The optimal plan length is

 $n^{\star}(\pi)=\min_{\mathbf{a}}\left\{|\mathbf{a}|:g\subseteq\Gamma(s_{0},\mathbf{a})\right\},$ 

(1)

where $\Gamma(s_{0},\mathbf{a})$ is the state reached by executing $\mathbf{a}$ from $s_{0}$ . We take $n^{\star}(\pi)$ as our operational definition of structural difficulty.

Here $O=\{a,b,c\}$ , $s_{0}$ is the init block, $g=\{(\text{on }a\ b)\}$ . One action, $\text{stack}(a,b)$ , reaches a state satisfying $g$ , so $n^{\star}(\pi)=1$ . Instances in our evaluation are larger; this minimal example fixes notation.

## Difficulty labels.

We label every instance by breadth-first search (BFS) within a budget $B=(T_{\text{max}}{=}12,N_{\text{max}}{=}5{,}000)$ , identically across domains: the regression target is $y_{r}=n^{\star}(\pi)$ . Instances BFS does not solve within $B$ carry no label and are excluded from every computation in this paper; a finite-budget failure neither establishes unsolvability nor yields a plan-length value, so we never assign one. Per-domain coverage is in Appendix A.1.

## Feature representation.

Each instance has three views. Structural features $x_{s}\in\mathbb{R}^{28}$ — object count, initial- and goal-fact counts and ratios, predicate-arity statistics, operator count — contain no domain identifier and nothing derived from the plan or $n^{\star}$ . Foundation-model embeddings $x_{e}\in\mathbb{R}^{768}$ encode a natural-language description built from object counts and a plain-language summary of the initial state and goal, with no raw PDDL syntax and nothing derived from the plan; no description contains a step count or difficulty word. The residual $x_{r}=x_{e}-\hat{x}_{e}(x_{s})$ is the component of $x_{e}$ not explained by $x_{s}$ , estimated by PCA-Ridge regression. All preprocessing is fit on pooled source-domain data and frozen before evaluation.

## FM embedding collapse.

ARC rests on a diagnostic hypothesis: $x_{e}$ should identify semantic similarity across domains but concentrate within a domain, limiting within-domain discrimination; $x_{s}$ should do the opposite. We embed every instance’s description with all-mpnet-base-v2 and measure mean within-domain pairwise cosine similarity and effective rank $\exp(\mathcal{H}(\sigma/\|\sigma\|_{1}))$ , $\sigma$ the embedding matrix’s singular values. On the three held-out domains, mean cosine similarity is $0.989$ / $0.986$ / $0.992$ (BW/LOG/MBW), effective rank $2.62$ ; two further encoders give $0.988$ / $0.988$ cosine and $2.64$ / $2.61$ rank (Appendix C.3), so this is a property of the encoder family on these descriptions, not one model.

Structural features, pooled-standardized, range $0.69$ – $0.95$ cosine and $2.1$ – $5.3$ effective rank (mean $3.8$ ) within a domain. The hypothesized asymmetry holds in direction — $x_{e}$ is consistently more collapsed than $x_{s}$ — but the effective-rank gap is smaller than the cosine gap suggests: $x_{s}$ is also concentrated within a domain, not uniformly higher-dimensional. Whether the asymmetric query/key assignment this motivates is in fact the best way to exploit this structure is answered empirically in Appendix C: on balance, no — it is the best of four tested configurations (Appendix C.2), but none is competitive with the non-episodic baselines in Table 1.

Figure 1: ARC architecture. A query instance’s FM embedding is projected to a 128-dimensional query vector via QueryMLP. Support instances’ structural features form keys (KeyMLP); a concatenation of structural and residual FM features forms values. Soft attention retrieves a weighted difficulty summary $\mathbf{z}$ . The fusion head concatenates $\mathbf{x}_{s}^{q}$ , $\mathbf{z}$ and $\mathbf{x}_{r}^{q}$ and produces a regression output (normalized plan length). An optional gate blending the attention output with a linear predictor is evaluated as a variant (Section 4).

## 4 ARC: Architecture and Training

ARC estimates difficulty by retrieving structural analogues of a query instance from a labeled support set (60 instances, at training and test time). No target-domain instance, label, or statistic is used at test time. The semantic embedding of the query retrieves structurally relevant support instances: $x_{e}$ aligns across domains as a query; $x_{s}$ discriminates within a domain as keys and values. Algorithm 1 gives inference.

The query encoder maps $x_{e}$ to a 128-dimensional space through a two-layer MLP (LayerNorm, dropout $0.1$ ); the key encoder does the same for $x_{s}$ . The value projection concatenates $x_{s}^{(i)}$ and $x_{r}^{(i)}$ , so retrieval carries both structural and residual semantic information. Attention temperature starts at $\tau=1$ .

## Optional complexity-aware gate.

We also evaluate, as a pre-declared variant, a gate $c=\sigma(\text{MLP}(x_{s}^{q}))\in[0,1]$ blending a linear predictor $\hat{y}_{\text{simple}}=w^{\top}x_{s}^{q}$ with the attention output, trained end-to-end with no auxiliary supervision. The main configuration below uses no gate; the gated variant is reported alongside it, and we do not select between them using test-domain scores.

## Budget-exceeded instances.

An instance BFS does not solve within its budget is not known to be unsolvable, nor necessarily harder than a solved one. Such instances carry no label and supervise no head of the model.

## 4.1 Training Objective

ARC trains for up to 5,000 episodes. Each episode samples one source domain, splits its labeled instances into a 60-instance support set and 30-instance query set, and minimizes

 $\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{L}_{\text{reg}}-\lambda_{e}\,\mathcal{H}(\boldsymbol{\alpha}),$ 

(2)

mean squared error between the prediction and the domain-level z-scored plan length, regularized by the negative entropy of the attention weights ( $\lambda_{e}=0.05$ ) to prevent collapse onto one support instance. Every term is optimized by one outer AdamW update (lr $3\times 10^{-4}$ , weight decay $10^{-4}$ , cosine annealing to $10^{-5}$ , gradient clipping $1.0$ ) per episode - there is no inner-loop adaptation step. Gripper is the validation domain: every 200 episodes we score ARC zero-shot on Gripper, keep the best checkpoint, and stop after 10 checks with no improvement; the kept, not the last, checkpoint is evaluated. We report mean $\pm$ std over five seeds (0, 1, 2, 42, 123) for every trained method.

## 5 Experiments

## 5.1 Setup

## Domains.

Seven classical STRIPS domains modeled on IPC domains, generated by our own code: source domains Depot, Rovers, Satellite; held-out test domains Blocksworld (BW), Logistics (LOG), Mystery-Blocksworld (MBW); validation domain Gripper (early stopping only). Each has 200 generated instances; only BFS-solved instances carry a label and enter any computation: 126 (BW), 164 (LOG), 133 (MBW), 132 (Depot), 199 (Rovers), 126 (Satellite), 181 (Gripper). Mystery-Blocksworld shares Blocksworld’s state space but replaces every predicate and action name with an uninformative symbol.

## Metric.

Our primary metric is absolute Spearman rank correlation $|\rho|$ between a predicted score and $n^{\star}$ , over each test domain’s BFS-solved instances; a ranking measure requiring no threshold tuning. For routing (Section 6.3), we report the fraction of instances solved, with plan validity checked by a state-simulating executor. Confidence intervals: Appendix B.1.

## Baselines.

Object count $|\mathcal{O}|$ ; unsatisfied goal-fact count; ridge regression on $x_{s}$ ; LogME, fit with evidence-optimal regularization rather than a fixed value; H-score; a non-episodic MLP (ERM); and $h_{\text{add}}$ , computed from each instance’s grounded action set via a relaxed planning graph with all delete effects ignored, with no learning and no target-domain data. $h_{\text{add}}$ is not an $O(1)$ read: it needs the same grounded action set a planner would use. We reconstruct this independently per domain — from object-naming conventions and each instance’s own saved initial state, never reusing the labeling code — and verify it: re-simulating every saved BFS-optimal plan against the reconstructed action set succeeds with zero failures across all $1{,}006$ labeled instances in seven domains.

## 6.2 Semantic Perturbations Separate Structural from Behavioral Difficulty

Section 6.1 asked whether difficulty can be predicted from structure; here we ask whether an LLM’s behavioral success tracks that same difficulty, or moves independently of it when only the problem’s symbols change. Qwen 2.5-72B is the system under study here — generating and attempting plans, not predicting difficulty (that role is evaluated separately in Appendix B.7).

We progressively corrupt the same 40 BFS-solved Blocksworld instances. For each $k\in\{0,25,50,75,100\}\%$ , we replace a fraction $k$ of predicate and action names with meaningless tokens, applied consistently across domain and problem files. This preserves every precondition, effect, state, goal, and optimal plan length $n^{\star}(\pi)$ ; only the vocabulary’s readability changes. A plan is valid only if every action uses a name from the renamed domain shown to the model and, mapped back, passes a state-simulating executor (parsing, arity, preconditions, goal; Appendix A.2).

Success falls from $67.5\%$ to $40.0\%$ (Table 3); the endpoint intervals, $[52.0,79.9]$ and $[26.3,55.4]$ , barely overlap. The decline is not perfectly monotonic step to step ( $47.5\to 37.5\to 40.0$ across the last three levels), consistent with $N=40$ being too small to resolve small adjacent differences; we read the endpoint-to-endpoint effect, not a strict trend. Refusal rate is $0\%$ at every level — the model always attempts a plan, and the effect is carried entirely by a rising rate of malformed and inapplicable actions.

Structural difficulty is unchanged across $k$ by construction. Comparing structural estimators across BW and MBW in Table 1 is not a paired test of this, since they are independently generated instance sets, not renamings of the same instances (goal count $0.764$ vs. $0.673$ ; ridge $0.644$ vs. $0.587$ ; object count $0.764$ vs. $0.662$ ). The paired test is the scrambling experiment itself, where $n^{\star}(\pi)$ is identical for every instance at every $k$ : the $27.5$ -point drop, with no accompanying refusal-rate change, is attributable entirely to semantic interpretability. The same transition system, the same optimal plan length, the same object count produce a substantially different success rate purely as a function of vocabulary interpretability.

## 6.3 Structural Difficulty Alone Is Insufficient for LLM Routing

Each instance is routed to Qwen 2.5-72B or a symbolic fallback; a routed instance counts as solved only if the selected component returns a valid plan. Static always uses the fallback; Oracle picks the successful component per instance. We read it instead as asking: among instances routed to the LLM, how often would it also have succeeded? A comparison between scoring methods, not between routing and never-routing.

ARC-FS is the strongest routing signal despite being the weakest ranker. ARC-FS reaches $99$ – $100\%$ on every domain, ahead of $|\mathcal{O}|$ -FS ( $86.0$ / $77.0$ / $85.0$ ) and Goal-FS ( $64.0$ everywhere); ARC-ZS is the best zero-shot signal on BW and LOG, tied on MBW. This is notable because ARC was the weakest ranker in Section 6.1. Ranking requires correct order across the full difficulty range; routing requires only separating “likely succeeds” from “likely fails” at one threshold, a coarser distinction a poor ranker can apparently still support. We do not read this as rehabilitating ARC as an estimator — routing performance should not be assumed to track ranking performance, in either direction.

LOG is where every zero-shot policy is weakest ( $\leq 50\%$ ) and where calibration helps most (ARC: $+49$ points ZS $\to$ FS), consistent with the LLM’s own LOG success being near zero regardless of structural difficulty (Appendix A.3): little for a zero-shot score to find, so calibration does most of the work of learning where the LLM can be trusted. A router calibrated on a small amount of target-model feedback adapts to vocabulary-specific LLM behavior far more than a zero-shot structural score does - using a score that is, in isolation, a poor ranker. Practical hybrid systems should combine a general structural score with behavioral feedback rather than relying on either alone: zero-shot scores here are modest (at best $73\%$ correctly identified as LLM-tractable), while 140 calibration examples close most of the remaining gap for every method tested.

## 7 Conclusion

We asked whether the structural difficulty of a classical planning instance can be estimated without solving it, and whether an LLM’s actual planning success tracks that difficulty. On seven STRIPS domains, structural difficulty is well estimated by simple, training-free means: $h_{\text{add}}$ reaches mean $|\rho|=0.931$ with optimal plan length, goal count $0.802$ , ridge on 28 structural features $0.707$ , object count $0.657$ . Ridge beats object count on six of seven domains leave-one-domain-out, and $h_{\text{add}}$ ’s correctness is independently verified: zero failures re-simulating $1{,}006$ saved BFS plans against freshly reconstructed action sets.

The learned-estimation result is squarely negative. ARC, built specifically to combine structural features with foundation-model embeddings under a protocol fixed before training (five seeds, early stopping, a pre-declared added-value criterion), does not meet that criterion: mean $0.400$ ( $0.403$ gated), below every deterministic baseline except LogME and H-score, below a linear model on its own inputs ( $0.707$ ), below its own weaker input scored alone ( $x_{e}$ alone: $0.412$ ), far below $h_{\text{add}}$ . The shortfall persists leave-one-domain-out, within object-count groups, on three custom domains outside the design loop, and across every attention-direction and residualization variant tested, including one post hoc setting we decline to adopt because selecting it required looking at the test domains. Prediction-level ensembling partially closes the gap ( $0.595$ ) but does not close it. We read this as a genuine negative result for cross-domain learned retrieval on this problem: a classical, zero-training heuristic transferring better than a purpose-built learned architecture is itself a useful finding, and future estimators in this space should be reported alongside $h_{\text{add}}$ , not only against weaker scalar baselines.

A controlled predicate-renaming intervention holds structural difficulty fixed by construction while varying vocabulary interpretability: valid-plan success falls from $67.5\%$ to $40.0\%$ as corruption rises from $0\%$ to $100\%$ , refusal rate flat at $0\%$ throughout, the entire effect carried by malformed and inapplicable actions rather than the model declining to answer. This separates structural difficulty, already well estimated by $h_{\text{add}}$ , from behavioral difficulty, which depends on semantic interpretability and is not reducible to it. The consequence for hybrid planning is more nuanced than “routing helps” or “routing fails”: with no solver-failure budget modeled, every policy is bounded above by always-use-the-solver, so routing instead measures which structural score best identifies LLM-tractable instances — and a calibrated ARC score is the best such signal despite being the weakest ranker in the paper, indicating routing and ranking are distinct tasks. An LLM given ARC’s own conditioning information, with no training, estimates difficulty via in-context reasoning (mean $|\rho|=0.826$ ) far better than ARC does after training, second only to $h_{\text{add}}$ overall.

Structural planning difficulty across these domains is close to a solved estimation problem: a 1970s-vintage AI-planning technique solves it better than the learned architecture we purpose-built for the task, and an LLM’s untrained in-context reasoning over the same inputs matches that technique closely. The open problem for hybrid LLM-planning systems is not a better structural-difficulty predictor, but modeling the semantic, vocabulary-dependent component of LLM behavior that a structural score, by construction, cannot see, and that routing cannot fully compensate for even when calibrated.

## A.2 LLM Prompt and Plan-Validity Protocol

We evaluate Qwen 2.5-72B on the 200 generated instances of each held-out domain for the routing experiment, and on 40 BFS-solved Blocksworld instances at each corruption level for the scrambling experiment. Generation uses temperature $0$ , a maximum of $2{,}048$ output tokens, and no domain-specific demonstrations. Each prompt contains the complete PDDL domain file and the problem file for one instance. The model is instructed to reason through the state transitions and to return only the final action sequence. The prompt template is reproduced in full in Appendix D.

Generated plans are evaluated by execution rather than by textual similarity to a reference plan. A custom PDDL state-space executor performs three checks: (1) every output line must parse as a syntactically valid grounded action; (2) each action’s preconditions must hold in the state produced by all preceding actions; and (3) the goal condition must hold after the final action is applied. A response is counted as a valid plan only if all three checks succeed. Any malformed action, inapplicable action, or final state that fails to satisfy the goal is counted as a planning failure; no partial credit is assigned. For renamed domains, an action name that does not belong to the renamed domain shown to the model is also counted as a failure, and the remaining actions are mapped back to the original names before the executor is applied (renaming preserves the transition system).

For the semantic-corruption analysis, a refusal is recorded when the model returns the designated NOPLAN response or produces an empty response. Refusals are reported separately from invalid generated plans: the former indicate that no executable action sequence was proposed, whereas the latter indicate that the model attempted a plan that failed formal verification.

## Appendix B Evidence for Cross-Domain Structural Estimation

This appendix provides additional evidence for the cross-domain structural-estimation results reported in the main paper. The analyses address seven complementary questions: whether the primary correlations are statistically stable; whether transfer persists under alternative domain splits; whether stronger non-episodic learners change the picture; whether inexpensive scalar statistics are sufficient; whether predictions carry information after controlling for object count; whether they are associated with search effort measured by a different planner; and whether a purely LLM-based predictor, given the same conditioning information, reproduces the result. These analyses are intended to delimit, rather than broaden, the main claim: structural statistics transfer across domains, and any added value of a learned estimator such as ARC has to be judged against them.

All five, however, are clearly exceeded by $h_{\text{add}}$ (mean $0.931$ ), a delete-relaxation planning heuristic in the family of classical additive and FF-style heuristics used to guide heuristic-search planners: we compute it as the sum, over each unsatisfied goal fact, of the level at which that fact is first achieved in a planning graph built with all delete effects ignored, from the instance’s own initial state and full grounded action set. This is not an $O(1)$ read: it requires the same grounded action set a real planner would use, together with a bounded graph expansion (capped at depth $30$ in our implementation), though it never performs a search over the real (non-relaxed) state space and uses no target-domain labels.

## Correctness verification.

Reconstructing each domain’s grounded action set independently of the labeling code is the step most likely to silently go wrong, so we verify it directly rather than assuming it: for every one of the seven domains, we re-simulate every already-saved BFS-optimal plan against the independently reconstructed action list and check it still reaches the goal. This succeeds with zero failures on all $1{,}006$ BFS-solved instances with $n^{\star}>0$ across the seven domains (BW $111/111$ , Depot $132/132$ , Gripper $157/157$ , Logistics $158/158$ , Mystery-BW $123/123$ , Rovers $199/199$ , Satellite $126/126$ ); $h_{\text{add}}$ is reported only for domains that pass this check.

## The Logistics tie is not a coincidence.

A paired bootstrap ( $2{,}000$ resamples) of $h_{\text{add}}$ against goal count gives $\Delta|\rho|=+0.119$ $[+0.057,+0.192]$ on Blocksworld and $+0.236$ $[+0.143,+0.344]$ on Mystery-Blocksworld — both robustly positive — but exactly $\Delta=0.000$ with a zero-width interval on Logistics. In our Logistics domain, the relaxed graph reaches every unsatisfied (at pkg city) goal fact at the same graph depth (any truck can relax-reach any city in one step, since the relaxed problem ignores the precondition that a truck already be present), so $h_{\text{add}}$ reduces to an exact constant multiple of the number of unsatisfied goal facts on this domain — a perfectly monotonic transform of goal count, hence an identical Spearman correlation on every resample. This is a property of this domain’s specific action structure, not a general reason to expect $h_{\text{add}}$ and goal count to agree elsewhere, and they clearly do not agree on the other two domains.

## $h_{\text{add}}$ as an additional feature, not just a standalone score.

We also tested appending $h_{\text{add}}$ as a 29th feature to ridge regression on $x_{s}$ , trained and evaluated identically to the $x_{s}$ -only ridge row elsewhere in this paper. The result is mixed, not uniformly positive: mean $|\rho|$ changes by $+0.095$ on Mystery-Blocksworld, $-0.001$ on Logistics, and $-0.077$ on Blocksworld, relative to ridge on $x_{s}$ alone. $h_{\text{add}}$ ’s strength as a standalone score therefore does not automatically transfer to a learned model that simply concatenates it with the existing features under an untuned ridge fit; whether a properly regularized or rescaled combination recovers more of that strength is untested.

Table 10 does not by itself show whether ARC captures information inaccessible to $h_{\text{add}}$ or to every scalar surrogate; the within-size analysis below addresses the narrower question of whether predictions are reducible to object count, and the comparison ARC must ultimately be judged against is $h_{\text{add}}$ ’s $0.931$ , not goal count’s $0.802$ .

## B.7 Comparison with a Purely LLM-Based Difficulty Predictor

A natural alternative to ARC’s dedicated architecture is to ask an LLM to predict instance difficulty directly, conditioned on the same information available to ARC’s support set: a collection of labeled source-domain instances, each paired with its optimal plan length. If an LLM given this conditioning information could match the ranking performance of structural estimators through in-context reasoning alone, this would suggest that a dedicated estimator is unnecessary. We test this directly using Qwen 2.5-72B, the same model used throughout Sections 6.2–6.3.

## Protocol.

We construct a support block of 20 labeled examples drawn from the three source domains (Depot, Rovers, Satellite), each rendered as its problem PDDL together with its BFS-optimal plan length, identical in content to the support set ARC conditions on at inference time. We evaluate two elicitation strategies. In the absolute scoring variant, the model is shown the support block followed by a query instance from a held-out domain and asked to output a single integer difficulty score from 0 (trivially easy) to 100 (very hard); we compute Spearman correlation between these scores and $n^{\star}$ over 60 sampled query instances per domain. In the pairwise comparison variant, the model is instead shown two query instances at a time and asked which requires the longer plan; we sample 150 random pairs per domain over 120 sampled instances and convert the resulting pairwise judgments into a per-instance score via win rate (the fraction of comparisons in which an instance is judged harder), then compute the same Spearman correlation against $n^{\star}$ . Full prompt templates for both variants are provided in Appendix D.

## Results.

The absolute-scoring variant is the second-strongest method in this entire paper after $h_{\text{add}}$ : mean $|\rho|=0.826$ , ahead of goal count ( $0.802$ ), ridge regression on $x_{s}$ ( $0.707$ ), and ARC ( $0.400$ ) by a wide margin, and within $0.105$ of $h_{\text{add}}$ itself. This is a genuinely useful result in its own right, distinct from the paper’s ARC-specific finding: in-context reasoning over raw PDDL text, given only 20 labeled examples and no training, estimates plan-length difficulty nearly as well as the best training-free heuristic we tested, and substantially better than every structural statistic except $h_{\text{add}}$ . Pairwise comparison is markedly weaker ( $0.524$ ) despite the common intuition that relative judgments are more reliable for LLMs than absolute ones; with only $\approx 2.5$ comparisons per instance on average, the win-rate aggregation is a noisy estimate of the underlying ranking, and we would expect this variant to improve with substantially more pairs per instance, though we did not run additional pairs due to compute cost. Both variants are weakest on Mystery-Blocksworld ( $|\rho|=0.723$ and $0.489$ , respectively), mirroring ARC’s own within-size difficulty on this domain; one plausible explanation is that the LLM must parse the same scrambled predicate names studied in Section 6.2 in order to reason about query difficulty, though we did not run the progressive-scrambling intervention on this baseline specifically to confirm it. Both methods perform best on Logistics, consistent with Logistics difficulty being comparatively easy to recover from coarse size cues (Appendix B.4).

We conclude that in-context reasoning over raw PDDL text, given the identical conditioning information as ARC’s support set, substantially exceeds the trivial object-cardinality baseline and approaches the strongest training-free heuristic we tested, while ARC’s learned representation does not. This supports the claim that ARC’s shortfall is specific to its architecture and training procedure, rather than to the conditioning information being insufficient: the same information, used in-context by a sufficiently capable LLM, is close to the best signal available in this paper.

## Appendix C Evidence for the ARC Design

This appendix evaluates the design choices underlying ARC. We first test whether semantic and structural representations provide different predictive signals and whether naive feature fusion is sufficient. We then isolate the direction of the asymmetric attention. Finally, we examine robustness to the sentence encoder and residualisation hyperparameters. These analyses are intended to test, not assume, a division of labour between the representations.

## Appendix D Prompts Used in LLM Evaluation

All LLM evaluations use temperature $0$ and maximum $2{,}048$ output tokens.
Prompts follow the chain-of-thought PDDL formatting of [17].
No domain-specific demonstrations are provided in the zero-shot setting.
Brackets [PLACEHOLDER] denote fields filled at runtime.

## D.1 Prompt 1: PDDL Plan Generation (Main Evaluation)

Used to evaluate LLM planning validity on all three test domains
(Blocksworld, Logistics, Mystery-Blocksworld) for Table 3
and Table 4. The domain file contains the full PDDL domain
description; the problem file contains the specific instance. This is the
canonical copy of the prompt; Appendix A.2 refers back
to it rather than reproducing it a second time.

Validity checking.
Generated plans are verified by a custom PDDL state-space executor that:
(1) parses action syntax; (2) checks each action’s preconditions against
the current state; (3) verifies the goal holds in the final state.
A plan is valid only if all three checks pass.

## Prompt 2: Mystery-Blocksworld Evaluation

Mystery-Blocksworld instances use the same prompt as Prompt 1,
but the domain file has all predicate and action names replaced
by semantically uninformative symbols
(e.g., on $\to$ pred7, stack $\to$ act3).
No additional instructions are given about the scrambling.
This tests whether LLMs can ground their world model from PDDL
structure alone, without semantic cues.

## Appendix E Custom Non-IPC Planning Domains

## E.1 Introduction

To evaluate ARC on domains completely outside the IPC benchmark suite,
we construct three custom STRIPS planning domains from scratch:
Rescue Mission, Robot Arm Stacking, and
Library Books.
These domains are designed to satisfy three criteria:
(1) pure STRIPS compatibility (no action costs, numeric fluents, or
conditional effects) to enable BFS-based difficulty labeling with
pyperplan;
(2) multi-dimensional difficulty structure, where optimal plan length
is governed by at least two interacting structural factors rather than
object count alone; and
(3) no overlap with any IPC 2002–2023 competition domain in terms of
predicate vocabulary, action structure, or generator seed.

Each domain is paired with a randomized instance generator that produces
problems across a controlled range of structural complexity parameters.
Difficulty labels (optimal plan length $n^{*}$ ) are obtained by BFS with
the same budget used in the main evaluation
( $T_{\text{max}}{=}12$ , $N_{\text{max}}{=}5{,}000$ ).
The domains are used exclusively for zero-shot evaluation;
no instances from these domains appear in ARC’s training or
validation set.

Table 19 shows the same pattern observed throughout this paper, generalizing to domains entirely outside both ARC’s training distribution and its architecture-design loop (these three domains were never used to tune any method). Goal count is the strongest zero-shot statistic on all three, including Library Books ( $0.856$ ) where it is substantially ahead of Ridge on $x_{s}$ ( $0.693$ ). ARC is competitive only on Library Books ( $0.679$ , close to Ridge’s $0.693$ ) and is clearly the weakest method on Rescue Mission ( $0.099$ , against $0.224$ – $0.805$ for the alternatives) and on Robot Arm, where it is close to but below Ridge and goal count. We do not have a generator-identified reason why Library Books is the one domain where ARC is competitive; one candidate explanation is that Library Books’ very small sample size ( $N=35$ ) gives its correlation estimate high variance in either direction, but we note this as a candidate, not a finding, since we did not design a test to distinguish it from a genuine difference in domain structure.

Example: A Rescue Mission planning instance

Consider one agent, two rooms connected by a corridor, one supply item located in the second room, and one victim in the second room requiring that supply.
⬇
(:objects agent1 room1 room2 supply1 victim1)

(:init (at agent1 room1) (connected room1 room2)

 (connected room2 room1) (at supply1 room2)

 (at victim1 room2) (needs victim1 supply1))

(:goal (rescued victim1))

The agent must move to room2, pick up the supply, and rescue the victim: $\text{move}(\text{room1},\text{room2})$ , $\text{pick-supply}(\text{supply1})$ , $\text{rescue-victim}(\text{victim1},\text{supply1})$ , giving $n^{\star}(\pi)=3$ . Unlike the Blocksworld example in Section 3, difficulty here depends jointly on the agent’s distance to the needed supply and on which victims require which supplies—the two-factor structure referenced in criterion (2) above, which does not reduce to a single scalar such as room count or victim count.

## E.2 Dataset Generation

All three domains are specified in typed STRIPS PDDL and generated
programmatically by Python scripts that vary structural parameters
across a fixed random seed range.
The generation process does not use any language model or neural network;
all PDDL domain and problem files are produced by deterministic
rule-based generators.

## Rescue Mission.

The Rescue Mission domain models an emergency response scenario in which
one or more agents navigate a graph of rooms, collect typed supplies,
and rescue victims that each require a specific supply type.
Room connectivity is generated as a random sparse graph (chain backbone
plus random extra edges), ensuring reachability.
Structural parameters: number of victims ( $n_{v}\in[2,12]$ ),
number of rooms ( $n_{r}=n_{v}+1$ ),
number of agents ( $n_{a}=\lceil n_{v}/3\rceil$ ),
number of supply types ( $n_{s}=n_{v}$ ).
Goals specify a random subset of victims to rescue.
Difficulty is jointly governed by agent–supply co-location distance
(navigation steps to reach supplies) and victim distribution across rooms,
making it multi-dimensional.
The domain has 3 actions (move, pick-supply,
rescue-victim), 8 predicates, and 4 typed object classes.
A total of 127 BFS-solvable instances were generated (from 200 attempts).

## Robot Arm Stacking.

The Robot Arm domain models a tabletop manipulation task in which a
single-gripper robot arm picks and places blocks across multiple tables,
achieving a specified stacking configuration.
Initial states are randomized: blocks are distributed across tables, and
a random subset are pre-stacked (the robot must partially disassemble
before re-assembling).
Structural parameters: number of blocks ( $n_{b}\in[3,12]$ ),
number of tables ( $n_{t}=\lfloor n_{b}/3\rfloor+1$ , minimum 2),
number of initial stacks (random in $[0,\lfloor n_{b}/3\rfloor]$ ),
goal configuration (random stacking of $2$ – $n_{b}$ blocks on one table).
The domain has 4 actions (pick-from-table,
pick-from-block, place-on-table, stack),
7 predicates, and 2 typed object classes.
A total of 95 BFS-solvable instances were generated (from 200 attempts).

## Library Books.

The Library Books domain models a library reshelving task in which
patrons check out books from shelves, books are collected onto a cart,
and carts must be moved to the correct shelf before books can be
reshelved.
Each book has a designated correct shelf; a book is returned only
when it is physically shelved at that location.
Structural parameters: number of books ( $n_{b}\in[2,5]$ for solvable
instances), number of shelves ( $n_{s}=n_{b}$ , minimum 2),
number of carts ( $n_{c}=1$ ), number of patrons ( $n_{p}=\lceil n_{b}/2\rceil$ ).
The domain has 4 actions (checkout-book, return-to-cart,
shelve-book, move-cart),
8 predicates, and 4 typed object classes.
A total of 15 BFS-solvable instances were generated (from 200 attempts;
the low solve rate reflects the compounded constraint that book, cart,
and correct shelf must all be aligned simultaneously).

## E.3 Dataset Verification

To verify that our instance generators produce valid, sensible planning
problems, three human annotators with graduate-level AI planning
background independently evaluated a random sample of 30 instances per
domain (90 instances total) on two criteria:

1. 

Validity: Is the PDDL problem well-formed?
Does the initial state satisfy all type constraints?
Is the goal reachable in principle (ignoring plan length)?

2. 

Difficulty coherence: Does the optimal plan length
obtained by BFS agree with the annotator’s intuitive difficulty
ranking? (Binary: agree / disagree with BFS rank ordering within
a pair of instances of the same object count.)

Annotators were provided with the domain PDDL, the problem PDDL,
and the BFS-computed $n^{*}$ value. They were asked to flag any instance
where (1) the initial state appeared inconsistent, (2) the goal was
trivially achieved from the initial state (degenerate), or (3) the
BFS plan length seemed implausible given the stated parameters.
Disagreements were resolved by majority vote; no instance required
exclusion, confirming generator correctness.

## Annotation Guidelines

The following guidelines were provided to all three annotators before
the verification session. Annotators completed a 10-instance
calibration set and discussed edge cases before the main annotation
began. All annotation was conducted independently.

Annotation Guidelines – Custom Planning Domain Verification

Task Overview.
You will evaluate PDDL planning problem instances for correctness
and difficulty coherence. Each instance consists of:
(1) a domain file (shared across all instances in a domain),
(2) a problem file specifying objects, initial state, and goal,
(3) the BFS-computed optimal plan length $n^{*}$ (or “UNSOLVED”).
Annotation Dimensions.
1. Validity (Binary: Valid / Invalid)
Mark an instance as Invalid if any of the following hold:

• 

The initial state contains a fact that violates type constraints
(e.g., an object declared as type block appears in a
predicate that expects type table).

• 

The goal is already satisfied in the initial state
(degenerate instance, $n^{*}=0$ ).

• 

The goal refers to objects not declared in :objects.

• 

Two facts in the initial state logically contradict each other
(e.g., a block declared both clear and on another
block that is itself clear).

Otherwise, mark as Valid. You are not asked to verify
that the goal is reachable (BFS handles this); only that the problem
is well-formed.
2. Difficulty Coherence (Binary: Agree / Disagree)
You will be given pairs of instances from the same domain with the
same object count ( $|O|$ ) but different BFS plan lengths.
For each pair $(A,B)$ where $n^{*}(A)<n^{*}(B)$ , indicate whether
you agree that $A$ is intuitively easier than $B$ based on the
initial and goal states.
Mark Agree if:

• 

The instance with smaller $n^{*}$ has a goal that appears
“closer” to the initial state (fewer rearrangements needed,
shorter chains of dependencies).

• 

The structural explanation is apparent: e.g., in Rescue Mission,
if $n^{*}(A)<n^{*}(B)$ because agent and supply are in the same room
in $A$ but not in $B$ , this is coherent.

Mark Disagree if:

• 

The BFS plan length ranking contradicts your inspection of the
initial and goal states.

• 

The pair appears to have been generated incorrectly (e.g.,
 $n^{*}(B)$ is lower than expected given visibly more complex initial
state).

Important: Do not flag a pair as Disagree simply because the
two instances are structurally similar; small differences in state
configuration can produce large differences in plan length, and this
is expected behavior.
Edge Cases.

• 

UNSOLVED instances: If $n^{*}$ = UNSOLVED (BFS budget
exceeded), annotate Validity only; skip Difficulty Coherence
for this instance.

• 

Uncertainty: If you are unsure, default to Valid /
Agree and note the instance ID in the comments field.

• 

Domain-specific notes: For Library Books, a book is
considered “placed” only when it is on its correct shelf
(check the correct-shelf predicate in the initial state).
For Robot Arm, table-free means no block is
directly on that table (stacked blocks do not occupy table
space directly). For Rescue Mission, connectivity is symmetric
(connected r1 r2 also means connected r2 r1);
verify both directions appear in the initial state.

Calibration.
The first 10 instances are a calibration set. After you complete them,
discuss any disagreements with the other annotators before proceeding
to the main set. Do not discuss individual annotations during
the main set.

Following the annotation session, annotators reported that the
Rescue Mission and Robot Arm instances were straightforward to evaluate,
while Library Books required careful attention to the
correct-shelf predicate.
No edge cases required adjudication beyond the calibration discussion.

Annotator qualifications.
All three annotators hold or are pursuing graduate degrees in
computer science with specialization in artificial intelligence.
Each had prior exposure to PDDL through coursework or research;
none had participated in the design or generation of the domains
being evaluated.
Annotation was conducted voluntarily with no financial compensation.

## VPFlow: Value-Predictive Planning with Imperfect World Models for Offline Goal Reaching

Yunzhe Hu, Dong Xu

Affiliation: School of Computing and Data Science 

The University of Hong Kong

Abstract

Offline goal-conditioned reinforcement learning enables skill acquisition by reaching any goal from any state without dense rewards. Model-based methods allow for planning by scaling test-time compute to solve complex tasks. Combining both holds appealing promise for pre-training on suboptimal demonstrations and zero-shot adaptation via goal specification and planning. Yet existing methods assume effective planning requires accurate next-state prediction in pixel or latent space. In this work, we show that value prediction is more useful for planning, whereas accurate state prediction is often unnecessary. Our key insight is that offline goal reaching naturally admits a closed-form Monte Carlo return, enabling value regression to replace temporal-difference bootstrapping. We use this return as the sole supervision for latent world models: imagined latents are trained to predict goal-reaching value rather than match future states, implicitly preventing representation collapse. These planning-oriented representations let imperfect world models imagine on the level sets of value field beyond dataset transitions. We then present VPFlow, a value-predictive planner that pairs the world models with a one-step flow policy and plans in its noise space via evolutionary search. It enables policy improvement with imagined generalized advantage and reduces out-of-distribution queries common in action-space planning. On 30 state and pixel OGBench datasets, VPFlow surpasses model-free baselines by at least 30.3 and 10.1 percentage points in success rate, respectively, and outperforms latent planners LeWorldModel, PLDM, and TD-MPC2. It achieves this despite a latent prediction error orders of magnitude larger than with state prediction, while minimizing this error lowers success rates. This demonstrates that value-predictive representations, not exact state prediction, are what offline planning requires.

## 1 Introduction

Offline reinforcement learning (RL) seeks to learn reusable behaviors purely from previously
collected trajectories with no additional environment interaction (Levine et al., 2020).
In its goal-conditioned setting, an agent is tasked with reaching a desired goal at test time without optimizing a hand-designed reward (Kaelbling, 1993; Schaul et al., 2015; Park et al., 2025a).
This makes offline goal reaching especially attractive for reward-free skill acquisition: relabeling future observations as goals turns one unlabeled dataset into supervision for many tasks (Andrychowicz et al., 2017).
Pairing it with model-based planning is a natural next step: pre-train on offline data once, then adapt to new goals in zero shot by searching over imagined futures at test time.
This combination is promising, yet comparatively unexplored.

Closed-form hindsight Monte Carlo (MC) returns provide the temporal signal and act as an implicit regularizer.

Strong offline goal-conditioned methods, spanning implicit Q-learning (Kostrikov et al., 2022), contrastive learning (Eysenbach et al., 2022), and quasi-metric representations (Wang et al., 2023; Myers et al., 2025), learn a reactive policy or value function from relabeled
trajectories and act greedily at deployment.
While robust and efficient, they are static at test time: once training ends, additional inference compute cannot improve decisions on challenging tasks.
Model-based planning offers a complementary advantage.
From Dyna to TD-MPC (Sutton, 1991; Hafner et al., 2019; Hafner et al., 2020; Hansen et al., 2022), learned world models convert extra test-time compute into better decisions by searching over imagined futures.
Yet these methods share a foundational assumption that has migrated from pixels to latents largely unquestioned: the world model must accurately predict the next state, or the next latent embedding,
for planning to succeed (Hafner et al., 2019; Schwarzer et al., 2021; Sobal et al., 2025; Maes et al., 2026b).

We argue that this state-predictive assumption is both overly restrictive and misdirected.
What a planner needs is not a model that reproduces the next observation, but one that preserves the
relative ordering of candidate futures according to their goal-reaching value.
This view builds on a rich lineage: value prediction networks learn dynamics only as much as they
reproduce Bellman backups (Oh et al., 2017); universal value functions and successor representations
factor value into future occupancy and a goal-dependent readout
(Dayan, 1993; Schaul et al., 2015; Barreto et al., 2017); and the value-equivalence
principle formalizes when a model that is inaccurate as a simulator remains exact for control
(Grimm et al., 2020).
We take this principle a step further: an imagined latent only needs to land on the right level set
of the value field, so the representation worth learning for planning is value-predictive rather than state-predictive.
Offline goal reaching makes this directly actionable.
Because the relabeled reward is sparse, the Monte Carlo (MC) return from any state to any future
goal on its trajectory is a closed-form function of their temporal distance.
Value learning thus simplifies from temporal-difference bootstrapping to direct regression, and
that regression target can supervise the latent dynamics end to end, acting as an implicit
regularizer against representation collapse.
A model trained this way may be an imperfect simulator yet remain an effective planner.

Motivated by this view, we introduce Value-Predictive Flow (VPFlow), which plans for offline goal-reaching tasks with a deliberately imperfect world model.
VPFlow jointly learns an observation encoder, a goal-agnostic latent dynamics model, and goal-conditioned value heads alongside a distilled flow-matching policy with action chunks.
Imagined latents are trained purely to be value-predictive: rolled forward with dataset
actions, the value predicted at each imagined latent is regressed towards the closed-form return of
the corresponding future state.
No observation is reconstructed, no target latent is matched, and no bootstrapped target network is
required.
Figure 1 contrasts alternative latent-predictive architectures with ours.

To translate a value-predictive model into reliable control, the planner must propose feasible,
in-support actions.
Standard zeroth-order planners such as the cross-entropy method (CEM) and model predictive path
integral (MPPI) (Rubinstein, 1999; Williams et al., 2017) perturb raw action sequences, which in
offline settings quickly leave the data support and expose the value function to out-of-distribution
(OOD) queries.
VPFlow instead searches in the noise space of its one-step flow policy (Park et al., 2025c),
where each candidate noise vector decodes into action chunks at the current latent state.
Because of behavior-cloning regularization, the noise search warm-starts at multi-modal, in-support
proposals and stays anchored to the data manifold.
We guide this noise steering using the generalized advantage estimator (GAE) (Schulman et al., 2016)
over imagined rollouts, instantiated as an evolution strategy via annealed mutation and
elite selection.

Across 30 locomotion and manipulation datasets from OGBench (Park et al., 2025a), VPFlow
raises the mean success rate from $32.9\%$ to $63.2\%$ over 17 state datasets and from $27.5\%$ to
 $37.6\%$ over 13 pixel datasets relative to the strongest non-hierarchical baselines, while
substantially outperforming the latent state-predictive planners LeWorldModel (Maes et al., 2026b), PLDM (Sobal et al., 2025), and TD-MPC2 (Hansen et al., 2024).
Crucially, these gains hold while the model’s next-latent prediction error sits orders of magnitude
above that of its state-predictive counterpart; conversely, enforcing state prediction closes that
gap at the cost of lowering the success rate.
This demonstrates that offline planning does not require a faithful state simulator, but
rather a value-predictive one. Our contributions are as follows:

1. 

We argue and demonstrate that value-predictive latent world models, trained solely via
closed-form Monte Carlo return regression, avoid representation collapse and suffice for offline
planning even as state-prediction error grows, challenging the pervasive assumption of
learning state-predictive world models for planning.

2. 

We propose a test-time planning algorithm tailored to generative flow policies in their
noise space, steering initial noise towards higher imagined generalized advantage while avoiding
the out-of-distribution action queries of existing action-space planning methods.

3. 

We show across 30 state and pixel datasets on OGBench that VPFlow surpasses model-free
baselines and latent state-predictive planners, and scales favorably with test-time compute on
long-horizon tasks.

## 3 Related Work

We position VPFlow within three lines of work with broader literature in
Appendix A.

Offline goal-conditioned RL.
Offline RL learns a policy from pre-collected datasets without interacting with the environment,
and needs techniques to keep actions on the data manifold during value learning
(Levine et al., 2020; Kostrikov et al., 2022; Kumar et al., 2020; Fujimoto and Gu, 2021; Park et al., 2025c). In the goal-conditioned setting, an
agent aims to reach any goal from any state (Kaelbling, 1993), and hindsight
relabeling (Andrychowicz et al., 2017) can convert any trajectories, even failed ones, into
successful ones with the goal actually reached. With high-coverage datasets, this may enable
learning a universal value function that empowers the policy to generalize to unseen goals in
zero shot (Schaul et al., 2015; Park et al., 2024).

Representations for planning with world models.
World models seek a compact representation and transition dynamics from observations, where value
can be learned in imagination (Sutton, 1991; Ha and Schmidhuber, 2018; Hafner et al., 2020; Hansen et al., 2022). Yet what representations are more effective for planning is
still an open question. Reconstruction-based methods harness the dense supervision from pixels and
have achieved remarkable success (Hafner et al., 2019; Hafner et al., 2025a). Self-predictive
representation holds promise for a more powerful model
(Schwarzer et al., 2021; LeCun, 2022; Bagatella et al., 2026), but needs stop-gradient, EMA,
or regularization to prevent collapse (Sobal et al., 2025; Maes et al., 2026b). More critically,
it fails to exploit the rich temporal-distance supervision naturally available from goal reaching.
This makes its usefulness for planning not directly obvious. Our work aims to reveal what is
essential and necessary for planning-oriented representations.

Test-time search.
With a learned world model, previous studies primarily plan actions by sampling-based optimization
(Williams et al., 2017; Janner et al., 2019; Hafner et al., 2019; Hansen et al., 2022; Park et al., 2026) or
search (Schrittwieser et al., 2020). Yet action-space planning may suffer heavily in the offline
setting, especially with low-coverage data, in that off-manifold actions can severely exploit the
planning objective.
Advances in generative policies have turned to steering the noise at test time
to keep actions in-distribution, but they need reward or other external signals and have yet to
show scaling effectiveness (Yang et al., 2024; Wagenmaker et al., 2025; Patil et al., 2026).
In contrast, VPFlow can improve itself at test time by noise steering11
 1

 We use “steering”, “planning”, and “searching” interchangeably in this paper. with imagined
rollouts, even with imperfect world models.

## 4 Problem Statement and Preliminaries

Offline goal reaching.
We consider the offline goal-conditioned reinforcement learning problem as a reward-free controlled
Markov decision process (MDP) $\mathcal{M}=(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{A},\gamma,P)$ , where
 $\mathcal{S}$ denotes the state space, $\mathcal{A}$ denotes the action space, $\gamma\in[0,1)$ 
denotes the discount factor, and $P:\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}\to\Delta(\mathcal{S})$ 
denotes the transition dynamics mapping state and action to another state distribution
 $\Delta(\cdot)$ . Given a dataset $\mathcal{D}=\{\tau^{(i)}\}_{i=1}^{N_{\mathcal{D}}}$ of
 $N_{\mathcal{D}}$ 
trajectories $\tau^{(i)}=(s_{0}^{(i)},a_{0}^{(i)},s_{1}^{(i)},\dots,s_{T_{i}}^{(i)})$ of length $T_{i}$ ,
collected by a behavior policy22
 2

 No assumption on its optimality., the objective is
to learn a goal-conditioned policy $\pi(a\mid s,g)$ that maximizes the expected discounted
utility $\mathbb{E}_{\tau}[\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\gamma^{t}\delta_{g}(s_{t})]$ for any goal $g\in\mathcal{S}$ . Here, $\delta_{g}(s_{t})$ has been referred to as the
“Dirac delta function” (Park et al., 2025a). In practice, we consider it as the indicator
function $\mathds{1}_{\{s_{t}=g\}}$ that represents the relabeled sparse reward:

 $r(s_{t},g)\coloneqq\begin{cases}1,&\text{if }s_{t}=g,\\
0,&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}$ 

(1)

Model predictive control.
Given a transition dynamics model $f:\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}\to\mathcal{S}$ , model
predictive control (MPC) addresses an online constrained optimal control problem with a lookahead
horizon $H$ and an action sequence $a_{t:t+H-1}=(a_{t},\dots,a_{t+H-1})$ at the current state $s_{t}$ ,
then executes the first action $a_{t}$ , and re-plans at the next state. More formally, MPC solves the
following optimization problem at time step $t$ :

 $\min_{a_{t:t+H-1}}\sum_{h=0}^{H-1}\ell_{t}(s_{t+h},a_{t+h})+\ell_{f}(s_{t+H}),\quad\text{s.t.}\quad s_{t+1}=f(s_{t},a_{t}),$ 

(2)

where $\ell_{t}(s,a)$ and $\ell_{f}(s)$ are the running and terminal cost functions of interest. After
finding the minimizer $a_{t:t+H-1}^{\star}$ of Equation 2, only the first action $a_{t}^{\star}$ 
is applied due to the compounding error in the dynamics.
In the model-based RL context, the cost has been instantiated using negative reward $r_{t}(s_{t},a_{t})$ and the value function $Q(s_{t},a_{t})$ or $V(s_{t})$ . Both are learned as part of
the world model $f(s_{t},a_{t})$ , which can be used to plan without interacting with the real
environment (Hafner et al., 2019; Hafner et al., 2020; Hansen et al., 2022). Planning is widely done by sampling-based trajectory optimizers (Rubinstein, 1999; Williams et al., 2017).
Another emerging way is to learn planning-enabled representations without the reward and value
function, and use a simple $\ell_{2}$ distance to the goal as the cost
(Assran et al., 2025; Sobal et al., 2025; Maes et al., 2026b).
Both lines of work assume the optimal objective is to predict the next state when learning world models. In this work, we provide evidence for another value-predictive representation view and show that it is more useful for planning.

## 5 VPFlow: Planning with Imperfect World Models

## 5.1 Value Equivalence Principle

Figure 2: The value equivalence principle view of world modeling. The value-predictive
representations of the same timestep constitute a level set of the value field.

We motivate VPFlow through the value equivalence principle (Grimm et al., 2020), which states
that two dynamics and reward models are value-equivalent with respect to a set of value functions if they induce the same Bellman operator. Consequently, one can learn another set of dynamics and reward models beyond the training examples but still keep the same effect for policy learning.

We extend this view to world modeling for offline goal reaching: two predictive representations $\hat{s},\hat{s}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}$ are value-equivalent with respect to a goal if they are evaluated identically by the goal-conditioned value function, i.e., $V_{\phi}(\hat{s},g)=V_{\phi}(\hat{s}^{\prime},g)$ (Figure 2).
In this setting, the Bellman operator can be written in closed form with sparse reward given a goal state, reducing recursive temporal-difference learning (Oh et al., 2017; Schrittwieser et al., 2020) to direct regression on fixed targets.
Intuitively, rather than constraining the dynamics models to match the future states, this principle only requires the predicted representations to lie on the corresponding level sets of value functions, and this relaxation can potentially expand the reachable space of dynamics models.

## 5.2 Hindsight Monte Carlo Return Supervision

Figure 3: VPFlow objectives.
VPFlow alternately trains (a) the goal-conditioned value function $V_{\phi}$ ,
latent dynamics $f_{\phi}$ , and encoder $E_{\phi}$ jointly, and (b) a distilled one-step flow policy
 $\pi_{\theta}$ .
The value function regresses the closed-form Monte Carlo returns $R_{h}$ on imagined rollouts. The
policy is improved by maximizing the imagined value from the learned dynamics model, regularized
by conditional flow matching to stay close to the
data manifold.

Q-learning typically uses bootstrapping that predicts Bellman backup online with a target network
and EMA to learn the action-value function $Q(s,a)$ . Though this heuristic stabilizes training
(Tang et al., 2023), it makes TD learning less principled for scaling on larger datasets
(Park et al., 2025b).

With hindsight relabeling, every trajectory in the dataset can be relabeled with a goal as if the
agent in that trajectory has reached that goal. This naturally admits a cleaner and simpler
objective for value learning. Concretely, the target value for a state $s_{t}$ is the discounted
Monte Carlo return from $s_{t}$ to the relabeled goal $g=s_{t+d}$ on the same trajectory:

 $R(s_{t},g)=R(d)=\sum_{k=0}^{d}\gamma^{k}r(s_{t+k},g)=\begin{cases}\gamma^{d},&r(s,g)=\mathds{1}_{\{s=g\}}\\
\frac{1-\gamma^{d}}{\gamma-1},&r(s,g)=\mathds{1}_{\{s=g\}}-1\end{cases},$ 

(3)

where $d\geq 0$ is the temporal distance, i.e., the number of steps from $s_{t}$ to the goal $g$ on
the same trajectory. In practice, we use the sparse reward $r(s,g)=\mathds{1}_{\{s=g\}}-1$ ,
following prior work (Physical Intelligence et al., 2025; Park et al., 2025a).
Intuitively, this quantity measures how far the agent is from the goal as a progress signal.

The training of VPFlow alternates between the value prediction and policy learning
objectives, as shown in Figure 3 and elaborated below.

## 5.3 Value-Predictive Latent Dynamics

Given this closed-form Monte Carlo return in Equation 3, VPFlow learns the observation
encoder $E_{\phi}:\mathcal{S}\to\mathcal{Z}$ for a latent space $\mathcal{Z}$ , latent dynamics
 $f_{\phi}$ , and a goal-conditioned value function
 $V_{\phi}:\mathcal{Z}\times\mathcal{Z}\to\mathbb{R}$ . All three components are optimized
jointly with the value-prediction (VP) objective. In particular, we use the action chunk
representation of horizon $H$ as ${\bm{a}}_{t}=a_{t:t+H-1}$ , which allows for a certain level of temporal
abstraction (Li et al., 2025).
After encoding the state and goal with $E_{\phi}$ :

 $z_{t}=E_{\phi}(s_{t}),\quad z_{g}=E_{\phi}(g),$ 

(4)

the latent dynamics predicts each future latent with an action prefix:

 $\hat{z}_{t+h}=f_{\phi}(z_{t},a_{t:t+h-1}),\quad h=1,\ldots,H,\quad\hat{z}_{t}\coloneqq z_{t}.$ 

(5)

Unrolling $f_{\phi}$ with the action chunk ${\bm{a}}_{t}$ yields intermediate latents $\hat{z}_{t+1},\dots,\hat{z}_{t+H}$ , where $\hat{z}_{t+H}=f_{\phi}(z_{t},{\bm{a}}_{t})$ denotes the final predicted latent. The VP loss is then defined as:

 $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{VP}}(\phi)\coloneqq\mathbb{E}_{(s_{t},{\bm{a}}_{t})\sim\mathcal{D},g\sim\mathcal{S}}\left[\frac{1}{H+1}\sum_{h=0}^{H}M_{t+h}\odot\|V_{\phi}(\hat{z}_{t+h},z_{g})-R_{t+h}\|_{2}^{2}\right],\quad\hat{z}_{t}\coloneqq z_{t},$ 

(6)

where $R_{t+h}=R(s_{t+h},g)$ is the target of Equation 3 for the dataset state $s_{t+h}$ 
and does not depend on $\phi$ , $\odot$ is the Hadamard product, and the mask $M_{t+h}\in\{0,1\}$ 
filters out goals behind time $t+h$ and across trajectories such that the value function only
exploits the clean temporal distance signal on the same trajectory.

Equation 6 trains the encoder, latent dynamics, and value function jointly.
The value supervision nudges the predictive representation of each rollout horizon towards the level
set of the value function defined by the state-goal temporal distance, instead of aiming for perfect
state prediction. It structures the representation space with explicit meaning as progress towards
reaching the goal, unlike the regularizers that are only statistically meaningful
(Bardes et al., 2022; Balestriero and LeCun, 2025). It also expands the hypothesis space for the policy
without being too conservative inside the data manifold, as we discussed in
Section 5.1.

## 5.4 Flow Policy as a Behavior Prior

For a more expressive policy to handle the multimodality of offline datasets, we opt for a
flow-based policy similar to Flow Q-learning (Park et al., 2025c). Beyond that, the
generative policy as a behavior prior is also a key design choice to enable planning with the data
manifold constraint at test time.

The policy improvement objective is to maximize the Q-function of an action chunk
(Li et al., 2025) given encoded state and goal, $z_{t}=E_{\phi}(s_{t}),z_{g}=E_{\phi}(g)$ ,
using the learned world models:

 $\displaystyle Q(z_{t},{\bm{a}}_{t},z_{g})$ 
 $\displaystyle\coloneqq\mathbb{E}_{\hat{z}_{t+H}\sim f_{\phi}(z_{t},{\bm{a}}_{t})}\left[\sum_{h=0}^{H-1}\gamma^{h}r(z_{t+h},z_{g})+\gamma^{H}V_{\phi}(\hat{z}_{t+H},z_{g})\right]$ 

(7)

 $\displaystyle\approx\mathbb{E}_{\hat{z}_{t+H}\sim f_{\phi}(z_{t},{\bm{a}}_{t})}\left[\gamma^{H}V_{\phi}(\hat{z}_{t+H},z_{g})\right]\qquad\text{(no $\theta$ in reward)},$ 

where the action chunk ${\bm{a}}_{t}=a_{t:t+H-1}$ is decoded from Gaussian noise
 $\bm{\varepsilon}\sim\mathcal{N}({\bm{0}},I)$ by the one-step flow policy
 $\pi_{\theta}(z_{t},z_{g},\bm{\varepsilon})$ with parameters $\theta$ . Together with a velocity field
 $v_{\theta}$ , the policy learning loss is defined as:

 $\mathcal{L}_{\pi}(\theta)\coloneqq-Q(z_{t},{\bm{a}}_{t},z_{g})+\alpha_{\mathrm{CFM}}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{CFM}}+\alpha_{\mathrm{Distill}}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{Distill}}.$ 

(8)

Here, $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{CFM}}$ is the conditional flow matching (CFM) loss that fits $v_{\theta}$ 
to the dataset actions, $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{Distill}}$ distills the multi-step decoding process
using $v_{\theta}$ into $\pi_{\theta}$ , and $\alpha_{\mathrm{CFM}},\alpha_{\mathrm{Distill}}$ are their
weights. The one-step policy makes noise steering more efficient.
Detailed objective functions are explained in Appendix B.3.

Noise space steering. Planning has been widely conducted by directly optimizing
open-loop actions on an unconstrained support.
For long-horizon rollouts, actions tend to drift away from the data distribution, making the
planning objective unreliable.
One of the key benefits of a generative policy is that after training, its
noise space can be optimized to search for better actions while still being close to the training
distribution (Yang et al., 2024; Wagenmaker et al., 2025).
We adopt this strategy to steer the noise to optimize Equation 10 that uses the
imagined states unrolled from the learned dynamics.

 $\varepsilon$ -ES: evolution strategy in noise space.
CEM planners used in model-based methods maintain an elite set of actions, from which an online mean
and standard deviation are updated (Janner et al., 2019; Hansen et al., 2022; Maes et al., 2026b).
However, this design choice does not directly translate to the noise space, as their distribution
differs. Inspired by a previous gradient-free optimizer for diffusion noise
(Yang et al., 2024), we instead develop an evolution strategy that mutates samples of each
elite in noise space and greedily keeps the ones that score higher by the objective in
Equation 10. This allows the policy prior learned during training to improve
itself by adding more compute at test time without weight updates. We present the search process,
which runs for $K$ iterations, in Algorithm 1 with
more details in Appendix B.6.

## 6 Experiments

We aim to address four questions in this section: Q1: How does VPFlow compare with
model-free baselines and model-based methods that similarly plan with latent world models for offline goal-reaching tasks? Q2: Are state-predictive representations useful for planning, or do the value-predictive ones play a more essential role? Q3: Does test-time planning help, and does the search space matter more than the search algorithm? Q4: How does the learned latent actually behave during rollouts?

Setup.
We use the official OGBench datasets and tasks (Park et al., 2025a): 17 state-based datasets and

## 6.1 Q1: How Does VPFlow Perform for Offline Goal Reaching?

Comparison against model-free baselines.
Table 1 reports the results grouped by environment (full numerics in
Appendix ). Averaged over the $17$ state datasets, VPFlow nearly doubles the strongest baseline ( $63.2$ vs.
 $32.9$ for GCIQL) and leads every environment group. The gains
concentrate on manipulation and shrink on
navigation (AntMaze $68.0$ vs. $61.8$ ), where a reactive policy may already solve much of the task. This suggests that model-based planning can be more suitable for contact-rich tasks, where
test-time compute translates more directly to success. On pixel-based datasets, the gains become relatively modest ( $37.6$ vs. $27.5$ for CRL) as VPFlow trails behind in a long-horizon Visual AntMaze dataset.

Figure 4: Success rate of planners with latent world models on
the only pixel dataset where the baselines do not completely fail.

Comparison against latent planning methods.
To our surprise, on 12 of the 13 pixel datasets, all three model-based baselines score zero,
so Figure 4 reports the only dataset on which any one of them achieves a
non-zero success rate. All three train their latent to predict a future state and then plan
over action sequences. On diverse play data, neither practice stands out as the optimal
design choice. We next reveal more about the possible failure modes on these two patterns.

## 6.2 Q2: Value-Predictive or State-Predictive Latents?

This experiment aims to verify which representations are more favorable for planning. The default model trains the rollout with the value-prediction
loss $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{VP}}$ (Equation 6); the state-predictive model
trains it with the latent-prediction loss $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{LP}}$ (Equation 13), which
matches the predicted latents to the encoded states. Other configurations stay the same. Figure 5 tracks the success rate and the next-latent prediction error during training on four datasets.

Lower state prediction error, worse planning.
The state-predictive controller reduces the next-latent error by one to eight orders of magnitude,
yet its success does not improve on any of the four datasets and falls most sharply on
scene-play ( $78.4\%$ to
 $28.0\%$ , Figure 5(a)) and visual-cube-single ( $79.2\%$ to $0.0\%$ , Figure 5(c)). A model that predicts its own next latent almost perfectly is therefore not
necessarily a model that plans well. Predicting the value is more essential for planning.

The failure mode is collapse, and value prediction prevents it.
On visual-cube-single (Figure 5(c)), the latent prediction term collapses to near
zero: the encoder finds a trivially predictable latent and the agent never reaches the goal, the
degeneracy that self-predictive objectives prevent through explicit anti-collapse techniques, none
of which our architecture has. In contrast, value prediction in Equation 6 promotes temporally ordered latents that implicitly prevent collapse. Appendix provides ablations on explicit regularization over latent prediction.

performance (Table 2). Extra test-time compute, by updating the noise

(Figure 6). This supports
Equation 10 as an effective objective enabling policy improvement via search
without external signals, and planning with more compute can be made possible with imperfect world models.

Noise space proves more effective than action space for planning.
Replacing $\varepsilon$ -ES with $\varepsilon$ -CEM that updates noise with standard CEM lowers the success rate.
This is expected, as noise is updated by advantage on the decoded actions rather than the noise
itself. More notably, the success rate of CEM in action space, $\mathcal{A}$ -CEM, is even worse than the direct output ( $10.0$ vs. $28.0$ ) and not even close to its noise-space counterpart ( $10.0$ vs. $40.2$ ). This can be attributed to the advantage exploitation of off-manifold
actions, and it explains why the action-space planning design choices in PLDM and LeWM
(Sobal et al., 2025; Maes et al., 2026b) could be suboptimal with offline data. The
closed-loop
planning in the noise space of a generative policy prevents this in that its decoded actions are
trained to be in the support of the data distribution.

## 6.4 Q4: How Do the Latents Behave in Rollouts?

((a)) visual-antmaze-large-navigate-v0 Task 1

((b)) visual-antmaze-large-navigate-v0 Task 3

Figure 7: t-SNE visualization of latent trajectories from successful Visual AntMaze episodes. The solid circle is the encoded current state,
and the dashed circles are the imagined latents via dynamics for that step. Under
value-predictive training, the imagined latents leave the trajectory manifold at test time, but
the agent can still reach the goal.

Figure 7 depicts the geometry of imagined rollouts on Visual AntMaze to demonstrate the behavior of imperfect yet planning-effective learned world models.
Under value-predictive training, the dynamics model evolves latents to where the correct goal-reaching value is forecasted by the value function, without being constrained to match the corresponding embeddings from the encoder.
This helps explain why the value-predictive controller plans effectively despite the large next-latent prediction error demonstrated in Section 6.2.
It also suggests how value prediction harnesses exploratory play data more effectively: closed-form value regression structures the latent space by temporal proximity to the goal, allowing it to leave out irrelevant routes and discover a latent geometry suited to reaching the goal.
In contrast, a state-predictive world model is trained to faithfully fit latent transitions in the dataset, which may limit its generalization to unseen goals.

To examine this value-equivalent behavior more quantitatively, Figure 8 compares agents trained with value prediction $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{VP}}$ and latent prediction $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{LP}}$ on successful episodes from visual-scene-play-v0.
Starting from the same encoded state, each agent unrolls its own executed actions in both the real environment and the learned world model.
Although VP exhibits larger relative latent error across the 16-step rollout, its value gap is typically smaller than LP’s (Figure 8(a)).
The value field provides a microscopic illustration: while VP’s observed and imagined latents are further apart in Euclidean space, they lie closer in the value space (Figure 8(b)).
The value-predictive latent also moves towards higher value, reflecting closer proximity to the goal.

((a)) Relative latent error $\|\hat{z}_{h}-z_{h}\|_{2}/\|z_{h}\|_{2}$ and value error $|V(\hat{z}_{h},z_{g})-V(z_{h},z_{g})|$ along rollout horizons with mean and standard deviation over 4 evaluation seeds.

((b)) Value fields and level sets of each agent’s learned critic measured on the current, observed, and predicted next latents.

Figure 8: Rollouts in the environment and learned world models, with their value fields. With value prediction, the agent maintains a lower value error and reaches the goal successfully despite substantially larger latent prediction error.

Taken together, these results demonstrate that accurate latent-state prediction is not strictly necessary for successful planning.
Imperfect world models can remain effective and even yield superior performance (Figure ), provided that they preserve value equivalence.
The evidence that the agent can successfully reach the goal despite substantial latent prediction error suggests that latent error is an unreliable indicator of planning success; instead, value prediction should serve as a more informative metric.

## 7 Conclusions

We have challenged the pervasive assumption that planning requires a state-predictive world model.
VPFlow trains its observation encoder and latent dynamics solely via closed-form Monte Carlo
return regression—a supervision signal naturally available in sparse-reward offline goal
reaching—and plans at test time by steering a distilled one-step flow policy within its noise space to maximize imagined generalized advantage.
Across 30 state and pixel datasets on OGBench, VPFlow substantially improves upon model-free baselines and latent-predictive planners despite much higher next-latent prediction error.
In contrast, enforcing state prediction closes that error gap at the cost of degraded planning performance.
Moreover, test-time noise steering reliably elevates the reactive policy prior, demonstrating that searching within an in-support noise space matters more than the specific optimization algorithm. More broadly, these findings indicate that effective latent planning favors value-predictive representations for world models and an in-distribution search space for planning, rather than a faithful state simulator.
We discuss limitations and future directions in Appendix .

## Appendix A Extended Related Work

This appendix expands upon the three lines of work discussed in Section 3,
positioning VPFlow within the broader literature.

## A.1 Offline and Goal-Conditioned Reinforcement Learning

Offline RL learns policies from static datasets without environment interaction, mitigating
distribution shift either by biasing the policy towards the behavior distribution
(Fujimoto et al., 2019; Wu et al., 2019; Peng et al., 2019; Fujimoto and Gu, 2021) or by regularizing critic
updates (Kumar et al., 2020; Kostrikov et al., 2022). To capture multi-modal play data and
improve long-horizon credit assignment, Flow Q-learning (FQL) distills a conditional flow-matching
policy into a one-step network (Lipman et al., 2023; Park et al., 2025c), and action chunking introduces
temporal abstraction (Li et al., 2025; Park et al., 2025b). In goal-reaching settings
(Kaelbling, 1993), universal value function approximators (Schaul et al., 2015)
and hindsight relabeling (Andrychowicz et al., 2017) extract supervision from reward-free
trajectories, which offline methods combine with iterated supervised learning
(Ghosh et al., 2021), latent plans from play (Lynch et al., 2020), contrastive learning
(Eysenbach et al., 2022), quasi-metric representations (Wang et al., 2023; Myers et al., 2025),
and subgoal hierarchies (Park et al., 2023), standardized by OGBench (Park et al., 2025a).
VPFlow adopts a distilled action-chunk flow policy as its behavior prior
(Park et al., 2025c; Li et al., 2025) but departs from these static, model-free methods in two
ways: it replaces TD, contrastive, or quasi-metric representation learning with closed-form
Monte Carlo return regression over multi-step imagined rollouts, and it scales test-time compute via
receding-horizon noise steering rather than acting greedily at deployment.

## A.2 Model-Based RL and World Models

Model-based RL dates back to Dyna (Sutton, 1991) and Gaussian-process policy search
(Deisenroth and Rasmussen, 2011), with deep variants learning dynamics via pixel reconstruction
(Watter et al., 2015; Finn and Levine, 2017; Hafner et al., 2019) or latent imagination
(Ha and Schmidhuber, 2018; Hafner et al., 2020; Hafner et al., 2025a; Hafner et al., 2025b) and limiting compounding
error through short rollouts (Janner et al., 2019; Park et al., 2026).
Offline model-based methods such as MOPO (Yu et al., 2020), MOReL (Kidambi et al., 2020), COMBO
(Yu et al., 2021), and MBOP (Argenson and Dulac-Arnold, 2021) penalize or truncate rollouts where the model is uncertain, or plan with an ensemble model and a behavior prior like MBOP (Argenson and Dulac-Arnold, 2021).
Yet they still assume that accurate state prediction is a prerequisite for
planning.
Value-equivalent models take an alternative position (Grimm et al., 2020; M. Moerland et al., 2023): value
prediction networks (VPN) (Oh et al., 2017) and MuZero (Schrittwieser et al., 2020) train
dynamics purely through predicted rewards, values, and policies.
VPFlow belongs to the value-equivalent family, but unlike online methods VPN and MuZero, it exploits closed-form hindsight Monte Carlo returns in offline goal reaching as the sole supervision for both the encoder and dynamics, eliminating target networks, EMA, state-matching loss, and TD learning from training.

## A.3 Predictive Representations

Predictive representations characterize a state by its future outcomes rather than its sensory
appearance (Littman and Sutton, 2001; Carvalho et al., 2024). One line of work builds on
successor representations and features
(Dayan, 1993; Barreto et al., 2017; Barreto et al., 2018; Borsa et al., 2019), forward-backward
representations (Touati and Ollivier, 2021; Blier et al., 2021), and behavior foundation models
(Pirotta et al., 2024; Park et al., 2024) to predict discounted future occupancy for zero-shot reactive
readout.
A parallel line learns with a self-predictive objective that forecasts future latent embeddings, relying on target encoders, stop-gradients (Schwarzer et al., 2021; Guo et al., 2022; Tang et al., 2023; Ni et al., 2024; Fujimoto et al., 2025; Bagatella et al., 2026), or explicit regularizers (LeCun, 2022; Dawid and LeCun, 2024; Assran et al., 2025; Van Assel et al., 2025) to prevent collapse. Whereas occupancy-based representations omit multi-step lookahead search at test time and self-predictive world models require explicit anti-collapse heuristics without exploiting temporal-distance signals, VPFlow bridges both perspectives: it supervises multi-step latent rollouts with goal-conditioned values, which implicitly prevents collapse and organizes the latent space around temporal proximity to the goal.

## A.4 Planning in Latent Space

Sampling-based optimizers such as CEM and MPPI (Rubinstein, 1999; Williams et al., 2017) are
standard planners in world models (Hafner et al., 2019; Janner et al., 2019; Hansen et al., 2022),
complemented in visual and offline domains by energy-based or functional-distance costs
(Du et al., 2020; Tian et al., 2021), hierarchical subgoal search
(Li et al., 2022; Zhang et al., 2026), and generative adversarial behavior priors in
GOPlan (Wang et al., 2024).
Closest to our setting, PLDM (Sobal et al., 2025) and LeWorldModel (Maes et al., 2026b) train latent-predictive dynamics with VICReg (Bardes et al., 2022)
or SIGReg (Balestriero and LeCun, 2025) regularization and plan in action space via CEM to minimize
Euclidean distance to the goal embedding, whereas recent generative policy methods steer diffusion
or flow noise at test time using external rewards or online interaction
(Salimans et al., 2017; Yang et al., 2024; Wagenmaker et al., 2025; Patil et al., 2026). In
contrast, VPFlow avoids both the out-of-distribution action queries of open-loop action-space CEM
and the external reward requirements of prior noise-steering methods: it searches directly in the noise space of its distilled flow policy to maximize imagined generalized advantage under its value-predictive world model, keeping planned actions on the data manifold.

## B.1 Datasets and Preprocessing

Datasets.
We use the official OGBench datasets (Park et al., 2025a) covering both locomotion and robotic
manipulation tasks without
modification. The state suite comprises 17 datasets: pointmaze-{medium, large, giant,
teleport}-navigate, antmaze-{medium, large, giant, teleport}-navigate,
cube-{single, double, triple, quadruple}-play, scene-play, and
puzzle-{3x3, 4x4, 4x5, 4x6}-play. The pixel suite comprises the 13 visual-
counterparts of the AntMaze, Cube, Scene, and Puzzle datasets, with $64\times 64\times 3$ 
observations. Each dataset has five evaluation tasks specified by start-goal pairs.

Training samples and goal relabeling.
A training sample is a length- $H$ segment $(s_{t},a_{t},\ldots,a_{t+H-1})$ drawn uniformly from the
dataset trajectories, with the chunk length $H$ in Table . The value goal
 $g^{V}$ ( $g$ in Section 5.3) is sampled from a mixture of three sources, with the
probabilities in Table : the current observation $s_{t}$ ; a future observation
 $s_{t+d}$ on the same trajectory, with the offset $d$ drawn from the geometric distribution
 $\mathrm{Geom}(1-\gamma)$ ; and a uniformly random dataset observation. For each offset
 $h\in\{0,\ldots,H\}$ , the MC target is $R_{t+h}=(1-\gamma^{d_{h}})/(\gamma-1)$ with $d_{h}$ as the
number of temporal steps from $s_{t+h}$ to $g^{V}$ , and the mask $M_{t+h}$ is one if and only if $g^{V}$ 
is on the same trajectory at or after index $t+h$ .
Random goals therefore contribute no value target.
The policy goal $g^{\pi}$ ( $g$ in Section 5.4) is randomly sampled from a future
observation on the same trajectory.

Data augmentation.
For pixel-based tasks, we apply random-crop augmentation (edge padding, then cropping back to
 $64\times 64$ ; Table ) using one shared crop per sample for the sampled
observation and both goals, except for the Visual AntMaze environment, following the practice in
OGBench.

## B.2 Networks

Observation encoder.
State-based tasks use the identity encoder $E_{\phi}(s)=s$ . Pixel-based tasks use one IMPALA-Small
convolutional
encoder (Espeholt et al., 2018) shared by
observations and goals, followed by a multilayer perceptron (MLP) head that produces the latent
(Table ).

Dynamics model.
The predictive world model $f_{\phi}$ maps the current latent $z_{t}$ and the action sequence
 $a_{t:t+H-1}$ to subsequent latents $\hat{z}_{t+1:t+H}$ .
To enable parallel rollouts, we instantiate $f_{\phi}$ as a Transformer (Vaswani et al., 2017)
with cross-attention and causal mask in which the current latent $z_{t}$ is the query and action
chunks $a_{t:t+H-1}$ are keys and values, as depicted in Figure 9.
It uses rotary position embeddings (Su et al., 2024), and its configuration is listed in
Table .
The prediction for prefix $h$ attends only to $a_{t:t+h-1}$ , so all $H$ prefix latents of a chunk are
produced in one parallel call.

Figure 9: World model architecture. A Transformer with cross-attention and causal mask
enables parallel rollouts of future latents induced by action chunks. $B$ is the batch size, $H$ 
the chunk length, $d_{a}$ the action dimension, $d_{z}$ the latent dimension, $d$ the Transformer
width.

Value heads.
Following previous practices to handle value overestimation and uncertainty
(Fujimoto and Gu, 2021; Kostrikov et al., 2022), we adopt a twin-value-head structure, where
each head is an MLP with layer normalization (Ba et al., 2016) and GELU activations
(Hendrycks and Gimpel, 2016) (Table ), acting on the
concatenation of the encoded state $z$ and goal $z_{g}$ . Policy improvement and planning use the minimum
of the two heads, i.e., $\min\{V_{\phi,1},V_{\phi,2}\}$ . The default MC objective uses no target
value network.

Flow policy.
The velocity field $v_{\theta}$ and the distilled one-step decoder $\pi_{\theta}$ are Transformers with
sinusoidal position embeddings whose size is tuned per dataset
(Table ). Each network outputs action chunks of length
 $H$ , the same as the horizon of training samples.

State and goal conditioning adopts a similar design to that of $\pi_{0}$ (Black et al., 2025): the
projected latent $z$ and goal $z_{g}$ are prefix tokens that the $H$ action tokens attend to, and the
flow time $u$ enters through a sinusoidal embedding fused into each action token by a two-layer MLP.
The prefix tokens attend only to each other while the action tokens attend to both the prefix and
themselves bidirectionally. The velocity field $v_{\theta}$ and the one-step policy $\pi_{\theta}$ share
this design.

## B.3 Objectives

Value-predictive model.
VPFlow trains its dynamics model and observation encoders by minimizing
 $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{VP}}$ in Equation 6.
The squared error is summed over the two value heads, and the per-sample loss is clipped at a
dataset-specific threshold.

Conditional flow matching.
Behavior cloning regularization is a critical technique to reduce OOD actions in offline RL
(Fujimoto and Gu, 2021). Nonetheless, trajectories in the offline play dataset $\mathcal{D}$ 
are mostly multi-modal, so a uni-modal Gaussian policy is not expressive enough as the behavior
prior. We follow FQL (Park et al., 2025c) in using conditional flow matching as the
behavior-cloning loss and a distilled one-step flow policy to predict multimodal actions.

Conditional flow matching (Lipman et al., 2023) learns a velocity field $v_{\theta}$ that
transports samples from a source distribution $\bm{\varepsilon}\sim\mathcal{N}({\bm{0}},I)$ to a provided
action distribution ${\bm{a}}$ along the linear path ${\bm{x}}_{u}=(1-u)\bm{\varepsilon}+u{\bm{a}}$ with flow time
 $u\in[0,1]$ , whose target velocity is ${\bm{a}}-\bm{\varepsilon}$ :

 $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{CFM}}(\theta)\coloneqq\mathbb{E}_{(s,{\bm{a}},g)\sim\mathcal{D},\,\bm{\varepsilon},\,u}\!\left[\left\|v_{\theta}({\bm{x}}_{u},u,E_{\phi}(s),E_{\phi}(g))-({\bm{a}}-\bm{\varepsilon})\right\|_{2}^{2}\right].$ 

(11)

Distillation.
The distillation trains the one-step flow policy $\pi_{\theta}(z,z_{g},\bm{\varepsilon})$ , conditioned on
the encoded state $z$ and goal $z_{g}$ , to match the action chunks decoded from the multi-step
integration of velocity $v_{\theta}$ using the same noise $\bm{\varepsilon}\sim\mathcal{N}({\bm{0}},I)$ by
minimizing:

 $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{Distill}}(\theta)\coloneqq\mathbb{E}_{(s,g)\sim\mathcal{D},\,\bm{\varepsilon}}\Big[\big\|\pi_{\theta}(E_{\phi}(s),E_{\phi}(g),\bm{\varepsilon})-\operatorname{sg}\!\big(\operatorname{EulerIntegration}(v_{\theta},\bm{\varepsilon})\big)\big\|_{2}^{2}\Big],$ 

(12)

where $\operatorname{sg}$ is the stop-gradient operation and
 $\operatorname{EulerIntegration}(v_{\theta},\bm{\varepsilon})$ is the action chunk obtained by integrating
 $v_{\theta}$ 
with the Euler method from $u=0$ (the noise
 $\bm{\varepsilon}$ ) to $u=1$ with the number of steps in Table .

Policy improvement.
The policy minimizes Equation 8 with the predictive model frozen.
Policy improvement imagines the future states with one chunk ( $H$ actions) with the value term
in Equation 7 divided by its stop-gradient batch mean $|Q_{\theta}|$ , stabilized by a small
constant $\epsilon_{Q}$ .

State-predictive model.

The (latent) state-predictive model described in Section 6.2 trains the value
heads on encoded states $z_{t+h}=E_{\phi}(s_{t+h}),h\in[0,H]$ and includes an additional term with
the weight $\lambda_{\mathrm{state}}$ (Table ) to train the rollout $\hat{z}_{t+h}$ to predict the next latent $z_{t+h}$ , using the latent-prediction (LP) loss:

 $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{LP}}(\phi;\lambda_{\mathrm{state}})\coloneqq\mathbb{E}_{({\bm{s}}_{t},{\bm{a}}_{t},{\bm{s}}_{t+1})\sim\mathcal{D},\,g\sim\mathcal{S}}\left[\frac{1}{H+1}\sum_{h=0}^{H}M_{t+h}\odot\|V_{\phi}(z_{t+h},z_{g})-R_{t+h}\|_{2}^{2}+\lambda_{\mathrm{state}}\frac{1}{H}\sum_{h=1}^{H}\|\hat{z}_{t+h}-z_{t+h}\|_{2}^{2}\right].$ 

(13)

The target latent $z_{t+h}$ has no stop-gradient operation, so the pixel encoder can be updated by
gradients from self-prediction.

## B.6 Planning and Evaluation Protocol

Planning setup.
At each planning step, we encode the current observation and goal and maximize the imagined
advantage $A$ in Equation 10, keeping the model and policy weights fixed.
The default horizon is one training-sized action chunk ( $n_{c}=1$ , $H=16$ ).
We execute the first $H_{\mathrm{exec}}$ actions and replan from the resulting observation.
Shared planner hyperparameters like the population size $N$ and elite count $N_{\mathrm{e}}$ are listed in
Table , and dataset-specific hyperparameters like execution horizons
 $H_{\mathrm{exec}}$ are listed in Table .

Noise-space decoding.
The distilled policy maps source noise to an action chunk, ${\bm{a}}=\pi_{\theta}(z,z_{g},\bm{\varepsilon})$ , with
 $\bm{\varepsilon}\sim\mathcal{N}({\bm{0}},I)$ in $\mathbb{R}^{Hd_{a}}$ during training, where $d_{a}$ is the action dimension.
Planning optimizes this noise through the decoder $\operatorname{Dec}$ in
Algorithm 1;
 $\bm{\varepsilon}={\bm{0}}$ gives the deterministic policy output, which we call the policy mode.
For multiple chunks ( $n_{c}\geq 1$ ), the decoder conditions each chunk $c$ on the latent reached by the
preceding chunk using its noise vector $\bm{\varepsilon}_{c}$ , where $\bm{\varepsilon}=(\bm{\varepsilon}_{0},\ldots,\bm{\varepsilon}_{n_{c}-1})$ ,
keeping dynamics queries within the training context length.
The score in Equation 10 then extends over $n_{c}H$ steps.
Shorter planning horizons use a prefix of the decoded chunk.
Setting $K=0$ bypasses search and executes the policy mode.

Zeroth-order steering.
 $\varepsilon$ -ES (Algorithm 1) uses forward evaluations of $A$ , allowing search without
informative gradients.
It initializes $N_{\mathrm{e}}$ elites at the policy mode and generates $N$ Gaussian mutations per
iteration, distributed evenly among the elites.
The mutation scale is annealed as $\sigma_{k}=\max(\sigma_{\min},\sigma_{\max}\rho^{k})$ for
 $k=0,\ldots,K-1$ .
Each parent is replaced only by a child with a higher score, so every elite’s score remains at least
as high as the initial policy score and the best score is non-decreasing.
The final plan is sampled from the elites with probabilities proportional to $\exp(\beta A_{e})$ ,
retaining several high-scoring alternatives rather than selecting only the maximum.
The selected plan therefore also matches or exceeds the policy mode’s score under the learned
objective $A$ .

First-order steering.
In practice, we find that first-order planners generally yield higher success rates than $\varepsilon$ -ES on
locomotion tasks (Table ).
As a result, we steer the noise by gradient ascent with stochastic gradient descent (SGD) or Adam,
and denote the resulting planners $\varepsilon$ -SGD and $\varepsilon$ -Adam, respectively.
The main results in Table 1, with complete evaluations in
Table , use these first-order planners for locomotion and state-based
cube-single-play-v0 datasets, and $\varepsilon$ -ES for the remaining manipulation datasets.

Both first-order planners (Algorithm 3) optimize a single noise candidate
initialized at $\bm{\varepsilon}^{(0)}={\bm{0}}$ .
Each of the $K$ ascent steps differentiates through the policy decoder, latent dynamics, and value
function:

Powers, square roots, and vector division are element-wise.

Planning complexity.
Ignoring initialization and final decoding, $\varepsilon$ -ES evaluates $KN$ candidate rollouts, whereas
first-order steering uses $K$ forward-backward evaluations of one candidate.
For a fixed model and rollout horizon, let $C_{\mathrm{fwd}}$ and $C_{\mathrm{bwd}}$ denote the
per-candidate complexity of decoding and scoring, and of backpropagation, respectively.
Under sequential computation, the total time complexities are $\mathcal{O}(KNC_{\mathrm{fwd}})$ for
 $\varepsilon$ -ES and $\mathcal{O}(K(C_{\mathrm{fwd}}+C_{\mathrm{bwd}}))$ for first-order steering.
The planner comparisons use separately tuned settings rather than matched compute budgets.

Evaluation.
We evaluate the final training checkpoint on each dataset’s five official OGBench tasks, using $25$ 
episodes per task in vectorized environments (Park et al., 2025a).
Each episode retains its task goal, and success is determined by the environment’s native indicator
within its time limit.
For each training seed, the dataset success rate is averaged over the five tasks.
We report the mean and standard deviation of success rate across 8 training seeds for state inputs
and 4 for pixel inputs, the same as in OGBench (Park et al., 2025a).

## B.7 Planner Variants

The CEM variants in Table 2 optimize the same advantage $A$ as $\varepsilon$ -ES,
but refit a diagonal Gaussian at each iteration rather than retain and mutate individual elites.
Each iteration scores $N$ candidates and refits the mean and standard deviation using the top
 $N_{\mathrm{e}}$ candidates with normalized weights $w_{e}\propto\exp(\beta A_{e})$ .
This distribution refit does not preserve $\varepsilon$ -ES’s monotonic elite-score guarantee.

Noise-space CEM.
 $\varepsilon$ -CEM (Algorithm 4) applies the CEM refitting scheme in the policy’s noise
space, initializing the sampling distribution as $\mathcal{N}({\bm{0}},I)$ .
Candidate noises are decoded through $\operatorname{Dec}$ of Algorithm 1 and ranked
by
 $A$ .
After each refit, the coordinate-wise standard deviations $\bm{\sigma}$ are clipped between $\sigma_{\min}$ and
the annealed upper bound $\sigma_{k}=\max(\sigma_{\min},\sigma_{\max}\rho^{k})$ , with $k$ indexed
from zero.
The final plan is decoded from an elite sampled with the same softmax weights.

Action-space CEM.
 $\mathcal{A}$ -CEM (Algorithm 5) searches directly over action sequences, warm-starting the
initial mean at the policy’s direct output ${\bm{\mu}}_{a}=\operatorname{Dec}({\bm{0}})$ and setting the
initial
standard deviation to $\bm{\sigma}_{a}=\sigma_{\max}{\bm{1}}$ .
This initialization is recomputed at each planning step from the current observation, without
carrying over the previous plan.
Candidates are clipped to $[-1,1]$ before scoring, and the sampling standard deviations use the same
annealed bounds.
The executed plan is the final fitted action mean.

Both variants execute the first $H_{\mathrm{exec}}$ actions, return the direct policy output when $K=0$ , and
add no execution noise.
They reuse $\varepsilon$ -ES’s planning hyperparameters on each dataset (Tables 
and ).
In the pseudocode below, $\operatorname{TopK}$ returns elite indices and ${\bm{1}}$ is the all-ones
vector; vector squaring, square roots, and clipping are element-wise.

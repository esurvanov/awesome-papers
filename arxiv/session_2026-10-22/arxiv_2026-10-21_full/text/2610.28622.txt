Abstract

Spatial intelligence has recently been assessed through generative tasks such
as spatially grounded image editing, yet language-guided generation of spatially
consistent views from partial multi-view observations remains underexplored. We introduce MindView, a benchmark for generative multi-view spatial
intelligence with 1,500 samples from 513 scenes across three indoor datasets.
Its seven tasks form two families: geometry-dominant tasks specify changes in
camera position and orientation, while
semantics-dominant tasks require inferring target viewpoints by grounding
referred objects and interpreting spatial relations and reference-frame cues. Evaluation of eleven generators reveals substantial room for improvement on
MindView. A paired context analysis further shows that all eleven generators obtain lower
overall scores under the expanded context setting. These findings motivate us to investigate whether task-specific supervision can
improve target-view generation and whether models can use additional multi-view
evidence effectively. We therefore fine-tune BAGEL on the MindView training dataset,
improving its overall score by 24.3 points and yielding gains across all seven MindView tasks.
Beyond these generation gains, the fine-tuned model also yields higher scores on three
spatial-understanding benchmarks without training on their annotations,
suggesting that learning to generate spatially grounded views may benefit
spatial understanding. To improve evidence utilization under expanded context, we propose
MindCue, a training-free spatial evidence selector guided
by a target-view hypothesis. MindCue reverses the drop in overall score under expanded context
and outperforms the standard-context baseline.
Overall, MindView provides a concrete testbed for generative multi-view spatial
intelligence. Our findings further highlight task learning and query-guided evidence selection
as complementary strategies for improving performance in this setting.

## 1 Introduction

Benchmarks such as MindCube (Wang et al., 2025b), MMSI-Bench (Yang et al., 2026), and SAT-v2 (Ray et al., 2025) evaluate the spatial intelligence of multimodal understanding models through question answering. On the generation side, GenSpace (Wang et al., 2025c) and GSI-Bench (Zhu et al., 2026b) have begun this exploration by asking whether spatial instructions are realized in a generated or edited image. We focus on evaluating generative multi-view spatial intelligence
through language-guided target-view generation from partial observations
of the same scene (Figure 1). This task requires models to integrate evidence across views,
infer the requested viewpoint, and predict what would be visible
from it while remaining consistent with the observed scene.

Figure 1: Overview of MindView, showing seven task examples, benchmark statistics, six metrics, and representative results. Red A marks anchor views, and targets are real images.

Related work differs in how reference images are used and how target viewpoints are specified. Multi-reference benchmarks such as MICE-Bench (Luo et al., 2026) and MultiBanana (Oshima et al., 2026) emphasize preserving and combining reference content, which alone does not establish that a generated image depicts the requested viewpoint of the observed scene. Pose-conditioned methods such as ZeroNVS (Sargent et al., 2024) evaluate view synthesis with the target camera supplied, leaving language-conditioned viewpoint inference outside their task setting. More recently, Astra (Zhu et al., 2026a) uses language-controlled novel-view generation
to support agentic spatial question answering and evaluates generated
views against real targets for pose and content consistency. However, its generation evaluation focuses on explicit camera motions
with a single dominant degree of freedom. We seek a unified evaluation of individual and composed
camera motions and target viewpoints inferred from object
references, spatial relations, and reference-frame cues.

We introduce MindView, a benchmark of 1,500 samples from 513 scenes in three indoor datasets (Figure 1). Its seven tasks form two families: Geometry-Dominant tasks specify camera translations, rotations, or their ordered composition, while Semantics-Dominant tasks require viewpoint inference from referred objects, spatial relations, and reference-frame cues. The model receives only RGB observations and a language query, with the target image and numerical camera parameters withheld. Six complementary metrics assess content fidelity, spatial agreement, rendering quality, and geometric consistency to identify content and spatial errors an aggregate score may obscure.

Across eleven off-the-shelf generators, preserving scene content
while matching the requested viewpoint remains challenging
(Table 1). Expanding context from six to ten views increases target-view coverage from 88.4% to 92.0%, yet lowers Overall for all eleven generators (Figure 5). These findings raise two questions: can task-specific supervision improve target-view generation, and can query-guided evidence selection help models benefit from additional observations?

To address these questions, we curate a target-view generation training set and fine-tune BAGEL (Deng et al., 2025), obtaining BAGEL-SFT with a 24.3-point Overall gain and improvements across all seven tasks. BAGEL-SFT also scores higher on three spatial-understanding benchmarks without training on their annotations, providing preliminary evidence of transfer from generation training to spatial understanding. To improve evidence utilization, we propose MindCue, a training-free spatial evidence selector using a target-view hypothesis to select supplementary observations from the expanded context. On both BAGEL and BAGEL-SFT, MindCue reverses the expanded-context degradation and outperforms the standard-context baseline and count-matched random selection.

Our main contributions are summarized as follows:

1. 

We introduce MindView, a benchmark for generative multi-view spatial intelligence, revealing two challenges across eleven generators: limited content fidelity and spatial consistency, and performance degradation with excessive context.

2. 

We curate target-view generation training data and show that task-specific supervision improves BAGEL across all seven tasks, with preliminary evidence of transfer to spatial understanding.

3. 

We propose MindCue, a training-free, query-guided evidence selector that improves both BAGEL and BAGEL-SFT over fixed and count-matched random baselines.

## 2 Related Work

Spatial Intelligence Benchmarks and Generative Reasoning.
Spatial intelligence is evaluated through both question answering and image generation. MindCube (Wang et al., 2025b), MMSI-Bench (Yang et al., 2026), and SAT-v2 (Ray et al., 2025) assess spatial understanding through question answering, whereas GenSpace (Wang et al., 2025c) and GSI-Bench (Zhu et al., 2026b) evaluate whether generated images satisfy spatial instructions. The latter also reports understanding gains from generation-focused training. Thinking with Novel Views (Zhang et al., 2026) uses generated views for spatial question answering, while UniG2U-Bench (Wen et al., 2026) examines unified models’ understanding and the effects of intermediate image generation. MindView evaluates generated views of observed scenes and tests whether target-view supervision aids spatial understanding.

Novel-View Synthesis and Viewpoint Control.
View generation methods differ in target-view specification, from explicit camera parameters to language instructions. NeRF (Mildenhall et al., 2020), ZeroNVS (Sargent et al., 2024), EscherNet (Kong et al., 2024), and LVSM (Jin et al., 2025) use supplied camera controls. CapFrame (Li et al., 2026) grounds language-specified viewpoints within a reconstructed 3D Gaussian scene, while Object-Uni (Tan et al., 2026) uses explicit object poses for controllable generation. Astra (Zhu et al., 2026a) generates language-guided imagined views for spatial question answering and also evaluates them against real targets. Existing methods either rely on explicit geometry or consider constrained language-specified viewpoints, leaving viewpoint inference and scene-consistent rendering from partial multi-view evidence underexplored.

Multi-Reference Image Generation and Editing.
Multi-reference generation and editing use multiple input images to preserve and combine visual content. IP-Adapter (Ye et al., 2023) and PhotoMaker (Li et al., 2023) provide image- or identity-based conditioning, while MultiRef (Chen et al., 2025) explicitly supports multiple visual references. MICE-Bench (Luo et al., 2026) evaluates multi-reference creation and editing, while MultiBanana (Oshima et al., 2026) tests generation from challenging combinations of visual references. Unlike these benchmarks, MindView treats inputs as partial observations of the same scene and evaluates language-guided target-view generation.

## 3 MindView Benchmark

In this section, we present MindView, a benchmark designed to evaluate
generative multi-view spatial intelligence. Section 3.1 formulates the target-view generation problem
and introduces seven tasks across two complementary families. Section 3.2 describes the data construction pipeline,
and Section 3.3 presents the evaluation protocol and metrics.

## 3.1 Task Formulation and Task Design

MindView evaluates language-guided target-view generation from partial
observations of the same scene. Let $C_{K}=(I_{1},\ldots,I_{K})$ denote an ordered list of RGB observations and let
 $q$ denote a spatial query. A generator $f$ predicts

 $\hat{I}_{\mathrm{target}}=f(C_{K},q).$ 

(1)

The primary benchmark setting uses $K=6$ . At inference time, the generator receives only $C_{K}$ and $q$ . The held-out real target image, camera poses, depth, and scene annotations are not
provided as generation inputs. When the query is defined relative to a starting viewpoint, $I_{1}$ serves as
the anchor. The generated image must preserve the observed scene while matching the
requested camera position and orientation.

The seven tasks share this interface and differ in how the target viewpoint
is specified. Geometry-Dominant Tasks express it through egocentric camera motions
from an anchor. Translation (Trans.) changes camera position through relative displacements
while preserving heading. Rotation (Rot.) changes heading by a specified angle while keeping position
fixed. Composition (Comp.) combines translation and rotation in the order specified
by the query.

Semantics-Dominant Tasks specify the target through referred objects,
spatial relations, and reference-frame cues. Relational Object Grounding (ROG) identifies the referred object through
relations and generates a view satisfying distance, centering, and visibility
constraints, without an anchor. Target-Facing Reorientation (TFR) requires locating the target object and
turning to face it while remaining at the anchor position. Object-Centric Repositioning (OCR) instead requires moving to a specified
side or angular offset around the object and facing it, with directions
defined relative to the anchor view. Multi-Cue Viewpoint Inference (MCVI) jointly infers position and heading
from observer-relative scene cues, without an anchor. Together, the two families test complementary forms of target-view
specification under a common generation interface.

Section A.4 provides real examples and full generation
prompts for all seven tasks.

## 3.2 Data Construction

We construct MindView from ScanNet (Dai et al., 2017),
ScanNet++ (Yeshwanth et al., 2023), and ARKitScenes
(Baruch et al., 2021). Figure 2 summarizes the five-stage
offline construction pipeline.

Figure 2: Five-stage offline construction pipeline for MindView.

Data preparation and spatial annotation.
We first apply blur filtering while preserving temporal coverage along each
recorded sequence. We then account for cropping and resizing in the camera model used for
geometric checks. Using camera poses, depth, scene geometry, and instance annotations, we derive
relative viewpoints, object relations, visibility, and object locations in
each image. These annotations form the spatial basis for constructing queries and targets.

Task construction.
For Geometry-Dominant Tasks, relative poses between recorded views yield
motion instructions. Candidates are filtered by task-specific pose constraints, and endpoints
reconstructed from rounded instructions are checked against recorded targets. Where point clouds are available, Translation and Composition also use
discrete collision checks along instructed segments. For Semantics-Dominant Tasks, identities, relations, and reference-frame cues
define query-target candidates. Visibility and viewpoint checks limit ambiguity across recorded views. Each query uses a real captured target and, when required, an anchor. Appendix A details the task-specific criteria.

Offline context selection.
For each query-target pair, we select six unique same-scene views as a
connected path. The target is excluded, any required anchor comes first, and other candidates
pass temporal and pose-separation filters. Selection enforces pairwise-overlap limits and coverage of target-visible
content and the broader scene. MCVI also requires every object cue constraining observer location to appear
across the selected views.

Review and release.
Candidate groups undergo human and LLM-assisted screening for residual
ambiguity and limited within-sample visual diversity. Section A.3 provides the exact GPT-5.6 (OpenAI, 2026b) screening prompt. The frozen benchmark contains 1,500 samples from 513 scenes across the three
datasets (Figure 3). 
We also provide 20,000 training examples from 3,406 scenes, using the same
interface for all seven tasks. These examples use the source datasets’ training splits and share no scenes
with the benchmark (Section C.1).

Figure 3: Distribution of the 1,500 MindView samples across source datasets and tasks.

## 3.3 Evaluation Protocol

Image-feature similarity.
DINO-I probes overall visual-content fidelity through DINOv2-Large
feature correspondence (Oquab et al., 2023). We use the cosine similarity between generated and real target features,
scaled by 100 without clipping.

VLM-based assessment.
Qwen3-VL-30B-A3B-Instruct (Qwen Team, 2025b) judges whether
scene content (Semantic) and visible viewpoint and layout
(Spatial) match the real target; Quality assesses
internal coherence and rendering defects in the generation alone. The judge receives no query or context; integer 0–10 ratings are scaled
to 0–100. See Sections B.2 and B.6
for inputs and prompts.

Geometric consistency.
We introduce a VGGT-based protocol (Wang et al., 2025a) for MindView
to assess target-viewpoint fidelity through estimated camera position
and orientation. Joint inference on fixed references and generated ( $\mathrm{gen}$ ) and
real ( $\mathrm{real}$ ) target images yields rotations $\hat{R}$ and camera
centers $\hat{\mathbf{c}}$ in a shared frame. For sample $i$ , the pose errors are

 $\bigl(e_{\mathrm{rot},i},e_{\mathrm{trans},i}\bigr)=\left(d_{\mathrm{SO}(3)}\!\left(\hat{R}_{\mathrm{gen}},\hat{R}_{\mathrm{real}}\right),\;a_{i}\left\|\hat{\mathbf{c}}_{\mathrm{gen}}-\hat{\mathbf{c}}_{\mathrm{real}}\right\|_{2}\right).$ 

(2)

Here, $d_{\mathrm{SO}(3)}$ is angular distance in degrees, and $a_{i}>0$ 
converts VGGT lengths to meters using real-image sensor depths, without
ground-truth poses. Per-sample RScore and TScore decrease linearly from 100 at zero error to
0 at $60^{\circ}$ and $2\,\mathrm{m}$ , respectively, and remain zero beyond
these thresholds. Section B.3 details the reference inputs,
calibration, and scoring.

All scores are higher-is-better. We average each component within tasks, then equally across the seven
tasks; Overall is the unweighted mean of the six task-macro scores.

Evaluator reliability.
We assess evaluator reliability through comparisons with human ratings
and reconstruction stability tests. Three multimodal experts independently rate outputs from four generators. Aggregated human and VLM scores yield identical model rankings for
Semantic, Spatial, and Quality. For VGGT, we vary the generated image while keeping all real views fixed. Across samples, the median changes in the reconstructed target pose are
 $0.71^{\circ}$ in relative rotation and 2.75 cm in reference-aligned position. Together, these experiments support the reliability of our VLM-based and
geometric metrics for comparing generators (Section B.4).

## 4 Experiments

We first evaluate current generators on MindView and analyze their
task-level performance. We then examine whether additional observations improve target-view
generation. Motivated by these findings, we investigate task-specific adaptation through
supervised fine-tuning and training-free, query-guided evidence selection
through MindCue.

## 4.1 Experimental Setup

Evaluation set and models.
All MindView generation comparisons use the frozen release of 1,500 aligned scene–query–target samples. The standard benchmark input consists of six context images and a language query per sample. All MindView generation results follow the evaluation protocol in Section 3.3. We compare eleven off-the-shelf generators: seven open-source models
(BAGEL (Deng et al., 2025), FLUX.2 (Black Forest Labs, 2026),
Qwen-Image-Edit-2511 (Wu et al., 2025; Qwen Team, 2025a),
Emu3.5 (Cui et al., 2025), DreamOmni2 (Xia et al., 2025),
SenseNova-U1 (Diao et al., 2026b), and
SenseNova-U1.5 (Diao et al., 2026a))
and four closed-source models (Nano Banana (Fortin et al., 2025),
Nano Banana Pro (Raisinghani, 2025),
GPT-Image-1.5 (OpenAI, 2025), and
GPT-Image-2 (OpenAI, 2026a)). We additionally evaluate BAGEL-SFT, obtained by supervised fine-tuning
of BAGEL on our curated training dataset for 10,000 steps.

## 4.3 Context Scaling and Evidence Utilization

Figure 5: Overall score trajectories across nested context settings.

We examine context expansion on the same 1,500 scene–query–target
samples using nested three-, six-, and ten-view settings, with $C_{6}$ 
as the standard input. $C_{3}$ is an ordered subsequence of $C_{6}$ , which is an ordered
subsequence of $C_{10}$ . Target images are excluded from generator inputs, and VGGT uses the
same ten evaluation references across settings. Computed only for offline analysis, target coverage measures the fraction
of content visible in the real target that is also observed in at least
one context view. Its sample mean rises from 57.6% at $C_{3}$ to 88.4% at $C_{6}$ and
92.0% at $C_{10}$ . Despite this increase, all eleven off-the-shelf generators and BAGEL-SFT
obtain lower Overall at $C_{10}$ than at $C_{6}$ 
(Figure 5). Greater coverage therefore does not ensure better generation under this
protocol. This observation motivates MindCue, which selects query-relevant evidence
from the four $C_{10}$ additions while retaining the complete $C_{6}$ core
(Section 4.5). Full component scores appear in Appendix
Table 13.

## 4.4 Supervised Adaptation and Transfer to Spatial Understanding

Supervised adaptation.
To examine whether task-specific supervision improves target-view
generation, we fine-tune BAGEL (Deng et al., 2025) on our curated
training dataset for 10,000 steps. Adaptation raises Overall from 40.0 to 64.4, a gain of 24.3 points,
as shown in Table 1, and all six metrics improve
on every task (Appendix
Tables 9–12). The adapted model’s task-macro advantage over GPT-Image-2 is larger
on geometry-dominant than semantics-dominant tasks
(10.8 versus 3.3 points; Table 2), possibly because explicit motion
supervision provides more regular patterns to learn, whereas
semantics-dominant tasks demand more complex semantic
interpretation and spatial reasoning. In the Composition example in Figure 4,
BAGEL-SFT more closely matches the GT’s painting-dominated close-up
than GPT-Image-2, which instead produces a broad room view. Training details are provided in Appendix C.

Transfer to spatial understanding.

We next examine whether learning to generate target views also benefits
spatial understanding. To this end, we evaluate BAGEL and BAGEL-SFT on SAT-v2 (Ray et al., 2025),
MMSI-Bench (Yang et al., 2026), and MindCube-Tiny
(Wang et al., 2025b), strictly following their respective
input-construction and answer-scoring protocols
(Appendix D). Our fine-tuning stage uses no annotations from these benchmarks. All results are obtained in our own evaluations. Our reproduced BAGEL accuracies on MMSI-Bench and MindCube-Tiny match
those reported by SenseNova-SI (Cai et al., 2026)
at the reported precision.

As shown in Table 3, BAGEL-SFT obtains
higher scores in all three comparisons. On SAT-v2, micro accuracy increases from 58.0% to 62.7%, while
both-order accuracy rises from 27.3% to 41.3%, indicating that more
questions are answered correctly under both evaluated answer orders. Its scores on MMSI-Bench and MindCube-Tiny also exceed the reproduced BAGEL
baselines by 1.2 and 1.8 percentage points, respectively. Together, these results provide preliminary evidence that learning to
generate spatially grounded views may also benefit spatial understanding,
even without direct supervision from the evaluated benchmarks.

## 4.5 MindCue: A Training-Free Spatial Evidence Selector

Motivated by the context-scaling results, MindCue uses a target-view
hypothesis to select additional spatial evidence without updating model weights
(Figure 6).

Method.
For sample $i$ , let $C_{i}^{\mathrm{pool}}$ be the ordered observations,
 $q_{i}$ the query, and $\mathcal{F}_{i},\mathcal{D}_{i}$ the core and additional-view
index sets. Frozen VGGT (Wang et al., 2025a) provides confidence-filtered scene points
 $\hat{\mathcal{P}}_{i}$ ; for semantics-dominant tasks, frozen SAM 3
(Carion et al., 2026) segments queried entities. A task-specific solver uses this evidence, the query, and core/reference-view
metadata to hypothesize a target camera $\hat{\mathcal{C}}^{\star}_{i}$ 
(pose and intrinsics). Its virtual surface is

 $\mathcal{S}_{i}=\operatorname{Occ}\!\left[\operatorname{ZBuf}\!\left(\Pi_{\hat{\mathcal{C}}^{\star}_{i}}\!\left(\hat{\mathcal{P}}_{i}\right)\right)\right].$ 

(3)

Here $\Pi$ projects points, $\operatorname{ZBuf}$ retains the nearest valid depth
per grid cell, and $\operatorname{Occ}$ returns the occupied cells.

For nonempty $\mathcal{S}_{i}$ , let $R_{i}(v)$ measure additional view $v$ ’s reliable coverage,
capping credit for geometry supported only by that view, and let $\tau$ 
be the coverage threshold. $X_{i}\subseteq\mathcal{D}_{i}$ contains at most one below-threshold candidate
passing connectivity and nonredundancy tests; without an explicit anchor,
it is empty. The retained additional-view indices are

 $A_{i}=\begin{cases}\varnothing,&\mathcal{S}_{i}=\varnothing,\\
\left\{v\in\mathcal{D}_{i}:R_{i}(v)\geq\tau\right\}\cup X_{i},&\mathcal{S}_{i}\neq\varnothing.\end{cases}$ 

(4)

The generator receives the original query and pool views indexed by
 $\mathcal{F}_{i}\cup A_{i}$ , preserving their order and the complete core. Selection uses no held-out targets, ground-truth geometry, generated outputs,
or evaluation scores. Appendix E specifies the support and selection rules;
Table 7 details the task-specific cameras.

Evaluation.
We compare vanilla and fine-tuned BAGEL on the same 1,500 samples with fixed
checkpoints and no-thinking inference. Fixed $C_{6}$ and $C_{10}$ supply the core and full pool, respectively. Both controls preserve the core: RandDrop randomly selects the same number of
additions as MindCue per sample, while CounterSel retains rejected additions
and is not count-matched. Table 4 reports Overall and view counts;
Appendix Table 14 gives all six components.

for BAGEL and all six for BAGEL-SFT. CounterSel scores lowest among all five policies for both models, consistent
with the value of target-guided retention.

Runtime.
On a single A100 80GB, MindCue adds 1.44 s on average, or 3.4% of BAGEL
inference time with identical selected views and input order. The task-averaged costs of VGGT reconstruction and SAM 3 segmentation with
mask lifting are 0.57 s and 0.82 s, respectively. SAM 3 is used only for semantics-dominant tasks. The remaining selection steps add 0.05 s. We sum separately timed stages with preloaded models, excluding preprocessing
(Section E.6). Thus, query-guided selection adds only modest computational overhead
relative to generation.

## 5 Conclusion

We introduce MindView, a benchmark for generative multi-view spatial
intelligence through language-guided target-view generation from partial
multi-view observations. Evaluation of eleven generators reveals challenges in content fidelity
and viewpoint consistency, while expanding from six to ten views lowers
Overall for all eleven. Fine-tuning BAGEL on our training set improves generation across all seven
tasks and provides preliminary evidence of transfer to spatial understanding. Complementing task-specific adaptation, MindCue uses a target-view
hypothesis for training-free evidence selection and improves both
BAGEL variants over standard and expanded inputs and count-matched
random selection. These findings highlight task learning and query-guided
evidence selection as complementary directions for advancing
generative multi-view spatial intelligence.

## Appendix A Benchmark Construction Details

This section expands the five offline stages in
Section 3.2.
Task-specific constructors may interleave candidate generation, query
construction, and validation. The generator receives none of these construction signals and sees only
selected RGB views and the query.

## A.1 Data Preparation and Spatial Annotation

Data preparation.
We apply the same blur-filtering procedure to ScanNet, ScanNet++, and
ARKitScenes. We convert each frame to grayscale and resize its long side to 320 pixels. Its Tenengrad sharpness score is the mean squared gradient magnitude
computed with $3\times 3$ Sobel filters (Santos et al., 1997). Within each sequence, we retain frames at or above the median
sharpness score and add the highest-scoring frame from each non-overlapping
five-frame window. This union favors image clarity while preserving temporal coverage. For geometric checks, we use a centered square region of each retained
image and adjust its camera intrinsics for resizing and cropping. Projection and visibility checks use these adjusted intrinsics and the
corresponding image coordinates.

Spatial annotation.
Recorded camera poses provide relative translations and heading changes. Horizontal displacements are expressed in a ground-plane frame aligned
with the relevant camera heading. Instance labels and 3D geometry provide object identities, locations,
extents, and spatial relationships, including proximity and vertical
arrangement. We also derive object directions and distances relative to the observer. For each view, we project instance points using its pose and adjusted
intrinsics, then compare their depths with recorded depth to estimate
visibility within the effective image crop. The visible points support image-space locations and view-dependent
object relationships. These annotations support query construction and target-view selection.

## A.2 Task-Specific Construction

Using these annotations, we construct each task around the information that
specifies its target viewpoint. Geometry-Dominant Tasks convert the relative pose between recorded views into
a motion query, whereas Semantics-Dominant Tasks use objects and spatial cues
to identify valid target views. Every query has a real recorded target and uses an anchor only when it is
defined relative to a starting view. Task-specific checks enforce geometric fidelity and, for semantic queries,
reduce ambiguity across recorded views.

Geometry-Dominant Tasks.
Translation encodes one or two ordered horizontal steps in the anchor camera
frame without a commanded turn. Rotation changes the horizontal heading with only small camera-center drift. Composition supports either motion order. A displacement uses the anchor frame before a turn and the rotated camera
frame after it. We retain pairs that satisfy task-specific position and heading constraints,
together with height, pitch, and roll gates. Movement distances are rounded and turn angles are quantized for the query. We execute the discrete instruction geometrically and require the resulting
camera center to remain close to the recorded target position. For Translation and Composition, available point clouds support collision
checks with vertical body cylinders sampled along each translated segment. We reject a candidate if any sampled cylinder reaches the occupancy threshold,
using these discrete checks to assess the traversability of the instructed path.

Relational Object Grounding.
Without an anchor, the query uses one to three clear relational facts to
identify an object. Its target is a real view in which that object is near the image center and all
referenced objects are sufficiently visible. We remove views with a clear same-category distractor and reject a description
that also matches another valid target candidate. Views supporting the same description are grouped when their camera poses and
projected object layouts are equivalent. We select among these clusters by the horizontal clearance from the camera to
the target object’s footprint and reject cases with a near-tied alternative. ScanNet++ and ARKitScenes allow a single-relation fallback only if no
multi-relation candidate exists and the scene records match the description to
exactly one target instance and one compatible view cluster. This fallback occurs in 44 of 127 released ROG queries. The final instruction also specifies target distance and centering and requires
all named objects to remain visible.

Target-Facing Reorientation.
The task asks for an approximately in-place turn from Image 1 toward a uniquely
described object. The object phrase must identify one scene instance, using either its category
alone or that category with at most one nearby reference. We retain a real target view only when its camera faces the object and the
object is sufficiently visible, fully inside the effective crop, and near the
image center. A target is rejected when a similarly suitable view from approximately the
same position points in a substantially different direction. We then select an anchor with a nearby camera center, similar height, a
different heading, and consistent roll. To ensure that the turn reveals the requested object, we exclude anchors in
which it passes a lenient depth-aware visibility test.

Object-Centric Repositioning.
The task begins with an anchor that clearly shows the reference object near the
image center, without another salient instance of the same category. We then select a target at the requested left or right angular offset, or on
the side of the object opposite the anchor, with left and right defined in the
anchor camera frame. The target must face and center the object from a similar distance while
remaining consistent with the anchor in height, pitch, and roll. We discard queries when comparably suitable candidates imply distinct target
poses. These checks validate the destination view, not a collision-free path around
the object.

Multi-Cue Viewpoint Inference.
Without an anchor, the query specifies the desired viewpoint through the
observer’s position relative to nearby objects and the image locations of
visible objects. We match the complete cue set against every valid annotated frame in the scene
and retain it only when all matches form one compact cluster in horizontal
position and heading. A representative real frame from this cluster serves as the target. Within each scene, we remove repeated cue signatures and near-duplicate target
poses. Because the search covers only recorded frames, it tests uniqueness among
annotated observations rather than over continuous camera space.

Together, these procedures yield validated query-target pairs whose contexts
are selected in the next stage.

## A.3 Context Selection, Review, and Release

Offline context selection.
The selector assembles six unique same-scene frames and orders them as a
connected path, with any required anchor first. The exact target is excluded. Non-anchor candidates must satisfy temporal separation and are filtered when
both horizontal position and viewing direction are too close to the target. Pairwise-overlap limits and coverage of target-visible content and the broader
scene are enforced. For MCVI, we additionally exclude views identified as equivalent answers
during query validation.

Human and LLM-assisted review.
We first screened 2,227 candidate groups with GPT-5.6 for residual target
ambiguity and limited context-view diversity. The model received the task type, query, six labeled context images, held-out
target, and anchor indicator, and returned a binary decision. The prompt restricted target use to consistency checks, not ambiguity
resolution. This stage retained 2,062 groups and rejected 165. The exact LLM screening prompt appears below.

Three experts in multimodal research then independently reviewed all 2,062
retained groups. All three were authors but had not participated in benchmark construction. They assigned initial accept-or-reject labels based on query-target
consistency, target ambiguity, and context adequacy and diversity. Initial judgments yielded 1,598 unanimous acceptances and 297 unanimous
rejections. Of the 167 disputed groups, 127 received two accept votes and 40 received two
reject votes. Before discussion, three-way agreement was 91.90% (1,895/2,062), and nominal
Krippendorff’s $\alpha$ was 0.815. Disputed groups were adjudicated primarily through discussion. The final review accepted 1,692 groups and rejected 370.

Release.
To improve the balance of task-specific displacement and rotation bins, we
subsampled accepted groups in Translation, Rotation, and Composition, omitting
191 groups. One additional ROG group was excluded after its context images triggered a
safety refusal from GPT-Image-2. The frozen benchmark contains 1,500 groups from 513 scenes across the three
source datasets.

Inputs:
- TASK: {task_type} - QUERY: {query} - Six images labeled CONTEXT 1–6 - One held-out image labeled TARGET - ANCHOR: {1 or NONE}
Return PASS only if:

1. 

The QUERY and CONTEXT images uniquely constrain the target camera position

2. 

The six CONTEXT images provide complementary scene information and are not

Use TARGET only to check consistency, not to resolve ambiguity. Use only

Return exactly one word: PASS or FAIL

## Appendix B Evaluation Protocol Details

This section details the six metrics in Section 3.3,
which assess agreement with the real target and the generation’s intrinsic
quality.

## B.1 Image-Feature Similarity

DINO-I compares the visual content of a generated image with its real target
using the pretrained DINOv2-Large encoder (Oquab et al., 2023). Let $\mathbf{f}_{i}^{\mathrm{gen}}$ and $\mathbf{f}_{i}^{\mathrm{real}}$ denote
their unit-normalized final-layer CLS embeddings. The score is their cosine similarity scaled by 100,

 $\mathrm{DINO\text{-}I}_{i}=100\,(\mathbf{f}_{i}^{\mathrm{gen}})^{\top}\mathbf{f}_{i}^{\mathrm{real}}.$ 

(5)

No clipping or offset is applied, so negative similarities remain negative. Before feature extraction, both images are converted to RGB, center-cropped
to a square, and resized to $512\times 512$ with Lanczos interpolation. The encoder’s standard preprocessing then produces normalized
 $224\times 224$ inputs.

## B.2 VLM-Based Assessment

We use Qwen3-VL-30B-A3B-Instruct (Qwen Team, 2025b) for two
separate evaluations. The first receives the real target followed by the generation, labeled as
the reference and candidate images, respectively. It returns separate Semantic and Spatial scores for scene-specific content
and agreement in viewpoint and layout. The second receives only the generation and assigns a Quality score based
on its internal coherence and visible defects.

Neither evaluation receives the query, context views, task type, camera
metadata, or the other evaluation’s response. Spatial therefore measures agreement with the target image, not direct
compliance with the language query. The first evaluation uses the initial RGB conversion, square crop, and
 $512\times 512$ resize described above. For Quality, the full RGB image is reduced to fit within $512\times 512$ 
without upscaling, preserving its aspect ratio, borders, and peripheral
defects. The judge’s standard image preprocessing follows in both cases.

Each evaluation uses deterministic decoding with at most 256 output tokens. The three ratings must be integers from 0 to 10 and are multiplied by ten
for reporting. Missing or invalid ratings constitute evaluation failures rather than zero
scores. Section B.6 reproduces both prompts verbatim,
apart from typesetting and line wrapping.

## B.3 Geometric Consistency

We use the pretrained VGGT-1B model (Wang et al., 2025a) to measure agreement
between the generated and real target viewpoints. For each sample, VGGT jointly processes ten ordered reference views, the real
target, and the generation, in that order. The references are drawn from the sample’s ten-context set and held fixed
across generators and context budgets. Images undergo the same initial square preprocessing and are then resized
to $518\times 518$ for VGGT.

Camera-pose comparison.
Joint inference places the reference views and both target images in a shared
reconstructed coordinate system for each evaluation. For sample $i$ , $k\in\{\mathrm{gen},\mathrm{real}\}$ indexes the generated
and real target images. Let $\mathbf{x}_{w}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}$ denote a point’s coordinates in this world
frame and $\mathbf{x}_{c}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}$ its coordinates in image $k$ ’s camera
frame. The estimated rotation $\hat{R}_{i,k}\in\mathrm{SO}(3)$ and translation
 $\hat{\mathbf{t}}_{i,k}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}$ map world coordinates to camera
coordinates through
 $\mathbf{x}_{c}=\hat{R}_{i,k}\mathbf{x}_{w}+\hat{\mathbf{t}}_{i,k}$ . Setting $\mathbf{x}_{c}=\mathbf{0}$ gives the camera center in the world frame,
 $\hat{\mathbf{c}}_{i,k}=-\hat{R}_{i,k}^{\top}\hat{\mathbf{t}}_{i,k}$ . We compare camera centers, rather than extrinsic translation vectors, and
compute the errors in Equation 2 as

 $\displaystyle e_{\mathrm{rot},i}$ 
 $\displaystyle=\frac{180}{\pi}\arccos\!\left(\operatorname{clip}\!\left(\frac{\operatorname{tr}(\hat{R}_{i,\mathrm{gen}}\hat{R}_{i,\mathrm{real}}^{\top})-1}{2},-1,1\right)\right),$ 

(6)

 $\displaystyle e_{\mathrm{trans},i}$ 
 $\displaystyle=a_{i}\left\|\hat{\mathbf{c}}_{i,\mathrm{gen}}-\hat{\mathbf{c}}_{i,\mathrm{real}}\right\|_{2}.$ 

Here, $\operatorname{tr}$ denotes the matrix trace, $\|\cdot\|_{2}$ the
Euclidean norm, and $\operatorname{clip}(x,l,u)$ restricts $x$ to $[l,u]$ . The rotation error is in degrees. The positive scale $a_{i}$ in Equation 7 converts
reconstructed lengths to meters, so $e_{\mathrm{trans},i}$ measures
camera-center displacement in meters.

Depth-based scale calibration.
We estimate $a_{i}$ from sensor depths for the ten reference views and the real
target, denoted collectively by $\mathcal{J}_{i}$ . Measured depths are converted to meters, and invalid readings are excluded. Nearest-neighbor resampling aligns them with the predicted depth maps under
the same centered image crop. For each real frame, we first retain pixels with finite measured and
predicted depths, positive measured depth, and predicted depth above
 $10^{-8}$ . When at least 512 such pixels have finite depth confidence, we select those
at or above their median confidence, provided that at least 256 remain. Otherwise, we retain the original valid set.

Let $\mathcal{V}_{if}$ be the retained pixels in frame $f$ , with measured
depth $d^{\mathrm{sens}}_{ifp}$ and predicted depth $\hat{d}_{ifp}$ at pixel $p$ . We aggregate log-depth ratios first within each frame and then across frames,

 $a_{i}=\exp\!\left[\operatorname{median}_{f\in\mathcal{J}_{i}}\left\{\operatorname{median}_{p\in\mathcal{V}_{if}}\left(\log d^{\mathrm{sens}}_{ifp}-\log\hat{d}_{ifp}\right)\right\}\right].$ 

(7)

Calibration requires at least 256 retained pixels per real frame and a
finite, positive $a_{i}$ . The generation participates in joint inference but contributes no sensor
depth to calibration. The real target and sensor depths are used only for evaluation, and neither
pose error requires ground-truth camera poses.

Geometric scores.
We convert the errors to higher-is-better scores before averaging samples,

 $\mathrm{RScore}_{i}=100\,\operatorname{clip}\!\left(1-\frac{e_{\mathrm{rot},i}}{60^{\circ}},0,1\right),\qquad\mathrm{TScore}_{i}=100\,\operatorname{clip}\!\left(1-\frac{e_{\mathrm{trans},i}}{2\,\mathrm{m}},0,1\right).$ 

(8)

Each score decreases linearly from 100 at zero error to zero at its threshold
and remains zero for larger errors.

## B.5 Score Aggregation

For each generator and context setting, let $s_{itj}$ be sample $i$ ’s score
on metric $j$ for task $t$ after the conversions above, and let $n_{t}$ be the
number of samples in that task. We first average samples within each task, then give equal weight to the six
components and the seven tasks,

 $\bar{s}_{tj}=\frac{1}{n_{t}}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{t}}s_{itj},\qquad S_{t}=\frac{1}{6}\sum_{j=1}^{6}\bar{s}_{tj},\qquad\mathrm{Overall}=\frac{1}{7}\sum_{t=1}^{7}S_{t}.$ 

(9)

Each benchmark-level component score is likewise averaged equally across
the seven tasks, so tasks with more samples do not receive greater weight.

Aggregation requires valid scores for every benchmark sample. Failed or missing evaluations are neither assigned zero nor silently
omitted. Scores and gains are displayed to one decimal place, while differences
and rankings are computed before rounding. Overall is a composite score, not a calibrated accuracy. Comparisons are descriptive, without significance tests.

## B.6 Evaluator Prompts

Semantic and Spatial prompt.

You are a strict, task-independent evaluator of image fidelity.
The labeled REFERENCE IMAGE is the ground-truth target.
The labeled CANDIDATE IMAGE is the model prediction.
Important rules:
- The two images may or may not depict the same scene. Do not assume that they match.
- Inspect each image independently before comparing them.
- Use only visible pixel evidence.
- Never attribute an object, region, or property to one image merely because it appears in the other.
- Do not use or infer any hidden prompt, context images, task type, or camera metadata.
- Evaluate Semantic Fidelity and Spatial Fidelity independently.
- Do not judge aesthetic preference or technical image quality in this evaluation.
1. Semantic Fidelity: WHAT is present
Evaluate:
- scene identity and scene category;
- main objects, structures, and recognizable content;
- approximate object count and identity-defining attributes;
- missing, extra, substituted, hallucinated, or unrecognizable content.
Do NOT evaluate:
- camera viewpoint, orientation, framing, crop, or visible side by itself;
- object position, scale, depth, occlusion, or relative layout;
- global color tone, lighting, rendering style, sharpness, or visual polish.
A spatially displaced object can still be semantically correct.
A viewpoint difference alone must not reduce Semantic Fidelity if the same important content remains visible and recognizable.
However, important content that is visibly missing or hallucinated is a semantic error, regardless of its cause.
Semantic Fidelity scoring:
10 = The same scene-specific content is preserved almost completely. All major objects and structures are present and recognizable, with no meaningful missing, extra, substituted, or hallucinated content.
8 = The same scene and all major content are preserved, with only minor errors involving secondary objects or identity-defining attributes.
6 = The core scene is clearly recognizable, but one major object or several secondary elements are missing, substituted, hallucinated, or unrecognizable.
4 = Only partial scene-specific content is preserved. Multiple major objects or structures are incorrect, although some meaningful correspondence remains.
2 = The images share only a generic scene category or a few isolated object categories; scene-specific correspondence is very weak.
0 = No meaningful semantic correspondence, or the candidate contains no interpretable scene content.
Semantic score constraints:
- Generic category similarity alone, such as both images being bedrooms or offices, cannot receive a score above 3.
- If one major or scene-defining object is missing, replaced, or hallucinated, the score cannot exceed 6.
- If multiple major objects are incorrect, the score cannot exceed 4.
- A score of 9 or 10 requires nearly complete scene-specific correspondence, not merely a similar category or overall impression.
2. Spatial Fidelity: WHERE things are and FROM WHERE the scene is viewed
Evaluate:
- camera viewpoint and orientation;
- framing, crop, and visible scene regions or surfaces;
- relative object positions, scale, depth ordering, and occlusion;
- scene layout, geometry, and composition.
Judge spatial relations using scene elements that can be meaningfully corresponded between the two images.
Do NOT evaluate:
- object category or semantic identity by itself;
- color, lighting, texture, rendering style, sharpness, or visual attractiveness;
- isolated generation artifacts unless they make spatial relationships impossible to assess.
Do not reward a generic similar composition unless corresponding scene elements support it.
If meaningful spatial correspondence cannot be established, use a score of 0, 1, or 2.
Spatial Fidelity scoring:
10 = Viewpoint, orientation, framing, visible regions, and spatial layout essentially match, with only negligible offsets.
8 = The intended view and layout are correct, with only minor shifts, crop or scale differences, or small geometric errors.
6 = The overall view is similar, but noticeable differences exist in camera pose, framing, visibility, scale, or relative placement.
4 = Some spatial correspondence exists, but the viewpoint, visible region, or layout is clearly incorrect.
2 = Very little spatial correspondence remains; there is a major camera-pose, framing, or layout mismatch.
0 = No usable spatial correspondence, or spatial structure cannot be assessed.
Spatial score constraints:
- A clearly incorrect viewing direction or target-facing direction cannot receive a score above 4.
- A major mismatch in the visible scene region or room side cannot receive a score above 4.
- Generic centered composition without scene-specific correspondence cannot receive a score above 2.
- A score of 9 or 10 requires both camera view and scene layout to match closely.
Use integers from 0 to 10. Integers between the stated anchors may be used when justified.
Apply all score constraints before producing the final scores.
Each reason must identify the strongest visible evidence and explicitly distinguish the Reference from the Candidate.
Keep each reason to one short sentence.
Return ONLY valid JSON in exactly this format:
{ "semantic_score": <integer 0-10>, "spatial_score": <integer 0-10>, "semantic_reason": "<short evidence-based reason>", "spatial_reason": "<short evidence-based reason>" }

Quality prompt.

You are a strict evaluator of the intrinsic visual quality of a generated image.
Only the labeled CANDIDATE IMAGE is provided.
Evaluate whether the image is visually clean, structurally coherent, and free of generation defects.
Use only visible pixel evidence.
Judge the candidate image independently, without assuming any unseen reference, prompt, context image, task, or intended camera pose.
Evaluate:

- duplicated or unnaturally repeated elements;

Do NOT evaluate:

Do not reward an image merely because it looks attractive, polished, colorful, or cinematic.

Judge coherence within the image’s own visual domain.
Visual Quality scoring:
10 = Technically clean and internally coherent. No meaningful deformation, broken geometry, duplication, border, seam, corruption, or unintended artifact is visible.
8 = High visual quality. Only minor and localized defects are visible, without affecting important objects or structures.
6 = Generally usable, but one prominent defect or several noticeable local artifacts are present.

## Appendix C Training and Inference Details

## C.1 Training Resource

The released training resource contains 20,000 examples from 3,406 scenes,
each with six context images, a language query, and a real target image. Training examples are constructed from the training splits of all three
source datasets, while the benchmark uses their validation splits for held-out
evaluation. The training resource and benchmark share no scenes within any source dataset.

## C.2 Released Training Configuration

The release configuration specifies 10,000 optimizer updates with eight
distributed processes and no gradient accumulation. It uses AdamW (Loshchilov & Hutter, 2019) with learning rate $10^{-5}$ , cosine decay to $10^{-6}$ ,
200 warmup steps, gradient clipping at 0.5, and EMA decay 0.9995. The VAE and vision transformer are frozen, while the language model and
understanding branch remain trainable. Conditioning dropout is disabled. Images are center-cropped and resized to $512\times 512$ for the VAE and
 $518\times 518$ for the vision transformer, with training timestep shift 4. These settings describe the released training recipe.

## C.3 Generation Configuration

The saved BAGEL-SFT inference configuration uses 50 sampling steps,
text and image guidance scales of 4 and 2, timestep shift 3,
and no-thinking inference. Input and saved output images are $512\times 512$ . Local generators share a deterministic per-sample seed across the
supplementary context settings. Closed-source APIs receive no seed. In our setup, directly supplying six separate images to Qwen-Image-Edit-2511
and Emu3.5 led to inference failures or severely degraded outputs. We therefore pack supporting views into a contact sheet and provide the anchor
separately when present. For anchor-free tasks, all context views are packed into a single contact sheet.

## Appendix D Spatial-Understanding Evaluation Details

All results in Table 3 are obtained in our own
evaluations. SAT-v2 uses 150 questions evaluated in two answer orders. Micro accuracy counts correct predictions among all 300 responses,
while both-order accuracy counts questions answered correctly in both
orders among the 150 pairs. BAGEL and BAGEL-SFT use identical inputs and greedy, non-thinking decoding. Their correct counts are 174 and 188 responses, respectively,
and 41 and 62 complete pairs. BAGEL-SFT answers 322 of 1,000 MMSI-Bench questions correctly under the
Vanilla non-circular protocol and 383 of 1,050 MindCube-Tiny questions
under raw-QA evaluation. Our reproduced BAGEL accuracies are 31.0% and 34.7%, respectively,
matching SenseNova-SI (Cai et al., 2026) at the reported precision. Eval. Size in Table 3 denotes the number of
evaluated responses or question pairs under each protocol.

## Appendix E MindCue Implementation

MindCue uses a target-view hypothesis to identify useful additional evidence
while preserving the complete core. Following Section 4.5, $\mathcal{I}_{i}$ indexes the ordered input
pool, $\mathcal{F}_{i}$ the core, and $\mathcal{D}_{i}$ the additional views. The selector uses only input RGB images, the query, task label, and core and
anchor metadata, with no access to held-out targets, sensor depth,
ground-truth poses, generated images, or evaluation scores.

## E.1 Scene Evidence

Frozen VGGT jointly predicts camera poses, intrinsics, depth, and confidence,
placing the input views in a shared reconstruction. For semantics-dominant tasks, a fixed parser extracts queried entities and
frozen SAM 3 segments them. After confidence filtering and duplicate suppression, masks are lifted into
the reconstruction and grouped into object clusters. Each cluster must include a core observation, while additional views can
refine its geometry. Geometry-dominant tasks use the predicted geometry without object masks.

## E.2 Target-View Hypothesis

The query and scene evidence define a hypothesized target camera
 $\hat{\mathcal{C}}^{\star}_{i}$ with pose and intrinsics. This camera guides evidence selection; it is not supplied to the generator
as a pose condition. For tasks with an explicit anchor, MindCue updates the first input camera
according to the requested motion or object reference. ROG and MCVI instead compare candidates derived from predicted objects and
input-camera positions, as detailed in Table 7. World-up is derived from predicted camera poses.

unsupported auxiliary constraints and treats generic between cues as
proximity to both objects.

## E.3 Reliable Target Coverage

To measure target coverage, we construct sparse per-view point sets
 $\hat{\mathcal{P}}_{iv}$ through depth sampling, confidence filtering,
and unprojection. Projecting their union through $\hat{\mathcal{C}}^{\star}_{i}$ retains the nearest
depth in each occupied grid cell, forming the virtual surface $\mathcal{S}_{i}$ 
in Equation 3. View $v$ supports a cell when one of its projected points agrees with this
surface within a depth tolerance. Thus, support requires depth agreement, not projection overlap alone.

Let $\mathcal{V}_{iv}\subseteq\mathcal{S}_{i}$ be the supported cells and
 $\mathcal{V}_{i,-v}=\bigcup_{u\in\mathcal{I}_{i}\setminus\{v\}}\mathcal{V}_{iu}$ 
the support from all other input views. For a nonempty surface, reliable coverage is

 $R_{i}(v)=\frac{|\mathcal{V}_{iv}\cap\mathcal{V}_{i,-v}|}{|\mathcal{S}_{i}|}+\min\!\left\{\frac{|\mathcal{V}_{iv}\setminus\mathcal{V}_{i,-v}|}{|\mathcal{S}_{i}|},\eta\right\}.$ 

(10)

The first term credits cross-view support; the second caps the total
contribution of evidence supplied only by the candidate. We use $\eta=0.05$ and retain every additional view whose reliable coverage
reaches $\tau=0.20$ . Cells are counted equally, and support is computed once from the original
pool, including views subsequently discarded. Unlike diagnostic Target Coverage in Section 4.3,
this score uses only predicted input geometry and a hypothesized camera.

## E.4 Connectivity and Final Selection

MindCue constructs the retained additional-view set $A_{i}$ while preserving
the complete core $\mathcal{F}_{i}$ . If the virtual surface $\mathcal{S}_{i}$ is empty, $A_{i}=\varnothing$ and the
generator receives only the core. Otherwise, it first retains every additional view $v\in\mathcal{D}_{i}$ with
reliable target coverage $R_{i}(v)\geq\tau=0.20$ .

For tasks with an explicit anchor, one below-threshold view may also be
retained to connect the core to the target hypothesis. Candidates must meet minimum coverage and connection-score requirements
and pass nonredundancy checks against the core and coverage-selected views. We select the eligible candidate maximizing

 $Q_{i}(v)=\sqrt{R_{i}(v)\max_{u\in\mathcal{F}_{i}}\delta_{i}(v,u)}.$ 

(11)

Here $\delta_{i}(v,u)$ is the larger of the two directed overlaps: the fraction
of source points reprojecting into the other view with consistent depth,
or zero for an empty source. The selected view forms $X_{i}$ ; without an anchor or an eligible candidate,
 $X_{i}=\varnothing$ .

For a nonempty surface, the final additional-view set is
 $A_{i}=\{v\in\mathcal{D}_{i}:R_{i}(v)\geq\tau\}\cup X_{i}$ ,
as defined in Equation 4. All coverage-selected additions are retained; connectivity adds at most
one further view. The generator receives the original query and the pool views indexed by
 $\mathcal{F}_{i}\cup A_{i}$ , preserving their original order and every core view.

## E.5 Experimental Controls

Our experiments use $C_{10,i}$ as the pool and $C_{6,i}$ as the core. RandDrop randomly selects additional views to match MindCue’s per-sample
count, whereas CounterSel retains the additions rejected by MindCue and is
not count-matched. Both preserve the core and input order, with generator checkpoints and
evaluation references fixed across policies. MindCue uses the same selection for BAGEL and BAGEL-SFT.

We use 63 samples stratified by task and dataset, with three repetitions. Dataset means are weighted by their release frequencies within each task,
then averaged equally across the seven tasks. Models are preloaded and timed separately with GPU synchronization. These stage-wise sums exclude RGB reading and preprocessing, model loading and
warmup, inter-stage handoff, output saving, and evaluation.

## Appendix G Limitations

MindView uses one real target image per query to maintain a simple, uniform
evaluation protocol across all seven tasks. This is a trade-off for ROG and MCVI, whose semantic constraints may admit
nearby valid viewpoints. We reduce ambiguity through object-reference and visibility checks, bounded
view clustering, checks for competing viewpoints, and LLM-assisted and
independent expert review (Appendix A). For example, the recorded matching sets of the 243 released MCVI samples
have median horizontal-position and yaw diameters of only $1.7$  cm and
 $0.93^{\circ}$ . Feature similarity, graded VLM ratings, and linearly decreasing geometric
scores allow partial credit for modest deviations
(Appendix B), so valid views with similar content and
layout within the same compact cluster are expected to receive similar
scores. Taken together, these ambiguity controls and graded metrics support using a
single representative GT as a reasonable practical compromise for consistent
evaluation across tasks.

Abstract

On-policy learning is central to post-training: training on a model’s own generations limits distribution shift and helps preserve existing capabilities.
On hard problems, however, on-policy RL provides no reward-based learning signal when all attempts fail. On-policy distillation provides next-token supervision from a teacher, but on hard problems, if a rollout goes wrong early, subsequent supervision is conditioned on prefixes that already contain the mistake.
Correct behavior can instead come from a gold or teacher solution, but such off-policy targets can differ substantially from the model’s generations and, as we show, may erode existing capabilities.
We therefore treat on-policyness as a continuum and propose projected distillation, which minimally edits the student’s failed attempts into correct targets.
The method uses contrastive updates to favor corrections over failed attempts and refreshes these corrections as the student evolves, enabling near-on-policy learning even when all student attempts fail.
Theoretically, we prove that the student’s learning regret grows with the edit size: it scales linearly with the horizon under minimal correction, as in DAgger, and quadratically under full-answer replacement, as in behavior cloning. Empirically, across two students (Qwen3-8B and OLMo-2-7B) and five domains spanning math, code, science, and tool use, projected distillation gives the highest area under the specialization–retention frontier (best on $8$ of $9$ cells against GRPO and OPD, and $7$ of $9$ against SPOT and SODA). Our ablations highlight the importance of online corrections, the contrastive loss, and on-policyness: moving targets farther off-policy steadily worsens the specialization–retention trade-off, with retention falling from $0.916$ to $0.744$ when minimal corrections are replaced by student-agnostic gold solutions.

## 1 Introduction

Post-training adapts general-purpose language models to reason, follow instructions, and use tools.
The dominant approaches are reinforcement learning from verifiable rewards (RLVR; Shao et al., 2024; Schulman et al., 2017), distillation from a stronger teacher (Agarwal et al., 2023), and supervised fine-tuning (Wei et al., 2022).
Across these approaches, the training signal is most effective when it remains on-policy, that is, close to the model’s own output distribution.
Updates near this distribution can strengthen the target behavior while preserving capabilities acquired during pretraining
(Agarwal et al., 2023; Yang et al., 2024).
Post-training is particularly important for models with only a few billion parameters: although they are inexpensive to run and easy to deploy, they often lack the proficiency required for specialized tasks.
We study how to teach such models to solve hard problems that they currently answer only rarely, if at all, without degrading their existing capabilities.

A fundamental challenge of on-policy RL is that on very hard problems the on-policy signal disappears. RLVR requires
the model to succeed occasionally, so when every sampled attempt fails the reward is zero
everywhere and nothing is reinforced. On-policy distillation (OPD; Agarwal et al., 2023)
keeps the update on the model’s own rollouts and corrects them token by token to be closer to a
response of a stronger teacher.
However, the student rollout of a difficult task contains many mistakes: feedback on the later tokens is
computed on top of an already-wrong prefix, so it largely patches earlier errors instead of
teaching the step at hand (Zhang et al., 2026). Additionally, the
later-token feedback in OPD is inefficient: as the student learns, the prefix on which later-token feedback was given and training budget was spent becomes less probable.

Alternatively, the training signal can come from a complete correct answer supplied from outside,
either ground-truth data or a stronger teacher’s solution. Unlike OPD, which corrects the student’s
own rollout token by token, this target replaces the rollout entirely: the whole training budget
goes on producing a correct answer rather than on patching the one the student wrote. Such a target
is drawn from a distribution other than the student’s own, so it is off-policy. The further it lies
from what the student would produce, the more the student specializes on the task while eroding its
abilities elsewhere. This suggests keeping the target a complete
correct answer but choosing the one closest to what the student already writes, so the supervision
stays near-on-policy.

Figure 1: Learning from the nearest correct answer. Correct answers form a set
 $\mathcal{C}(x)$ , but the student assigns them very different likelihoods. On a hard problem,
its $k$ on-policy attempts () are all wrong and earn no reward. A teacher or gold

Projected distillation instead minimally edits a failed attempt into a newly computed projection

correct answer itself ( $\checkmark$ ). The resulting small edits keep supervision near-policy;

by a term proportional to $mH$ .

Several recent works pursue similar ideas. SPOT (Lin and Han, 2026),
SODA (Chen et al., 2026), and SuperCorrect (Yang et al., 2025) fix the student’s
mistakes with a strong black-box teacher and learn from the corrections. What they leave
unexamined is the on-policyness of the correction itself, how it changes with the size of the
edit, and whether refreshing the corrections online against the evolving student matters. We focus on these aspects. We call the recipe of minimal, online corrections projected
distillation (Fig. 1) and study it along five axes: how well it learns from close,
correct solutions relative to other post-training approaches; why the edit must be minimal to keep
the correction near-on-policy; why the corrections must be refreshed against the still-training
student; which objective learns best from a correction; and how the projector itself can be
improved. A regret analysis in the imitation-learning setting shows the importance of the edit size.

Our contributions.

1. 

We introduce projected distillation, a post-training recipe that learns from minimal,
online corrections of the student’s mistakes. It needs only the correction text, so any capable
black-box model can serve as the projector. Across two students and five benchmarks it attains the
best specialization–retention trade-off, and retains prior abilities where standard post-training
forgets on continual-learning metrics (Sec. 4.1).

2. 

Holding correctness fixed, we show that the correction must be both minimal and online.
Pushing it off-policy monotonically worsens the trade-off (Sec. 4.2), and
freezing the pool instead of refreshing it makes it collapse as the student drifts
(Sec. 4.3). We further give an efficient training objective, a diff-localized
contrast with self-training on correct rollouts (Sec. 4.4), and a refinement procedure
that improves the projections from a mid-capacity ( $32$ B) teacher without updating its parameters
(Sec. 4.5).

3. 

We theoretically analyze projected distillation through imitation learning. The edit size
interpolates between DAgger’s linear-in-horizon regret and behavior cloning’s quadratic regret
(Sec. 5).

## 2 Related Work

Methods for improving a model on hard problems.
Approaches differ in where the correct target comes from and how far that target lies from the
model’s own distribution; together these largely determine the specialization–retention trade-off.
Reinforcement learning from verifiable rewards (RLVR; GRPO (Shao et al., 2024),
PPO (Schulman et al., 2017)) draws the target from the model’s own rollouts, but on the
 $\mathrm{pass}@k{=}0$ set every rollout is wrong and no signal remains. On-policy distillation
(GKD (Agarwal et al., 2023)) and self-distillation (Yang et al., 2024) keep the update on those
rollouts while matching a teacher’s token distribution, which requires teacher logits. Supervised
fine-tuning on an external gold or teacher solution supplies correctness directly but is maximally
off-policy, so fitting it forgets. Between these, there is scope to manufacture a correct target based on the student’s attempt. This opens up the possibility of treating the correct solutions along a continuum from on-policy to off-policy with respect to the model’s current generation style. Our work studies how far the edits may go, whether they need to be recomputed as
the model changes, and what objective to use for effective learning.

Closest work.
The literature presents approaches similar to ours: LeMa (An et al., 2023)
mines corrections once, from an assortment of models rather than the student, with free-form
rewrites and an objective that uses response likelihood maximization. SPOT (Lin and Han, 2026) gathers mistakes of the base model, rectifies the mistakes using minimal edits from an oracle, creates a preference dataset and learns with a reward-based binary cross-entropy objective.
SODA (Chen et al., 2026) creates mistake–correction pairs without emphasizing minimal edits, performs an SFT warm-up, and then, with the SFT’ed policy fixed, learns from static preference pairs. SuperCorrect (Yang et al., 2025) distills thought templates
from a teacher and applies collaborative DPO over the teacher’s correction traces. We also work in the hard-task regime where the model fails. However, our paper focuses mainly on the regime of online learning from close, correct targets to teach the student how to effectively solve hard tasks. We perform a controlled study of the edit sizes used to provide corrections (Sec. 4.2), static vs. online-refreshed pools of corrections (Sec. 4.3) and effective learning objectives for black-box learning (Sec. 4.4). In addition, we theoretically analyze how the edit-size influences the learning regret, which distinguishes our work from the previous ones. Concurrent work (Karan et al., 2026) explores self-projection with an MCMC sampler, in which the student itself rewrites its response with the reference solution in view; Appendix I compares it with black-box projection on its math benchmark.

## 3 Projected Distillation

We study post-training on problems the model rarely solves, where its own samples earn no reward and
correctness has to come from outside.

Setup.
Given a problem $x$ , a solution is a token sequence $y\in\mathcal{V}^{\leq H}$ drawn from a vocabulary $\mathcal{V}$ with a generation horizon $H$ .
The student produces $y$ autoregressively via $\pi_{\theta_{S}}(y\mid x)=\prod_{t}\pi_{\theta_{S}}(y_{t}\mid x,y_{<t})$ , where $y_{t}$ denotes the $t$ -th token, $y_{<t}$ its prefix, and $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$ the frozen initialization of the student.
Correctness is judged by a verifier $R(x,y)\in\{0,1\}$ (e.g., exact-match checking or unit tests), inducing the set of accepted solutions $\mathcal{C}(x)=\{y:R(x,y)=1\}$ .
Our focus is on problems that lie beyond the student’s current ability, i.e., $\mathrm{pass}@k{=}0$ set, for which all $k$ sampled attempts fail.
Further, in Sec. 5, we formalize the generation process using a dynamic-token sequence MDP to provide theoretical insights.

Projection.
The accepted set $\mathcal{C}(x)$ typically contains many solutions, but these solutions can differ substantially in likelihood under the student.
Training toward a solution the student already considers likely requires less movement of its output distribution and therefore better preserves unrelated capabilities (Kirkpatrick et al., 2017; Kumar et al., 2022).
We therefore choose, among the verifier-accepted solutions in $\mathcal{C}(x)$ , the one with highest likelihood under the student.

Definition 1 (Projection).

For a student $\pi_{\theta_{S}}$ and a problem $x$ , the projection is the correct answer the
student is most likely to produce, $\mathrm{proj}(x)\in\argmax_{y^{\prime}\in\mathcal{C}(x)}\log\pi_{\theta_{S}}(y^{\prime}\mid x)$ .

Practically, searching the true projection within $\mathcal{C}(x)$ is intractable. We propose to
find a close approximation with the help of the student’s attempt itself. The intuition behind this
is that a wrong attempt is a sample from the student and is therefore already likely under it; a
correction that preserves most of its tokens preserves most of that likelihood, while the verifier
places the result in $\mathcal{C}(x)$ . Minimal editing is thus a tractable surrogate for
Definition 1, with the verifier supplying correctness and the retained tokens supplying
proximity. We test the surrogate directly in Sec. 4.2, varying the edit size and
recording the likelihood shift
 $\Lambda=\log\pi_{\theta_{S}}(y^{+}\mid x)-\log\pi_{\theta_{S}}(y^{-}\mid x)$ that each size demands.
A projector receives the problem and the attempt in context and is prompted to make the
smallest change that corrects the answer (examples in Appendix B; prompts in
Sec. C.3).

Learning from projection. 
In each round we prompt the student on the hard problems and collect a batch of responses. Most of
them are wrong. We write $y^{-}$ for a wrong response and $y^{+}$ for its projection, and
 $y^{\mathrm{c}}$ for the responses the student gets correct on its own. We have access only to the text the projector returns, not to its logits, so we cannot
match its token distribution. We learn from the pair $(y^{-},y^{+})$ by preference instead. The
projection edits only a small part of the attempt, so $y^{-}$ and $y^{+}$ agree almost everywhere, and a
contrast over their full length would spend most of its gradient on text that was already correct.
We therefore restrict the contrast to the tokens the edit changed. A token-diff of $y^{-}$ against
 $y^{+}$ marks a mistake span $E^{-}$ and a fix span $E^{+}$ ; for instance, for
 $y^{-}=\mathtt{ABCD}$ and $y^{+}=\mathtt{AXCZ}$ , $E^{-}=\{\mathtt{B},\mathtt{D}\}$ and
 $E^{+}=\{\mathtt{X},\mathtt{Z}\}$ .
We apply a DPO
contrast (Rafailov et al., 2023) to those spans alone, using the frozen initialization
 $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$ as reference and $\beta$ for its strength:

 $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{Diff\text{-}Local\text{-}DPO}}=-\,\mathbb{E}_{x}\,\log\sigma\!\Big(\beta\big[\textstyle\sum_{t\in E^{+}}\log\tfrac{\pi_{\theta_{S}}(y^{+}_{t}\mid x,\,y^{+}_{<t})}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y^{+}_{t}\mid x,\,y^{+}_{<t})}-\sum_{t\in E^{-}}\log\tfrac{\pi_{\theta_{S}}(y^{-}_{t}\mid x,\,y^{-}_{<t})}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y^{-}_{t}\mid x,\,y^{-}_{<t})}\big]\Big),$ 

(1)

Here $\sigma$ is the logistic function. The contrast constrains only how likely $y^{+}$ is relative to
 $y^{-}$ , so we add a term that supervises the student’s own correct responses directly:

 $\!\!\!\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{Projected\text{-}Distillation}}=\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{Diff\text{-}Local\text{-}DPO}}+\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{SFT}},~\text{where}~~\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{SFT}}=-\,\mathbb{E}_{x}\,\textstyle\sum_{t}\log\pi_{\theta_{S}}(y^{\mathrm{c}}_{t}\mid x,y^{\mathrm{c}}_{<t}).\!$ 

(2)

Figure 2: One round of projected distillation. The student answers each problem $k$ 
times, and its responses split into the wrong ones $y^{-}$ and the correct ones $y^{\mathrm{c}}$ . A
projector rewrites a wrong response into a correct one $y^{+}$ with the smallest edit that works.
The two are aligned token by token, and the preference term acts only on the spans that differ
(removed, added), while $y^{\mathrm{c}}$ is supervised
directly. The updated student begins the next round, so its corrections are recomputed as it
changes.

Therefore, the wrong responses supply the contrast and the correct
ones the supervised term. The next round re-prompts the student on the same problems and projects
whatever it gets wrong then, so the targets follow the current policy. Fig. 2 summarizes one round of our approach. Sec. 4.3 compares
rebuilding the targets each round against reusing a static correction pool, and Sec. 4.4 compares our loss (2) against five alternatives on the same data.

## 4 Experiments

First, we compare projected distillation with standard post-training approaches in the
hard-task regime, and ask whether what a student learns on one benchmark’s hard set carries over to
the hard problems of other benchmarks (Sec. 4.1). The remaining sections examine the recipe itself, each
component of which could have been set differently: the correction may change more or fewer of the
attempt’s tokens (Sec. 4.2), it may be computed once or recomputed as the student
learns (Sec. 4.3), and several objectives may be applied to it (Sec. 4.4).
We vary each in turn while holding the remainder fixed, then ask whether the projections can
be improved further by test-time scaling (Sec. 4.5), and finally whether the same model
can serve as its own projector when given privileged information (Sec. 4.6).

Experimental Setup:
We study two instruction-tuned students from different families, Qwen3-8B
(Qwen Team, 2025) and OLMo-2-7B (OLMo Team, 2024), on five benchmarks
spanning math, code, tools and science: GSM8K (Cobbe et al., 2021),
MATH (Hendrycks et al., 2021b), LiveCodeBench v6 (Jain et al., 2024),
SciKnowEval (Feng et al., 2024), and ToolAlpaca (Tang et al., 2023).
For each (student, benchmark) pair we train only on its hard set: the problems on which the
base student fails all $k{=}16$ sampled attempts at temperature $0.8$ . The hard set is
mined from the benchmark’s training split (Sec. C.2), and retention is measured in-domain on a disjoint held-out slice of
the same benchmark.
All methods use matched budgets for optimization updates and per-step generation. The remaining configuration is in Sec. C.1, with prompt
templates in Sec. C.3 and further training details in Sec. C.4. Unless noted,
results are averaged over $3$ training seeds, and Sec. C.5 separates training-seed
from evaluation-seed spread.
Sec. D.3 additionally evaluates the trained checkpoints on suites they never saw, and
Sec. D.5 on the hard problems of benchmarks they never saw.

Baselines.
We compare against four baselines, each probing a different axis of the post-training design space.
GRPO (Shao et al., 2024) performs on-policy RL with verifiable rewards, where
positive rewards are scarce.
OPD (Agarwal et al., 2023) performs white-box distillation from the token-level
logits of a strong teacher conditioned on the student’s own prefixes, using a same-family 32B
teacher (Qwen3-32B or OLMo-2-32B) for each student; our method uses these same 32B teachers as its
projection models, and both methods are given the gold answer as privileged information.
Surgical Post-Training (SPOT; Lin and Han, 2026) minimally corrects the student’s
initial attempts and trains on the resulting frozen preference data, while
Semi-On-policy Distillation (SODA; Chen et al., 2026) fine-tunes the student on
teacher corrections and then contrasts a teacher response against a frozen copy of that fine-tuned
student.
Against SPOT and SODA every method draws its corrections from one shared black-box model, which
isolates the learning algorithm from the projector.

Metrics.
We report specialization, pass@1 on the hard set, and retention, pass@1 on the
held-out slice. A method can raise either at the other’s expense, so neither effectively ranks methods on its
own. Our headline metric is the area under the specialization–retention Pareto frontier
(AUPF), the retention a method sustains as it specializes further, anchored at a shared untrained
base. AUPF is low both for a method that never specializes and for one that specializes by giving
up retention. We report it alongside three continual-learning summaries of the retention
trajectory, backward transfer (Lopez-Paz and Ranzato, 2017), forgetting (Chaudhry et al., 2018) and
training-averaged accuracy (Kemker et al., 2018), and retention-safe specialization, the
highest specialization reached while retention stays within $\varepsilon$ of base. Full definitions
are in Appendix D.

## 4.2 RQ2: How does edit size influence learning in projected distillation?

A correction may be correct and still lie far from what the student would have produced, and this
question concerns the cost of that distance. We construct four arms that hold correctness fixed and
vary only how much the correction rewrites the attempt: a minimal edit, two progressively larger
rewrites obtained by re-prompting the projector, and gold, the dataset’s GSM8K reference
solution, which discards the attempt altogether. Each arm trains a separate Qwen3-8B student from

## 4.3 RQ3: How much does refreshing corrections online help?

Projected distillation recomputes its corrections at every round, so that each target is an edit
of an attempt the current student has just produced. Naturally, we ask what role this mechanism plays in projected distillation learning. For comparison, we gather a static
pool: minimal-edit corrections of the initial checkpoint’s responses, mined once before training
and reused for the remainder of the run. This is the same regime occupied by methods like SPOT (Lin and Han, 2026) and LeMa (An et al., 2023). The projector for both static and online-refreshed arms is
the same black-box projector. We report results pooled over all model $\times$ benchmark cells and three
seeds in figure 5.

Figure 5: RQ3: online refresh against a static pool, pooled over all
model $\times$ benchmark cells and $3$ seeds. Both arms take minimal edits from the same
black-box projector; the static pool mines them once against the base checkpoint and reuses
them for the run, the regime of mined-once correction methods. Refreshing holds retention at
base throughout, while the pool climbs briefly above base and then falls to
 $0.60$ .

Refreshing the corrections is essential. Online refresh improves specialization steadily throughout
training, reaching $0.37$ pass@1, whereas the static pool rises over the first few updates and then
flattens near $0.20$ (Fig. 5, left). Retention separates more sharply: online refresh
holds it at base for the whole run, while the static pool climbs slightly above base over the first
 $25$ steps, falls to $0.76$ by step $50$ , and declines from there to $0.60$ (Fig. 5,
right). The fall sets in once the pool has been reused for several rounds. A static
pool cannot follow
the student to the failures it develops afterwards, so repeated passes fit it to a fixed set of
targets, while its generalization degrades.

## 4.4 RQ4: How best to learn from projections of wrong responses?

Figure 6: RQ4: loss recipes. Specialization of each recipe as a fraction of
diff-local DPO $+$ self-SFT, the recipe adopted in Sec. 3. Qwen3-8B, GSM8K hard set, mean and stderr on $3$ seeds. The adopted recipe is

amber, and the arms without a contrast
violet. Held-out retention stays between $0.88$ and $0.94$ for every
arm, so the ordering reflects specialization and not differences in forgetting. SFT on the
projection carries a wider spread across seeds.

In a typical round of projected distillation, we have access to wrong attempts $y^{-}$ , their corrections $y^{+}$ and
correct student responses $y^{\mathrm{c}}$ . These three
quantities admit several learning objectives, and in this section we study an effective objective for learning. The
simplest is supervised fine-tuning on the projection, optionally combined with a term on the
student’s own correct responses. Keeping only that second term is rejection fine-tuning
(RFT; Yuan et al., 2023), which learns from the student’s successes alone and ignores the
projection. Further, because the wrong attempt is also retained, a contrastive
objective is available: DPO (Rafailov et al., 2023) over the full response, again
optionally combined with the self-SFT term. Since a minimal edit leaves most of the response
unchanged, the contrast may instead be localized to the edited spans ((1)). We
compare the six resulting recipes on the GSM8K hard set and report the outcome in Fig. 6.

The projection is necessary: training on the student’s own correct responses alone recovers $0.16$ of the
adopted recipe, since the hard set is mined at $\mathrm{pass}@k{=}0$ and the student’s rollouts stay correct on
about $6\%$ of attempts through the run, so RFT has almost nothing to train on. Among objectives
that do use the projection, the contrast is the more reliable choice: SFT on the projection reaches
 $0.81$ on average but carries a standard error of $0.078$ across seeds, three to sixteen times
that of any contrastive arm. The self-SFT term then settles the ordering among those arms. A
contrast constrains the likelihood of $y^{+}$ only relative to $y^{-}$ , and a localized one does so
over a handful of tokens, so both gain from a term that raises the likelihood of correct text
outright, and the localized one gains more: without it the diff-localized contrast underperforms
the full-sequence one ( $0.73$ against $0.78$ ), and with it the ordering reverses ( $1.00$ against
 $0.92$ ). We adopt the diff-localized contrast with self-SFT as the default recipe
(Sec. 3); per-arm objectives and absolute values are in
Appendix F.

## 4.5 RQ5: Can we improve the quality of projections for better learning?

Figure 7: RQ5: refining the projector at test time. A frozen Qwen3-32B
projector iteratively refines its own correction and the Qwen3-8B student trains on the result
(GSM8K hard set, $K\in\{0,1,2\}$ , $3$ seeds), with the running correction rate on the left and

In this section, we explore ways to make projections from a mid-capacity projector more effective. The
natural approach would be to fine-tune the projector; we instead take a computationally efficient test-time route and ask a
frozen Qwen3-32B to refine its own correction over several rounds. At each round it is re-prompted
with its previous correction and the spans it edited, and across rounds we retain the valid
correction most likely under the base student, measured by log-likelihood over the edited tokens.
We sweep the refinement budget over $K\in\{0,1,2\}$ on a Qwen3-8B student and the GSM8K hard set
with the default recipe.

Refinement improves the corrections in two ways (Fig. 7). More of the student’s failures become
correctable, the correction rate rising from $9.2\%$ at $K{=}0$ to $15.4\%$ at $K{=}1$ and
 $16.7\%$ at $K{=}2$ (Fig. 7, left), so a larger part of the hard set contributes a training
pair at all. At the same time the edits become smaller, from $256$ to $130$ tokens
(Fig. 7, right), which by the account of Sec. 4.2 should
make each target easier to learn from. Downstream, hard-set pass@1 rises from $0.37$ to $0.44$ while
retention stays near $0.91$ – $0.93$ , with most of the gain arriving in the first round. Test-time
refinement can therefore improve the projections, leading to effective performance. Additional details are in Appendix G.

## 5 A Patch-MDP Analysis of Projected Distillation

Setup.
We model generation as a deterministic patch MDP with a token budget $H$ (Definition 2).
A state is a prompt together with the tokens generated so far. An action is a patch, a span
of tokens appended to that state, and every token carries a reward in $[0,1]$ . A patch may span
several tokens, but the reward and every bound below decompose token by token
(Lemma 1), so decisions are indexed by token position throughout and $H$ counts them. The student is a policy
 $\pi$ in a class $\Pi$ , with expected return $J(\pi)$ and optimum $J^{\star}$ . Choosing action $w$ at
state $s$ forfeits some return against the best action available in $s$ . We denote this optimality gap with
 $\Delta(s,w)$ , which is upper-bounded by a constant $\Delta_{\max}$ (Definition 3). Owing to
telescoping (Lemma 1), a full rollout’s shortfall can be accounted for one token at a
time. The regret $J^{\star}-J(\pi)$ then equals $H$ times the average per-token optimality gap, taken over $\pi$ ’s state occupancy (Lemma 2). We write $m$ for the edit size (Definition 4): the tokens
the correction changes relative to the attempt, counted over the $H$ decisions Definition 2
gives both and so including the padding that equalizes their lengths. It relates to the token diff
 $|E^{+}|+|E^{-}|$ of Sec. 3, the quantity Sec. 4.2 reports, by
 $m=|E^{+}|+|E^{-}|+\big||E^{+}|-|E^{-}|\big|=2\max(|E^{+}|,|E^{-}|)$ , the extra term being the change in answer
length. Secs. J.1 and J.2 give the
full treatment.

Learning protocol.
Training runs for $M$ rounds. Each round is one batch of rollouts, the corrections made to
them, and the gradient update that follows. $\pi_{i}$ is the policy entering round $i$ . Ideally we
would query the projector at every student mistake as the student generates a response. That would
let us minimize the gap under the student’s own state occupancy. This is exactly the DAgger
(Ross et al., 2011) approach, where an expert labels states under the learner’s own occupancy,
leading to a learning regret of $O(H)$ , linear in the horizon. Per-mistake labeling as an LLM
student responds is infeasible, since it demands a projector call and a forward pass at every
mistake. In projected distillation, we instead correct the rollout once and perform a single forward
pass over the response. This minimizes the optimality gap under a state occupancy drifted
away from the student’s own. 

Fitting error.
The projector edits the student’s wrong response such that for each state in the rewritten response, the next token has zero optimality gap. We define two further quantities: (i) the policy fit error $\tilde{\varepsilon}$ , the irreducible
error in fitting the best policy in the class to the expert projections; and (ii) the optimization
term $\tilde{\gamma}_{M}$ , the student’s excess disagreement over that best policy, which vanishes for a
no-regret learner (Definition 5). Since a single disagreement costs at most $\Delta_{\max}$ , the gap loss at these prefixes is bounded by
 $\Delta_{\max}$ times the disagreement rate (Lemma 3).

We assume that the drift caused by earlier token edits at a given decision point has a smooth, bounded effect on the optimality gap. We denote the maximum per-token effect on the optimality gap by $\eta$ .

Assumption 1 (Prefix sensitivity).

Write $e(s,s^{\prime})$ for the number of insertions and deletions carrying the generated tokens of one
history into those of the other (Definition 4). The map
 $s\mapsto\mathbb{E}_{w\sim\pi(\cdot\mid s)}[\Delta(s,w)]$ is $\eta$ -Lipschitz in that count:

 $\bigl|\mathbb{E}_{w\sim\pi(\cdot\mid s^{\prime})}[\Delta(s^{\prime},w)]-\mathbb{E}_{w\sim\pi(\cdot\mid s)}[\Delta(s,w)]\bigr|\;\leq\;\eta\,e(s,s^{\prime})\qquad\forall\,\pi\in\Pi,\ \forall\,s,s^{\prime}.$ 

Theorem 1 (Best-iterate suboptimality of projected distillation).

Let Assumption 1 hold, let the projector be as above, and let $m$ be the mean edit size over
the rounds. Then after $M$ rounds

 $\min_{i\leq M}\big[J^{\star}-J(\pi_{i})\big]\;\leq\;H\,\Delta_{\max}\,\big(\tilde{\varepsilon}+\tilde{\gamma}_{M}\big)\;+\;\eta\,m\,H.$ 

The first term collects the fitting and optimization costs. The second is drift, which does not decrease with $M$ ; a smaller edit is the only lever on it.

Horizon regimes.
For a minimal edit the edit size is
 $m=O(1)$ , making the drift $O(\eta H)$ and hence the regret $O(H)$ , linear in the horizon. A
complete rewrite of the response leads to $m=\Theta(H)$ , making the drift $O(\eta H^{2})$ and
therefore the regret $O(H^{2})$ , quadratic in the horizon (Corollary 1). In a controlled
experiment in Sec. 4.2 we vary $m$ (Definition 4) and observe that the
student’s performance suffers as the edit size increases. The two endpoints recover the rates of DAgger and of behavior cloning, though not those algorithms
themselves.

## 6 Conclusion

We introduced projected distillation, a post-training recipe to enable lower-capacity LLMs to solve hard problems beyond their current reach. We demonstrated that standard on-policy post-training methods such as RLVR and OPD struggle to specialize on the hard task, while training on gold solutions leads to catastrophic forgetting. Our proposed approach avoids these issues by maintaining rich learning targets close to the student’s current policy. This is done by iteratively transforming the student’s failed attempts into correct solutions through minimal edits.
Our extensive empirical analyses showed that our approach achieved a better specialization–retention Pareto frontier than previous approaches. We showed that it is important to learn from minimal edits rather than from larger edits or full rewrites, and from the mistakes of the latest checkpoint rather than only those of the base model. Theoretical analysis with a smoothness assumption on the effects of token edits described a spectrum where minimal edits lead to DAgger-like linear-in-horizon regret, while full rewrites lead to behavior-cloning-like quadratic regret. Thus, our work provides empirical and theoretical evidence on the utility of the underlying continuum of on-policyness for continual learning on hard tasks.

Limitations and future work.
We instantiate the projection as a minimal token edit;
exploring likelihood-based alternatives is also an important future direction. The uniform per-token sensitivity assumed in Assumption 1 may be strong and needs further investigation. Our recipe assumes a cheap correctness check, leaving open the domains without a readily available verifier. We also study single-turn tasks, where the student produces one response and the projector edits that response. Multi-turn tasks, in which a failure can originate several turns before it becomes visible in the outcome, would require deciding which turn to project, and we leave this to future work.

## Appendix C Experimental setup

## C.1 Reproducibility Details

Model families and projectors.
The two students are open-weight instruction-tuned models from different families,
Qwen3-8B (Qwen Team, 2025) and OLMo-2-7B (OLMo Team, 2024), chosen so
the effect is tested as a property of the method rather than of one pretraining recipe. The
same-family projector shares the student’s tokenizer, as OPD requires, and unless noted it is
Qwen3-32B for the Qwen student and OLMo-2-32B for the OLMo student. The
black-box path (Sec. 3) needs only the correction text, so it is
tokenizer-free and draws its corrections from a single black-box model shared across students.
The exact checkpoints are:
students Qwen/Qwen3-8B and allenai/OLMo-2-1124-7B-Instruct; same-family
 $32$ B teachers Qwen/Qwen3-32B and allenai/OLMo-2-0325-32B-Instruct.

Benchmarks.
Five benchmarks spanning math, code, tools, and science, each contributing a mined hard set
for training and a held-out slice for evaluation:

• 

GSM8K (Cobbe et al., 2021): grade-school math, numeric answers graded by
exact match. Mine from the 7,473-example train split; evaluate on a $200$ -problem
held-out slice of the test split.

• 

MATH (Hendrycks et al., 2021b): competition math,
\boxed{} answers graded by symbolic equivalence. Mine from the
12k train split; evaluate on the MATH-500 slice (Lightman et al., 2023).

• 

ToolAlpaca (Tang et al., 2023): tool use and API calling. Mine from the
 $468$ -API, ${\sim}4{,}255$ -instance release. The model must emit the first tool call for a
request, and a call counts as correct when the action is a valid function of that API, its
arguments parse as JSON with all required parameters present, and the action matches the
reference call.

• 

LiveCodeBench v6 (Jain et al., 2024): contamination-resistant
competitive-programming problems, graded by unit-test execution (pass@1). Mine hard
problems from the v6 release; evaluate on a held-out slice.

• 

SciKnowEval (Feng et al., 2024): multi-level scientific knowledge. We
use the L3 four-choice subset ( $4{,}135$ items) of the $28{,}392$ -item release, graded by a
letter match against the reference option.

Evaluation protocol. Specialization and retention are evaluated identically at
step $0$ and every $25$ steps thereafter, so each student and benchmark serves as its own
reference; the continual-learning summaries built from these two series are defined in
Appendix D. Every benchmark is scored by the grader listed above, always as
sampled pass@1 on its held-out slice, a $200$ -problem slice for GSM8K. The
full-suite evaluations of Sec. D.3 instead score the final checkpoints on complete
test splits. Answer extraction is per benchmark: the ####-delimited final
number for GSM8K, with a last-number fallback for free-form generations; the
\boxed{} expression for MATH, compared by symbolic equivalence; the
final Answer: letter for SciKnowEval; the first Action and
Action Input pair for ToolAlpaca; and the extracted program for LiveCodeBench, executed
against the unit tests.

Hardware and software. The main experiments ran on a single pod of $8\times$ 
NVIDIA A100-SXM4 (40 GB) GPUs, which was the compute available to us. The stack is PyTorch 2.8.0 (CUDA 12.8), TRL 1.6.0 (von Werra et al., 2020),
Transformers 5.12.1, and Accelerate 1.14.0. Multi-GPU training uses fully-sharded
data parallelism (FSDP2, full-shard) with gradient checkpointing. Final checkpoints are
gathered and consolidated from the sharded
(DTensor) state.

Training configuration. Unless noted, all methods train for $250$ update steps at a
constant learning rate with the Adam optimizer in bf16, computing loss on assistant response
tokens only. GRPO and OPD use $5\times 10^{-6}$ , and SPOT, SODA, and projected distillation use
 $1\times 10^{-5}$ . The per-step generation budget is matched at $8$ rollouts: the
distillation methods (OPD, SPOT, SODA, and ours) draw $8$ problems per step, while GRPO draws a
group of $8$ samples on a single problem. The DPO-based objectives use $\beta{=}0.1$ ; SODA runs
a brief SFT warmup on $y^{+}$ (first $25$ steps) before its preference phase. GRPO keeps TRL’s
default KL coefficient of zero toward the reference policy.

## C.2 Hard-Set Construction and Statistics

Mining rule. Training targets problems the student cannot already solve. For each
candidate problem we draw $k{=}16$ samples from the untrained student at temperature $0.8$ 
(seed $0$ ) and keep the problem only if all $16$ are graded wrong by that benchmark’s grader, i.e. pass@ $16=0$ . The retained problems form the frozen hard set; the $16$ failed samples are stored
with each record, which is what allows an offline arm to be built without re-generating.

Because the criterion is defined against a specific student, hard sets are per student: the
same benchmark yields a different hard set for Qwen3-8B than for OLMo-2-7B, and a checkpoint is only
ever scored against the hard set mined for its own base model.

## C.3 Prompt Templates

All prompts are single user turns passed through the model’s own chat template. For Qwen3 the
thinking mode is disabled in mining, training and evaluation alike, so the same decoding regime
holds throughout. The instruction appended to a question is selected by the benchmark’s answer type,
which fixes the output format the grader expects; the grader always follows the evaluation
benchmark, not the benchmark a checkpoint was trained on.

## C.3.1 Student prompts

The student prompt is the question followed by one of the four instructions below.

Numeric (GSM8K):

Solve the problem by showing your full step-by-step reasoning. After the reasoning, write the final
answer on a new line by itself in the exact format "#### N", where N is just the final number
(no units, words, or commas). Always include this #### line as the last line.

Boxed (MATH, MATH-500, AMC23):

Solve the problem by showing your full step-by-step reasoning. Put your final answer inside
\boxed{...} at the end. The content of the box must be exactly the final answer (a
number or a simplified expression), with no surrounding words.

Multiple choice (SciKnowEval, MMLU):

Answer the multiple-choice question. Think step by step, then write the final answer on a new line
by itself as "Answer: X" where X is A, B, C, or D.

Code (LiveCodeBench, HumanEval):

Complete the Python function. Return the full function definition inside a ```python code
block. Do not include tests or explanation.

## C.3.2 Projection prompt

The projector is instructed to edit the student’s wrong attempt rather than to solve the
problem afresh; this is what keeps the target near the student’s own distribution and makes the
token-level diff small. The role is set per domain (“expert math tutor” for numeric and boxed
answers, “expert science tutor” for multiple choice, “expert assistant for tool/function
calling” for tool use).

System message:

You are an {role}. A student answered a problem incorrectly. PROJECT their attempt onto a correct
solution: keep the student’s overall approach, wording, and structure as much as possible, changing
ONLY what is needed to make it correct. Do NOT rewrite from scratch in your own style --- stay as
close to how the student writes as possible. {end-format requirement}

User message:

Problem:

{problem}

Student’s incorrect attempt:

{wrong attempt}

Minimally edit the student’s attempt into a correct solution, staying as close to their style as
possible. {end-format requirement}

The end-format requirement names the graded target exactly: End with the correct final
answer, {gold}. for math, the correct option for multiple choice, and the required
Action / Action Input pair for tool calling. A projection that does not end in the
graded format is rejected by the verifier, so a correction cannot be accepted on the
strength of its prose alone; every accepted projection has been re-graded by the same grader used
for evaluation.

Code is different in kind. For programming problems the correctness signal given
to the projector is execution feedback on the student’s own attempt — which tests failed,
with input, expected and actual values — and not the hidden test suite:

You are an expert competitive programmer helping a student. The student wrote a solution that FAILS
some tests. PROJECT their attempt onto a correct one: keep the student’s overall approach,
structure, and variable names as much as possible, changing ONLY what is needed to make it pass. Do
NOT rewrite from scratch in your own style --- stay as close to how the student writes as possible.
Return the corrected solution as a single code block.

## C.4 Training Configuration: Additional Details

Sec. C.1 gives the models, learning rates, optimizer, per-step generation budget and
hardware. This section records the details that a re-implementation additionally needs.

Generation budget per domain, and why it varies. The rollout budget must be at
least the budget the hard set was mined with. A problem is in the hard set precisely because the
student failed it under the mining budget, so giving the student a smaller budget during
training truncates its derivation mid-proof and the problem cannot be solved even in principle.
Competition MATH attempts run a median of roughly $805$ tokens, with $61\%$ needing at least $690$ ,
so a $700$ -token budget both pinned hard-set accuracy near zero and trained the model on truncated
derivations, collapsing held-out accuracy from $0.39$ to $0.16$ . The experiments of
Sec. 4 therefore use a uniform $2{,}048$ -token rollout budget, across both students
and all five benchmarks, which is above the mining budget everywhere. Rollouts are sampled at
 $T{=}0.8$ . This is also why the mean deleted span reported for the gold arm in
Tab. 15 exceeds $700$ tokens. The transfer runs of Sec. D.5 and the
replication of Appendix I use their own budgets, stated in those sections.

Projector configuration. The black-box projector is Claude Opus 4.8, accessed
through a hosted API and run off-GPU, which is what allows the whole pod to be spent on the
student. Each wrong sample gets at most two projection attempts and the first verified-correct
projection is kept; if neither verifies, the sample contributes no preference pair for that step.
The same-family projector variant is served locally.

GPU allocation. Of the eight GPUs in the pod, seven hold FSDP2 shards of the
student and one is reserved for the in-loop vLLM (Kwon et al., 2023) generator that produces
on-policy rollouts and runs
the periodic evaluations. Sharing the generator GPU with a training rank starves the generator and
stalls the step, so the split is fixed rather than dynamic.

Loss variants. The arms and their objectives are given in
Appendix F. Two further switches are fixed throughout: the adopted default uses
unnormalized rather than token-mean-normalized DPO log-probabilities, and the auxiliary SFT term
acts on the student’s own correct rollouts $y^{\mathrm{c}}$ , as in (2), not on
the projection $y^{+}$ .

## C.5 Evaluation: Statistical Resolution and Seed Variance

Two kinds of seed. Two sources of variation are easy to conflate and differ by
roughly a factor of fifty in magnitude.

• 

A training seed indexes an independently trained checkpoint. Different weights.
The spread is large: the three math-trained projected-distillation seeds score $0.749$ , $0.311$ 
and $0.688$ on MATH-500.

• 

An evaluation seed indexes which generations were sampled from one fixed
checkpoint. The model is identical across replicates. The spread is small, typically
 $\pm 0.006$ on the large suites.

The two are reported in different places. The $\pm$ in Tabs. 11 and 12
is the standard error over evaluation seeds of a single training-seed- $0$ checkpoint: it certifies
that a measurement would reproduce under re-sampling, and says nothing about whether a different
training run would land near it. Everywhere else, including Tabs. 8, 9 and 10,
the $\pm$ is the standard error over the three training seeds. Claims about
a method rather than about a particular checkpoint have to be read against training-seed
spread, which is the dominant term.

Greedy decoding cannot be averaged this way. It is deterministic, so replicates return the same
number and a standard deviation of exactly zero, which reports nothing about how well the mean is
pinned down. Sampling one generation per problem and repeating over independent seeds gives an error
bar that means something. For pass@ $k$ estimated from $n>k$ samples we use the unbiased estimator of
Chen et al. (2021), $\text{pass@}k=1-\binom{n-c}{k}\big/\binom{n}{k}$ . The pass@ $16$ figures
in this paper are not estimated that way: they are computed from $n{=}16$ draws as the share of
problems solved at least once. That indicator is unbiased for pass@ $16$ when $n=k$ , but it carries
more variance than an estimate from more draws would.

Resolution limits of the small suites. Suite size sets the granularity of any
difference that can be claimed. AMC23 has only $40$ problems, so one problem is $0.025$ and the
base standard error alone is $\pm 0.030$ ; differences below roughly $0.06$ there should not be
argued. GSM8K ( $1{,}319$ ) and MMLU ( $14{,}042$ ) are the suites where small differences resolve.

A note on greedy reproducibility. Repeating a greedy evaluation with identical
prompts and identical suite size did not return bit-identical scores. The cause is
continuous-batching non-determinism in the inference server, which changes numerics enough to flip
borderline argmax tokens. Greedy numbers therefore carry a small amount of unreported jitter of
their own, which is a further reason to report a sampled mean with an explicit error bar.

## Appendix D RQ1: Comparison with baselines

## D.1 Metrics

Area under the Pareto frontier (AUPF).
For a single run, evaluation at checkpoints $i=1,\dots,n$ yields points $(x_{i},y_{i})\in[0,1]^{2}$ ,
where $x_{i}$ is specialization (hard-set pass@1) and $y_{i}$ is retention (held-out pass@1). A
point is dominated if another point is at least as good on both axes, and the Pareto
frontier is the non-dominated set

 $\mathcal{F}=\bigl\{\,i:\ \nexists\,j\ \text{with}\ x_{j}\geq x_{i},\ y_{j}\geq y_{i},\ (x_{j},y_{j})\neq(x_{i},y_{i})\,\bigr\}.$ 

(3)

We anchor the frontier at a shared untrained base $B=(0,H_{0})$ : specialization $0$ and
retention $H_{0}$ , the untrained held-out accuracy, both estimated as the mean step- $0$ value over
the methods that start untrained. Specialization at the anchor is measured at step $0$ rather than
inferred from the mining criterion; it averages $0.012$ across those methods, which we round to zero. $B$ is a forced left anchor (it is never subject to
domination); using a shared base rather than each run’s own first checkpoint prevents methods
that begin with an SFT warmup (e.g. SODA) from being scored against an already-elevated
starting specialization. Indexing $\{B\}\cup\mathcal{F}$ in order of increasing specialization,
 $0=\tilde{x}_{0}<\tilde{x}_{1}<\dots<\tilde{x}_{K}$ , AUPF is the area under the piecewise-linear frontier
 $y_{\mathcal{F}}(\cdot)$ by the trapezoidal rule:

 $\mathrm{AUPF}\;=\;\int_{0}^{\tilde{x}_{K}}y_{\mathcal{F}}(x)\,dx\;=\;\sum_{k=1}^{K}\tfrac{1}{2}\,(\tilde{y}_{k-1}+\tilde{y}_{k})\,(\tilde{x}_{k}-\tilde{x}_{k-1}).$ 

(4)

We compute AUPF per seed and report the mean  $\pm$  s.e. across seeds. Because retention is
integrated over specialization from the base at $x{=}0$ , a method that never specializes
( $\tilde{x}_{K}\!\approx\!0$ ) has $\mathrm{AUPF}\!\approx\!0$ ; a higher AUPF means pushing
specialization further while holding retention.

Continual-learning metrics (Tab. 3).
For a run with per-step specialization $x_{t}$ and retention $y_{t}$ ( $t=1,\dots,T$ ) and the
shared untrained base $(0,H_{0})$ , define

 $\displaystyle\bar{x}$ 
 $\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{T}\textstyle\sum_{t=1}^{T}x_{t},$ 
 $\displaystyle\bar{y}$ 
 $\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{T}\textstyle\sum_{t=1}^{T}y_{t},$ 

 $\displaystyle\mathrm{BWT}$ 
 $\displaystyle=y_{T}-H_{0},$ 
 $\displaystyle F$ 
 $\displaystyle=\max\nolimits_{\tau\leq T}y_{\tau}-y_{T}\ \text{with}\ y_{0}{:=}H_{0},$ 

 $\displaystyle\mathrm{SafeSpec}_{\varepsilon}$ 
 $\displaystyle=\max\!\bigl\{x_{t}:y_{t}\geq H_{0}-\varepsilon\bigr\},$ 

 $0$ if the set is empty.

 $\bar{x},\bar{y}$ are training-averaged accuracies in the spirit of the AUC metrics of
Kemker et al. (2018); $\mathrm{BWT}$ is the backward transfer of Lopez-Paz and Ranzato (2017)
and $F$ the forgetting measure of Chaudhry et al. (2018). $\mathrm{SafeSpec}_{\varepsilon}$ is the highest specialization
reached at any checkpoint whose retention is within $\varepsilon$ of the untrained base. For the aggregate
row in Tab. 3, retention is expressed as a fraction of base so panels with
different retention scales are commensurate: $\bar{y}_{\mathrm{norm}}=\bar{y}/H_{0}$ ,
 $F_{\mathrm{norm}}=F/H_{0}$ , $\mathrm{BWT}_{\mathrm{norm}}=(y_{T}-H_{0})/H_{0}$ , and pooled
 $\mathrm{SafeSpec}$ uses $y_{t}/H_{0}\geq 1-\varepsilon_{\mathrm{rel}}$ with
 $\varepsilon_{\mathrm{rel}}\in\{0.02,0.05\}$ . Each metric is computed per seed and averaged
over $3$ seeds; the aggregate pools further over $($ family, benchmark $)$ , with GRPO on
Qwen-LiveCodeBench entered as no learning.

## D.5 Transfer to other benchmarks’ hard problems

This subsection gives the setup and the full results of the transfer experiment summarized in
Sec. 4.1 and Tab. 2.

Hard sets. For every problem of every pool we draw $16$ samples from the
untrained Qwen3-8B at $T{=}0.8$ (seed $0$ , thinking mode disabled, $3{,}072$ new tokens) and grade
them with the MATH grader. A problem is hard when all $16$ are wrong, as in
Sec. C.2. The budget is set so that a hard problem is one the student cannot solve rather
than one it cannot finish (Sec. C.4). This leaves $628$ of the $11{,}998$ MATH
training problems hard. We split the $628$ into $501$ training and

## E.1 Setup and measurement

Qwen3-8B student, GSM8K hard set (mined pass@ $k$ $=$ $0$ , $122$ problems), black-box projector,

the projector for progressively larger rewrites of the same attempt, plus the

 $\Lambda\;=\;\log\pi_{\theta}(y^{+}\mid x)\;-\;\log\pi_{\theta}(y^{-}\mid x),$ 

(5)

dominate an average. The median is stable across seeds
(Tab. 15) whereas the mean is not. For gold, which replaces a long attempt

## E.3 Mechanism

Full-utterance and diff-localized shift.
Fig. 11 compares $\Lambda$ measured over the whole utterance against the
same quantity restricted to the changed spans $E^{-}\!/E^{+}$ . The two agree at $r=0.9999$ across
all $2{,}249$ logged steps of the edit arms, with the localized quantity retaining $94$ to
 $95\%$ of the magnitude, because the tokens before the first edit are both identical and identically
conditioned, so their contributions cancel exactly. Shared tokens after an edit do not: they
are scored on prefixes that have already diverged, and they are what the remaining $5$ to $6\%$ 
measures. Localizing the measurement, as the objective of (1) does, therefore keeps
almost all of the signal.

Figure 11: Full-utterance against diff-localized likelihood shift. Left: every logged
step of the three edit arms, on symmetric log axes with the identity line dotted.
Right: median $|\Lambda|$ per arm under both measurements, with the localized value
as a percentage of the full one.

Edit size and the likelihood shift, step by step.
Fig. 12 plots every logged step of the edit arms, with the edit size realized
at that step against the likelihood shift it demands. The relation is strongly monotone but not
linear. Pooling the arms, the median $|\Lambda|$ rises across every edit-size decile, from $82$ in
the smallest to $570$ in the largest (Spearman $\rho=0.83$ ), while the Pearson correlation is only
 $0.28$ . Both concavity and a heavy tail depress it, and the tail dominates: dropping the largest
 $2\%$ of $|\Lambda|$ raises Pearson to $0.71$ , whereas linearizing the concavity alone, by
correlating against $\log|\Lambda|$ , reaches $0.50$ . The effect also diminishes with the size of the edit: the
per-step Theil–Sen slope is $0.72$ [ $0.63$ , $0.82$ ] at the minimal edit against $0.17$ 
[ $0.16$ , $0.18$ ] and $0.14$ [ $0.13$ , $0.15$ ] at the two larger rewrites, and the relation holds
within each arm on its own ( $\rho=0.52$ , $0.82$ , $0.78$ ). We therefore read edit size as a
monotone indicator of the likelihood shift rather than a proportional one.

## Appendix F RQ4: Loss-recipe details

## F.1 Objectives

Rollout terminology. Each training step, the student rolls out attempts on the
hard problems and the verifier splits them: a correct rollout $y^{\mathrm{c}}$ is one
it solves on its own ( $R(x,y^{\mathrm{c}})=1$ ); a wrong rollout $y^{-}$ is one it fails
( $R=0$ ). For each wrong rollout the frozen projector produces the minimal-edit
projection $y^{+}$ (verified-correct, Sec. 3), and a token-diff aligns
 $(y^{-},y^{+})$ into the mistake span $E^{-}$ and fix span $E^{+}$ used by (1). The
full-sequence DPO variant replaces $E^{+},E^{-}$ in (1) with the full
sequences $y^{+},y^{-}$ .

Per-arm objectives. The four contrastive arms optimize
 $\mathcal{L}^{E}_{\mathrm{DPO}}$ (diff-local, on the changed spans $E^{\pm}$ ) or
 $\mathcal{L}^{\mathrm{seq}}_{\mathrm{DPO}}$ (full-sequence, on $y^{\pm}$ ), each with or without
an added self-SFT term $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{SFT}}(y^{\mathrm{c}})$ on the student’s own
correct rollouts. RFT optimizes $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{SFT}}(y^{\mathrm{c}})$ alone (no
projected target, no contrast); SFT on projection $+$ self-SFT optimizes
 $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{SFT}}(y^{+})+\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{SFT}}(y^{\mathrm{c}})$ (SFT on the
projected correction $y^{+}$ plus self-SFT on the student’s own correct rollouts, no contrast).

## Appendix G RQ5: Refinement details

## G.1 Refinement protocol

A correction is valid only if a reasoning check
confirms that its derivation process actually yields the answer, so a projection that reaches the
right answer by an unsound route is discarded. Round $0$ is a single correction pass. In each subsequent round
the frozen projector is re-prompted with its own previous correction together with the spans it
edited in that correction, and returns a refined correction. Across rounds we keep the valid
correction that the student itself is most likely to produce, scored by the base student’s
log-likelihood on the edited tokens, which selects for corrections the student can absorb rather
than for the ones the projector prefers. The projector is never updated. All arms train the
Qwen3-8B student for $250$ steps with the default recipe of Sec. 3 over $3$ 
seeds.

## Appendix H RQ6: Self-projection details

Everywhere else in this paper the projector is a separate and stronger model. Here we make the same
model write the correction. We give the student its own wrong attempt together with privileged
information, the verified final answer, and ask it for a correct solution that stays close to what
it wrote. That output is then used exactly as a projector’s correction would be, with the same
objective and the same training loop. Nothing else changes, so the run measures how much of the
method depends on the projector being stronger than the student.

The student is Qwen3-8B and the hard set is the $122$ GSM8K problems it fails on all $16$ attempts.
The teacher is a frozen copy of the student’s own base weights, so it does not improve as the
student does. We train for $250$ updates over $3$ seeds, with the same data, budget and evaluation
as the edit-size runs of Sec. 4.2, from which the reference rows are taken. The answer is supplied with an instruction to derive it rather than assert it;
without that, the model appends the answer line to unchanged wrong reasoning and the grader accepts
it. We also run the same idea at the logit level, where the frozen base model scores the student’s
own generation under a prompt carrying the answer and the student trains on that token distribution
instead of on edited text.

The logit version gives almost nothing, $0.046$ after $250$ updates. The answer does reach the
teacher, and the student never sees it, so the two token distributions differ from the first step;
what is missing is not information but capability. The teacher is the student’s own base weights,

measured loss stays between $0.003$ and $0.006$ throughout, and making the target denser, a pure

learning rates from their respective families in the main experiments ( $10^{-5}$ and
 $5\times 10^{-6}$ ), so each is comparable to its own column rather than to the other; the comparison
against the black-box projector is at a matched $10^{-5}$ . The 8B also does not edit minimally. It
appends a fresh derivation rather than repairing the attempt, which is part of why it falls short:
producing a small, targeted edit is precisely what the weaker projector cannot do.

## Appendix J Theoretical analysis

## J.1 The Patch MDP: Model and Assumption

This appendix gives the model and the assumption behind Sec. 5; Sec. J.2
gives the proofs.

Definition 2 (Patch MDP).

We define a patch MDP as the tuple $\mathcal{M}=(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{A},p,r,\rho,\gamma)$ over a
vocabulary $\mathcal{V}$ with token budget $H$ :

• 

 $\mathcal{S}$ : a state is a full history $s=(x,y^{\prime})$ , a problem together with the tokens $y^{\prime}$ 
generated so far; $s_{t}=(x,y_{1:t})$ is the state before decision $t$ , and $|s|$ counts the
generated tokens.

• 

 $\mathcal{A}$ : an action is a patch, a token span fitting the remaining budget,
 $\mathcal{A}(s)=\{w\in\mathcal{V}^{h}:1\leq h\leq H-|s|\}$ .

• 

 $p$ : transitions are deterministic and append the patch, $p(s\oplus w\mid s,w)=1$ .

• 

 $r$ : the reward is token-additive,
 $r(s,w)=\sum_{j=1}^{|w|}r_{\mathrm{tok}}(s\oplus w_{<j},w_{j})$ with
 $r_{\mathrm{tok}}\in[0,1]$ , so a rollout returns at most $H$ .

• 

 $\rho$ : the initial state distribution is the distribution over problems,
 $s_{0}=(x,\varnothing)$ with $x\sim\rho$ .

• 

 $\gamma=1$ : no discounting. $\mathcal{V}$ contains a terminal symbol, and a rollout that emits
it moves to an absorbing state of zero reward and zero value.

Each decision emits one token, so a rollout takes exactly $H$ of them, indexed $t=0,\dots,H-1$ with
 $s_{H}$ absorbing. Then
 $d^{\pi}(s)=\tfrac{1}{H}\sum_{t=0}^{H-1}\Pr_{\pi}[s_{t}=s]$ is a probability distribution over states, the
normalized occupancy of $\pi$ .

Write $V^{\star}$ and $Q^{\star}$ for the optimal value and action-value of $\mathcal{M}$ . The return of a
policy and its optimum are

 $J(\pi)\;=\;\mathbb{E}_{s_{0}\sim\rho}\,\mathbb{E}_{\pi}\Big[\sum_{t=0}^{H-1}r_{\mathrm{tok}}(s_{t},w_{t})\Big],\qquad J^{\star}\;=\;\max_{\pi}J(\pi)\;=\;\mathbb{E}_{s_{0}\sim\rho}\big[V^{\star}(s_{0})\big],$ 

(6)

the second equality because an optimal policy is optimal from every state.

Definition 3 (Optimality gap).

 $\Delta(s,w)\coloneqq V^{\star}(s)-Q^{\star}(s,w)$ is the return an action forfeits against the best one
available at $s$ , and $\Delta_{\max}\coloneqq\sup_{s,w}\Delta(s,w)$ , finite because returns lie in
 $[0,H]$ . It is a constant of $\mathcal{M}$ , and plays the role of the cost-to-go constant $u$ of
Ross et al. (2011).

Definition 4 (Edit count and edit size).

For histories $s,s^{\prime}$ ,

 $e(s,s^{\prime})\;\coloneqq\;|s|+|s^{\prime}|-2\,\big|\mathrm{LCS}(s,s^{\prime})\big|,$ 

where $\mathrm{LCS}(s,s^{\prime})$ is a longest common subsequence (Hirschberg, 1975) of their
generated tokens. This counts the insertions and deletions carrying one into the other, the
string-to-string correction distance of Wagner and Fischer (1974) restricted to those two operations.
The edit size of a correction is $m\coloneqq e(y^{-},y^{+})$ , the two rollouts compared at the $H$ 
decisions Definition 2 gives them, absorbing padding included. It exceeds the token-diff count
 $|E^{-}|+|E^{+}|$ of Sec. 3, which Sec. 4.2 measures, by exactly the change
in answer length: $m=|E^{-}|+|E^{+}|+\big|\,|y^{-}|-|y^{+}|\,\big|$ .

The learning problem.

Training runs for $M$ rounds. In round $i$ the learner $\pi_{i}$ samples rollouts, a frozen projector
rewrites the wrong ones, and the learner is scored on those rewrites and updated once. The learner
ranges over a policy class $\Pi=\{\pi_{\theta}:\theta\in\Theta\}$ with $\Theta\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}$ 
convex of Euclidean diameter $D$ , so minimizing over $\pi\in\Pi$ and over $\theta\in\Theta$ are the
same operation; only Proposition 1 uses this.

Where the loss is read.

The learner corrects a whole rollout once and scores the correction in a single forward pass, so the
loss is evaluated at corrected prefixes, not at the states the learner visits. Write
 $\tilde{d}_{i}(\tilde{s})=\tfrac{1}{H}\sum_{t=0}^{H-1}\Pr[\tilde{s}_{t}=\tilde{s}]$ for the occupancy of those
corrected prefixes in round $i$ , the state-visitation distribution the loss is read under and the
counterpart of $d^{\pi_{i}}$ . For any distribution $d$ over states, let

 $L(d,\pi)\;\coloneqq\;\mathbb{E}_{s\sim d}\,\mathbb{E}_{w\sim\pi(\cdot\mid s)}\big[\Delta(s,w)\big]$ 

(7)

be the mean gap of $\pi$ under $d$ . Performance is controlled by $L(d^{\pi_{i}},\pi_{i})$ , but the
algorithm can only reduce $L(\tilde{d}_{i},\pi)$ .

The projector.

The projector edits a wrong attempt into a correct one, and $w^{\star}(s)$ denotes its token at $s$ . We
idealize it as an expert: its token is optimal wherever we read it, so $\Delta(s,w^{\star}(s))=0$ .
Corollary 2 gives the bound for a projector that is suboptimal.

Definition 5 (Surrogate, realizability, average regret).

The surrogate objective $\tilde{\varepsilon}_{i}(\pi)$ for policy $\pi$ is the $0$ – $1$ disagreement
with the projector, measured under $\tilde{d}_{i}$ :

 $\tilde{\varepsilon}_{i}(\pi)\;\coloneqq\;\mathbb{E}_{\tilde{s}\sim\tilde{d}_{i}}\Big[\Pr_{w\sim\pi(\cdot\mid\tilde{s})}\big[w\neq w^{\star}(\tilde{s})\big]\Big]\;=\;\mathbb{E}_{\tilde{s}\sim\tilde{d}_{i}}\big[\,1-\pi(w^{\star}(\tilde{s})\mid\tilde{s})\,\big].$ 

The approximation and optimization terms are

 $\tilde{\varepsilon}\;\coloneqq\;\min_{\pi\in\Pi}\frac{1}{M}\sum_{i=1}^{M}\tilde{\varepsilon}_{i}(\pi),\qquad\tilde{\gamma}_{M}\;\coloneqq\;\frac{1}{M}\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{M}\tilde{\varepsilon}_{i}(\pi_{i})-\min_{\pi\in\Pi}\sum_{i=1}^{M}\tilde{\varepsilon}_{i}(\pi)\Big),$ 

so that $\tfrac{1}{M}\sum_{i}\tilde{\varepsilon}_{i}(\pi_{i})=\tilde{\varepsilon}+\tilde{\gamma}_{M}$ and the
learner is no-regret when $\tilde{\gamma}_{M}\to 0$ . Here $\tilde{\varepsilon}$ is a minimum
over the policy class, so it measures how well the class can reproduce the projector’s tokens. It is
unaffected by how much return those tokens forfeit; that enters separately, as $\bar{\delta}$ in
Corollary 2.

## J.1.1 The assumption

The analysis rests on Assumption 1, stated in Sec. 5: the map
 $s\mapsto\mathbb{E}_{w\sim\pi(\cdot\mid s)}[\Delta(s,w)]$ is $\eta$ -Lipschitz in the edit count
 $e(\cdot,\cdot)$ , uniformly over $\pi\in\Pi$ . It is the only assumption the analysis makes about
the MDP. Three points about it follow.

The Lipschitz constant is uniform over $\Pi$ and over pairs of histories, but the proof invokes it only at the
pairs $(s_{t},\tilde{s}_{t})$ that Lemma 4 compares, so $\eta$ may be taken as the largest
shift over those pairs.

The condition is placed on the averaged gap rather than on $\Delta(s,w)$ at a fixed $w$ . The
proof compares $\mathbb{E}_{w\sim\pi}[\Delta]$ at two different prefixes, and moving from one to the
other shifts the action distribution as well as the gap, so both must be controlled. Two pointwise
conditions suffice. Suppose the gap is itself Lipschitz,
 $|\Delta(s^{\prime},w)-\Delta(s,w)|\leq\eta_{\Delta}\,e(s,s^{\prime})$ , and the policy is smooth in the same count,
 $\mathrm{TV}\big(\pi(\cdot\mid s),\pi(\cdot\mid s^{\prime})\big)\leq\kappa\,e(s,s^{\prime})$ . Adding and
subtracting $\mathbb{E}_{w\sim\pi(\cdot\mid s^{\prime})}[\Delta(s,w)]$ splits the difference in two, bounded by
 $\eta_{\Delta}e$ and, since $\Delta$ takes values in $[0,\Delta_{\max}]$ and its oscillation is
therefore at most $\Delta_{\max}$ , by $\Delta_{\max}\kappa e$ . Together they give Assumption 1
with $\eta\leq\eta_{\Delta}+\Delta_{\max}\kappa$ .

Assumption 1 is also the only reason any result below depends on the size of an edit. An argument
that knows only which decisions sit downstream of an edit pays $\Delta_{\max}$ at each of
them, which is identical for a one-token edit and for a hundred-token edit at the same position:
“the prefix differs” is a binary predicate and it saturates. Assumption 1 instead charges in
proportion to $e(\cdot,\cdot)$ , which every edit-size claim below uses.

## J.2 Proofs

## J.2.1 The gap decomposes across a patch

Lemma 1 (Patch additivity).

For patch MDP,
 $\ \Delta(s,w_{1:h})=\sum_{j=1}^{h}\Delta(s\oplus w_{<j},w_{j})$ .

Proof.

Since the transitions of Patch MDP are deterministic (c.f. Definition 2), $Q^{\star}(s,w_{1:h})=r(s,w_{1:h})+V^{\star}(s\oplus w_{1:h})$ , and by
Definition 2 the reward splits as
 $r(s,w_{1:h})=\sum_{j=1}^{h}r_{\mathrm{tok}}(s\oplus w_{<j},w_{j})$ . Hence

 $\displaystyle\Delta(s,w_{1:h})$ 
 $\displaystyle=V^{\star}(s)-r(s,w_{1:h})-V^{\star}(s\oplus w_{1:h})$ 

(by determinism)

 $\displaystyle=V^{\star}(s)-V^{\star}(s\oplus w_{1:h})-\sum_{j=1}^{h}r_{\mathrm{tok}}(s\oplus w_{<j},w_{j})$ 

(by Definition 2)

 $\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{h}\big[V^{\star}(s\oplus w_{<j})-V^{\star}(s\oplus w_{<j+1})\big]-\sum_{j=1}^{h}r_{\mathrm{tok}}(s\oplus w_{<j},w_{j})$ 

(telescoping)

 $\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{h}\big[V^{\star}(s\oplus w_{<j})-r_{\mathrm{tok}}(s\oplus w_{<j},w_{j})-V^{\star}(s\oplus w_{<j+1})\big]$ 

 $\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{h}\Delta(s\oplus w_{<j},w_{j})$ 

(by determinism)

where the last step uses
 $Q^{\star}(s\oplus w_{<j},w_{j})=r_{\mathrm{tok}}(s\oplus w_{<j},w_{j})+V^{\star}(s\oplus w_{<j+1})$ .
∎

The total gap along a rollout is therefore the same however the rollout is cut into patches; only
the per-step decomposition changes.

## J.2.2 Regret is the accumulated gap, under the learner’s own occupancy

Lemma 2 (No distribution shift).

Under Definition 2, for every round $i$ ,

 $J^{\star}-J(\pi_{i})\;=\;\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{i}}\Big[\sum_{t=0}^{H-1}\Delta(s_{t},w_{t})\Big]\;=\;H\,L(d^{\pi_{i}},\pi_{i}),$ 

where $\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{i}}$ is over trajectories $(s_{0},w_{0},\dots,s_{H-1},w_{H-1})$ generated by
 $\pi_{i}$ from $s_{0}\sim\rho$ , and $L$ is as in (7).

Proof.

Fix one trajectory $(s_{0},w_{0},\dots,s_{H-1},w_{H-1})$ generated by $\pi_{i}$ . By Definition 2 it
has exactly $H$ decisions and $V^{\star}(s_{H})=0$ , since $s_{H}$ is the absorbing state with zero value. Then

 $\displaystyle V^{\star}(s_{0})-\sum_{t=0}^{H-1}r_{\mathrm{tok}}(s_{t},w_{t})$ 
 $\displaystyle=\sum_{t=0}^{H-1}\big[V^{\star}(s_{t})-r_{\mathrm{tok}}(s_{t},w_{t})-V^{\star}(s_{t+1})\big]$ 

(telescoping, $V^{\star}(s_{H})=0$ )

 $\displaystyle=\sum_{t=0}^{H-1}\Delta(s_{t},w_{t})$ 

( $Q^{\star}(s_{t},w_{t})=r_{\mathrm{tok}}(s_{t},w_{t})+V^{\star}(s_{t+1})$ )

Taking $\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{i}}$ over trajectories turns the left side into
 $\mathbb{E}[V^{\star}(s_{0})]-\mathbb{E}\big[\sum_{t}r_{\mathrm{tok}}(s_{t},w_{t})\big]=J^{\star}-J(\pi_{i})$ , which is the stated
identity. For the second equality,

 $\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{i}}\Big[\sum_{t=0}^{H-1}\Delta(s_{t},w_{t})\Big]$ 
 $\displaystyle=\sum_{t=0}^{H-1}\mathbb{E}\big[\Delta(s_{t},w_{t})\big]$ 

(exactly $H$ terms, by Definition 2)

 $\displaystyle=H\,\mathbb{E}_{s\sim d^{\pi_{i}}}\mathbb{E}_{w\sim\pi_{i}}\big[\Delta(s,w)\big]$ 

(by the definition of $d^{\pi_{i}}$ )

 $\displaystyle=H\,L(d^{\pi_{i}},\pi_{i})$ 

∎

Both expectations in $L(d^{\pi_{i}},\pi_{i})$ are taken under $\pi_{i}$ ’s own occupancy. No change of
measure is performed, so no concentrability coefficient appears.

## J.2.3 Disagreement with the projector dominates the gap

Lemma 3 (Reduction).

Under Definition 3, with the projector idealized as above, for every $\pi\in\Pi$ and every
round $i$ ,

 $L(\tilde{d}_{i},\pi)\;\leq\;\Delta_{\max}\,\tilde{\varepsilon}_{i}(\pi).$ 

Proof.

Fix a corrected state $\tilde{s}$ and split the expectation over the projector’s token and the rest:

 $\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{w\sim\pi}\big[\Delta(\tilde{s},w)\big]$ 
 $\displaystyle=\pi(w^{\star}\mid\tilde{s})\,\Delta(\tilde{s},w^{\star})+\sum_{w\neq w^{\star}}\pi(w\mid\tilde{s})\,\Delta(\tilde{s},w)$ 

 $\displaystyle=\sum_{w\neq w^{\star}}\pi(w\mid\tilde{s})\,\Delta(\tilde{s},w)$ 

( $\Delta(\tilde{s},w^{\star})=0$ , idealized projector)

 $\displaystyle\leq\Delta_{\max}\sum_{w\neq w^{\star}}\pi(w\mid\tilde{s})$ 

(by Definition 3)

 $\displaystyle=\Delta_{\max}\big(1-\pi(w^{\star}\mid\tilde{s})\big)$ 

Taking $\mathbb{E}_{\tilde{s}\sim\tilde{d}_{i}}$ of both sides and applying Definition 5 gives the
claim.
∎

This is the reduction of Ross et al. (2011), applied at the corrected prefixes rather than at
the learner’s own states.

## J.2.4 Transfer from the corrected prefixes to the learner’s own

Lemma 4 (Transfer).

Under Assumption 1 and Definition 2, for every round $i$ ,

 $L(d^{\pi_{i}},\pi_{i})\;\leq\;L(\tilde{d}_{i},\pi_{i})+\eta\,\bar{e}_{i},~\textit{where}~\bar{e}_{i}\;\coloneqq\;\mathbb{E}_{i}\Big[\tfrac{1}{H}\sum_{t=0}^{H-1}e_{t}\Big]$ 

and $e_{t}\coloneqq e(s_{t},\tilde{s}_{t})$ is the edit count between the learner’s own prefix at decision
 $t$ and the corrected prefix at that decision. A round draws one problem, one rollout of $\pi_{i}$ on
it, and the one correction the projector returns for that rollout; $\mathbb{E}_{i}$ is the average over
that draw.

Proof.

Pair the two distributions decision by decision: the $t$ -th term pairs the learner’s own prefix
 $s_{t}$ with the corrected prefix $\tilde{s}_{t}$ , which differ by $e_{t}$ tokens (Definition 4). By Definition 2 both sides of (7) are
averages of exactly $H$ such terms, so

 $\displaystyle L(d^{\pi_{i}},\pi_{i})$ 
 $\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{H}\sum_{t=0}^{H-1}\mathbb{E}_{i}\,\mathbb{E}_{w\sim\pi_{i}(\cdot\mid s_{t})}\big[\Delta(s_{t},w)\big]$ 

(by Definition 2)

 $\displaystyle\leq\tfrac{1}{H}\sum_{t=0}^{H-1}\mathbb{E}_{i}\Big[\mathbb{E}_{w\sim\pi_{i}(\cdot\mid\tilde{s}_{t})}\big[\Delta(\tilde{s}_{t},w)\big]+\eta\,e_{t}\Big]$ 

(by Assumption 1)

 $\displaystyle=L(\tilde{d}_{i},\pi_{i})+\eta\,\mathbb{E}_{i}\Big[\tfrac{1}{H}\sum_{t=0}^{H-1}e_{t}\Big]$ 

 $\displaystyle=L(\tilde{d}_{i},\pi_{i})+\eta\,\bar{e}_{i}$ 

∎

## J.2.5 How much edited text a rollout carries

Lemma 5 (Edit budget).

Let $y$ be the learner’s rollout in round $i$ and $\tilde{y}$ its correction, each of $H$ decisions by
Definition 2, and let $m=e(y,\tilde{y})$ be the edit size, with $e_{t}$ as in
Lemma 4. Then $e_{t}\leq m$ at every decision
 $0\leq t<H$ , and consequently

 $\sum_{t=0}^{H-1}e_{t}\;\leq\;m\,H.$ 

Proof.

Let $u$ index the tokens of $y$ and $v$ those of $\tilde{y}$ . A monotone matching between them
is a set $\Phi$ of pairs $(u,v)$ with $y_{u}=\tilde{y}_{v}$ , whose order agrees on both sides:
 $u<u^{\prime}\iff v<v^{\prime}$ for distinct $(u,v),(u^{\prime},v^{\prime})\in\Phi$ . The matched tokens read in order form a common
subsequence of $y$ and $\tilde{y}$ , so a largest matching has $|\Phi|=|\mathrm{LCS}(y,\tilde{y})|$ .
Take such a $\Phi$ . Since $y$ and $\tilde{y}$ each have $H$ tokens, $m=2H-2|\Phi|$ by
Definition 4. For a decision $t$ put $\Phi_{t}=\{(u,v)\in\Phi:u\leq t,\ v\leq t\}$ , the pairs
falling inside both prefixes, whose matched tokens form a common subsequence of $y_{1:t}$ and
 $\tilde{y}_{1:t}$ .
Both coordinates increase along $\Phi$ , so $\max(u,v)$ does too, strictly; every pair outside
 $\Phi_{t}$ has $\max(u,v)\geq t+1$ , and distinct pairs take distinct values there, so

 $\big|\Phi\setminus\Phi_{t}\big|\;\leq\;H-t.$ 

(8)

Therefore

 $\displaystyle e_{t}$ 
 $\displaystyle=2t-2\,\big|\mathrm{LCS}(y_{1:t},\tilde{y}_{1:t})\big|$ 

(by Definition 4)

 $\displaystyle\leq 2t-2\,|\Phi_{t}|$ 

( $\Phi_{t}$ is a matching)

 $\displaystyle=2t-2\,|\Phi|+2\,\big|\Phi\setminus\Phi_{t}\big|$ 

 $\displaystyle\leq 2t-2\,|\Phi|+2\,(H-t)$ 

(by (8))

 $\displaystyle=2H-2\,|\Phi|\;=\;m$ 

(by Definition 4)

Summing over the $H$ decisions gives the claim.
∎

An insertion early in the answer shifts every later prefix, and a count of mismatched positions would
read that shift as a rewrite of the whole suffix. Definition 4 charges it at the size of the
insertion instead, which is why the drift of Theorem 1 scales with $m$ and not with $H$ .

## J.2.6 The bound

Theorem 1 of Sec. 5 states that under Definitions 2 and 3, Assumption 1,
an idealized projector and $m$ the mean edit size over the rounds,

 $\min_{i\leq M}\big[J^{\star}-J(\pi_{i})\big]\;\leq\;\underbrace{H\,\Delta_{\max}\,\big(\tilde{\varepsilon}+\tilde{\gamma}_{M}\big)}_{\text{fit and optimization}}\;+\;\underbrace{\eta\,m\,H}_{\text{corrected-prefix drift}}.$ 

Proof of Theorem 1.

 $\displaystyle\min_{i\leq M}[J^{\star}-J(\pi_{i})]$ 
 $\displaystyle\leq\tfrac{1}{M}\sum_{i=1}^{M}[J^{\star}-J(\pi_{i})]$ 

 $\displaystyle\leq\tfrac{H}{M}\sum_{i}L(d^{\pi_{i}},\pi_{i})$ 

(by Lemma 2)

 $\displaystyle\leq\tfrac{H}{M}\sum_{i}L(\tilde{d}_{i},\pi_{i})+\eta H\bar{e}$ 

(by Lemma 4)

 $\displaystyle\leq H\Delta_{\max}\cdot\tfrac{1}{M}\sum_{i}\tilde{\varepsilon}_{i}(\pi_{i})+\eta H\bar{e}$ 

(by Lemma 3)

 $\displaystyle\leq H\Delta_{\max}(\tilde{\varepsilon}+\tilde{\gamma}_{M})+\eta H\bar{e}$ 

(by Definition 5)

 $\displaystyle\leq H\Delta_{\max}(\tilde{\varepsilon}+\tilde{\gamma}_{M})+\eta\,m\,H$ 

(by Lemma 5)

where $\bar{e}=\tfrac{1}{M}\sum_{i}\bar{e}_{i}$ . Lemma 5 bounds round $i$ by its own edit size,
 $H\bar{e}_{i}=\mathbb{E}_{i}\big[\sum_{t=0}^{H-1}e_{t}\big]\leq m_{i}\,H$ , so
 $\bar{e}\leq\tfrac{1}{M}\sum_{i}m_{i}=m$ ; no bound on the largest round is needed.
∎

## J.2.7 Rate in the number of rounds

Proposition 1 (Convex reference rate).

Let $\Pi$ and $\Theta$ be as in Sec. J.1, and suppose in addition that $\theta\mapsto\tilde{\varepsilon}_{i}(\pi_{\theta})$ 
is convex on $\Theta$ for every $i$ , with gradient bound
 $G=\max_{i}\sup_{\theta\in\Theta}\lVert\nabla_{\theta}\tilde{\varepsilon}_{i}(\pi_{\theta})\rVert$ . Then projected online
gradient descent on $\Theta$ with step size $\alpha=D/(G\sqrt{M})$ attains $\tilde{\gamma}_{M}\leq DG/\sqrt{M}$ .

Proof.

Since $\Pi$ is parameterized by $\Theta$ , the minimum over $\pi\in\Pi$ in Definition 5 is a minimum over
 $\theta\in\Theta$ . Write $\theta^{\star}\in\arg\min_{\theta\in\Theta}\sum_{i}\tilde{\varepsilon}_{i}(\pi_{\theta})$ for a minimiser, so that the comparator in the definition and the
comparator in the analysis are the same point. Then, with
 $g_{i}=\nabla_{\theta}\tilde{\varepsilon}_{i}(\pi_{\theta_{i}})$ ,

 $\displaystyle M\tilde{\gamma}_{M}$ 
 $\displaystyle=\sum_{i}\tilde{\varepsilon}_{i}(\pi_{\theta_{i}})-\sum_{i}\tilde{\varepsilon}_{i}(\pi_{\theta^{\star}})$ 

(by Definition 5)

 $\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i}g_{i}^{\top}(\theta_{i}-\theta^{\star})$ 

(convexity)

 $\displaystyle\leq\tfrac{D^{2}}{2\alpha}+\tfrac{\alpha}{2}MG^{2}$ 

(potential $\tfrac{1}{2\alpha}\lVert\theta_{i}-\theta^{\star}\rVert^{2}$ telescopes)

 $\displaystyle\leq DG\sqrt{M}$ 

(at $\alpha=D/(G\sqrt{M})$ )

where $D$ is the Euclidean diameter of $\Theta$ and $G$ the gradient bound.
Dividing by $M$ gives $\tilde{\gamma}_{M}\leq DG/\sqrt{M}$ .
∎

Remark 1 (Scope of the rate).

Proposition 1 is the standard online-convex-optimization guarantee, stated to show how
regret decays in the number of rounds. Its convexity hypothesis does not hold for a neural policy.
Theorem 1 uses only $\tilde{\gamma}_{M}$ as defined, so nothing below depends on
Proposition 1; the $O(1/\sqrt{M})$ form should be read as a convex reference point.

## J.2.8 Reading the bound

Corollary 1 (Edit size affects the horizon dependence).

Under the hypotheses of Theorem 1, the drift term has two extremes:

(a) 

minimal edits, $m=O(1)$ , give drift $O(\eta H)$ ;

(b) 

a correction rewriting the whole answer, $m=\Theta(H)$ , gives drift $O(\eta H^{2})$ .

The quantity being bounded is itself of order $H$ , since the return lies in $[0,H]$ by
Definition 2. Read against that scale, case (a) puts the drift at the same order as the fit
term and the bound is informative, while case (b) puts it an order above the trivial bound
 $J^{\star}-J\leq H$ and the bound says nothing. The horizon dependence is therefore linear at one end
and quadratic at the other, and what selects between them is the edit we choose rather than any
property of the environment.

## J.2.9 Scope of the analysis

Remark 2 (The analysis assumes a dense per-token reward).

Definition 2 makes the reward token-additive with $r_{\mathrm{tok}}\in[0,1]$ , so a rollout’s
return is at most $H$ and the trivial bound on $J^{\star}-J(\pi)$ is $H$ . The experiments verify only
the final answer, a single $0$ – $1$ outcome, under which the trivial bound is $1$ . The drift term
 $\eta mH$ is therefore informative only where $\eta m$ is small: the guarantee is a statement about
the dense-reward model, and the per-token credit it assumes is not what the verifier supplies.

Remark 3 (A statement about horizon dependence, not about algorithm identity).

Setting $e_{t}\equiv 0$ , which corresponds to a projector queried at every mistake on the learner’s own
prefix so that no edited token enters a conditioning prefix, removes the drift and leaves
 $H\Delta_{\max}(\tilde{\varepsilon}+\tilde{\gamma}_{M})$ , the shape of the DAgger guarantee
(Ross et al., 2011). The linear–quadratic dichotomy of Corollary 1 is the same dichotomy
that separates DAgger from behavior cloning. It does not follow that projected distillation
is either algorithm: minimal-edit projection does not query a projector at every visited
state and carries a drift term DAgger does not have, and a full rewrite is not behavior cloning for
the reason in Remark 4. The correspondence is between rates.

Remark 4 (The drift does not vanish under a perfect fit).

In Ross et al. (2011) the quadratic term for behavior cloning scales with the fit error, so a
learner that imitates perfectly suffers no compounding. The drift $\eta mH$ of Theorem 1
carries no factor of $\tilde{\varepsilon}$ . A learner that matches the projector exactly at every
corrected prefix still pays it, because the mismatch between $\tilde{d}_{i}$ and $d^{\pi_{i}}$ is
exogenous, fixed by the edit that was chosen, rather than a consequence of imperfect fitting.

Remark 5 (The analyzed loss is not the deployed loss).

Theorem 1 is proved for the $0$ – $1$ disagreement of Definition 5. Three consequences.

First, no gradient method minimizes a $0$ – $1$ loss. Following Ross et al. (2011) we state
the guarantee for the $0$ – $1$ loss and note that any convex upper bound may be substituted, with
 $\tilde{\varepsilon}$ and $\tilde{\gamma}_{M}$ read as the floor and regret of that surrogate; the bound
holds verbatim with a larger $\tilde{\varepsilon}$ .

Second, cross-entropy on the correction is such a bound. Since $\log p\leq p-1$ for
 $p\in(0,1]$ , we have $1-\pi(w^{\star}\mid\tilde{s})\leq-\log\pi(w^{\star}\mid\tilde{s})$ , so
Theorem 1 covers maximum-likelihood fine-tuning on the projection.

Third, the deployed objective is not covered. The recipe minimizes a diff-localized contrast
on the changed spans together with a self-SFT term on the learner’s own correct rollouts. A
preference contrast is not an upper bound on the disagreement with the projector: it can be reduced
by lowering the likelihood of $y^{-}$ without raising that of $y^{+}$ , so a small contrast loss does not
imply a small $\tilde{\varepsilon}$ . The analysis characterizes the target: how far the
correction lies from the attempt, the quantity the edit-size experiment varies and the one both
objectives share. It does not characterize the contrast.

Corollary 2 (A projector that is not optimal).

Suppose the projector’s token forfeits $\delta(\tilde{s})\coloneqq\Delta(\tilde{s},w^{\star}(\tilde{s}))\geq 0$ 
rather than nothing, and let $\bar{\delta}$ be its average over the corrected prefixes. Then, under the
remaining hypotheses of Theorem 1,

 $\min_{i\leq M}\big[J^{\star}-J(\pi_{i})\big]\;\leq\;H\bar{\delta}\;+\;H\,\Delta_{\max}\,(\tilde{\varepsilon}+\tilde{\gamma}_{M})\;+\;\eta\,m\,H.$ 

Proof.

The term $\pi(w^{\star}\mid\tilde{s})\,\Delta(\tilde{s},w^{\star})$ dropped in Lemma 3 no
longer vanishes, so that lemma reads
 $L(\tilde{d}_{i},\pi)\leq\bar{\delta}+\Delta_{\max}\tilde{\varepsilon}_{i}(\pi)$ . Substituting it at the
Lemma 3 step of Theorem 1 adds $H\bar{\delta}$ , every term there already carrying
the factor $H$ from Lemma 2, and leaves the rest of the chain unchanged.
∎

The two error sources stay separate: $\bar{\delta}$ measures the projector and shrinks only with a
better one, while $\tilde{\varepsilon}$ measures the policy class and shrinks with a larger one.

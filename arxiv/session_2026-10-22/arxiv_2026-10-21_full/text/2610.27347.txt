An LLM-Based Agent for Legal Compliance Assessment: Architecture, Memory, and Evaluation in a Canadian and Quebec Context

Soukaina Alaoui Ismaili and Jean-Sébastien Dessureault

Department of Electrical and Computer Engineering

Université du Québec à Trois-Rivières (UQTR), Trois-Rivières, Québec, Canada

Soukaina.Alaoui.Ismaili@uqtr.ca, Jean-Sebastien.Dessureault@uqtr.ca

October 2026

Keywords: legal compliance, large language models, agent memory, AI governance, Canadian and Quebec law, evaluation.

Abstract

Checking whether a text complies with a body of law is slow when done by hand and fragile when done with keyword rules. This paper presents ComplianceAgent, the legal compliance sub-agent of COMPASS, a multi-agent framework that evaluates texts along four axes: digital sovereignty, carbon impact, compliance, and ethics. The agent relies on a large language model that scores a text on a fixed rubric, grounds its judgment in a reference legal corpus, and, when the score falls at or below a critical threshold, runs a corrective chain made of self-healing rewrites, a compliance label, an explainability report, and escalation to a human reviewer. We describe what the language model does at each step, how the memory of the agent is organized, and how the same implementation can be pointed at different reference corpora. We evaluate the agent in a Canadian and Quebec context, on a corpus of thirteen legal instruments that brings together federal and provincial texts, international treaties, and municipal documents of the City of Trois-Rivières. The evaluation relies on a hand-annotated gold standard of fifteen texts and combines classification metrics, a repeated-query stability test, retrieval precision, and a composite score. On this gold standard the agent separates every conforming text from every non-conforming one, its scores remain stable under repeated queries, and the index returns relevant passages.

## 1 Introduction

Organizations that build or deploy automated systems are increasingly asked to show that their practices respect the rules that apply to them. In Canada and Quebec these rules come from several levels at once: federal statutes such as the Personal Information Protection and Electronic Documents Act (Government of Canada, 2000), provincial statutes such as Quebec’s Law 25 (Gouvernement du Québec, 2021), municipal policies and by-laws, and the international treaties that frame them. Voluntary frameworks such as the NIST AI Risk Management Framework (National Institute of Standards and Technology, 2023) and management system standards such as ISO/IEC 42001 (International Organization for Standardization, 2023) add further expectations. All of this turns into documents, statements, and policies that someone has to read and judge. Doing so by hand does not scale with the volume of text, and rule-based checkers break as soon as a statement does not match a fixed pattern. A language model that can read a statement in context and compare it with the relevant provisions offers a practical middle path, provided that its judgment can be tied to the source text and explained to a human reader.

Using a language model in this role raises questions that are rarely answered together. What exactly does the model decide, and what is left to the surrounding code? What does the system remember, and what does it retrieve at the moment of judgment? How should its output be evaluated when no accepted benchmark exists for the legal context of interest? Work on legal language processing tends to evaluate models on reasoning tasks, and work on language agents tends to describe memory and refinement loops in general terms. An agent that scores, corrects, explains, and escalates over a given legal corpus is seldom documented in enough detail to be reproduced and criticized. We organize the study around three research questions. The first concerns design: how can the roles of the model, of retrieval, and of memory be organized in a compliance agent so that each can be described and tested? The second concerns performance: how well does the agent separate conforming from non-conforming texts in a Canadian and Quebec legal corpus, and how stable are its scores? The third concerns evidence: what can a small evaluation made by a single annotator actually support?

This paper describes ComplianceAgent, the compliance sub-agent (COM) of COMPASS, a research framework (Dessureault et al., 2026) that evaluates a text along four independent axes: digital sovereignty (SOV), carbon impact (CAR), regulatory compliance (COM), and ethics (ETH). Each axis is handled by a dedicated sub-agent built on a shared base class, and an orchestrator sends a text to the relevant sub-agents, either on explicit request or through a filter that is itself a language model call. The work reported here concerns the compliance axis only. The other sub-agents are developed by other members of the project and fall outside the scope of this paper.

The agent is configurable by domain, which means that the reference corpus is a parameter and not a path written into the code. In this study it is pointed at a corpus of thirteen Canadian, Quebec, municipal, and international legal instruments. This paper makes three contributions. First, we document the architecture precisely enough for a reader to know what the language model does, what each kind of memory holds, and what the retrieval step does and does not contribute. Second, we define the evaluation metrics mathematically and apply them to a hand-annotated gold standard. Third, we report what the evaluation shows and, just as importantly, what it cannot show given its size and design.

The rest of the paper follows the usual structure of a scientific article. Section 2 reviews the state of the art. Section 3 presents the methodology, namely the architecture of the agent, the language model and its prompts, the data, and the evaluation protocol and metrics. Section 4 reports the results. Section 5 discusses them, with their limits and the perspectives they open, and Section 6 concludes.

## 2 State of the Art

Retrieval-augmented generation.

Retrieval-augmented generation (Lewis et al., 2020) addresses the fact that the knowledge stored in a model’s parameters is static and sometimes wrong, by conditioning generation on passages fetched from an external corpus. Surveys such as Gao et al. (2023) describe the many ways in which indexing, retrieval, and prompting can be combined. In legal settings the cost of ungrounded output is high: Dahl et al. (2024) show that general-purpose models hallucinate frequently on legal queries, which is one reason to tie a compliance judgment to a retrievable source. Our retrieval layer uses MPNet-based sentence embeddings (Song et al., 2020; Reimers and Gurevych, 2019), a persistent open-source vector store (Chroma, 2023), and a composable application framework (LangChain, Inc., 2023).

Language models as evaluators.

Using a language model to grade text against a rubric has been studied under the name LLM-as-a-judge (Zheng et al., 2023). That line of work mostly concerns open-ended generation quality and pairwise preference. ComplianceAgent applies the same principle to a narrower task: it scores one text against a defined body of law with a fixed rubric, and the result is compared with a human annotation. This framing makes ordinary classification metrics applicable, in contrast with open-ended judgment where no gold label exists.

Legal language processing.

Domain-specific pretrained models such as LEGAL-BERT (Chalkidis et al., 2020) and benchmarks such as LegalBench (Guha et al., 2023) evaluate legal reasoning on tasks like classification, rule application, and issue spotting. They do not target the end-to-end behavior of an agent that retrieves, scores, rewrites, and escalates, which is the object of study here.

Agent memory and iterative refinement.

The cognitive architecture for language agents proposed by Sumers et al. (2024) distinguishes working memory from long-term stores, themselves divided into episodic, semantic, and procedural memory. We use this vocabulary in Section 3.1.5 to say plainly what our agent has and does not have. Our self-healing loop belongs to the family of iterative refinement methods, such as Self-Refine (Madaan et al., 2023) and Reflexion (Shinn et al., 2023), in which a model’s output is critiqued and revised. In our case the critique is the explanation produced by the scoring step and the stopping test is the compliance threshold. The compliance label we produce plays a role comparable to a model card (Mitchell et al., 2019): a short structured document that states what was evaluated, with what outcome, and on what sources.

Positioning.

Taken together, these lines of work provide the components of our agent: retrieval over a corpus, a model that grades against a rubric, a memory vocabulary, and iterative refinement. What they leave open is how these components are assembled into one compliance agent, what each of them contributes at the moment of judgment, and how such an agent behaves on a Canadian and Quebec legal corpus.

## 3 Methodology

This section describes how the agent is built and how it is evaluated. We first present its architecture, then the language model and its prompts, the data used for the evaluation, and finally the evaluation protocol and the metrics.

## 3.1 System Architecture

## 3.1.1 The COMPASS Framework

COMPASS was introduced by Dessureault et al. (2026). It is built from four sub-agents that inherit from a common SubAgent base class. The base class provides what all sub-agents share: reading prompt templates from disk, calling the language model, extracting a JSON object from the model’s reply, and building a vector index from a PDF. An orchestrator, COMPASS_Agent, instantiates the four sub-agents and sends an incoming text to those that are relevant. The compliance sub-agent is the object of this paper.

The version of the framework used here adds a feature that was not part of the initial paper: a filter. Before any sub-agent runs, the orchestrator questions a local language model about the subject of the input text, with a dedicated prompt that lists the four modules and asks for a bare list of module keys. The model decides which sub-agents to call: one, several, or none. The reply is parsed as a list, keys that do not name an existing sub-agent are discarded, and a reply that cannot be parsed selects no sub-agent. Only the selected sub-agents then analyze the text, which avoids calling all of them on every input and keeps the calls to the relevant ones. A caller can bypass the filter by naming a sub-agent explicitly. Figure 1 shows the resulting architecture. The filter is described here for completeness, but it is not evaluated in this paper, whose experiments address the compliance sub-agent alone.

## 3.1.2 The ComplianceAgent Sub-Agent

ComplianceAgent exposes two operations. The method analyze $(x,\text{rag})$ takes a text $x$ and returns a score $s\in[0,1]$ together with a short explanation. When the model’s reply cannot be parsed, or when the model answers that the information is insufficient, no score is returned and the code stores the value None. The method act $(x)$ rescores the text and, if $s\leq\tau$ with the critical threshold $\tau=0.80$ , triggers the corrective chain of Section 3.1.6. This two-level design deliberately mirrors the ethics sub-agent of the same framework, so that the user meets the same interaction pattern on every axis.

## 3.1.3 The Role of the Language Model

No step of the agent is implemented by hand-written scoring logic. A single general-purpose language model is called with a different instruction at each step, and the instructions live in prompt files (Table 1). The model acts as a scorer in analyze, as an editor in self_healing, as a technical writer in compliance_label and explainability, and as a case officer in human_in_the_loop. One further call, executed once when the agent is constructed, makes the model a summarizer of the reference corpus (Section 3.1.5).

Only the scoring step has a strict output contract. Its prompt demands a JSON object with the keys score and explanation, and the base class extracts the substring between the first opening brace and the last closing brace and parses it. If parsing fails, or if the model answers N/A, no score is returned and the explanation carries an error message. The other steps return free text. Because the score is the only quantity on which the control flow depends, this single contract is enough to keep the pipeline composable.

## 3.1.4 Retrieval Pipeline

Indexing is done offline, once per domain. Each source PDF is parsed page by page and split with a recursive character splitter into chunks of 800 characters with an overlap of 150 characters. Each chunk is embedded with the all-mpnet-base-v2 sentence encoder and stored in a persistent Chroma collection. The thirteen instruments of the corpus yield 847 chunks. Similarity between a query embedding $\mathbf{q}$ and a chunk embedding $\mathbf{d}$ is the cosine

 $\mathrm{sim}(\mathbf{q},\mathbf{d})=\frac{\mathbf{q}^{\top}\mathbf{d}}{\lVert\mathbf{q}\rVert\,\lVert\mathbf{d}\rVert}.$ 

(1)

At analysis time, the text under evaluation is not used as a retrieval query. The context injected into the scoring prompt is the distilled summary $m$ described in Section 3.1.5, which is the same for every text. The only place where a text is embedded and matched against the index is compliance_label, which retrieves its three nearest chunks and passes the first 300 characters of each to the labeling prompt. The retrieval quality measured in Section 4 is therefore the quality of the index queried directly, and not that of a per-text retrieval inside the scoring step.

Semantic memory.

The persisted vector index is the store of knowledge of the agent about the reference corpus. On top of it, the constructor computes a distilled form. It retrieves the three chunks nearest to a fixed query, whose wording is “what are the key points of this document?”, and asks the model, with a dedicated system prompt, to extract the most useful information from them. Formally, if $q_{0}$ is that fixed query and $d^{(1)},d^{(2)},d^{(3)}$ are the chunks with the highest similarity to it under Equation 1, then

 $m=\mathrm{LLM}\bigl(S_{\mathrm{rag}},\;d^{(1)}\,\|\,d^{(2)}\,\|\,d^{(3)}\bigr),$ 

(2)

where $S_{\mathrm{rag}}$ is the summarization system prompt and the double bar denotes concatenation. The summary $m$ is stored in self.long_term_memory and never updated. Every later scoring call receives the same $m$ . This design is cheap, since it costs one model call per lifetime of the agent, but it also means that the context seen by the model is generic. It is not specific to the provision that a given text concerns.

Working memory.

State that lives for the duration of a call consists of the most recent score, the text currently being rewritten, and the attempt counter of the self-healing loop. The loop rescores the current version of the text at each iteration and not the original one, so that the stopping test reflects the state of the correction. The score attribute is shared across calls, which means that an agent object should not be shared between concurrent requests.

Procedural memory.

The behavior of the agent is largely fixed by its prompt files. Each domain has its own directory of twelve templates: scoring (system and user), corpus summarization (system and user), and one system prompt and one user prompt for each of the four corrective steps. Changing the corpus therefore means changing documents and prompts, not code.

No episodic memory.

The agent does not remember previous cases. Each text is judged on its own, and the escalation record, which contains the score, a case summary, a priority, and the status pending_human_review, is returned to the caller and printed. Persisting it to a queue is left as future work in the code.

## 3.1.6 The Corrective Action Chain

When a text scores at or below $\tau$ , act runs four steps, shown in Figure 2.

Self-healing.

The loop runs for at most $r=3$ iterations. Each iteration scores the current text, and if the score exceeds $\tau$ the loop stops. Otherwise the model receives the current text together with the explanation of the failure and returns a rewrite, which becomes the current text. If three rewrites were produced and the latest recorded score is still at or below $\tau$ , the case is marked as escalated.

Compliance label.

The final text, rescored once more after the loop, is passed to a labeling step that retrieves three source excerpts and asks the model for a short structured summary: the dimensions evaluated, the score, a confidence level, and the sources.

Explainability.

A separate step asks for a four-part justification aimed at a non-specialist reader, covering what was evaluated, the relevant legal points, the reasoning behind the score, and the conclusion with its practical consequences.

Human escalation.

If the case was marked as escalated, the model drafts a case summary, and a priority is assigned by a simple rule on the last recorded score $s_{\mathrm{last}}$ :

 $\mathrm{priority}=\begin{cases}\text{high}&\text{if }s_{\mathrm{last}}<\tau/2,\\
\text{medium}&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}$ 

(3)

With $\tau=0.80$ the boundary is $0.40$ .

Cost.

Because each step is a model call, the cost of act is bounded. A text that passes costs a single call. A flagged text costs between 5 and 11 calls: one scoring call in act, one to six calls inside the loop, made of a scoring call per iteration plus one rewrite per failed iteration, then a final scoring call, the label, the explanation, and, in the worst case, the escalation note. The worst case is $1+6+4=11$ . The first scoring call inside the loop repeats the one made by act on the same text, which is a small redundancy that could be removed.

## 3.1.7 Domain Configurability

The constructor takes a domain argument that selects the reference corpus. For a given domain, the agent looks for its prompts, its persisted index, and its documents in a subdirectory of a single compliance directory, for example compliance/legal/. This keeps every corpus under one owned location of the project. If the domain also contains a data/ directory of individual PDFs, each file is chunked separately and all chunks are merged into one index. The corpus of this study uses this mechanism to index thirteen instruments one by one, so that each one stays identifiable as a source. A domain without such a directory falls back to a single reference PDF. Other corpora can be added in the same way, without modifying the logic of the agent.

## 3.2 Language Model and Prompting

## 3.2.1 Language Model Backend

All model calls go through a thin adapter to a hosted inference service (Groq, Inc., 2024), which serves the open-weight model openai/gpt-oss-120b (OpenAI, 2025). The adapter issues plain HTTP chat-completion requests, with temperature $0.2$ and a cap of 512 generated tokens, and exposes the same generate interface as the local model adapter of the framework, so the agent code does not depend on the provider. The model identifier is read from configuration. This turned out to matter in practice: the model used at the start of the development was withdrawn by its provider, and the agent was redirected to the current one by changing one configuration value, without touching any prompt.

A hosted service enforces rate limits, and a flagged text can trigger up to 11 calls in a row. When the service answers with an HTTP 429 status, the adapter waits and retries, up to five times. When the service provides a Retry-After header, the adapter waits for the duration it gives. Otherwise it applies an exponential backoff capped at 30 seconds, so that the waiting time before the $i$ -th retry is

 $w_{i}=\min\bigl(2^{\,i-1},\,30\bigr)\ \text{seconds},\qquad i=1,\dots,5.$ 

(4)

The five waiting times are therefore 1, 2, 4, 8, and 16 seconds.

## 3.2.2 Prompting Scheme

The scoring prompt consists of a fixed system prompt $S$ and a user template $U$ with two placeholders. Writing $x$ for the text and $m$ for the corpus summary, the user prompt is

 $U(x)=U\bigl[\{\mathrm{RAG1}\}\mapsto m,\;\{\mathrm{keyword1}\}\mapsto x\bigr],\qquad s=\pi\bigl(\mathrm{LLM}(S,U(x))\bigr),$ 

(5)

where $\pi$ is the JSON extraction described in Section 3.1.3. With retrieval switched off, $m$ is replaced by the string *No RAG*.

The system prompt of the legal domain presents the model as an expert in international legal compliance covering international law, fundamental rights, data protection, international trade, and sanctions. It anchors the scale on five reference values defined by the violations found in the text: $1.0$ for a perfectly conforming text with no identifiable violation and principles explicitly respected, $0.75$ for a mostly conforming text with minor gaps or imprecisions, $0.5$ for partial compliance where positive elements coexist with clear breaches, $0.25$ for a mostly non-conforming text with a significant violation and some mitigating elements, and $0$ for a grave violation such as an explicit rejection of international law or fundamental rights, or the use of disproportionate coercive measures. It also asks for intermediate values, with examples such as $0.95$ , $0.53$ , and $0.20$ , and forbids assuming any earlier evaluation. The prompts are written in French, the model is told to answer in French, and the texts to evaluate are in French. This choice follows from the local setting of the project: in Quebec, the documents to be checked are written in French, and the thirteen reference documents are French versions as well. The rest of the framework, including the ethics sub-agent, is written in English from end to end, so the use of French in this sub-agent is a deliberate adaptation to the Quebec context and not a property of the framework.

Five queries were written with lists of relevant keywords. They cover digital trade, the Paris Agreement, the United Nations Charter, the protection of the family and fundamental rights, and the protection of personal information. They are written in French, except the first, which is in English.

## 3.4 Evaluation Protocol and Metrics

## 3.4.1 Protocol

The evaluation is organized in five blocks, implemented in a notebook. The first block loads the agent and the gold standard. The second runs the stability test, in which the three texts C1, V1, and M1 are each scored five times. The third scores each of the fifteen texts once and computes the metrics of Section 3.4.2 at $\tau=0.80$ . The fourth runs the retrieval queries against the index. The fifth computes the composite score and exports a JSON report. Calls are spaced by one second, and the temperature is $0.2$ throughout. The results below come from the archived report dated 16 September 2026. Only the scoring step is evaluated here. The corrective chain of act is described but not scored, since evaluating rewrites would require a second gold standard.

## 3.4.2 Decision Rule and Classification Metrics

Let the gold standard contain $N$ texts, each with a label $y_{i}\in\{0,1\}$ where $y_{i}=1$ means non-conforming, and let $s_{i}$ be the score returned for text $i$ . The predicted label is

 $\hat{y}_{i}=\begin{cases}1&\text{if }s_{i}\leq\tau\text{ or no score was returned},\\
0&\text{otherwise,}\end{cases}$ 

(6)

so that a failed analysis counts as flagged, which is the conservative choice for a compliance tool. From the counts of true positives $\mathrm{TP}$ , false positives $\mathrm{FP}$ , true negatives $\mathrm{TN}$ , and false negatives $\mathrm{FN}$ , we compute

 $P=\frac{\mathrm{TP}}{\mathrm{TP}+\mathrm{FP}},\quad R=\frac{\mathrm{TP}}{\mathrm{TP}+\mathrm{FN}},\quad F_{1}=\frac{2PR}{P+R},\quad\mathrm{Acc}=\frac{\mathrm{TP}+\mathrm{TN}}{N},\quad\mathrm{Spec}=\frac{\mathrm{TN}}{\mathrm{TN}+\mathrm{FP}}.$ 

(7)

Each text also carries an expected score interval $[\ell_{i},u_{i}]$ chosen by the annotator, and the calibration rate is the fraction of scores that fall inside it,

 $\kappa=\frac{\bigl|\{\,i:\ell_{i}\leq s_{i}\leq u_{i}\,\}\bigr|}{N}.$ 

(8)

With so few texts, point estimates are fragile. We therefore attach Wilson score intervals (Wilson, 1927) to proportions. For $k$ successes out of $n$ , with $\hat{p}=k/n$ and $z=1.96$ , the interval is

 $\frac{\hat{p}+\dfrac{z^{2}}{2n}\pm z\sqrt{\dfrac{\hat{p}(1-\hat{p})}{n}+\dfrac{z^{2}}{4n^{2}}}}{1+\dfrac{z^{2}}{n}}.$ 

(9)

## 3.4.3 Stability

Because the model is sampled at a nonzero temperature, the same text can receive different scores. For a representative text $j$ we submit it $R=5$ times and compute the sample standard deviation

 $\sigma_{j}=\sqrt{\frac{1}{R-1}\sum_{r=1}^{R}\bigl(s_{jr}-\bar{s}_{j}\bigr)^{2}},\qquad\bar{\sigma}=\frac{1}{J}\sum_{j=1}^{J}\sigma_{j},$ 

(10)

over the $J=3$ test texts. The agent is deemed stable if $\bar{\sigma}<0.10$ , a limit that we fixed in advance.

## 3.4.4 Retrieval Quality

For a query $q$ we take the three chunks nearest to it in the index and mark each as relevant if its lowercased text contains at least one keyword from a list written for that query. With $\mathrm{rel}_{k}(q)$ equal to $1$ if the $k$ -th passage is relevant and $0$ otherwise, precision at three and its mean over $Q$ queries are

 $\mathrm{P@3}(q)=\frac{1}{3}\sum_{k=1}^{3}\mathrm{rel}_{k}(q),\qquad\overline{\mathrm{P@3}}=\frac{1}{Q}\sum_{q}\mathrm{P@3}(q).$ 

(11)

## 3.4.5 Composite Score

We combine the four blocks into a single number with weights that we fixed in advance and did not fit to the results,

 $S=0.40\,F_{1}+0.25\,\bigl(1-5\bar{\sigma}\bigr)+0.20\,\overline{\mathrm{P@3}}+0.15\,\kappa,$ 

(12)

clipped to $[0,1]$ . The factor $5$ maps the stability limit $\bar{\sigma}=0.10$ to half of the range of the stability term. The composite is a convenience for comparing runs. It is not a validated measure, and we report its components alongside it.

## 3.4.6 Supplementary Analyses

Two analyses were added after seeing the first results, and we label them as post hoc. The first is the area under the ROC curve (Fawcett, 2006), which does not depend on $\tau$ and measures only whether non-conforming texts receive lower scores than conforming ones. With $\mathcal{P}$ the set of non-conforming texts and $\mathcal{N}$ the set of conforming ones, it is the average over all pairs $(p,n)$ of the pair score $c(p,n)$ ,

 $\mathrm{AUC}=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{P}|\,|\mathcal{N}|}\sum_{p\in\mathcal{P}}\sum_{n\in\mathcal{N}}c(p,n),\qquad c(p,n)=\begin{cases}1&\text{if }s_{n}>s_{p},\\
1/2&\text{if }s_{n}=s_{p},\\
0&\text{if }s_{n}<s_{p}.\end{cases}$ 

(13)

The second is a sweep of the threshold $\tau$ from $0.05$ to $0.95$ in steps of $0.05$ , recomputing Equation 7 at each value, to see how sensitive the decision is to the one parameter that was set in advance.

## 5 Discussion and Perspectives

## 5.1 What the Results Say

On this gold standard the agent separates the two classes without error and with a wide margin. Conforming texts score above $0.90$ and the others at or below $0.05$ . The agent is also stable, since five repetitions of the same text spread by at most $0.07$ for the conforming text and by $0.05$ for the others. Together with a retrieval step that returns on-topic passages, this shows that the pipeline works as designed on the cases it was tested on: the model produces parsable scores, the threshold rule flags the right texts, and nothing in the control flow breaks.

Answers to the research questions.

On design, the architecture separates the roles of the model, of the index, and of the working state, and the single JSON contract on the score keeps the pipeline testable. The generic corpus summary shows, however, that the contribution of retrieval at the moment of judgment is limited. On performance, the agent separated every conforming text from every non-conforming one on this gold standard, with stable scores. On evidence, fifteen texts, one annotator, and blunt violations support the claim that the pipeline works, but not a measure of accuracy in realistic conditions.

## 5.2 How Far These Results Go

The perfect scores should not be read as a measure of accuracy in realistic conditions, and we want to say why. First, the non-conforming statements are blunt. Most of them declare outright that international law or fundamental rights do not apply, or announce coercive measures, so that a language model may recognize them without consulting the corpus at all. Second, the conforming statements are uniformly positive, and several of them name instruments that belong to the corpus, such as the United Nations Charter, the Paris Agreement, Law 25, and PIPEDA, which is a strong cue. Third, the states are fictional and the texts are short. Fourth, the scale is used as a binary one. The intermediate anchors $0.25$ , $0.5$ , and $0.75$ are never used: every observed score is either at most $0.05$ or at least $0.88$ . We therefore have no evidence on how the agent grades a partially conforming text, or a statement that is ambiguous, which are the cases where a compliance tool is the most needed. Finally, the evaluation includes no baseline without retrieval, although the agent supports one, so we cannot attribute any part of the result to retrieval.

## 5.3 Limits of the Evaluation

The conclusions are bounded by several limitations. The gold standard contains fifteen texts, so the intervals are wide, and a perfect score is compatible with an accuracy near $0.80$ . We assigned the labels and the expected score intervals ourselves, with no independent annotator, so we have no measure of inter-annotator agreement, and the calibration rate is partly circular. The stability test covers three texts only, and one model, hosted by a third party, was used throughout, whose version may change. The relevance judgments for retrieval are keyword based and lenient, as discussed. The evaluation covers a single corpus and a single formulation of the rubric, and we make no claim that the results carry over to other regulatory corpora.

Two points concern the implementation. The escalation flag of the self-healing loop is computed from the last score recorded before the final rewrite, so a final rewrite that would have passed is still marked as escalated, and the first scoring call of the loop duplicates the one made by act. Neither affects the metrics of this paper, since only the scoring step was evaluated, but both should be corrected before the corrective chain is itself evaluated.

## 5.4 Perspectives

The validation presented here is enough to show that the pipeline works, but a journal version needs a stronger one. The test data should be more numerous, documented, and include borderline cases, and annotation by several people would allow agreement to be measured. Stability runs on more texts and an ablation without retrieval would complete it. Further ablations should compare the current design with a per-text retrieval and with a summary built from several queries, and real documents such as municipal and provincial policies could be added to the texts. An embedding model suited to French, and a chunking that follows the structure of articles, could improve the retrieval step. The corrective chain needs its own evaluation, including whether a rewrite preserves the meaning of the original text. Finally, an episodic memory that stores the decisions of reviewers on escalated cases would let the agent adjust to the practice of the people who use it. A separate study should then apply the method in a real setting, with the documents of the City of Trois-Rivières and the work carried out with it, as an applied study of the compliance sub-agent.

## 6 Conclusion

We presented ComplianceAgent, a compliance sub-agent for COMPASS in which one language model scores, rewrites, labels, explains, and escalates, supported by a persisted vector index and a one-time corpus summary, and configurable by domain. We described what the retrieval step does and does not do at analysis time, we mapped the memory of the agent to a standard taxonomy while noting what it lacks, and we defined the evaluation metrics. On a corpus of thirteen Canadian, Quebec, municipal, and international instruments, the agent classified fifteen of fifteen annotated texts correctly with stable scores. The size and the bluntness of the gold standard limit what this result proves, and the next step is a larger and better documented evaluation, with borderline texts and annotation by several people, followed by an applied study in a real setting with the City of Trois-Rivières. The evaluation notebook, the prompts, and the JSON report are part of the project and are available from us on request.

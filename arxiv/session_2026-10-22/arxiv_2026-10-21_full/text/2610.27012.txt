Abstract

Reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards (RLVR) trains a language model by sampling responses, scoring them with an automatic verifier, and updating the policy from the resulting rewards. The policy therefore receives direct learning signals only from trajectories that appear in its rollouts. Yet repeated sampling from the same policy can concentrate on similar reasoning paths, leaving useful alternatives unexplored under a fixed rollout budget. We introduce Activation-Space Exploration (ASE), an online method for diversifying training rollouts. By clustering activation differences between correct and incorrect responses to the same problem, ASE builds a structured bank of latent exploration vectors. Different vectors are then applied to subsequent rollouts to explore alternative reasoning paths. On Qwen3-1.7B-Base and Qwen3-8B-Base trained on DAPO-Math-17k, ASE improves average Mean@8 across six mathematical reasoning benchmarks over GRPO by 4.87 and 1.87 percentage points, respectively, under the same rollout budget.

## 1 Introduction

Reinforcement learning improves a model by repeatedly sampling behaviors, evaluating their outcomes with rewards, and increasing the probability of behaviors that receive higher rewards (Sutton and Barto, 2018). Reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards (RLVR) makes this process scalable by using automated verifiers to assign outcome-based rewards to generated responses, such as mathematical solutions and programs (Le et al., 2022; Shao et al., 2024; Yu et al., 2025). A fundamental constraint, however, is that the policy can only learn from behaviors that appear in its sampled rollouts (Shao et al., 2024; Yue et al., 2025). Consequently, policy optimization is shaped by which trajectories the rollout procedure makes available for learning, a dependence that creates an exploration bottleneck under a finite rollout budget. Standard GRPO independently samples multiple trajectories from the same current policy (Shao et al., 2024). Increasing the number of rollouts provides more Monte Carlo samples, but leaves the underlying proposal distribution unchanged. As the policy concentrates probability mass on a limited set of reasoning patterns, multiple samples may repeatedly visit similar solution modes, reproduce the same intermediate steps, or make correlated errors. Useful alternatives that occupy low-probability regions of the policy can therefore remain rarely observed and receive little direct reward feedback. The bottleneck is thus not merely how many trajectories are sampled, but how a fixed rollout budget is distributed across the policy’s reasoning space.

Several approaches seek to use a finite RLVR rollout budget more effectively. Adaptive sampling allocates more rollouts to difficult problems (Liao et al., 2025), while data filtering and curricula prioritize more informative training problems (Yu et al., 2025; Chen et al., 2025b). These methods change where sampling effort is spent, rather than how trajectories for the same problem are generated. Adjusting decoding temperature increases token-level variation (Dang et al., 2026), but does not specify which alternative reasoning patterns to explore. Replay reuses previously observed trajectories (Li et al., 2025), while novelty rewards and high-entropy prefix regeneration diversify generation around observed paths (Hu et al., 2026). Together, these methods improve how rollouts are allocated, change how randomly tokens are drawn, and reuse previously generated trajectories. They leave the model’s internal representations largely unexplored as a way to construct different rollout proposals for the same prompt.

We investigate activation-space exploration, where temporary interventions on hidden representations are used to construct multiple rollout proposal distributions while leaving both the problem context and model parameters unchanged. Activation steering has shown that directional interventions on Transformer representations can systematically alter model behavior and generation trajectories (Beaglehole et al., 2026; Chen et al., 2025a). Rather than using such interventions as inference-time behavior controllers, we ask whether they can serve as a training-time exploration mechanism: different latent directions can perturb generation toward different regions of the policy’s reasoning space, exposing the optimizer to useful trajectories that ordinary sampling may rarely encounter.

Based on this idea, we propose Activation-Space Exploration (ASE), an online exploration mechanism for RLVR that learns where to explore from the policy’s own verifier results. For each problem, successful and unsuccessful trajectories generated during training provide within-problem contrasts that isolate activation differences associated with task outcome while controlling for problem content. We find that the activation contrasts exhibit heterogeneous structure; we therefore cluster and aggregate them into multiple exploration directions maintained in an online vector bank. During subsequent rollout generation, different trajectories for the same problem are routed through different latent directions, producing a collection of steering-conditioned proposal distributions rather than repeatedly sampling from a single one (Figure 1). Stochastic decoding is retained within every route, so ASE complements rather than replaces ordinary token sampling. Verifier rewards from the steered rollouts drive both subsequent exploration updates and optimization of the base policy, leaving evaluation and deployment intervention-free.

Figure 1: Motivation and high-level overview of activation-space exploration. Standard GRPO samples all responses from one proposal distribution, whereas ASE uses outcome feedback to maintain multiple activation-space directions, routes responses through different proposals, and updates the actor and vector bank online.

We evaluate ASE on Qwen3-1.7B-Base (Qwen Team, 2025) and Qwen3-8B-Base (Qwen Team, 2025) using DAPO-Math-17k (Yu et al., 2025) under the same rollout budget as standard GRPO. All activation interventions are disabled during evaluation. Across six mathematical reasoning benchmarks, ASE improves average Mean@8 over GRPO by 4.87 percentage points on Qwen3-1.7B-Base and 1.87 points on Qwen3-8B-Base, while also transferring to out-of-distribution reasoning benchmarks. These results show that activation-space exploration during training can improve the learned policy across model scales and benchmark distributions under a fixed rollout budget.

## 2 Related Work

## Reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards.

RLVR is now a major paradigm for improving mathematical and code reasoning in language models. Shao et al. (2024) introduced GRPO, which constructs policy-gradient signals from relative rewards among multiple trajectories for the same problem; subsequent work studied the stability and efficiency of scaling this formulation (Yu et al., 2025). RLVR gains depend strongly on trajectories collected by the current policy. Policy optimization may mostly redistribute probability mass toward high-reward paths already available to the base model and can reduce solution-space coverage under large-scale sampling (Yue et al., 2025); policy entropy can also decrease continuously and eventually limit further exploration (Cui et al., 2025). At the token level, a small number of high-entropy branching positions have a disproportionate effect on reasoning paths (Wang et al., 2025). Recent methods encourage flatter distributions and answer diversity in the objective (Li et al., 2026), or combine trajectory novelty rewards with high-entropy prefix regeneration to discover different correct solutions (Hu et al., 2026). RePO retrieves historical off-policy trajectories from a replay buffer and estimates their advantages separately from current on-policy rollouts (Li et al., 2025); GRESO uses reward dynamics across epochs to probabilistically skip prompts expected to remain zero-variance (Zheng et al., 2025b). These methods improve exploration through objectives, reward shaping, explicit trajectory operations, or data scheduling. We instead use historical rollout outcomes to construct multiple directional proposals in activation space, directly changing the sampling distribution of later training trajectories without an additional diversity reward or a modified GRPO objective.

## Activation steering.

Activation steering builds on the observation that high-level behaviors have controllable directions in language-model representations. Representation engineering and inference-time intervention show that identifying and modifying intermediate representations can change truthfulness, safety, and other behaviors (Zou et al., 2023; Li et al., 2023). More systematic studies have verified transferable and compositional linear concept representations across hundreds of concepts in language, vision-language, and reasoning models, and demonstrated their use for steering and monitoring (Beaglehole et al., 2026). Activation Addition (ActAdd) constructs a direction from activation differences of contrastive prompts (Turner et al., 2023); Contrastive Activation Addition (CAA) pairs examples with opposite behavior labels, averages residual-stream differences, and adds the result during generation (Rimsky et al., 2024). Later work extends this idea to instruction following (Stolfo et al., 2025) and reasoning behaviors such as uncertainty, backtracking, and hypothesis testing (Venhoff et al., 2025). Other methods automatically construct steering vectors from labeled data (Cui and Chen, 2026), or optimize a small number of trainable vectors with RL so that they carry reasoning gains (Sinii et al., 2026). These methods generally treat a steering vector as a behavior controller applied at inference time and rely on predefined attributes, externally constructed contrast sets, or optimization of the vector itself. We reposition activation steering as a temporary training-time exploration mechanism: directions are estimated online from success and failure trajectories of the current policy, several directions form heterogeneous rollout proposals, and GRPO absorbs useful behaviors into the base policy parameters. The vector bank is discarded after training.

## 3 Method

## 3.1 Overview

Let $x\sim\mathcal{D}$ be a problem and let $\pi_{\theta}$ be a language-model policy. For a fixed $x$ , standard GRPO independently samples $N$ trajectories:

 $y_{i}^{(t)}\sim\pi_{\theta^{(t)}}(\cdot\mid x),\qquad i=1,\ldots,N.$ 

(1)

With a fixed policy and sampling configuration, all trajectories are generated under the same proposal distribution. Increasing $N$ gives more Monte Carlo samples while leaving the proposal distribution unchanged. We use historical rollout trajectories to construct structured activation-space exploration directions.

These exploration directions form a steering vector bank that is updated across iterations. At iteration $t$ ,

 $\mathcal{V}^{(t)}=\{\mathbf{v}_{k}^{(t)}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\}_{k=1}^{K},\qquad\|\mathbf{v}_{k}^{(t)}\|_{2}=1.$ 

(2)

Here $d$ is the hidden dimension of the target Transformer block and $K$ is the number of exploration routes. Each vector is a smoothed direction obtained from mean activation differences between correct and incorrect trajectories in a cluster. The vector bank is a non-parametric training state independent of actor parameters and is estimated directly from historical rollouts.

During rollout, trajectories for the same problem are assigned different routes. For route $s_{i}\in\{1,\ldots,K\}$ , we add $\alpha\mathbf{v}_{s_{i}}^{(t)}$ to the output of the target Transformer block during generation. The scalar $\alpha$ controls the intervention strength. We also include a clean route with no activation intervention, represented by $\mathbf{v}_{0}^{(t)}=\mathbf{0}$ . This changes hidden states and subsequent token distributions, yielding a route-specific proposal distribution:

 $y_{i}^{(t)}\sim\begin{cases}\pi_{\theta^{(t)}}(\cdot\mid x),&s_{i}=0,\\
\widetilde{\pi}_{\theta^{(t)},\mathbf{v}_{s_{i}}^{(t)}}(\cdot\mid x),&s_{i}\in\{1,\ldots,K\}.\end{cases}$ 

(3)

Token sampling remains stochastic within each route, and the main configuration allocates the group budget uniformly across the clean route and the $K$ steering-guided proposals. If $\mathcal{V}^{(t)}=\emptyset$ , the rollout distribution reduces to the unsteered policy $\pi_{\theta^{(t)}}(\cdot\mid x)$ .

The verifier supplies a reward $r_{i}^{(t)}$ after generation. GRPO converts group rewards into relative advantages and updates the base policy with these routed trajectories. Steering changes only the proposal distribution during rollout, allowing training to discover candidates rarely covered by ordinary sampling. GRPO then increases the probability of useful behaviors under the base policy, making the exploratory experience persistent. Validation and deployment disable steering, so the resulting policy can reason independently.

The same verifier-scored trajectories update future exploration. We construct positive–negative pairs to estimate $\mathcal{V}^{(t+1)}$ ; a newly estimated bank becomes active only after actor optimization and weight synchronization. The resulting loop is structured exploration, outcome evaluation, policy learning, and exploration update (Figure 2). Section 3.2 describes vector-bank estimation and Section 3.3 describes steering-guided rollout optimization.

Figure 2: ASE pipeline. We replay successful and unsuccessful responses to read out hidden activations, cluster their pairwise differences to form exploration directions, match and smooth the directions across updates, and inject one selected direction into the residual stream for each routed response.

## 3.2 Online Exploration Vector Bank

## Within-problem positive–negative pairing.

For each problem, we compare trajectories with positive and negative rewards. Positive trajectories are successful samples from the current policy, while negative trajectories provide unsuccessful references. Let $\mathcal{P}=\{i:r_{i}=1\}$ and $\mathcal{N}=\{i:r_{i}=0\}$ . Only groups containing both reward types provide a within-problem contrast. For each such group, we randomly select and pair $n_{\mathrm{pair}}=\min(|\mathcal{P}|,|\mathcal{N}|)$ trajectories from the two sides. Groups whose rewards are all one or all zero do not contribute to the current steering state.

## Activation readout.

We replay each selected full prompt–response sequence with the current actor without steering. Let $\mathbf{h}_{i}=\phi_{\ell,p}(x,y_{i})\in\mathbb{R}^{d}$ be the activation selected by position function $p$ from the output of Transformer block $\ell$ . Each within-problem pair gives a raw activation difference $\mathbf{d}_{j}=\mathbf{h}^{+}_{j}-\mathbf{h}^{-}_{j}$ for $j=1,\ldots,n_{\mathrm{pair}}$ .
Because the paired trajectories share the same prompt and use the same readout position rule $p$ , this difference removes much of the variation caused by problem content and difficulty and provides a basis for structured exploration directions.

Paired activations accumulate across iterations in a buffer $\mathcal{B}$ . Let $B_{\min}$ be the minimum number of pairs required for an update, $\Delta$ the minimum interval between updates, and $t_{\mathrm{last}}$ the iteration index of the most recent vector-bank update. When $|\mathcal{B}|\geq B_{\min}$ and $t-t_{\mathrm{last}}\geq\Delta$ , we re-estimate the vector bank from the full buffer and then clear it. The readout layer, position function, $B_{\min}$ , and $\Delta$ are implementation hyperparameters.

## Difference clustering.

A single global mean difference would compress diverse exploration patterns into one direction. These patterns may reflect variation in early strategy choice, the sequencing of intermediate steps, or later checking and revision. We therefore cluster in a dimension-reduced pair-difference space.

Suppose the buffer contains $M$ pairs $\{(\mathbf{h}_{b}^{+},\mathbf{h}_{b}^{-})\}_{b=1}^{M}$ collected across problems. To stabilize clustering when the number of pairs is limited relative to the hidden dimension, we fit a PCA projection $\mathbf{Q}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathrm{PCA}}\times d}$ on the pooled positive and negative activations and compute each pair’s difference in this shared subspace as $\bm{\delta}_{b}=\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{h}_{b}^{+}-\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{h}_{b}^{-}$ . Here $d_{\mathrm{PCA}}$ denotes the number of retained principal components. K-means partitions $\{\bm{\delta}_{b}\}_{b=1}^{M}$ into $K$ clusters $\{\mathcal{C}_{k}^{(t+1)}\}_{k=1}^{K}$ . The set $\mathcal{C}_{k}^{(t+1)}$ contains the indices of all pairs assigned to cluster $k$ .
PCA only defines the clustering space. The final direction is the within-cluster mean difference in the original hidden space:

 $\widehat{\mathbf{u}}_{k}^{(t+1)}=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{C}_{k}^{(t+1)}|}\sum_{b\in\mathcal{C}_{k}^{(t+1)}}(\mathbf{h}_{b}^{+}-\mathbf{h}_{b}^{-}).$ 

(4)

The clusters construct multiple local rollout proposals; we do not assume that they correspond to discrete, stable, or human-namable semantics.

## Cross-iteration stabilization.

Let $\mathbf{u}_{k}^{(t)}$ denote the unnormalized, temporally smoothed direction, with $\mathbf{v}_{k}^{(t)}=\mathbf{u}_{k}^{(t)}/\|\mathbf{u}_{k}^{(t)}\|_{2}$ .
A finite buffer is affected by rollout noise, so we use an EMA with coefficient $\beta\in[0,1)$ to smooth updates. Since K-means cluster indices have no fixed meaning across updates, we first align the new candidate directions with the existing bank by solving

 $P^{(t+1)}=\arg\max_{P\in\mathfrak{S}_{K}}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\cos(\mathbf{u}_{k}^{(t)},\widehat{\mathbf{u}}_{P(k)}^{(t+1)}),$ 

(5)

where $\mathfrak{S}_{K}$ is the set of permutations. We use the Hungarian algorithm, then update

 $\mathbf{u}_{k}^{(t+1)}=\beta\mathbf{u}_{k}^{(t)}+(1-\beta)\widehat{\mathbf{u}}_{P^{(t+1)}(k)}^{(t+1)}.$ 

(6)

For the first update, we set $\mathbf{u}_{k}^{(t+1)}=\widehat{\mathbf{u}}_{k}^{(t+1)}$ . Finally, we normalize $\mathbf{u}_{k}^{(t+1)}$ to unit $L_{2}$ norm to obtain $\mathbf{v}_{k}^{(t+1)}$ .
The new bank is staged until actor optimization and rollout weight synchronization finish.

## 3.3 Steering-Guided Rollout Optimization

## Routed activation steering.

When $\mathcal{V}^{(t)}\neq\emptyset$ , we assign routes $s_{i}\in\{0,\ldots,K\}$ to trajectories for each problem, where $s_{i}=0$ is the clean route. Our main method uses all $K+1$ routes uniformly within each group. For any target-layer output $\mathbf{h}^{(\ell)}$ on a trajectory assigned to a steering route $s_{i}\in\{1,\ldots,K\}$ , the intervention is

 $\widetilde{\mathbf{h}}^{(\ell)}=\mathbf{h}^{(\ell)}+\alpha\mathbf{v}_{s_{i}}^{(t)}.$ 

(7)

For the clean route, the output is unchanged. The intervention is applied to every forward pass of the target layer, including prompt prefill and autoregressive decoding. Since each vector has unit $L_{2}$ norm, $\alpha$ directly controls the injected residual. Steering is executed by the rollout engine with fixed actor parameters and is outside backpropagation.

## GRPO update.

For the $N$ rollouts of a fixed problem $x$ , let $\mu$ and $\sigma$ be the mean and sample standard deviation of their verifier rewards. The group-relative advantage is $A_{i}=(r_{i}-\mu)/(\sigma+\epsilon)$ , where $\epsilon>0$ stabilizes normalization.
We update the base policy using trajectories from both clean and steered rollout routes, and apply a bounded token-level importance weight in the clipped GRPO objective. For each response token,

 $\rho_{i,\tau}(\theta)=\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,\tau}\mid x,y_{i,<\tau})}{\pi_{\theta^{(t)}}(y_{i,\tau}\mid x,y_{i,<\tau})}.$ 

(8)

For $s_{i}\in\{0,\ldots,K\}$ , the truncated importance ratio is

 $w_{i,\tau}=\min\!\left(c_{\mathrm{IS}},\frac{\pi_{\theta^{(t)}}(y_{i,\tau}\mid x,y_{i,<\tau})}{\widetilde{\pi}_{\theta^{(t)},\mathbf{v}_{s_{i}}^{(t)}}(y_{i,\tau}\mid x,y_{i,<\tau})}\right).$ 

(9)

Here $c_{\mathrm{IS}}>0$ caps the importance weight. This weight is computed separately at each response position. The token-level objective is

 $\mathcal{L}(\theta)=-\mathbb{E}_{x,i,\tau}\!\left[w_{i,\tau}\min\left(\rho_{i,\tau}(\theta)A_{i},\operatorname{clip}(\rho_{i,\tau}(\theta),1-\varepsilon,1+\varepsilon)A_{i}\right)\right].$ 

(10)

The expectation averages over generated response tokens, and $\varepsilon>0$ sets the PPO clipping range.
Minimizing this objective updates the base policy from $\theta^{(t)}$ to $\theta^{(t+1)}$ . The verifier and advantage estimator follow GRPO. The bounded importance weight adjusts the contribution of each sampled token according to its likelihood under the base policy and its rollout route. The complete training procedure is given in Algorithm 1.

## 4 Experiments

## 4.1 Experimental Setup

## Models and datasets.

We train Qwen3-1.7B-Base and Qwen3-8B-Base (Qwen Team, 2025) with DAPO-Math-17k (Yu et al., 2025). We report six mathematical test benchmarks: MATH500 (Hendrycks et al., 2021; Lightman et al., 2023), Minerva (Lewkowycz et al., 2022), AIME24 (Zhang and Math-AI Team, 2024), AIME25 (Zhang and Math-AI Team, 2025), AMC24 (ExtraordinaryLab, 2025), and OlympiadBench (He et al., 2024); and three out-of-distribution benchmarks: MMLU-Pro (Wang et al., 2024), GPQA-Diamond (Rein et al., 2024), and BBH (Suzgun et al., 2023).

## Baselines.

We compare with standard GRPO and four representative baselines. DAPO (Yu et al., 2025) dynamically identifies and discards zero-variance prompts; Dr.GRPO (Liu et al., 2025) redesigns advantages and loss aggregation to address length bias; GSPO (Zheng et al., 2025a) performs importance weighting, clipping, and optimization at the sequence level; and RePO (Li et al., 2025) reuses complete historical trajectories from a replay buffer. We follow method-specific configurations from the corresponding papers or public implementations.

## Implementation details.

All methods use four epochs, a training and actor mini-batch size of 64, and eight rollouts per problem. The actor is optimized with AdamW (learning rate $1\times 10^{-6}$ , weight decay 0.01), a constant learning-rate schedule, and no warmup; we use neither KL loss nor entropy regularization. Training rollouts use temperature 1.0, top- $p$ 1.0, top- $k=-1$ , and maximum prompt/response lengths of 1024/4096, while final evaluation uses temperature 0.6, top- $p$ 0.95, top- $k=0$ , and maximum response length 4096. For ASE, we use $K=3$ steering clusters plus a clean route, steering strength $\alpha=0.5$ , and EMA coefficient $\beta=0.5$ . Training uses verl (Sheng et al., 2025), vLLM (Kwon et al., 2023), BF16, and FlashAttention-2 (Dao, 2024) on four NVIDIA A800 GPUs for the 1.7B model and eight for the 8B model. Math-Verify11

## 4.4 Inference-Time Steering versus Training-Time Steering

Activation steering can be used in two distinct ways. An inference-time intervention changes the output distribution of a fixed model during evaluation, whereas our training-time intervention diversifies the rollouts used to train the policy. We compare these two uses of steering to determine whether the main benefit comes from the direct effect of steering a fixed policy or from cumulative gains of online exploration during training.
We compare six conditions: Base, BaseS, GRPO, GRPOS, ASE-GRPO, and ASE-GRPOS, where S denotes inference-time intervention. To assess the potential gains from inference-time steering, we sweep its parameters separately for each checkpoint; details are reported in Appendix D.

Table 3 summarizes the comparison. Inference-time intervention provides only marginal changes for the untrained and GRPO checkpoints: BaseS changes Math Mean@8 from 18.74 to 18.86 and GRPOS changes it from 25.47 to 25.81. ASE-GRPO already outperforms standard GRPO without inference-time intervention, and applying inference-time intervention to ASE-GRPO gives a further 0.51-point Math Mean@8 increase and 0.15-point OOD Mean@8 increase, while OOD Pass@8 decreases from 78.98 to 78.44. These results indicate that the main gain comes from training-time exploration, with only modest direct effects from steering a fixed policy.

## 4.5 Ablating Verifier-Guided Direction

The vector bank is estimated from within-problem success–failure contrasts. This raises a specific attribution question: does verifier feedback matter, or is the gain explained by injecting any additional activation perturbation? Random directions replace each cluster candidate with an independent random vector of the same norm; shuffled labels randomly swap the positive and negative labels within each pair before clustering and direction estimation; and reversed directions negate the learned vectors while preserving their cluster assignments. These controls retain the same downstream bank update and routing procedure.

## 5 Conclusion

We introduced Activation-Space Exploration (ASE), an online method that uses verifier feedback to construct and update multiple activation-space directions for training rollouts. By distributing a fixed rollout budget across these directions, ASE improves mathematical reasoning on two model scales and also benefits Dr.GRPO and GSPO. Matched ablations show that success–failure contrasts yield stronger gains than random, label-shuffled, or reversed directions, while comparisons with inference-time steering support the value of using these interventions during training. All steering is removed at evaluation, so the gains persist in the learned base policy without additional inference-time intervention. These findings support activation space as a complementary exploration axis for RLVR.

## Appendix A Complete Training Algorithm

Algorithm 1 summarizes the implementation of ASE-GRPO.

Algorithm 1 ASE-GRPO.

Input: initial policy $\pi_{\theta^{(0)}}$ , training distribution $\mathcal{D}$ , rollout count $N$ , bank size $K$ , steering strength $\alpha$ , EMA coefficient $\beta$ , minimum pair count $B_{\min}$ , and update interval $\Delta$ .

Initialize: $\mathcal{V}^{(0)}\leftarrow\emptyset$ and paired-activation buffer $\mathcal{B}\leftarrow\emptyset$ .

1. 

For $t=0,1,2,\ldots$ , sample a batch of problems from $\mathcal{D}$ and generate routed rollouts with $\pi_{\theta^{(t)}}$ and $\mathcal{V}^{(t)}$ .

2. 

Compute binary verifier rewards, then balance and pair correct and incorrect trajectories within each group.

3. 

Replay the selected trajectories with the unsteered actor, read the corresponding activations, and append the paired activations to $\mathcal{B}$ .

4. 

If $|\mathcal{B}|\geq B_{\min}$ and at least $\Delta$ iterations have elapsed, fit PCA on the pooled activations and run K-means on the PCA-space pair differences. Compute cluster means in the original hidden space, match candidates to historical directions, apply EMA and normalization, stage $\mathcal{V}^{(t+1)}$ , and clear $\mathcal{B}$ .

5. 

Compute group-relative advantages and update the base policy with the clipped GRPO objective.

6. 

Synchronize actor weights to the rollout engine. Activate a staged vector bank if one exists; otherwise set $\mathcal{V}^{(t+1)}\leftarrow\mathcal{V}^{(t)}$ .

Output: the base policy; discard the vector bank.

## Appendix B Evaluation and Checkpoint Selection

Training uses only the local DAPO-Math-17k training file. The original JSONL contains 17,398 samples; filtering by maximum prompt length retains 17,390 samples in the standard configuration, while RePO uses 17,344 samples under its fixed rollout configuration.

We use MATH500 for test-set adaptive model selection by evaluating it periodically during training. For ASE and all baselines, we select the checkpoint with the highest MATH500 Mean@8. We evaluate MATH500 every 100 optimization steps using eight responses per problem, temperature 0.6, top- $p$ 0.95, top- $k=0$ , and seed 42. Evaluation on Minerva, AIME24, AIME25, AMC24, OlympiadBench, and the out-of-distribution benchmarks (MMLU-Pro, GPQA-Diamond, and Big-Bench Hard) is performed only on the selected checkpoint for each experiment.

For layer choice, we read out activations and apply steering at the output of Transformer block 0 in all ASE experiments. We use a single intervention layer to avoid accumulating direct vector additions across multiple depths, while an early intervention allows the injected direction to propagate through the remaining blocks. This choice is heuristic: intermediate or later layers may provide more informative representations for constructing exploration directions. We did not run a layer sweep within the available compute budget, so we do not claim that block 0 is optimal; comparing readout and intervention layers, including multi-layer configurations, remains future work.

The three sweeps suggest that a small route bank is sufficient under the
tested rollout budget. For the cluster-count sweep, $K=3$ gives the highest
mathematical Mean@8 and Pass@8, at 29.64 and 47.02, respectively, whereas
 $K=2$ gives the highest OOD Mean@8 and Pass@8, at 40.08 and 78.44. Increasing
 $K$ beyond three does not produce a monotonic improvement: both mathematical
metrics are lower for $K\in\{4,5,6\}$ than for $K=3$ . This indicates that
adding routes alone does not guarantee better coverage, and that the
available rollout evidence must also support reliable estimation of each
direction.

The EMA sweep supports using temporal smoothing when updating the vector bank.
Relative to the unsmoothed setting $\beta=0$ , the intermediate setting
 $\beta=0.5$ improves mathematical Mean@8 from 26.48 to 30.21 and OOD Mean@8
from 35.84 to 40.79. It also gives the highest OOD Pass@8, 79.71, while the
highest mathematical Pass@8 is obtained at $\beta=0.7$ , with 48.83. Thus,
moderate-to-strong smoothing performs well across the reported metrics, with
 $\beta=0.5$ providing a useful overall operating point rather than being the
strict optimum for every metric.

The strength sweep shows non-monotonic variation across the tested values of
 $\alpha$ . Mathematical Mean@8 ranges from 28.38 to 30.31 and Pass@8 from
44.63 to 48.67; OOD Mean@8 ranges from 38.75 to 41.35 and Pass@8 from
76.52 to 79.97. All four metrics reach their highest values at $\alpha=0.5$ :
30.31/48.67 for Math Avg. and 41.35/79.97 for OOD Avg. Increasing the
strength to $\alpha=0.6$ lowers all four metrics, so stronger steering does
not consistently improve the final unsteered policy over this range.

These tables are separate one-parameter sensitivity analyses rather than a
joint factorial study. In particular, the cluster-count sweep fixes
 $\alpha=0.4$ , the EMA sweep fixes $\alpha=0.2$ , and the strength sweep varies
 $\alpha$ with $K=3$ and $\beta=0.5$ . The different fixed strengths reflect
separate experimental runs that inherited the working configuration used at
the time of each sweep, rather than a rerun of every sweep after selecting a
single global anchor. This design keeps each table’s within-sweep comparison
well-defined, but it prevents comparisons of absolute values across tables
from being interpreted as a unified hyperparameter ranking. In particular,
the best row in each table should not be combined to claim a jointly optimal
 $(K,\beta,\alpha)$ configuration. A fully matched sensitivity study would
hold one common anchor fixed for all three scans, or would evaluate a
prespecified joint grid; the present results are intended to characterize
local sensitivity around the configurations used in the respective runs.

We also vary the response-relative readout position on Qwen3-1.7B-Base in a
separate sweep, selecting activations at a fraction $f$ of each response’s
length. Its absolute scores are not directly comparable with those of the
preceding ablations since the sweeps were not conducted under a common matched
configuration. Within this sweep, $f=0.25$ performs best on the mathematical
benchmarks, the primary target of ASE. One possible explanation is that earlier
response states reflect the initial solution strategy before later
calculations and answer formatting add trajectory-specific variation, making
success–failure contrasts more reusable across problems. This explanation
remains a hypothesis based on single-run comparisons.

## D.2 Ablating Verifier-Guided Direction

The matched controls in Table 4 change direction construction while retaining the clustered $K=3$ bank, routing, and EMA updates. For random directions, each independent random vector is rescaled to the corresponding cluster candidate’s original $L_{2}$ norm before the EMA update. For shuffled labels, the positive and negative labels within each activation pair are randomly exchanged before PCA, K-means, and cluster-direction estimation. Consequently, the controls’ gains over GRPO do not isolate the effect of arbitrary activation perturbations: the shared bank structure and temporal averaging may also contribute.

To examine this possibility, we additionally use unstructured random steering: eight sampled unit random vectors, one per rollout, with no clustering or EMA. The vectors do not depend on activations or verifier labels. As shown in Table 14, this control reaches 26.65/41.59 on Math Avg., compared with 26.00/43.91 for GRPO and 30.31/48.67 for ASE-GRPO. Its Mean@8 is only 0.65 points above GRPO, while its Pass@8 is 2.32 points lower, so arbitrary random steering does not yield a consistent improvement in this comparison. This pattern is consistent with the structured bank and EMA contributing to the gains of the matched controls.

## D.3 Inference-time steering sweep

The sweep compares three checkpoints: Qwen3-1.7B-Base and its GRPO- and ASE-GRPO-trained variants. For each model, vectors are constructed from response-relative activations captured on the 17,390 filtered DAPO-Math-17k prompts. We use block 0, within-problem verifier-reward pairing, CAA-style positive-minus-negative differences, no cluster-feature scaling, PCA dimension 64, K-means n_init=512, balanced-uniform route allocation, and a clean route. We evaluate

 $K\in\{1,2,3,4,5,6,7\},\qquad f\in\{0.25,0.50,0.75\},\qquad\alpha\in\{0.25,0.50,0.75,1.00\}.$ 

(11)

This gives 84 steering conditions per model. Each steering condition is independently repeated three times, with eight responses per problem. The Base and GRPO clean references use three standalone evaluations; the ASE-GRPO clean reference combines its selected-checkpoint training validation with two standalone evaluations. Evaluation uses MATH500, Minerva, AIME24, AIME25, and AMC24, temperature 0.6, top- $p$ 0.95, top- $k=0$ , maximum response length 4096, and thinking mode.

For a sweep cell, Core Math Mean@8 and Pass@8 are the unweighted averages across the five benchmarks. We report the mean and sample standard deviation over the three evaluation repeats. The corresponding clean-relative changes use the same model’s clean mean:

 $\Delta_{\mathrm{mean}}=\overline{\mathrm{Mean@8}}_{\mathrm{steer}}-\overline{\mathrm{Mean@8}}_{\mathrm{clean}},\qquad\Delta_{\mathrm{pass}}=\overline{\mathrm{Pass@8}}_{\mathrm{steer}}-\overline{\mathrm{Pass@8}}_{\mathrm{clean}}.$ 

(12)

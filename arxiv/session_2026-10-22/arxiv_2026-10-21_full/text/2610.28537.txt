Abstract

Post-training quantization (PTQ) compresses the weights of a trained model with limited compute
and data. Down to 4 bits it is near-lossless, but at 2 bits scalar PTQ configurations fall to chance-level accuracy. We study three choices usually treated as implementation details that matter little at 4 bits but decide 2-bit quality: the quantization representation, the calibration data, and the recovery fine-tuning data, across six dense and mixture-of experts models (ranging from $3.8$ to $32$ B parameters). Representation is the largest factor and vector codebooks restore usable models where scalar PTQ pipelines collapse. Most of the remaining loss in accuracy is on generative
tasks, and the amount of performance drop is model-dependent. Furthermore, at a short recovery budget, calibration and recovery data
are coupled. The best recovery data depends on the calibration data, so the two must be chosen
jointly. The resulting recipe involves a vector codebook representation, a small task-aligned
calibration mixture, and an inexpensive codebook-only recovery stage.
Including codebooks, it uses about $2.3$ effective bits per weight and retains $89$ to $97\%$ of
full-precision question-answering (QA) accuracy where scalar PTQ is at chance, and, including
recovery, uses $1.4$ to $30\times$ less compute than the strongest quantization-aware-training (QAT) baseline.

Post-training quantization (PTQ) is standard for serving large language
models under tight memory budgets. At 4 bits (W4A16: 4-bit weights,
16-bit activations), it costs only a few accuracy points
(Frantar et al., 2023; Lin et al., 2024; Shao et al., 2024; Ashkboos et al., 2024; Liu et al., 2025b).
At 2 bits, scalar PTQ falls to chance-level accuracy
(Egiazarian et al., 2024; Chee et al., 2023; Liu et al., 2024a; Figure 1a), and
quantized reasoning models can overthink and loop (Lotfi et al., 2026). Richer codebooks
(Van Baalen et al., 2024; Liu et al., 2024a; Egiazarian et al., 2024; Tseng et al., 2024a) or learned
quantization (Liu et al., 2024b; Du et al., 2024; Dadgarnia et al., 2026) trade compute for quality, yet 2-bit models
still lag their 4-bit counterparts.

Three choices usually treated as implementation details govern 2-bit quality: the
quantization representation, the calibration data that shape quantization, and the
recovery data used to fine-tune the quantized model. They matter little at 4 bits but
become consequential below 4 bits, where calibration and recovery data also interact, so tuning
them separately can be misleading. We study 2-bit quantization and recovery across six dense and
mixture-of-experts (MoE) models ( $3.8$ to $32$ B parameters) and propose an actionable recipe (Section 5) for low-budget 2-bit quantization. Our primary observations are:

• 

Representation dominates at 2 bits. Every scalar PTQ pipeline we test falls to
random chance-level, while vector PTQ at ${\sim}2.3$ effective bits keeps $89$ – $97\%$ of a full-precision model’s question-answering accuracy
without training, using $39$ – $304\times$ less compute than trained scalar methods
(Table 4).

• 

Loss of reasoning ability is model-dependent. Question-answering retention is similar across models, but HumanEval (codegen) retention spans $45\%$ (Phi-4-mini, Qwen3-8B) to $81\%$ (Qwen3-MoE),
tracking the model and its scale more than the quantizer (Figure 4).

• 

Calibration and recovery data interact. Choosing them jointly improves Phi-4-mini’s accuracy on generation tasks from $11.1\%$ (web calibration) to $45.7\%$ (math calibration, web recovery); the best recovery data flips with calibration on all three dense models we study (Tables 1 and 2).

The proposed recipe is a vector representation with a task-aligned calibration mixture and a short codebook-only recovery stage. With recovery, it narrows the gap to 4-bit models while using $1.4$ to
 $30\times$ less compute than the strongest quantization-aware-training (QAT) baseline; a cheap
per-model check handles model dependence (Section 5).
Upon acceptance, we will release our code, Hessian mixtures, and quantized models.

## 2 Related Work

Scalar PTQ. GPTQ (Frantar et al., 2023) sets the near-lossless 4-bit
weight-only baseline via approximate second-order error minimization; AWQ
(Lin et al., 2024) and SmoothQuant (Xiao et al., 2023) rescale salient
channels, while QuaRot (Ashkboos et al., 2024) and SpinQuant (Liu et al., 2025b)
rotate weights before rounding. The scalar PTQ configurations we evaluate collapse at 2 bits without any further quantization-aware training or recovery (Section 4.4).

Vector and ultra-low-bit quantization. Codebooks give better rate-distortion below 3 bits. QuIP (Chee et al., 2023) adds incoherence processing to adaptive scalar rounding;
QuIP# (Tseng et al., 2024a) pairs it with lattice codebooks and fine-tuning. AQLM, GPTVQ, VPTQ, and QTIP
(Egiazarian et al., 2024; Van Baalen et al., 2024; Liu et al., 2024a; Tseng et al., 2024b) use additive,
hierarchical, or trellis-coded codebooks. YAQA (Tseng et al., 2026) adds a
Fisher-weighted rounding objective. Non-uniform (Kim et al., 2024) and 1-bit
(Huang et al., 2024; Yuan et al., 2024; Xu et al., 2024b) methods also target lower precision to go even beyond 2-bits.

Trained scalar quantization. OmniQuant (Shao et al., 2024) learns
clipping thresholds block-wise, and GSQ (Dadgarnia et al., 2026) learns scalar-grid
assignments through a Gumbel-Softmax relaxation; we use GSQ as an intermediate-cost
scalar baseline between PTQ and QAT.

Calibration data. PTQ usually calibrates on a few hundred C4 or
WikiText-2 sequences
(Frantar et al., 2023; Lin et al., 2024; Shao et al., 2024; Egiazarian et al., 2024). Calibration
data affect 4-bit models (Williams and Aletras, 2024) and pruning
(Bandari et al., 2024; Ji et al., 2025), and self-generated text can replace curated corpora
(Williams et al., 2025). We find that calibration data effects 2-bit PTQ methods much more than on 4-bit methods and this effect is consistent across representations and recipe grids (Appendix A.6).

Evaluating quantized LLMs. Quantization costs generation, reasoning, and
knowledge-intensive tasks more than perplexity or multiple-choice QA as suggested by
(Jaiswal et al., 2024; Li et al., 2024a; Jin et al., 2024; Kurtic et al., 2025; Liu et al., 2025a),
and robustness depends on model size and token density
(Dettmers and Zettlemoyer, 2023; Kumar et al., 2025; Ouyang et al., 2025). We confirm this gap at 2 bits and show that the degradation of generation quality depends on calibration, recovery data and model-scale.

Recovery for quantized models. Distillation-based recovery
(Hinton et al., 2015) narrows the quantization gap without full QAT. LLM-QAT and
BitDistiller (Liu et al., 2024b; Du et al., 2024) retrain all weights; LoRA and
QLoRA (Hu et al., 2022; Dettmers et al., 2023) add high-precision parameters; closest to ours, EfficientQAT (Chen et al., 2025) trains all weights block-wise, then
quantization parameters end to end; ParetoQ (Liu et al., 2026) studies
extremely low-bit QAT. AQLM and QuIP#
fine-tune codebooks or continuous parameters after quantization. PV-Tuning (Malinovskii et al., 2024) additionally also learns discrete assignments to the codebook vectors. In this work we use codebook-only recovery and show that its best data flips with the
calibration data, so the two must be chosen jointly (Section 4.2).

## 3 Background and Method

We study post-training quantization followed by recovery fine-tuning at the same
format and bitwidth.

## 3.1 Scalar and Vector Quantization

Scalar quantization maps each weight to one of $2^{b}$ levels, $\hat{x}=s(q-z)$ , with
integer code $q$ , per-group scale $s$ , and optional zero-point $z$ . Error-aware PTQ, such
as GPTQ (Frantar et al., 2023), minimizes output error on a calibration
distribution $X$ with input Hessian $H_{X}=\mathbb{E}_{x\sim X}[xx^{\top}]$ :

 $\min_{\hat{W}\in\mathcal{Q}}\mathbb{E}_{x\sim X}\!\big\|(W-\hat{W})x\big\|_{2}^{2}=\min_{\hat{W}\in\mathcal{Q}}\operatorname{Tr}\!\big[(W-\hat{W})H_{X}(W-\hat{W})^{\top}\big],$ 

(1)

where $\mathcal{Q}$ is the set of representable weights. Vector quantization
maps each $d$ -dimensional sub-vector $w_{i}$ to an entry of a learned codebook
 $\mathcal{C}=\{c_{k}\}_{k=1}^{K}$ , $\hat{w}_{i}=c_{a_{i}}$ , using weight correlations that scalar grids miss.
VPTQ (Liu et al., 2024a) solves Equation 1 over codebooks.
Structured-curvature methods such as KronQ (Lee et al., 2026) and YAQA
(Tseng et al., 2026) use Kronecker-factored full-model KL curvature and YAQA rounds onto trellis-coded sets.

Calibration mixtures. The objective depends on the data only through
 $H_{X}$ . A mixture with weights $\alpha_{j}\geq 0$ , $\sum_{j}\alpha_{j}=1$ , over domains with
Hessians $H_{j}$ has $H_{\bm{\alpha}}=\sum_{j}\alpha_{j}H_{j}$ would allow one to approximate the hessian for different data distributions, cheaply. We sweep mixtures by reweighting precomputed Hessians without re-collecting hessians on actual dataset mixtures; each
mixture still needs its own quantization pass. For YAQA we mix the Kronecker
input and output factors separately, approximating re-estimation on different dataset mixtures.

## 3.2 Recovery Fine-Tuning

Recovery freezes the quantized backbone and trains only quantization parameters:
the codebook $\mathcal{C}$ with assignments fixed, or the scales $s$ 
(and $z$ ). QAT backpropagates through the quantizer
with a straight-through estimator (Liu et al., 2024b) and keeps optimizer state for
all weights ( ${\approx}\,14P$ bytes for $P$ parameters). Recovery keeps the bitwidth
and needs only a teacher and student ( ${\approx}\,2P+bP/8$ bytes. We distill from the full-precision model with
 $\mathcal{L}=\alpha\,\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{CE}}+(1-\alpha)\,\tau^{2}\operatorname{KL}\big(\sigma(z_{t}/\tau)\,\|\,\sigma(z_{s}/\tau)\big)$ ,
where $z_{t}$ and $z_{s}$ are teacher and student logits, $\sigma$ is the softmax, $\tau$ 
the temperature, and $\alpha{=}0.5$ unless noted. Our recovery pipeline involves codebook-only
recovery. In the appendix we cover alternative losses and logit truncation
(Appendix A.7), a scale-only recovery scalar control
(Appendix A.7), and finetuning with low-rank adapters
(Hu et al., 2022; Dettmers et al., 2023; Xu et al., 2024a; Li et al., 2024b), which add
half-precision parameters (Appendix A.11).

## 4 Experiments and Results

## 4.1 Experimental setup

Models and quantizers. We evaluate Phi-4-mini (Abouelenin et al., 2025)
(3.8B, instruct), Llama-3.1-8B (Grattafiori et al., 2024) (base), and Qwen3-30B-A3B
(Yang et al., 2025) (reasoning MoE, ${\sim}3$ B active). Qwen3-8B (reasoning)
checks the calibration and recovery choices; OLMoE-1B-7B (Muennighoff et al., 2025)
(instruct MoE) and Qwen3-32B (reasoning, dense) extend the comparisons to smaller MoEs and large-scale dense models, respectively. The scalar baselines we use are
SpinQuant (Liu et al., 2025b) and QuaRot (Ashkboos et al., 2024) at group size 128. Furthermore, we observe that learned rotations do not help at 2 bits (Appendix A.3). The vector-quantization baselines we study are VPTQ (Liu et al., 2024a)
( $2^{16}$ -entry codebook over 8-dimensional vectors) and YAQA (Tseng et al., 2026), a
Fisher-weighted trellis quantizer, both in the same incoherence-rotated frame; we report
native (unrotated) VPTQ where it is stronger. We also compare vector quantization against GSQ
(Dadgarnia et al., 2026) (scalar PTQ with learned assignments) and BitDistiller
(Du et al., 2024) (scalar QAT). In our experiments we quantize attention and MLP projections,
including every MoE expert. Routers, embeddings, norms and the language modeling head stay
in half precision. Our ablation on the router precision in Appendix A.10, suggests that the router is pretty robust to quantization. Counting indices, codebooks, and scales, every
method uses $2.09$ – $2.33$ effective bits per weight. In the MoEs,
VPTQ shares codebooks across experts, while YAQA operates with a single large codebook for all weights. We evaluate dequantized weights, report packed-size
storage estimates, and count dominant model passes, amortizing Hessian collection over the
calibration sweep. Configurations and protocols are in Appendices A.3, A.2.

Calibration and evaluation. For calibration data we primarily focus on web, instruction, math
(OpenR1-Math), code, and their mixtures. We report an AvgQA metric which averages over seven QA tasks: MMLU (5-shot) (Hendrycks et al., 2020),
WinoGrande (Sakaguchi et al., 2021), and LAMBADA (Paperno et al., 2016), scored by
accuracy, and ARC-Easy, ARC-Challenge (Clark et al., 2018), HellaSwag
(Zellers et al., 2019), and PIQA (Bisk et al., 2020), scored by length-normalized
accuracy (all 0-shot except MMLU). We report an AvgGen metric which averages GSM8K (5-shot, strict match)
(Cobbe et al., 2021) and HumanEval (0-shot, pass@1) (Chen et al., 2021); we also
report IFEval (0-shot, prompt-strict) (Zhou et al., 2023). Results use bfloat16 evaluation (P-bf16)
unless a table is marked P-fp16 (float16); the two differ only marginally in our analyses.

## 4.6 Codebook-only recovery is model-dependent

Figure 5: Recovery gains saturate early. Gain over the unrecovered base as the
recovery budget grows (Phi-4-mini, math-calibrated 2-bit YAQA, codebook-only recovery on web
data; Table 22). Generation gains $6.3$ points by $3{,}200$ 
examples and then plateaus; QA rises slowly.

Table 8 pairs each 2-bit base with long codebook-only recovery on
the same recipe data. On Phi-4-mini, recovery changes QA little but roughly doubles HumanEval. On Qwen3-MoE it changes both little; on Llama-3.1-8B base model, QA decreases in all
three long runs and generation has no consistent gain. IFEval falls on most Phi-4-mini
recipes under completion prompts and is mostly, but not uniformly, preserved under the chat
template (Appendix Table 35). Furthermore recovery does not help 2-bit models which hit the random floor. Scale-only recovery on a
collapsed scalar 2-bit model leaves it near the floor
(Appendix A.7).

Under the deployment prompt, short recovery need not cost instruction following.
With the chat template, web recovery of the math-calibrated Phi-4-mini base raises GSM8K from
 $55.0$ to $58.9$ , HumanEval from $34.1$ to $43.9$ , and IFEval from $40.3$ to $44.5$ (four recovery
seeds); math recovery raises GSM8K further ( $61.5$ ) but lowers IFEval to $37.9$ 
(Table 36). The best recovery data still flips with calibration and math recovery
gives the highest AvgGen from the web base and web recovery from the math base.

Recovery data matter more than distillation hyperparameters. The type of knowledge distillation loss, the teacher
precision, temperature, and top- $k$ truncation change AvgQA little. A 4-bit teacher matches a
bf16 one, and dropping the teacher keeps most generation gain

Table 9 and the highlighted box below give the recipe. The representation default holds
across six models, including two MoEs; the calibration defaults come from the three primary
model families. Whether and how to recover depends on the model and the calibration data, so the recipe adds a
cheap per-model check. Screening $k$ calibrated bases costs $k$ 
quantization passes, $2k$ short recoveries ( ${\approx}100$ PFLOP each for a 4B-parameter
model and $3k$ evaluations, far less than QAT.

Recipe: five steps to an accurate 2-bit model

1. 

Use a vector codebook (e.g., YAQA); scalar PTQ collapses at 2 bits.

2. 

Calibrate on target-domain data; mix in broad web text when QA matters.

3. 

Choose recovery data together with calibration: for each calibrated base, compare
no, web, and target-domain recovery.

4. 

Keep recovery short and optional: codebook-only on $3{,}200$ examples; keep it
only if it clears a preset margin without losing QA or instruction following.

5. 

Evaluate as deployed: Report QA and
generation separately against a matched full-precision reference. Evaluate quantized model at higher repetition penalty to avoid overthinking behavior.

## 6 Conclusion and Future work

## Conclusion

At 2 bits, quality depends heavily on representation, calibration data, and recovery. A vector
codebook restores most collapsed accuracy and a task-aligned calibration mixture and short
codebook-only recovery stage narrow the generation gap at much less compute
than QAT. The data choices are not separable at a short recovery budget: calibration domain
drives generation, and the best recovery data depends on it. Across three models and a held-out composite, math recovery helps a web-calibrated base but not a
math-calibrated one (clearest on Phi-4-mini). Tuning the two data choices separately can therefore
select the wrong recovery data, and whether to recover must be validated per model. Most remaining loss is in
generation, whose measurement depends on prompt format and numerical precision.

## Limitations and Future Work.

We measure base and data-sample variability only on Phi-4-mini, and
replicate bases share one rotation and one recovery seed. The interaction covers one domain
pair and one quantizer (YAQA) at a short budget; we did not re-run the $2\times 2$ design at
long budgets. We leave these studies for future work.

## Appendix A Additional Experimental Details and Results

In this section we provide additional details about out pipeline, evaluations and ablation studies on different knobs of the 2-bit quantization pipeline.

## A.1 Evaluation protocols and result provenance

Our results use four protocols. All of them run with lm-evaluation-harness $0.4.12$ ,
transformers $4.53.3$ , and greedy decoding. They use 5-shot MMLU and GSM8K (strict match)
and 0-shot HumanEval (pass@1) and IFEval (prompt-strict). Most controlled comparisons
use one protocol. Table 4 mixes labeled native-VPTQ P-bf16
entries into a P-fp16 comparison.

## P-fp16

(completion prompts, float16) covers the main sweeps in
Tables 3, 4, and 5.
It also covers the Qwen3-MoE row of Table 8, the calibration sweeps,
the recovery budget curve, and the Core6 seed table.

## P-fp32

repeats P-fp16 in float32 for the two Phi-4-mini checkpoints
affected by float16 decoding (Appendix A.8).

## P-bf16

is the canonical controlled protocol. It uses completion prompts, bfloat16,
per-item logs, and a finite-logit probe. It covers the Phi-4-mini and Llama-3.1-8B
 $2\times 2$ design (Figure 2 and Table 21)
and the Phi-4-mini and Llama-3.1-8B rows of Table 8.
It also covers teacher-free recovery, learned SpinQuant, calibration-side controls,
and the dense-32B and OLMoE evaluations.

## P-chat

covers chat-template tables. It uses each model’s chat template with
thinking disabled and at most $1{,}024$ generated tokens. It runs in bfloat16 unless marked.

## Evaluation and reproducibility.

We score tasks with an lm-evaluation-harness-based split-task
orchestrator (Gao et al., 2021). Multiple-choice tasks use log-likelihood
scoring. MMLU and GSM8K use $5$ -shot prompting. GSM8K and HumanEval use greedy
decoding with temperature $0$ and no sampling. They are scored by strict match and pass@1.
Compute accounting uses $128$ scalar-calibration sequences and $128$ – $2{,}048$ 
vector-Hessian sequences per domain. Seed $42$ is
the default.

## QuaRot and SpinQuant (scalar PTQ).

QuaRot and SpinQuant apply an incoherence rotation before scale-only
 $\approx\!2.12$ -bit GPTQ quantization (Appendix A.2). QuaRot uses a
fixed randomized Hadamard transform with activation ordering, Hessian-ordered
columns, error feedback, and damping $0.1$ . SpinQuant uses residual-stream and
per-head rotations ( $R_{1}$ , $R_{2}$ ) at randomized-Hadamard initialization, with
experts rotated separately on Qwen3-MoE. Both methods consume $128$ calibration
sequences and add no trained weights.

## Learned-rotation in Spinquant.

We test learned SpinQuant rotations on the two dense primary models
(Table 12). We optimize $R_{1}$ and $R_{2}$ with Cayley SGD
for $100$ steps on the same $128$ calibration sequences from each domain. We use
learning rates $1.5$ and $0.25$ , then the same GPTQ quantization. Learned rotations
leave all 2-bit models at the random-accuracy floor and barely change best-domain
4-bit AvgQA (Table 12). The scalar cliff is not from
the unoptimized rotation. We could not fit the full-precision copy needed for
Qwen3-30B-A3B, so its SpinQuant entries keep the Hadamard initialization.

## VPTQ (vector PTQ).

VPTQ maps each $8$ -dimensional weight sub-vector to one of $65{,}536$ ( $2^{16}$ )
codebook entries ( $2.00$ index bits). Dense models use one $1$ MiB fp16
codebook per linear layer ( $2.12$ – $2.33$ effective bits). MoEs use expert-shared
codebooks ( $2.09$ – $2.14$ ). VPTQ uses channel-independent second-order optimization
over a shared Hadamard frame with input Hessians collected by calibration domain.
The rate control of §A.4 uses one model-wide $262{,}144$ -entry
( $2^{18}$ ) codebook, giving $2.25$ index bits and $2.26$ effective bits.

## YAQA (vector PTQ).

YAQA uses a trellis codebook with Fisher-weighted, Kronecker-factored curvature
from the full-model KL objective and $2.25$ index bits. Sign vectors and a shared
lookup table bring the rate to $2.26$ – $2.31$ effective bits. YAQA also retains a
fine-tuning-ready codebook under the same incoherence rotation, with curvature
estimated from $128$ – $2{,}048$ sequences per calibration domain.

## GSQ (trained scalar).

GSQ learns discrete grid assignments through a Gumbel-softmax relaxation initialized
with GPTQ ( $512$ sequences). It optimizes one transformer block at a time with
detached inputs. It runs $10$ epochs over a $0.1$ B-token calibration set ( $48{,}828$ 
sequences of length $2048$ ) with group size $128$ and a Lion optimizer.

## BitDistiller (full QAT).

BitDistiller trains a Hadamard-rotated $2$ -bit model with asymmetric activation
clipping and CAKLD ( $\beta\approx 0.84$ ). We run one FSDP full-shard epoch across
 $8$ GPUs. We use sequence length $1024$ , per-device batch $1$ , gradient accumulation $8$ 
(effective batch $64$ ), learning rate $2\times 10^{-5}$ with cosine decay, and
 $10\%$ warmup. The recovery mixture has ${\sim}500$ k dense-model sequences and
 ${\sim}50$ k Qwen3-MoE sequences.

## Codebook-only recovery (PTQ+RFT).

Recovery freezes the quantized backbone and discrete assignments, then updates only
the continuous codebook, leaving the effective bitwidth unchanged. Scalar
scale-only recovery is a limited control (Appendix A.7).
We do not test zero-point recovery. This stage distills from a bf16 or
a x4 times smaller $4$ -bit teacher. The objective optimized is a temperature- $1$ forward-KL loss plus next-token
cross-entropy. We optimize it with AdamW at learning rate $10^{-4}$ and a cosine schedule.
Long Phi-4-mini runs (Table 8 and the main
run) use $8$ GPUs, per-device batch $1$ , gradient accumulation $2$ , sequence length
 $4{,}096$ ( $65{,}536$ tokens per optimizer step), $5{,}500$ steps
( ${\approx}360$ M tokens), and $550$ warmup steps on the recipe mixture. Llama uses
the same layout for ${\approx}185$ M tokens. Qwen3-MoE uses
sequence length $512$ , gradient accumulation $2$ , a device-sharded teacher, and
 ${\approx}1.2$ M tokens.
Controlled short runs cover the $2\times 2$ design, budget curve, seeds, and
teacher-free control. They run on one GPU with per-device batch $1$ , gradient accumulation
 $8$ , sequence length $1{,}024$ ( $8$ sequences per step), and $40$ warmup steps. The $51{,}200$ -example point keeps $8$ sequences
per step with $8$ -GPU data parallelism. Ablations and LoRA are in §A.7
and §A.11.

## Teacher-free recovery.

We recovered the math-calibrated Phi-4-mini
base of Appendix A.8 on the same $3{,}200$ web
examples with plain next-token cross-entropy and no teacher, keeping every other
setting fixed (seed $42$ , bfloat16 evaluation). It reaches the same qualitative
level as forward-KL distillation from a 4-bit teacher (three seeds,
Table 21) and stays above the no-recovery base. In this
single-seed Phi-4-mini setting, the teacher improves the result, but recovery data
accounts for most of the effect.

## Scale-only recovery of a scalar 2-bit model.

We applied the same recovery recipe (forward KL, web data, $1{,}024$ -token
context) to a scalar 2-bit Phi-4-mini model. This setup uses Hadamard rotation,
GPTQ with activation ordering, group size $128$ , and scale-only recovery. We trained only
the per-group scales, with the integer codes frozen ( ${\approx}P/128$ 
parameters). Recovery moves AvgQA only slightly off the floor, while GSM8K and
HumanEval stay near zero. With fixed codes, rescaling does not rescue the scalar
model. The same $3{,}200$ -example web-recovery recipe helps 2-bit codebook models
of Phi-4-mini (Tables 1 and 8).
BitDistiller trains all weights and still trails no-recovery vector PTQ in
generation (Figure 1b).

## Quantity control.

Holding the web base, FineWeb-Edu recovery source, context length, and all
optimization settings fixed, increasing recovery data from $800$ to $3{,}200$ 
examples leaves Core6 nearly unchanged and improves AvgGen. The complete budget curve
uses the math-calibrated base (Table 22).

## Data-source control.

At the same $3{,}200$ -example budget, with every other recovery setting fixed,
replacing filtered FineWeb-Edu with unfiltered C4 leaves Core6 and held-out IFEval
essentially unchanged but lowers both generation tasks and AvgGen. The generation
benefit is not explained by recovery-set size alone. FineWeb-Edu and C4 differ in
filtering, distribution, and collection history, so this control identifies a
data-source effect rather than isolating filtering quality. Over the tested
range, quality and quantity both matter for recovery data. The
calibration-side analogue follows.

## Calibration-side controls.

We also varied the Hessian calibration set of the web-calibrated Phi-4-mini YAQA
base (no recovery, bfloat16 evaluation, seven-task AvgQA). At a matched $256$ 
sequences, filtered FineWeb-Edu and unfiltered C4 Hessians give similar QA and
only small generation differences. Growing the FineWeb-Edu set from $256$ to the
 $2{,}048$ sequences of our web base mainly improves QA while leaving AvgGen
unchanged. The source effect is small and generation-side (the HumanEval gap is
three problems), so we treat it only as initial experimental evidence.

## Generalization to a second model family.

Repeating the $2\times 2$ design on Llama-3.1-8B reproduces the same sign with
smaller magnitude than Phi-4-mini. Filtered FineWeb-Edu recovery improves AvgGen while
leaving Core6 essentially unchanged.

## Recovery budget.

All cells use $3{,}200\times 1{,}024\approx 3.3$ M recovery tokens. This is about
 $100$ PFLOP at $8N$ per token on Phi-4-mini ( $N=3.84\times 10^{9}$ ) and roughly
 $1\%$ of the ${\sim}360$ M-token main recovery run. The math-calibrated,
web-recovered cell reaches the same range as the mw64 (math web 0.6 and 0.4) checkpoint under
comparable protocols). The budget curve in
Table 22 shows generation saturating near $3{,}200$ 
examples.

## A.16 Overthinking behavior at 2-bits: Additional Details

Figure 15 presents the effect of repetition penaty on accuracy and 4-gram lopp score at bf16 and 2-bit precisions.

## Qualitative examples.

Two representative MATH-500 generations from
the 2-bit Qwen3-MoE checkpoint illustrate bounded solving and looping under
default greedy decoding.

2-bit, bounded and correct (Level-4, gold $=\sqrt{5}$ )

“I need the radius of a right cylindrical tank filled at $20\pi$ m3/hr
while the level rises $4$ m/hr. Since $V=\pi r^{2}h$ , $\tfrac{dV}{dt}=\pi r^{2}\tfrac{dh}{dt}$ , so $20\pi=\pi r^{2}\cdot 4\Rightarrow r^{2}=5\Rightarrow r=\sqrt{5}$ .” The model emits a bounded $1{,}740$ -token chain and terminates
with the correct \boxed{ $\sqrt{5}$ }.

2-bit, looping failure (Level-5, gold $=6-5i$ )

“I need to rotate $z$ around $c$ by $\pi/4$ : $w=(z-c)e^{i\pi/4}+c$ . Let me
compute $z-c$ …” The chain reasons coherently at first. It then collapses
into a degenerate repetition of one algebraic line (loop score $0.94$ ). It fills
all $8{,}192$ tokens without ever emitting a boxed answer, so it is scored
wrong. With a repetition penalty of $1.3$ the same model solves this problem.

Figure 15: A repetition penalty helps bf16 and 2-bit alike
(Qwen3-30B-A3B, $40$ MATH-500 level-4/5 problems, greedy, same penalty for both
models; the 2-bit model is expert-shared VPTQ). The penalty improves both precisions and
lowers the $4$ -gram loop score for both. The matched-decoding gap remains
( $0.15$ at $1.3$ ).

# Mathematical Foundations

CayleyPy RL is grounded in a rich interplay between group theory, combinatorics, and the theory of algorithms, with a particular emphasis on Cayley graphs and their properties. ## Cayley Graphs and Their Properties

A Cayley graph is a fundamental object in group theory, capturing the structure of a group in terms of a directed (or undirected) graph. Given a group \( G \) and a generating set \( S \), the Cayley graph \( \mathrm{Cay}(G, S) \) has vertex set \( G \), with edges \( (g, sg) \) for all \( g \in G \), \( s \in S \). These graphs not only serve as visualizations of group structure but also play a crucial role in applications ranging from interconnection networks in computer science to bioinformatics, cryptography, and quantum computing[16][4].
A central parameter of interest in Cayley graphs is the diameter, defined as the maximal shortest path between any two vertices. For many finite groups, especially symmetric groups \( S_n \), the determination of diameters for various generating sets is a major open problem with significant implications in both pure mathematics and algorithm design[16][17]. For example, the LRX Cayley graph of \( S_n \), generated by certain involutive and cyclic permutations, exhibits intricate structure and connections to well-known mechanical puzzles such as the Rubik’s Cube and TopSpin[17]. A conjecture attributed to V. M. Glushkov asserts that the diameter of the LRX Cayley graph is \( n(n-1)/2 \), although a proven lower bound is \( n(n-1)/2 - n/2 - 1 \)[13][17].

## Combinatorial Decomposition and Pathfinding

The pathfinding problem in a Cayley graph is equivalent to decomposing a target group element as a product (word) in the given generators. This problem underlies classical combinatorial puzzles and sorting problems, including the Rubik’s Cube, pancake sorting, and genome rearrangement problems[18][19]. For instance, the move set \( X \) (cyclic shift) and \( L \) (adjacent swap) on \( S_n \) not only define interesting Cayley graphs but also reflect natural operations in puzzles and biological sequences[17][19].
Decompositions often exploit the symmetries of the group and the combinatorial properties of the generators. For the LRX Cayley graph, computational and theoretical analysis reveals that moves must account for disruptions in the cyclic order of triples, leveraging the fact that cyclic shifts preserve certain combinatorial invariants. Such insights lead to both algorithmic strategies and lower bounds for the path lengths required to reach particular permutations[13][17].

## Bellman Equation and Dynamic Programming

Solving the shortest path problem in a Cayley graph can be mathematically formalized via the Bellman equation. Let \( d(s) \) denote the distance from a group element \( s \) to the identity.
\[
d(s) = 1 + \min_{a \in S} d(a \circ s), \quad d(e) = 0
\]
can be solved using dynamic programming or iterative methods[20]. In reinforcement learning (RL) contexts, this approach is analogous to value iteration, where the function \( d \) is iteratively updated based on generator actions until convergence. CayleyPy RL implements such algorithms, combining them with modern AI techniques to efficiently compute or approximate solutions on massive graphs[20][18][3].

Cayley graph diameter conjectures, especially for symmetric and alternating groups, remain a focal point of research in combinatorics and group theory[16]. Moreover, the interplay between combinatorial properties of the group, the algebraic structure of the generators, and the complexity of the pathfinding problem offers fertile ground for advancements in algorithms, artificial intelligence, and pure mathematics[21][22].

# Reinforcement Learning Framework

Reinforcement learning (RL) provides a natural and powerful framework for addressing the pathfinding problem on Cayley graphs, which is equivalent to decomposing a group element into a sequence of generators[2][6][23]. Unlike supervised learning, which operates on a fixed training set, RL involves an agent that must actively explore the state space and generate its own training examples, aiming to optimize cumulative rewards over sequences of actions[7]. This dynamic setup aligns well with the mathematical structure of Cayley graphs, where each node represents a group element (or state), and edges correspond to transitions via group generators (actions)[6].

## RL and Pathfinding in Cayley Graphs

The fundamental task in Cayley graph pathfinding is to determine the shortest (or otherwise optimal) sequence of generators that transforms an initial group element (state) into a target element[23]. This problem can be formalized in the language of graph theory, where the shortest path corresponds to the minimal decomposition of an element, and is naturally modeled via RL[6]. The key mathematical insight comes from the Bellman equation, which, in the context of pathfinding, describes the distance function \(d(s)\) as follows: \(d(s) = 1 + \min_{t \in N(s)} d(t)\), with the boundary condition \(d(e) = 0\), where \(N(s)\) is the set of neighbors of state \(s\), and \(e\) is the target node[7]. This recursive structure mirrors the value iteration principle at the heart of RL algorithms[24].

## RL Terminology and Graph Analogues

A dictionary mapping between RL, graph theory, and group theory helps to clarify the correspondence: states in RL are nodes (group elements) in the Cayley graph; actions correspond to applying generators (edges in the graph); rewards are typically set as negative step costs (e.g., \(r(s, a) = -1\)), incentivizing shorter paths; and the value function \(v^*(s)\) reflects the minimum expected cumulative cost to reach the target from state \(s\)[6][24]. In deterministic environments such as group operations on Cayley graphs, the Bellman optimality equation simplifies, and optimal policies are closely tied to shortest-path strategies[24].

# Algorithms and Methods

The study of pathfinding on Cayley graphs, particularly in the context of permutation groups such as \(S_n\), has traditionally posed significant computational challenges due to the complexity and size of these graphs. Recent advances leverage both classical brute-force algorithms and modern reinforcement learning (RL) techniques to address these challenges, with notable improvements in efficiency and scalability[26][27][9].

## Brute-Force and Classical Methods

Initial approaches to pathfinding on Cayley graphs employed brute-force breadth-first search (BFS) algorithms, optimized using bit operations over large integers. This enabled the traversal of all permutations for \(n \leq 15\), surpassing previous computational records and data from the OEIS by two steps for \(n = 15\). These computations revealed unique features of the longest elements in the graph, providing insights into their structure and supporting conjectures about their mathematical properties[26].
Despite these advances, optimal pathfinding on generic Cayley graphs remains NP-hard, and the performance of classical methods is typically limited to relatively small graphs (e.g., \(n \leq 20\) for \(S_n\)). Computer algebra systems such as GAP are effective in this regime but do not scale well to larger groups[27].

## Reinforcement Learning for Pathfinding

Reinforcement learning (RL) frameworks naturally fit the pathfinding paradigm on graphs. In this setup, nodes correspond to states, generators to actions, and the objective is to maximize the return, typically measured as the negative of the path length or distance to the target[9][28]. The RL process is often guided by the Bellman equation, with neural networks trained to approximate the value function (i.e., the estimated distance from a given node to the target node, such as the identity element)[9][29].
Training is conducted by generating trajectories through the Cayley graph using a mixture of random walks and policy-guided steps. After training, the neural network can serve as an efficient heuristic for greedy search algorithms: at each step, the algorithm selects the neighboring node predicted to be closest to the target, iteratively constructing a solution path[29]. This approach can outperform classical search techniques, especially as the size of the group increases, with AI-based methods showing efficacy for \(n \approx 40\) and, with some prior knowledge, up to \(n = 90\)[27].

## Diffusion Distance Pretraining

A key innovation in recent work is the use of diffusion distance as a pretraining signal for RL models[30][31][9]. Diffusion distance is defined via random walks of fixed length (\(K_{max}\)) from a selected node (often the identity), measuring the expected number of steps required to reach other nodes. Formally, the diffusion distance to node \(V\) from node \(S\) is given by summing, over all possible steps \(k \leq K_{max}\), the probabilities \(P(V, k)\) of reaching \(V\) from \(S\) in exactly \(k\) steps, normalized appropriately[31].
This method offers a computationally efficient alternative to neural network-based value function estimation, as random walks can be generated quickly and at scale. Pretraining with diffusion distance ensures that the RL network is initialized with meaningful targets, mitigating issues associated with random weight initialization and accelerating convergence[30][32].
Additionally, non-backtracking random walks are often employed, as they better reflect the true graph distance and benefit from faster mixing properties, thus improving the quality of the training signal[32].

## Integration with Heuristic Search

Once trained, RL models or diffusion distance estimators are integrated into greedy or beam search algorithms for actual pathfinding. The process involves evaluating all neighboring nodes and advancing to the one predicted to be closest to the target, iterating until the destination is reached[29]. The terminology and conceptual framework of reinforcement learning can be mapped directly onto graph-theoretic and group-theoretic language, as summarized in comparative tables[28].

# System Architecture and Implementation

The CayleyPy RL system is designed to address the challenging problem of pathfinding on extremely large Cayley graphs—graphs with up to 10<sup>70</sup> nodes—by leveraging modern machine learning and reinforcement learning techniques. ## Core Architecture

At its core, the CayleyPy RL system provides a modular framework for encoding, searching, and learning on permutation groups and their associated Cayley graphs. The key pathfinding task is formulated as decomposing a group element into a product of generators, analogous to finding a sequence of moves to transition from a start to a goal state in robotics, combinatorial puzzles, or games like chess and Go[23][3]. ### Graph Pathfinding Module

The main graph pathfinding module is centered around a beam search approach optimized for Cayley graphs. A notable innovation is the so-called “X-trick”: a single-line code modification that dramatically improves performance by allowing the feasible pathfinding size to reach n ≈ 100 or more, given sufficient hardware resources. Mathematically, the X-trick accelerates the mixing of random walks on these graphs, although its current effectiveness is limited to specific classes of Cayley graphs (notably LRX Cayley graphs)[10]. The beam search and X-trick are supported by fast product computations, efficient state hashing, and mechanisms for extracting unique states.

## Neural Network Models

The system supports several neural network architectures for heuristic learning and policy approximation, including feedforward perceptrons, convolutional neural networks (CNNs), and transformers[33][34].

### Perceptron and CNN Variants

Empirical results suggest that deeper neural networks are more effective than wider ones, particularly for larger group sizes. However, for the LRX Cayley graphs, this effect is less pronounced, and simple one-layer perceptrons with 128 neurons can achieve comparable results to deeper models. Notably, models can be trained quickly (e.g., a single epoch for S<sub>20</sub> in under a minute), and increasing the training set size yields diminishing returns[34].
CNN-based architectures utilize two 1-dimensional convolutional layers, combined with batch normalization and dropout for regularization and to mitigate overfitting. A successful variation includes temporal gated CNNs, where the initial residual block employs convolutional layers with gating (sigmoid activation), followed by stacking residual blocks with increasing dilation to expand the receptive field. These CNN models achieve reasonable solution quality, though they train more slowly than perceptrons and do not significantly outperform them for the tested group sizes (e.g., solution length for n = 20 is 216, with an ideal of 190)[33][34].

### Transformer Models

Transformer-based models, incorporating multi-head self-attention, positional encoding, feed-forward layers, and dropout, have also been explored. However, their effectiveness is limited to smaller group sizes (n ≤ 15), as they struggle to converge or generalize to longer sequences or larger graphs[33].

# Limitations and Challenges

Pathfinding and reinforcement learning (RL) on Cayley graphs present a number of theoretical and practical challenges, many of which remain open areas of research.

## Computational Complexity

Finding optimal paths, particularly the shortest paths on general finite Cayley graphs, is an NP-hard problem and in some cases even P-space complete, making it computationally intractable for large groups and graph sizes[12]. For example, the first optimal algorithm for solving the standard 3 × 3 × 3 Rubik’s Cube was only developed in 1997, requiring significant computational resources: 82 megabytes of precomputed data and up to 24 hours to solve a single instance, though later improvements reduced this to several cubes per second[12][14]. Extending such algorithms to larger groups, such as those associated with 4 × 4 × 4 or 5 × 5 × 5 Rubik’s cubes, remains a major challenge due to the exponential growth in state space and the lack of effective optimal algorithms for these cases[12][14].

## Non-optimal Algorithms and Scalability

While algorithms like the Schreier-Sims algorithm are commonly used for decomposing group elements, they are non-optimal and become impractical for large groups due to their high computational demands[12]. Even with optimized implementations and hardware resources, brute-force approaches such as breadth-first search quickly become infeasible as the group size grows[26]. Efficient heuristic methods, often inspired by RL or AI, are necessary but may not guarantee optimality or even tractable run times for very large instances[9][6].

## Estimating Diameters and "God’s Number"

A central mathematical challenge is determining the diameter of a Cayley graph for a given generating set, which corresponds to the maximum minimal number of moves needed to reach any element from the identity—also known as "God’s number" in puzzle communities[14]. Computing or even estimating the diameter for many important groups is an open and difficult problem; for higher-dimensional Rubik’s cubes and for Cayley graphs with various generating sets, only conjectures or broad bounds are available[14][26]. The problem is further complicated by the dependency of the diameter on the choice of generators, making general results elusive.

## Growth, Random Walks, and Statistical Properties

Analyzing the statistical properties of pathfinding, such as the distribution of path lengths and the behavior of random walks, reveals additional difficulties. For LRX Cayley graphs, for instance, empirical studies suggest that the growth statistics do not fit simple distributions but may instead exhibit asymmetric Gumbel-like distributions, indicating a complex intermediate regime between exponential and Gaussian behaviors[36]. Such findings suggest that classical probabilistic tools are only partially effective for understanding growth and mixing in these graphs, posing further challenges for both theoretical analysis and practical RL algorithms.

## Generalization and Pretraining

Recent approaches propose combining RL with pretraining methods based on metrics such as diffusion distance to improve efficiency, but integrating these techniques into scalable and general-purpose algorithms remains non-trivial[9]. RL agents must often "create their own training set" by exploring vast state spaces without prior knowledge, further complicating the development of effective strategies for large or highly symmetric Cayley graphs[7][9].

## Open Problems

Many questions about the structural properties of Cayley graphs, such as precise diameter growth, longest element characterization, and mixing times for random walks, are still unresolved despite computational advances[26][36][13]. Furthermore, applying RL and AI-based methods to these problems is still in its early stages, and there is considerable scope for future research to address these foundational limitations and challenges.
